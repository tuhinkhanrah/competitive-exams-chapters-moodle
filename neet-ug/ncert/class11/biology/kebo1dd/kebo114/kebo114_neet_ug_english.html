NEET UG Biology Human Physiology

Breathing and Exchange of Gases

A complete, exam-oriented tutorial covering respiratory anatomy, ventilation mechanics, gas diffusion, transport curves, neural regulation, and clinical disorders.

1. Learning Objectives and Physiological Overview

Living cells continuously oxidize simple organic substrates—such as glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids—to release metabolic energy. Because aerobic catabolism consumes molecular oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)) and generates carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)), an organism must maintain an uninterrupted inward flux of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and outward clearance of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\). The physical process of exchanging atmospheric \(\mathrm{O_2}\) with cellular \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is called breathing (commonly termed respiration).

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Compare respiratory surfaces across major invertebrate and vertebrate taxa.
  • Distinguish the conducting zone from the respiratory (exchange) zone in the human airway.
  • Explain pulmonary ventilation using Boyle's law pressure gradients and muscle actions.
  • Calculate lung volumes and capacities from spirometric data.
  • Analyze partial pressure gradients, the three-layered diffusion membrane, and gas solubility differences.
  • Interpret the oxygen-haemoglobin dissociation curve, Bohr shift, and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) transport equilibria.
  • Trace medullary and pontine neural control circuits along with major respiratory disorders.

Five Sequential Stages of Human Respiration

  1. Pulmonary Ventilation (Breathing): Bulk movement of atmospheric air into the alveoli and expulsion of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-rich alveolar air.
  2. External Diffusion: Passive movement of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) across the alveolar-capillary membrane.
  3. Gas Transport: Vascular delivery of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) from lungs to tissues and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) from tissues to lungs via blood.
  4. Internal Diffusion: Exchange of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) between systemic capillary blood and interstitial tissues.
  5. Cellular Respiration: Intracellular catabolic oxidation using \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and liberating \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

2. Comparative Respiratory Strategies Across Animals

Respiratory architecture in animals reflects both their ecological habitat (aquatic versus terrestrial) and their body-plan complexity. Simple aquatic organisms rely on direct diffusion across their outer surface, whereas larger or terrestrial animals require specialized, highly vascularized surfaces.

Earthworms (Annelids) Moist cuticle Cutaneous gas exchange

3. Functional Anatomy of the Human Respiratory System

Air enters through a pair of external nostrils above the upper lip, passes through the nasal passage into the nasal chamber, and opens into the pharynx—a shared passageway for both food and air. From the pharynx, air moves through the larynx (a cartilaginous sound box) into the trachea.

During swallowing, a thin elastic cartilaginous flap called the epiglottis covers the glottis (the opening of the larynx) to prevent food from entering the airway. The trachea runs as a straight tube down to the mid-thoracic cavity, bifurcating at the level of the 5th thoracic vertebra (T5) into right and left primary bronchi. Each primary bronchus branches repeatedly into secondary bronchi, tertiary bronchi, and finer bronchioles, terminating in very thin terminal bronchioles. Incomplete C-shaped cartilaginous rings reinforce the trachea, primary, secondary, and tertiary bronchi, and the initial bronchioles, preventing airway collapse.

Conducting Zone vs. Exchange (Respiratory) Zone
  • Conducting Part (External nostrils to terminal bronchioles): Transports atmospheric air to the alveoli, traps and clears foreign particles, humidifies incoming air, and warms it to body temperature. No gas exchange occurs here.
  • Respiratory / Exchange Part (Alveoli and alveolar ducts): Very thin, irregular-walled, richly vascularized bag-like structures where actual diffusion of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) takes place between alveolar air and capillary blood.

Thoracic Cage and Pleural Mechanics

The two lungs sit inside an anatomically airtight thoracic chamber bounded dorsally by the vertebral column, ventrally by the sternum, laterally by the ribs, and inferiorly by the dome-shaped muscular diaphragm. Each lung is enclosed by a double-layered pleura containing pleural fluid between its layers to minimize friction during lung movement. The outer (parietal) pleural membrane adheres closely to the thoracic wall, while the inner (visceral) pleural membrane contacts the lung surface. Because humans cannot directly contract or expand lung tissue, any volume change in the thoracic cavity is transmitted directly to the pulmonary cavity.

4. Mechanism of Breathing and Respiratory Volumes

Airflow into and out of the lungs obeys pressure gradients generated between the alveoli (intra-pulmonary pressure) and the atmosphere. Because pressure varies inversely with volume at constant temperature, expanding the thoracic cavity lowers intra-pulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure, drawing air inward; compressing the thoracic cavity raises intra-pulmonary pressure above atmospheric pressure, expelling air.

Feature Normal Inspiration (Active) Normal Expiration (Passive)
Diaphragm Contracts and flattens; increases thoracic volume along the antero-posterior axis. Relaxes and arches upward into a dome; decreases thoracic volume.
External Intercostal Muscles Contract, lifting ribs and sternum; increase volume along the dorso-ventral axis. Relax, allowing ribs and sternum to return to resting position.
Pulmonary Volume Increases (mirrors thoracic expansion). Decreases.
Intra-pulmonary Pressure Drops below atmospheric pressure (negative pressure gradient). Rises slightly above atmospheric pressure (positive pressure gradient).
Air Movement Atmospheric air enters the lungs. Alveolar air is expelled out of the lungs.

Additional abdominal muscles can be recruited to increase the force of inspiration and expiration during vigorous breathing. Under resting conditions, a healthy adult breathes 12–16 times per minute. Clinical assessment of pulmonary function uses a spirometer to measure respiratory air volumes (note: residual volume cannot be measured directly by simple spirometry).

5. Exchange of Gases: Partial Pressures and Diffusion Membrane

Alveoli are the primary sites of gas exchange, alongside systemic capillary beds at the tissues. Both \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) move strictly by simple diffusion down their partial pressure gradients. The pressure exerted by an individual gas in a mixture is its partial pressure, denoted \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\).

Respiratory Gas Atmospheric Air Alveoli Deoxygenated Blood Oxygenated Blood Tissues
\(p\mathrm{O_2}\) (mm Hg) 159 104 40 95 40
\(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) (mm Hg) 0.3 40 45 40 45

Three Factors Governing Diffusion Rate

  1. Partial Pressure Gradient: \(\mathrm{O_2}\) flows from alveoli (\(104\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)) into deoxygenated blood (\(40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)), and from oxygenated blood (\(95\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)) into tissues (\(40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)). Conversely, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) flows from tissues (\(45\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)) into blood (\(40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)) and from deoxygenated blood (\(45\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)) into alveoli (\(40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)).
  2. Gas Solubility: The solubility of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is 20–25 times higher than that of \(\mathrm{O_2}\). Consequently, per unit difference in partial pressure, far more \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffuses across the membrane than \(\mathrm{O_2}\)—explaining why a modest \(5\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\) gradient suffices for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) clearance.
  3. Thickness of the Diffusion Membrane: The alveolar-capillary barrier consists of three layers whose combined thickness is much less than \(1\,\mathrm{mm}\):
    • Thin squamous epithelium of the alveolus (one cell thick)
    • Endothelium of the alveolar capillary
    • Basement substance sandwiched between them (formed by the epithelial and endothelial basement membranes)

6. Transport of Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide in Blood

Blood serves as the transport medium for both respiratory gases, using distinct carrier fractions:

  • Oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)): ~97% bound reversibly to haemoglobin inside RBCs as oxyhaemoglobin; ~3% dissolved in plasma.
  • Carbon Dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)): ~70% as bicarbonate ions (\(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\)); ~20–25% bound to haemoglobin as carbamino-haemoglobin; ~7% dissolved in plasma.

Oxygen-Haemoglobin Binding and the Dissociation Curve

Haemoglobin (\(\mathrm{Hb}\)) is an iron-containing red pigment; each \(\mathrm{Hb}\) molecule binds up to four \(\mathrm{O_2}\) molecules reversibly. Plotting percentage saturation of \(\mathrm{Hb}\) against \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) yields a characteristic sigmoid (S-shaped) curve called the Oxygen Dissociation Curve. Cooperative binding among the four subunits accounts for this sigmoidal shape.

  • In Alveoli (Association favoured): High \(p\mathrm{O_2}\), low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), lower \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration (higher pH), and lower temperature promote oxyhaemoglobin formation.
  • In Tissues (Dissociation favoured): Low \(p\mathrm{O_2}\), high \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), higher \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration (lower pH), and higher temperature shift the curve to the right (Bohr effect), promoting \(\mathrm{O_2}\) release to metabolically active cells.

Under normal physiological conditions, every \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of oxygenated blood delivers ~\(5\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to the tissues.

Carbon Dioxide Transport and Carbonic Anhydrase

Around 20–25% of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) binds to amino groups of haemoglobin to form carbamino-haemoglobin. This binding depends heavily on both \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(p\mathrm{O_2}\): high \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) and low \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) in tissues favour \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) binding, whereas low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) and high \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) in alveoli cause \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) dissociation.

The majority (~70%) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is transported as bicarbonate (\(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\)). RBCs contain a very high concentration of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (with minute amounts in plasma), which rapidly catalyzes the reversible hydration of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\):

\[ \mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O \xrightleftharpoons{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}} H_2CO_3 \xrightleftharpoons{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}} HCO_3^- + H^+} \]

At the tissues (high \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\)), the equilibrium shifts right, trapping \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) as \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\). At the alveoli (low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\)), the reaction reverses to regenerate \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) for exhalation. Every \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of deoxygenated blood delivers ~\(4\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to the alveoli.

7. Neural Regulation and Respiratory Disorders

The nervous system dynamically adjusts breathing rate and depth to match tissue metabolic demand:

  • Respiratory Rhythm Centre (Medulla oblongata): Primary pacemaker region responsible for generating and maintaining the basic respiratory rhythm.
  • Pneumotaxic Centre (Pons): Moderates the medullary rhythm centre; neural signals from the pneumotaxic centre reduce the duration of inspiration, thereby increasing the respiratory rate.
  • Central Chemosensitive Area (adjacent to the medulla rhythm centre): Highly sensitive to elevated \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ions; stimulates the rhythm centre to accelerate ventilation and eliminate excess \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).
  • Peripheral Chemoreceptors (Aortic arch and Carotid artery): Also detect rising \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentrations and relay corrective signals to the medullary rhythm centre.
High-Yield NEET Fact: Role of Oxygen in Regulation

Under normal physiological conditions, the role of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) in regulating respiratory rhythm is quite insignificant. Ventilation is driven primarily by changes in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentrations.

Key Respiratory Disorders

Disorder Primary Mechanism / Pathology Key Cause / Clinical Feature
Asthma Inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles Difficulty in breathing accompanied by wheezing
Emphysema Chronic damage to alveolar walls, reducing respiratory surface area Major cause is cigarette smoking
Occupational Respiratory Disorders Excessive dust overwhelms lung defense; chronic inflammation leads to fibrosis (proliferation of fibrous tissue) Common in stone-breaking and grinding industries; prevented by protective masks

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Spirometric Volume and Capacity Calculation (Hypothetical Values)

Scenario: Assume an adult athlete undergoes spirometry and records the following hypothetical volumes: \(\mathrm{TV} = 550\,\mathrm{mL}\), \(\mathrm{IRV} = 3100\,\mathrm{mL}\), \(\mathrm{ERV} = 1150\,\mathrm{mL}\), and an estimated \(\mathrm{RV} = 1200\,\mathrm{mL}\). Calculate the athlete's Vital Capacity (VC), Functional Residual Capacity (FRC), and Total Lung Capacity (TLC).

Solution:

1. Vital Capacity is the sum of all exchangeable volumes: \[ \mathrm{VC} = \mathrm{IRV} + \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{ERV} = 3100 + 550 + 1150 = 4800\,\mathrm{mL} \]

2. Functional Residual Capacity is the air remaining after a normal expiration: \[ \mathrm{FRC} = \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{RV} = 1150 + 1200 = 2350\,\mathrm{mL} \]

3. Total Lung Capacity includes VC plus RV: \[ \mathrm{TLC} = \mathrm{VC} + \mathrm{RV} = 4800 + 1200 = 6000\,\mathrm{mL} \]

Worked Example 2: Minute Ventilation and Hourly Air Exchange (Hypothetical Values)

Scenario: Suppose a resting student breathes at a hypothetical rate of \(14\,\mathrm{breaths/min}\) with a tidal volume of \(500\,\mathrm{mL}\). Calculate the volume of air inspired per minute (minute ventilation) and per hour.

Solution:

Minute ventilation: \[ \text{Volume per minute} = 500\,\mathrm{mL/breath} \times 14\,\mathrm{breaths/min} = 7000\,\mathrm{mL/min} = 7\,\mathrm{L/min} \]

Hourly tidal volume: \[ \text{Volume per hour} = 7\,\mathrm{L/min} \times 60\,\mathrm{min/h} = 420\,\mathrm{L/h}\; (420{,}000\,\mathrm{mL/h}) \]

Worked Example 3: Interpreting Curve Shifts in Exercising Muscle

Scenario: During intense sprinting, skeletal muscle generates excess lactic acid and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), raising local temperature and lowering tissue pH. How does this alter the oxygen dissociation curve in muscle capillaries?

Solution:

Elevated \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), increased \(\mathrm{H^+}\) (lower pH), and higher temperature all decrease haemoglobin's affinity for \(\mathrm{O_2}\), shifting the oxygen dissociation curve to the right. Consequently, at any given tissue \(p\mathrm{O_2}\), oxyhaemoglobin dissociates more readily, delivering extra \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to the working muscle.

10. Common Pitfalls and NEET Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Axis Confusion: Diaphragm contraction expands the thorax antero-posteriorly; external intercostal contraction expands it dorso-ventrally—never reverse these two.
  • Residual Volume in Spirometry: \(\mathrm{RV}\) and capacities containing \(\mathrm{RV}\) (\(\mathrm{FRC}\) and \(\mathrm{TLC}\)) cannot be measured directly by a simple spirometer.
  • \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) vs. \(\mathrm{O_2}\) Regulation: Remember that \(\mathrm{O_2}\) plays an insignificant role in normal respiratory rhythm regulation; chemoreceptors respond primarily to \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\).
  • Bicarbonate vs. Carbamino-\(\mathrm{Hb}\): ~70% of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) travels as \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\), while only 20–25% travels as carbamino-haemoglobin.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Trachea bifurcates at T5; cartilaginous rings extend down to initial bronchioles.
  • Conducting zone = nostrils to terminal bronchioles; Exchange zone = alveolar ducts + alveoli.
  • Normal breathing rate = 12–16 breaths/min; \(\mathrm{TV} \approx 500\,\mathrm{mL}\).
  • \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) solubility is 20–25× greater than \(\mathrm{O_2}\); diffusion membrane thickness < \(1\,\mathrm{mm}\).
  • \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) oxygenated blood delivers ~\(5\,\mathrm{mL}\) \(\mathrm{O_2}\); \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) deoxygenated blood delivers ~\(4\,\mathrm{mL}\) \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).
  • Medulla = rhythm centre; Pons = pneumotaxic centre (shortens inspiration).

11. Concise Final Recap

Human respiration integrates mechanical ventilation, passive gas diffusion, reversible blood transport, and chemosensory neural feedback. Thoracic expansion by the diaphragm and external intercostals creates negative intra-pulmonary pressure for inspiration, while passive relaxation drives expiration. Across a three-layered alveolar-capillary membrane thinner than \(1\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffuse down their partial pressure gradients, aided by \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)'s high solubility. Haemoglobin transports 97% of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) along a sigmoidal dissociation curve tuned by local \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{H^+}\), and temperature, while carbonic anhydrase converts 70% of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) into bicarbonate. Finally, medullary and pontine centres monitor \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\) levels to keep alveolar ventilation matched to cellular metabolism.
