NEET UG Biology Cell Biochemistry & Enzymology

Biomolecules: Chemical Foundations of Life

A complete, standalone exam-oriented tutorial covering cellular chemical analysis, micromolecules, biomacromolecules, protein architecture, and enzyme kinetics.

1. Learning Objectives & Syllabus Scope

Every living cell—whether bacterial, plant, or animal—operates through a shared chemical vocabulary. For NEET UG preparation, this topic bridges structural organic chemistry with cellular physiology. After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between elemental abundances in living tissues and non-living matter, and explain the acid-fractionation and ash-analysis experiments.
  • Classify cellular compounds into micromolecules (acid-soluble pool) and biomacromolecules (acid-insoluble fraction), including why lipids behave as an exception.
  • Identify the structures, ionizable states (zwitterions), and functional classifications of amino acids, lipids, sugars, and nucleotides.
  • Differentiate primary and secondary metabolites with their biological and commercial examples.
  • Describe the four hierarchical levels of protein structure, polysaccharide linkages, and polynucleotide composition.
  • Explain enzyme catalysis via activation energy lowering, substrate saturation kinetics (\(V_{\max}\) and \(K_m\)), competitive inhibition, the six enzyme classes, and cofactor types.

2. Probing Cellular Chemistry: Elemental & Fractionation Analysis

2.1 Living Matter vs. Non-Living Matter

Qualitative elemental analysis reveals that living tissues and the Earth's crust contain the same chemical elements. However, quantitative comparison shows a sharp contrast: living organisms are vastly enriched in carbon (C) and hydrogen (H) relative to other elements, whereas the Earth's crust is dominated by oxygen and silicon.

2.2 Acid Fractionation & Destructive Ash Analysis

To separate organic biomolecules by size and solubility, a living tissue (such as liver or a leaf) is ground in trichloroacetic acid (\(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH}\)) using a mortar and pestle to form a thick slurry. Straining this slurry through cheesecloth or cotton yields two fractions:

  • Acid-Soluble Pool (Filtrate): Contains small organic compounds (molecular weight roughly \(18\text{ to }800\,\mathrm{Da}\)) along with soluble inorganic ions such as sulphate (\(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\)) and phosphate (\(\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}\)). It roughly represents the cytoplasmic composition.
  • Acid-Insoluble Fraction (Retentate): Contains true polymeric macromolecules—proteins, nucleic acids, and polysaccharides (molecular weight \(\ge 10{,}000\,\mathrm{Da}\))—plus membrane-derived lipids.

To detect inorganic elements specifically, a destructive method is used. A fresh tissue sample is weighed (wet weight) and dried in an oven until all water evaporates, leaving the dry weight. Complete combustion oxidizes all carbon compounds into gaseous \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and water vapour, leaving behind ash, which contains inorganic elements such as calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)), magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\)), sodium (\(\mathrm{Na^+}\)), and potassium (\(\mathrm{K^+}\)).

3. Micromolecules: Building Blocks & Metabolites

Compounds in the acid-soluble pool have molecular weights below \(1{,}000\,\mathrm{Da}\) and are termed micromolecules (or simply biomolecules). They serve as monomers for larger polymers or as metabolic intermediates.

3.1 Amino Acids & Zwitterionic Behaviour

Protein-forming amino acids are α-amino acids: substituted methanes in which the central α-carbon is bonded to four groups—a hydrogen atom (\(-\mathrm{H}\)), a carboxyl group (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)), an amino group (\(-\mathrm{NH_2}\)), and a variable side chain (\(-\mathrm{R}\)). Although many amino acids exist in nature, only 20 types occur in proteins:

  • Simplest R-groups: Glycine (\(\mathrm{R} = -\mathrm{H}\)), Alanine (\(\mathrm{R} = -\mathrm{CH_3}\)), and Serine (\(\mathrm{R} = -\mathrm{CH_2OH}\)).
  • Charge-based classes: Acidic (e.g., glutamic acid), basic (e.g., lysine), and neutral (e.g., valine).
  • Aromatic amino acids: Tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan.
  • Nutritional classes: Non-essential amino acids can be synthesized by the human body, whereas essential amino acids must be obtained from dietary proteins.

Because both \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) and \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) groups are ionizable, an amino acid changes its electrical state with solution pH. At a specific intermediate pH, it exists as a dipolar ion carrying both a positive and a negative charge—called a zwitterion.

3.2 Lipids: Water-Insoluble & Non-Polymeric

Lipids are generally insoluble in water and include several structural types:

  • Fatty Acids: A carboxyl group attached to an \(\mathrm{R}\) group containing 1 to 19 carbons. Including the carboxyl carbon, palmitic acid has 16 carbons and arachidonic acid has 20 carbons. Fatty acids may be saturated (no \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds) or unsaturated (one or more \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds).
  • Glycerides: Glycerol is trihydroxypropane. Fatty acids esterify with glycerol to form monoglycerides, diglycerides, and triglycerides. Based on melting point, they are termed fats (higher melting point, solid at room temperature) or oils (lower melting point, e.g., gingelly oil, which stays liquid in winter).
  • Phospholipids & Complex Lipids: Phospholipids contain phosphorus and a phosphorylated organic group; lecithin is a classic membrane phospholipid. Neural tissues contain lipids with even more complex structures.
High-Yield NEET Exception: Why Are Lipids in the Acid-Insoluble Fraction?

Lipids have molecular weights below \(800\,\mathrm{Da}\) and are not true polymers. However, when tissue is ground in trichloroacetic acid, water-insoluble cell membranes break into fragments that seal into tiny vesicles. These vesicles cannot pass through the filter and therefore separate into the acid-insoluble (macromolecular) fraction alongside true macromolecules.

3.3 Nitrogenous Bases, Nucleosides & Nucleotides

Living cells contain heterocyclic nitrogenous bases classified into two ring families: purines (adenine and guanine) and pyrimidines (cytosine, uracil, and thymine).

  • Nucleoside = Nitrogenous base + Pentose sugar (ribose or 2'-deoxyribose). Examples: adenosine, guanosine, thymidine, uridine, cytidine.
  • Nucleotide = Nucleoside + Phosphate group (esterified to the sugar). Examples: adenylic acid, thymidylic acid, guanylic acid, uridylic acid, cytidylic acid.

3.4 Primary vs. Secondary Metabolites

Primary metabolites (amino acids, sugars, nucleotides, fatty acids) have identifiable roles in normal physiological processes across all organisms. Secondary metabolites—found predominantly in plants, fungi, and microbes—do not yet have fully understood roles in the host organism's core physiology, though many provide ecological advantages and immense benefit to human welfare.

4. Biomacromolecules: The Polymers of Life

The acid-insoluble fraction contains only three true polymeric macromolecules: polysaccharides, nucleic acids, and proteins. Together with water (the single most abundant chemical in cells) and ions, they make up the average cellular mass:

Cellular Component % of Total Cellular Mass
Water 70–90%
Proteins 10–15%
Nucleic acids 5–7%
Carbohydrates 3%
Lipids 2%
Ions 1%

4.1 Polysaccharides

Polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharides linked together like threads. A polymer built from a single type of monomer repeating \(n\) times is a homopolymer, whereas one built from multiple monomer types is a heteropolymer.

  • Cellulose: A glucose homopolymer forming plant cell walls (and paper pulp/cotton fibre). It lacks complex helices and therefore cannot trap \(\mathrm{I_2}\) molecules.
  • Starch: The storage polysaccharide of plants. It forms helical secondary structures capable of holding \(\mathrm{I_2}\) molecules inside the helix, producing a characteristic blue colour.
  • Glycogen: The branched energy-storage polysaccharide of animals. In a glycogen chain, the right end is the reducing end and the left end is the non-reducing end.
  • Inulin: A homopolymer of fructose.
  • Chitin: A complex polysaccharide forming arthropod exoskeletons, built from chemically modified amino-sugars (such as glucosamine and N-acetyl galactosamine). Such complex polysaccharides are mostly homopolymers.

4.2 Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids are polynucleotides. Each nucleotide unit consists of a heterocyclic nitrogenous base, a pentose monosaccharide, and a phosphate group. Nucleic acids containing 2'-deoxyribose are called DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), whereas those containing ribose are called RNA (ribonucleic acid). Both function as genetic material, carrying hereditary information from parent to progeny.

4.3 Proteins & Their Four Structural Levels

Proteins are linear polypeptides in which amino acids are joined by peptide bonds. Because they are built from 20 different amino acids, proteins are always heteropolymers. In the animal world, collagen is the most abundant protein; across the entire biosphere, RuBisCO (Ribulose bisphosphate Carboxylase-Oxygenase) is the most abundant protein.

Protein Primary Biological Function
Collagen Intercellular ground substance
Trypsin Digestive enzyme
Insulin Hormone regulating blood glucose
Antibody Fights infectious agents
Receptor Sensory reception (smell, taste, hormones)
GLUT-4 Enables glucose transport into cells

Biologists describe protein architecture at four hierarchical levels:

  1. Primary Structure: The linear sequence (positional information) of amino acids. By convention, the first amino acid on the left is the N-terminal amino acid, and the last amino acid on the right is the C-terminal amino acid.
  2. Secondary Structure: Local folding of the polypeptide backbone into regular patterns such as the α-helix (only right-handed helices are observed in proteins) and the β-pleated sheet.
  3. Tertiary Structure: The long polypeptide chain folds upon itself like a hollow woolen ball, stabilized by hydrogen bonds and disulphide bonds. This 3D conformation creates crevices and pockets and is absolutely necessary for most biological activities of proteins.
  4. Quaternary Structure: The spatial arrangement of multiple folded polypeptide subunits in a multi-subunit protein. For example, adult human haemoglobin (Hb) consists of 4 subunits: two identical α-subunits and two identical β-subunits (\(\alpha_2\beta_2\)).

5. Enzymes: Biological Catalysts & Kinetics

5.1 Nature of Enzymes & Thermal Stability

Almost all enzymes are proteins, though a few nucleic acids called ribozymes also exhibit catalytic power. When a protein folds into its tertiary structure, its polypeptide chain criss-crosses to form crevices or pockets; one such pocket is the active site, where the substrate binds.

Unlike inorganic catalysts that operate efficiently at high temperatures and pressures, most enzymes denature above \(40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). A notable exception is enzymes isolated from thermophilic organisms inhabiting hot vents and sulphur springs—these retain catalytic activity up to \(80\text{–}90\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

5.2 Reaction Rates & Catalytic Power

A chemical reaction involves breaking existing bonds and forming new ones (as opposed to physical changes in shape or state of matter). The rate of a reaction is the amount of product formed per unit time:

\[ \text{Rate} = \frac{\delta P}{\delta t} \]

As a general rule of thumb, reaction rate doubles or halves for every \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) change in temperature. Enzymes accelerate reactions dramatically. For example, the hydration of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to carbonic acid (\(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\)) produces only about \(200\) molecules per hour without a catalyst, but in the presence of cytoplasmic carbonic anhydrase, it produces roughly \(600{,}000\) molecules per second—an acceleration of about 10 million times.

5.3 Activation Energy & The Catalytic Cycle

During conversion of a substrate (\(\mathrm{S}\)) to a product (\(\mathrm{P}\)), the substrate must pass through a high-energy, unstable transition state. The difference in average energy content between the substrate and the transition state is the activation energy. Enzymes speed up reactions by lowering this activation energy barrier, without altering the overall energy difference between \(\mathrm{S}\) and \(\mathrm{P}\) (if \(\mathrm{P}\) is at a lower energy level than \(\mathrm{S}\), the reaction is exothermic).

\[ \mathrm{E} + \mathrm{S} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{ES} \longrightarrow \mathrm{EP} \longrightarrow \mathrm{E} + \mathrm{P} \]

The four-step catalytic cycle proceeds as follows: (1) Substrate binds to the enzyme's active site; (2) Binding induces a conformational change in the enzyme (tighter fit); (3) The active site breaks/forms substrate bonds to yield an enzyme-product (\(\mathrm{EP}\)) complex; (4) Products are released, freeing the unchanged enzyme for another cycle.

5.4 Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity & Inhibition

  • Temperature & pH: Enzymes show maximal activity at an optimum temperature and optimum pH, declining on either side. Low temperature preserves the enzyme in a temporarily inactive state, whereas high temperature permanently destroys activity by heat denaturation.
  • Substrate Concentration (\([\mathrm{S}]\)): Reaction velocity initially rises with \([\mathrm{S}]\) and then plateaus at a maximum velocity (\(V_{\max}\)) because all enzyme active sites become saturated. The substrate concentration at which reaction velocity reaches half of maximum (\(\frac{V_{\max}}{2}\)) is the Michaelis constant, \(K_m\).
  • Competitive Inhibition: A chemical structurally similar to the substrate competes for the active site, reducing enzyme activity. A classic example is malonate inhibiting succinic dehydrogenase by mimicking succinate. Competitive inhibitors are also used to control bacterial pathogens.

5.5 Classification of Enzymes (6 Classes)

Enzymes are divided into 6 major classes (each with 4–13 subclasses) and designated by a four-digit Enzyme Commission number:

Class Reaction Catalysed General Reaction Pattern
1. Oxidoreductases / Dehydrogenases Oxidoreduction between two substrates \(\mathrm{S_{reduced}} + \mathrm{S'_{oxidised}} \longrightarrow \mathrm{S_{oxidised}} + \mathrm{S'_{reduced}}\)
2. Transferases Transfer of a group \(G\) (other than H) between two substrates \(\mathrm{S-G} + \mathrm{S'} \longrightarrow \mathrm{S} + \mathrm{S'-G}\)
3. Hydrolases Hydrolysis of ester, ether, peptide, glycosidic, \(\mathrm{C-C}\), \(\mathrm{C-halide}\), or \(\mathrm{P-N}\) bonds Cleavage using \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)
4. Lyases Removal of groups by mechanisms other than hydrolysis, leaving double bonds \(\mathrm{X-C-C-Y} \longrightarrow \mathrm{X-Y} + \mathrm{C=C}\)
5. Isomerases Inter-conversion of optical, geometric, or positional isomers \(\mathrm{A} \longrightarrow \mathrm{A'}\) (isomer)
6. Ligases Linking together of two compounds (joining \(\mathrm{C-O}\), \(\mathrm{C-S}\), \(\mathrm{C-N}\), \(\mathrm{P-O}\) bonds) \(\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{B} \longrightarrow \mathrm{A-B}\)

5.6 Cofactors

When an enzyme requires a non-protein constituent to become catalytically active, the protein portion is called the apoenzyme and the non-protein portion is the cofactor (removing the cofactor abolishes catalytic activity). Cofactors fall into three categories:

  • Prosthetic Groups: Organic compounds tightly bound to the apoenzyme. Example: Haem is the prosthetic group at the active site of peroxidase and catalase (which break down \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) to water and oxygen).
  • Co-enzymes: Organic compounds that associate transiently with the apoenzyme during catalysis and often serve multiple enzymes. Many contain vitamins as essential components—for instance, NAD and NADP contain the vitamin niacin.
  • Metal Ions: Form coordination bonds both with side chains at the active site and with the substrate. Example: Zinc (\(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\)) is a cofactor for the proteolytic enzyme carboxypeptidase.

6. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Interpreting Cellular Fractionation

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): A biology student grinds \(10\,\mathrm{g}\) of spinach leaf tissue in trichloroacetic acid and filters the slurry. She isolates four pure compounds from the two fractions: Compound W (molecular weight \(180\,\mathrm{Da}\)), Compound X (molecular weight \(750\,\mathrm{Da}\), water-insoluble membrane component), Compound Y (molecular weight \(45{,}000\,\mathrm{Da}\), contains peptide bonds), and Compound Z (molecular weight \(120{,}000\,\mathrm{Da}\), yields blue colour with \(\mathrm{I_2}\)). Identify which compounds appear in the retentate and which are true macromolecules.

Solution:

  • Compound W (\(180\,\mathrm{Da}\)): Being small and acid-soluble (like a monosaccharide), it passes into the filtrate (acid-soluble pool).
  • Compound X (\(750\,\mathrm{Da}\), membrane lipid): Although its molecular weight is under \(800\,\mathrm{Da}\) and it is not a true macromolecule, it forms insoluble membrane vesicles upon grinding and is therefore recovered in the retentate.
  • Compound Y (\(45{,}000\,\mathrm{Da}\), protein): A true polymeric macromolecule; recovered in the retentate.
  • Compound Z (\(120{,}000\,\mathrm{Da}\), starch): A true helical polysaccharide macromolecule; recovered in the retentate.

Conclusion: Compounds X, Y, and Z appear in the retentate, but only Y and Z are true biomacromolecules.

Worked Example 2: Enzyme Kinetics & Competitive Inhibition

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): An enzyme-catalysed reaction shows a maximum velocity \(V_{\max} = 80\,\mathrm{µmol\,min^{-1}}\) and reaches a velocity of \(40\,\mathrm{µmol\,min^{-1}}\) at a substrate concentration of \(5\,\mathrm{mM}\). If a competitive inhibitor is added, what happens to the apparent \(K_m\) and \(V_{\max}\)?

Solution:

By definition, \(K_m\) is the substrate concentration at which reaction velocity equals \(\frac{V_{\max}}{2}\). Here, \(\frac{80}{2} = 40\,\mathrm{µmol\,min^{-1}}\), so the normal \(K_m = 5\,\mathrm{mM}\).

A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for the active site. At very high substrate concentrations, the substrate outcompetes the inhibitor, so \(V_{\max}\) remains unchanged at \(80\,\mathrm{µmol\,min^{-1}}\). However, more substrate is required to reach half-maximal velocity, meaning the apparent \(K_m\) increases (greater than \(5\,\mathrm{mM}\)).

Worked Example 3: Temperature Rule & Enzyme Denaturation

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): An uncatalysed chemical reaction proceeds at a rate of \(12\,\mathrm{units\,s^{-1}}\) at \(20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Using the general \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) rule of thumb, estimate its rate at \(40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Would a typical human cellular enzyme follow this same doubling indefinitely up to \(70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)?

Solution:

From \(20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), there are two \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) increments. Since the rate roughly doubles for every \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) rise:

\[ \text{Rate at } 40\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \approx 12 \times 2^2 = 48\,\mathrm{units\,s^{-1}}. \]

Biological Caveat: A typical human enzyme does not keep doubling up to \(70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\); above roughly \(40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), heat disrupts its tertiary structure (denaturation) and catalytic activity drops sharply—unlike thermophilic bacterial enzymes that remain stable at \(80\text{–}90\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following elements exhibits the highest percentage by weight in the human body while also being the most abundant element in the Earth's crust?

  • (A) Carbon
  • (B) Oxygen
  • (C) Silicon
  • (D) Hydrogen
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Oxygen accounts for \(65.0\%\) of human body weight and \(46.6\%\) of the Earth's crust weight—making it the most abundant element by weight in both. Silicon (\(27.7\%\) in crust) is negligible in the human body, while carbon and hydrogen are enriched in living tissue relative to the crust.

  • (B) Serine; \(-1\)
  • (C) Alanine; \(0\)

Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: When \(\mathrm{R} = -\mathrm{CH_3}\), the amino acid is alanine (glycine has \(\mathrm{R} = -\mathrm{H}\), serine has \(\mathrm{R} = -\mathrm{CH_2OH}\)). In the zwitterionic form (\(\mathrm{^+H_3N-CH(R)-COO^-}\)), the \(+1\) and \(-1\) charges balance, giving a net charge of \(0\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

An enzyme catalyses the removal of a chemical group from a substrate without hydrolysis, resulting in the formation of a double bond. To which enzyme class does it belong?

  • (A) Hydrolases
  • (B) Transferases
  • (C) Lyases
  • (D) Ligases
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Lyases catalyse the non-hydrolytic removal of groups from substrates, leaving behind double bonds (\(\mathrm{C=C}\)). Hydrolases cleave bonds using water, transferases move groups between substrates, and ligases join two molecules.

Practice Question 12 (Statement-Based)

Consider the following statements about enzyme catalysis:
Statement I: Enzymes increase the rate of an exothermic reaction by lowering the potential energy of the product.
Statement II: In competitive inhibition, malonate inhibits succinic dehydrogenase because it closely resembles succinate in molecular structure.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.

Worked Solution: Enzymes do not alter the potential energy of either the substrate or the product; they accelerate reactions solely by lowering the activation energy (the energy of the transition state). Statement II is a classic example of competitive inhibition.

8. Common Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Lipids as macromolecules: Lipids are not polymers and weigh \(<800\,\mathrm{Da}\); they appear in the acid-insoluble fraction only due to membrane vesicle formation.
  • Cellulose vs. Starch with Iodine: Starch traps \(\mathrm{I_2}\) in its helices (blue colour); cellulose lacks complex helices and does not.
  • Glycogen ends: Right end = reducing; left end = non-reducing. Do not swap them.
  • Nucleoside vs. Nucleotide: "-sine" / "-dine" names (adenosine, uridine) are nucleosides (no phosphate); "-ylic acid" names (adenylic acid, uridylic acid) are nucleotides (with phosphate).
  • Prosthetic group vs. Co-enzyme: Both are organic cofactors, but prosthetic groups (e.g., haem) are tightly bound, whereas co-enzymes (e.g., NAD/NADP) associate transiently.

Revision Checklist

  • Can you recall the top 3 elements in the human body (O > C > H) vs. Earth's crust (O > Si > Al/Fe/Ca)?
  • Can you draw/identify glycine (\(-\mathrm{H}\)), alanine (\(-\mathrm{CH_3}\)), and serine (\(-\mathrm{CH_2OH}\)) and explain zwitterion formation?
  • Do you remember the cellular mass order: Water (70–90%) > Proteins (10–15%) > Nucleic acids (5–7%) > Carbohydrates (3%) > Lipids (2%) > Ions (1%)?
  • Can you distinguish primary, secondary (right-handed α-helix), tertiary (active site, biological activity), and quaternary (Hb: \(2\alpha + 2\beta\)) protein structures?
  • Can you explain how enzymes lower activation energy without changing \(\Delta G\) between S and P, and define \(V_{\max}\) and \(K_m\)?

9. Concise Final Recap

Living tissues share the same elements as non-living matter but are enriched in carbon and hydrogen, with water being the most abundant cellular molecule. Acid fractionation separates small micromolecules (\(<1{,}000\,\mathrm{Da}\); amino acids, sugars, nucleotides) into the soluble filtrate from polymeric biomacromolecules (proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides) and vesicle-forming lipids in the insoluble retentate. Proteins are heteropolymers of 20 amino acids whose 3D tertiary structure enables biological function—including enzyme catalysis, which accelerates biochemical reactions millions of times by lowering activation energy, operating optimally at specific pH and temperature, and often relying on non-protein cofactors.
