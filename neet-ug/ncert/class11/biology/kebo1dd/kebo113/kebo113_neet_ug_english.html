Plant Growth and Development

Complete Concept Tutorial, Quantitative Growth Models, Phytohormone Mechanisms, Worked Examples, and NEET-Style Practice

1. Learning Objectives and Overview

Every flowering plant begins life as a single-celled zygote and follows a strictly ordered developmental programme—germination, vegetative expansion, reproduction, senescence, and death. For NEET UG, this chapter tests both conceptual clarity (how meristems, cellular differentiation, and environmental plasticity operate) and precise factual recall (chemical classes, discoveries, and agricultural applications of the five major plant growth regulators).

What You Will Master

  • Distinguishing true biological growth from reversible physical swelling, and understanding why plant growth is termed open and indeterminate.
  • Identifying the cellular characteristics of the meristematic, elongation, and maturation zones, and applying arithmetic and geometric growth equations.
  • Calculating and comparing Absolute Growth Rate (AGR) and Relative Growth Rate (RGR).
  • Tracing the relationships among differentiation, dedifferentiation, redifferentiation, and developmental plasticity (heterophylly).
  • Mastering the chemical nature, historical discovery, physiological effects, and antagonistic/synergistic interactions of Auxins, Gibberellins, Cytokinins, Ethylene, and Abscisic Acid (ABA).

2. Fundamentals of Plant Growth

2.1 Defining Growth and Its Measurability

In biology, growth is defined as an irreversible, permanent increase in the size of an organ, its parts, or even an individual cell. True growth is always coupled with metabolic processes (both anabolic synthesis and catabolic energy release) that occur at the expense of cellular energy (ATP).

Crucial Distinction: When a dry piece of wood is submerged in water, it swells. However, this swelling is driven by physical imbibition and reverses upon drying without metabolic energy expenditure—therefore, it is not biological growth. In contrast, the expansion of a young leaf is permanent and metabolically driven, qualifying as true growth.

At the cellular level, growth primarily reflects an increase in protoplasmic content. Because protoplasm cannot be weighed directly inside living tissues, biologists measure proportional parameters:

  • Cell Number: A single maize root apical meristem can produce more than 17,500 new cells per hour.
  • Cell Size / Volume: Cells in a developing watermelon can enlarge up to 3,50,000 times their original volume.
  • Length: Used to track the growth of cylindrical structures such as a pollen tube or a root axis.
  • Surface Area: Used to quantify growth in flat organs such as a dorsiventral leaf.
  • Fresh Weight & Dry Weight: Fresh weight fluctuates with water content, whereas dry weight measures true organic biomass accumulation.

2.2 Open and Indeterminate Growth

Unlike most animals, plants retain the capacity for unlimited growth throughout their lifespan. This unique feature—called the open form of growth—is possible because specialised regions called meristems continuously divide and add new cells to the plant body. While meristematic cells self-perpetuate, their daughter cells soon lose the ability to divide and mature into permanent tissues.

  • Primary Growth: Driven by the Root Apical Meristem (RAM) and Shoot Apical Meristem (SAM) (and intercalary meristems in grasses), which elongate the plant along its longitudinal axis.
  • Secondary Growth: In dicotyledonous plants and gymnosperms, lateral meristems—specifically the vascular cambium and cork cambium—appear later in life to increase the girth (thickness) of stems and roots.

Note that while the plant body as a whole shows indeterminate growth, individual organs such as leaves, flowers, and fruits exhibit determinate growth—they reach a genetically fixed dimension, mature, and eventually fall off.

2.3 Three Phases of Growth

Along a growing root or shoot tip, growth progresses sequentially through three distinct zones:

Growth Phase Location Relative to Apex Key Cellular Characteristics
1. Meristematic Phase At the extreme root and shoot apices Cells divide continuously; rich in protoplasm; possess large, conspicuous nuclei; cell walls are thin, primary, and cellulosic with abundant plasmodesmatal connections.
2. Elongation Phase Proximal (just behind) the meristematic zone Marked by increased vacuolation, rapid cell enlargement driven by water uptake, and deposition of new cell wall material.
3. Maturation Phase Further proximal to the elongation zone Cells attain maximal size, undergo secondary wall thickening, and show protoplasmic modifications suited for specialised functions.

2.4 Essential Conditions for Growth

  • Water: Maintains cell turgidity required for extension growth and serves as the aqueous medium for enzymatic reactions.
  • Oxygen: Required for aerobic respiration to release metabolic energy (ATP) needed for synthesis and transport.
  • Nutrients: Both macro- and micro-essential elements act as raw materials for protoplasm synthesis and energy metabolism.
  • Temperature: Every plant species has an optimum temperature range; deviations beyond this range impair enzyme activity and survival.
  • Environmental Signals: Light and gravity regulate specific developmental phases and tropic orientations.

3. Quantitative Growth Analysis: Rates and Curves

The increased growth per unit time is called the growth rate. Depending on how daughter cells behave after mitosis, growth follows either an arithmetic or a geometric pattern.

3.1 Arithmetic vs. Geometric Growth

  • Arithmetic Growth: Following mitosis, only one daughter cell retains the capacity to divide, while the other differentiates and matures. A root elongating at a constant rate is the classic example. Plotting length against time yields a straight line:
    \[ L_t = L_0 + r t \] where \(L_t\) = length at time \(t\), \(L_0\) = initial length at time zero, and \(r\) = growth rate (elongation per unit time).
  • Geometric (Exponential) Growth: Following mitosis, both progeny cells retain the ability to divide and continue doing so. In a natural environment with finite nutrients, growth starts slowly (lag phase), accelerates exponentially (log or exponential phase), and eventually slows down due to nutrient limitation (stationary phase), producing a characteristic S-shaped (sigmoid) curve.
    \[ W_1 = W_0 e^{rt} \] where \(W_1\) = final size (weight, height, or cell number), \(W_0\) = initial size, \(r\) = relative growth rate (also called the efficiency index), \(t\) = time, and \(e\) = base of natural logarithms.
    Because \(W_1\) is directly proportional to \(W_0\), the final size depends heavily on the initial size.
  • Embryo Development: During early embryogenesis from a zygote, all cells divide (geometric phase), and later the embryo transitions to a phase where only specific meristematic cells divide (arithmetic phase).

3.2 Absolute Growth Rate (AGR) vs. Relative Growth Rate (RGR)

Quantitative comparisons between two growing organs or systems are made using two distinct metrics:

\[ \text{Absolute Growth Rate (AGR)} = \frac{\text{Total Growth}}{\text{Time Interval}} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta t} \] \[ \text{Relative Growth Rate (RGR)} = \frac{\text{Total Growth}}{\text{Initial Size} \times \text{Time Interval}} = \frac{\Delta S}{S_0 \times \Delta t} \]
Worked Example 1: Comparing AGR and RGR (Hypothetical Values)

Suppose two leaves are monitored over a period of \(5\,\mathrm{days}\) (assumed values): Leaf X has an initial surface area of \(8\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) and expands to \(16\,\mathrm{cm^2}\). Leaf Y has an initial surface area of \(40\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) and expands to \(48\,\mathrm{cm^2}\). Compare their absolute and relative growth rates.

Solution:

  • Absolute increase in area:
    For Leaf X: \(\Delta A_X = 16 - 8 = 8\,\mathrm{cm^2}\); \(\text{AGR}_X = \frac{8\,\mathrm{cm^2}}{5\,\mathrm{days}} = 1.6\,\mathrm{cm^2/day}\).
    For Leaf Y: \(\Delta A_Y = 48 - 40 = 8\,\mathrm{cm^2}\); \(\text{AGR}_Y = \frac{8\,\mathrm{cm^2}}{5\,\mathrm{days}} = 1.6\,\mathrm{cm^2/day}\).
    Both leaves have the same AGR.
  • Relative growth rate (percentage or fractional increase per unit initial area):
    For Leaf X: \(\text{RGR}_X = \frac{8}{8 \times 5} = 0.20\,\mathrm{day^{-1}}\) (a \(100\%\) total increase over 5 days).
    For Leaf Y: \(\text{RGR}_Y = \frac{8}{40 \times 5} = 0.04\,\mathrm{day^{-1}}\) (a \(20\%\) total increase over 5 days).

Conclusion: Even though both leaves gained the same absolute area, the smaller leaf (Leaf X) exhibits a five-fold higher relative growth rate.

Worked Example 2: Arithmetic Growth Calculation (Hypothetical Values)

A seedling root (assumed values) has an initial length of \(3.5\,\mathrm{cm}\) at time \(t = 0\) and elongates arithmetically at a constant rate of \(0.4\,\mathrm{cm/day}\). What will be its length after \(12\,\mathrm{days}\)?

Solution:

Using the arithmetic growth equation \(L_t = L_0 + rt\):

\[ L_{12} = 3.5\,\mathrm{cm} + (0.4\,\mathrm{cm/day} \times 12\,\mathrm{days}) = 3.5 + 4.8 = 8.3\,\mathrm{cm} \]

4. Differentiation, Dedifferentiation, Redifferentiation, and Development

4.1 Cellular Transitions

  • Differentiation: Cells derived from root apical meristems, shoot apical meristems, and cambium undergo structural changes in their cell walls and protoplasm to mature and perform specialised functions. For example, to form a tracheary element (xylem vessel/tracheid), a cell loses its protoplasm and develops a strong, elastic, lignocellulosic secondary cell wall capable of transporting water under extreme tension.
  • Open Differentiation: Just as plant growth is open, differentiation is also open—cells arising from the same meristem can differentiate into completely different structures depending on their final location. For instance, cells positioned away from the root apical meristem form the root cap, whereas cells pushed to the periphery mature into the epidermis.
  • Dedifferentiation: Living differentiated cells that have lost the capacity to divide can regain the ability to divide under specific conditions. Key examples include the formation of interfascicular cambium and cork cambium from fully differentiated parenchyma cells.
  • Redifferentiation: Meristematic cells produced by dedifferentiation divide to form new cells that once again lose the capacity to divide and mature to perform specialised functions (e.g., secondary xylem, secondary phloem, cork, and secondary cortex in woody dicots).

4.2 Development and Plasticity

Development encompasses all changes an organism undergoes during its life cycle from seed germination to senescence. Broadly, it is the sum of two processes:

\[ \text{Development} = \text{Growth} + \text{Differentiation} \]

Plants follow different developmental pathways in response to their environment or life-cycle stages to form varied structures. This ability is called plasticity. The classic manifestation of plasticity is heterophylly (presence of different leaf shapes on the same plant):

  • Developmental Heterophylly (Juvenile vs. Adult phase): Seen in cotton, coriander, and larkspur, where juvenile leaves differ markedly in shape from mature adult leaves.
  • Environmental Heterophylly (Habitat response): Seen in buttercup (Ranunculus), where leaves produced in terrestrial/aerial conditions are broad and lobed, whereas submerged aquatic leaves are finely dissected.

Development is governed by both intrinsic factors (intracellular genetic factors and intercellular chemical regulators called PGRs) and extrinsic factors (light, temperature, water, oxygen, and nutrition).

5. Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs)

5.1 Characteristics and Classification

Plant growth regulators (also called plant growth substances, plant hormones, or phytohormones) are small, simple molecules of diverse chemical composition. Functionally, they fall into two broad categories:

  • Plant Growth Promoters: Involved in cell division, cell enlargement, pattern formation, tropic growth, flowering, fruiting, and seed formation. Includes Auxins, Gibberellins, and Cytokinins.
  • Plant Growth Inhibitors: Involved in responses to wounds and biotic/abiotic stresses, dormancy, and abscission. Includes Abscisic Acid (ABA).
  • Dual / Largely Inhibitory: The gaseous regulator Ethylene could fit either group, but acts predominantly as an inhibitor of growth activities.

5.2 Physiological Effects and Applications

1. Auxins

Natural auxins include IAA and IBA (indole butyric acid), whereas NAA (naphthalene acetic acid) and 2,4-D (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid) are synthetic auxins. Auxins are produced at growing stem and root apices and migrate to target regions. Key functions:

  • Rooting: Initiates adventitious rooting in stem cuttings (widely used in vegetative propagation).
  • Flowering & Fruit Drop: Promotes flowering in pineapples. Prevents premature drop of young fruits and leaves, while promoting abscission of older, mature leaves and fruits.
  • Apical Dominance: The growing apical bud inhibits the growth of lateral (axillary) buds. Removing the shoot tip (decapitation) releases lateral buds from inhibition, a practice widely used in tea plantations and hedge-making to encourage bushy growth.
  • Parthenocarpy: Induces seedless fruit development in tomatoes.
  • Weedicide: Synthetic 2,4-D selectively kills broad-leaved dicotyledonous weeds without harming mature monocotyledonous crops/grasses, making it ideal for weed-free lawns.
  • Cellular Role: Controls xylem differentiation and aids cell division.

2. Gibberellins

Over 100 gibberellins (\(\mathrm{GA_1, GA_2, GA_3}\), etc.) have been identified from fungi and higher plants; \(\mathrm{GA_3}\) is the most intensively studied. All GAs are acidic. Key functions:

  • Axis Elongation: Increases the length of grape stalks and causes fruits such as apples to elongate and improve in shape.
  • Sugarcane Yield: Spraying sugarcane with GAs elongates the stem (where carbohydrate is stored as sugar), increasing yield by up to 20 tonnes per acre.
  • Delays Senescence: Allows fruits to remain on the tree longer, extending the market period.
  • Brewing Industry: \(\mathrm{GA_3}\) speeds up the malting process.
  • Juvenile Conifers: Hastens maturity period, leading to early seed production.
  • Bolting: Promotes rapid internode elongation just prior to flowering in rosette plants such as beet and cabbages.

3. Cytokinins

Kinetin (from autoclaved herring sperm DNA) does not occur naturally in plants. The first natural cytokinin, zeatin, was isolated from corn kernels and coconut milk. Natural cytokinins are synthesised in regions of rapid cell division (root apices, developing shoot buds, young fruits). Key functions:

  • Cytokinesis & Shoot Formation: Promotes cell division, new leaf production, chloroplast formation in leaves, lateral shoot growth, and adventitious shoot formation.
  • Overcoming Apical Dominance: Acts antagonistically to auxin in lateral bud outgrowth.
  • Delay of Senescence: Promotes nutrient mobilisation, which delays leaf senescence.

4. Ethylene

Synthesised in large amounts by senescing tissues and ripening fruits. The most widely used agricultural source is ethephon (an aqueous solution readily absorbed and transported, releasing ethylene slowly). Key functions:

  • Triple Response in Dicot Seedlings: Horizontal growth of seedlings, swelling of the axis, and apical hook formation.
  • Fruit Ripening & Respiratory Climacteric: Enhances respiration rate during fruit ripening (respiratory climacteric). Ethephon hastens ripening in tomatoes and apples.
  • Senescence & Abscission: Promotes senescence and abscission of leaves and flowers; ethephon is used for thinning cotton, cherry, and walnut.
  • Breaking Dormancy: Breaks seed and bud dormancy; initiates germination in peanut seeds and sprouting in potato tubers.
  • Deep-Water Rice: Promotes rapid internode/petiole elongation so leaves stay above water.
  • Root & Flowering Effects: Promotes root growth and root hair formation (increasing absorption surface); initiates flowering and synchronises fruit-set in pineapples; induces flowering in mango; promotes female flowers in cucumbers (increasing yield).

5. Abscisic Acid (ABA)

Acts as a general plant growth and metabolism inhibitor. Key functions:

  • Stress Hormone: Stimulates stomatal closure and increases plant tolerance to abiotic/biotic stresses.
  • Seed Dormancy & Maturation: Inhibits seed germination; plays a major role in seed development, maturation, and dormancy, helping seeds withstand desiccation and unfavourable conditions.
  • GA Antagonist: In most physiological situations, ABA acts as an antagonist to gibberellins.

6. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: The following are original practice questions designed in the NEET UG format.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following is the most accurate example of biological growth in plants?

  1. (A) Swelling of a dry wooden block when submerged in water
  2. (B) Reversible opening and closing of stomatal guard cells
  3. (C) Permanent expansion of a dorsiventral leaf blade accompanied by ATP-dependent synthesis
  4. (D) Temporary wilting of herbaceous stems during midday heat

Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Biological growth is an irreversible, permanent increase in size coupled with metabolic processes at the expense of energy. Swelling of dry wood (A) is reversible imbibition without metabolism. Stomatal movement (B) and wilting (D) are reversible turgor changes. Only leaf expansion (C) represents permanent, metabolically driven growth.

Practice Question 2 Numerical / Calculation MCQ

Two leaves, P and Q, are observed over a 4-day interval. Leaf P expands from \(10\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) to \(20\,\mathrm{cm^2}\), while Leaf Q expands from \(50\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) to \(60\,\mathrm{cm^2}\). Which statement correctly compares their growth rates?

  1. (A) Leaf Q has a higher absolute growth rate and a higher relative growth rate than Leaf P.
  2. (B) Both leaves have the same absolute growth rate, but Leaf P has a higher relative growth rate.
  3. (C) Both leaves have the same relative growth rate, but Leaf Q has a higher absolute growth rate.
  4. (D) Leaf P has a higher absolute growth rate, while both have identical relative growth rates.

Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Absolute growth is \(\Delta A_P = 20 - 10 = 10\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) and \(\Delta A_Q = 60 - 50 = 10\,\mathrm{cm^2}\), so both have an AGR of \(10/4 = 2.5\,\mathrm{cm^2/day}\). Relative growth rate is \(\text{RGR}_P = \frac{10}{10 \times 4} = 0.25\,\mathrm{day^{-1}}\) and \(\text{RGR}_Q = \frac{10}{50 \times 4} = 0.05\,\mathrm{day^{-1}}\). Thus, Leaf P has a 5-times higher RGR.

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Decapitation of the shoot apex is a standard practice in tea plantations and hedge-making.
Reason (R): Removal of the apical bud eliminates the source of auxin that inhibits the growth of lateral (axillary) buds.

  1. (A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
  2. (B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  3. (C) A is true but R is false.
  4. (D) A is false but R is true.

Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Auxin produced by the growing apical bud suppresses lateral bud outgrowth (apical dominance). Decapitation removes this apical auxin source, allowing axillary buds to grow into lateral branches, producing bushy tea plants and dense hedges.

Practice Question 4 Statement I & II

Statement I: The formation of interfascicular cambium and cork cambium from fully differentiated parenchyma cells is an example of dedifferentiation.
Statement II: Cells produced by these dedifferentiated meristems lose the capacity to divide again and mature to perform specific functions through redifferentiation.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Living differentiated parenchyma cells regain mitotic capacity to form secondary meristems (interfascicular cambium and cork cambium)—this is dedifferentiation. Their derivatives subsequently mature into secondary vascular tissues or periderm, losing division capacity—this is redifferentiation.

7. Common Pitfalls and NEET Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET Traps to Avoid

  • Kinetin vs. Zeatin: Kinetin was discovered from autoclaved herring sperm DNA and does not occur naturally in plants; zeatin is the naturally occurring cytokinin isolated from corn kernels and coconut milk.
  • 2,4-D Selectivity: 2,4-D kills broad-leaved dicotyledonous weeds and does not affect mature monocotyledonous plants.
  • Auxin on Abscission: Auxins prevent premature drop of young fruits/leaves, but promote abscission of older, mature leaves and fruits.
  • Bolting vs. Deep-Water Rice Elongation: Bolting in rosette plants (beet, cabbage) is promoted by Gibberellins, whereas rapid internode/petiole elongation in deep-water rice is promoted by Ethylene.
  • ABA vs. GA Antagonism: ABA inhibits seed germination and induces dormancy; GAs break dormancy and promote germination/growth.
  • Open Growth & Open Differentiation: Both growth and differentiation in higher plants are "open"—final cell fate depends on cell position.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Maize RAM cell production: >17,500 cells/hour; watermelon cell enlargement: up to 3,50,000 times.
  • Arithmetic equation: \(L_t = L_0 + rt\); Geometric equation: \(W_1 = W_0 e^{rt}\) (\(r\) = efficiency index).
  • Sigmoid curve phases: Lag → Log (Exponential) → Stationary.
  • \(\text{Development} = \text{Growth} + \text{Differentiation}\).
  • Heterophylly: Cotton, coriander, larkspur (developmental); Buttercup (environmental).
  • Auxin: Darwin & Darwin (canary grass), F.W. Went (oat coleoptile); Gibberellin: Kurosawa (1926, Gibberella fujikuroi); Cytokinin: Skoog & Miller (1955); Ethylene: Cousins (1910); ABA: Inhibitor-B, Abscission II, Dormin.

8. Concise Final Recap

Plant growth is an irreversible, energy-dependent increase in size driven by meristems (open growth) through meristematic, elongation, and maturation phases. Quantitatively, cell division follows arithmetic (linear) or geometric (sigmoid) kinetics, and growth efficiency is best compared via relative growth rate. Differentiated cells can dedifferentiate into secondary meristems and redifferentiate into mature tissues, while plasticity allows environmental or stage-specific forms such as heterophylly. Finally, five major phytohormones—auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, ethylene, and abscisic acid—coordinate every developmental milestone through synergistic and antagonistic interactions.
