Mechanical Properties of Fluids

A complete, exam-oriented tutorial covering hydrostatics, fluid dynamics, viscosity, surface tension, original worked examples, and NEET UG practice questions.

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Context

Fluids—encompassing both liquids and gases—are substances that flow when subjected to even an infinitesimally small shear stress. In the NEET UG Physics syllabus, this chapter bridges mechanics and thermal/molecular physics, regularly contributing conceptual and formula-driven problems on pressure variation, Pascal's hydraulic systems, Bernoulli's theorem, terminal velocity, and capillarity.

Learning Objectives
  • Distinguish fluids from solids via shear response and compressibility, and define pressure as a scalar quantity.
  • Apply Pascal's law to hydraulic machines and compute absolute and gauge pressures at depth in incompressible fluids.
  • Use the equation of continuity and Bernoulli's principle to analyze steady flow, efflux speed, and dynamic lift.
  • Quantify internal fluid friction using the coefficient of viscosity, Stokes' law, and terminal velocity.
  • Explain surface energy, contact angles, excess pressure in drops and bubbles, and capillary rise.

2. Fluid Statics: Pressure, Depth, and Pascal's Law

2.1 What Makes a Substance a Fluid?

Unlike a solid, which resists shear deformation with an elastic restoring force proportional to shear strain, a fluid at rest cannot sustain any tangential (shear) stress. Even a tiny tangential force causes fluid layers to slide continuously over one another. Consequently, a fluid has no fixed shape of its own and conforms to the geometry of its container.

  • Liquids: Nearly incompressible (density remains practically constant over ordinary pressure ranges) and possess a definite free surface.
  • Gases: Highly compressible (density varies strongly with pressure and temperature) and expand to fill the entire available volume without forming a free surface.

2.2 Pressure and Density

When a fluid is at rest, the force it exerts on any submerged surface or container wall is strictly normal (perpendicular) to that surface. If a tangential component existed, Newton's third law would produce a tangential reaction on the fluid, causing it to flow—contradicting static equilibrium.

If a normal force of magnitude \(F\) acts uniformly on an area \(A\), the average pressure is \(P_{\mathrm{av}} = F/A\). In the local limit, pressure at a point is:

\[P = \lim_{\Delta A \to 0} \frac{\Delta F}{\Delta A}\]

Crucial conceptual point: Pressure is a scalar quantity because at any given point inside a static fluid, it acts equally in all directions and has no unique direction of its own. Its SI unit is the pascal (\(\mathrm{Pa} = \mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)), and its dimensional formula is \([\mathrm{M L^{-1} T^{-2}}]\).

Common practical pressure units used in physiology, meteorology, and physics problems include:

  • \(1\,\mathrm{atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\) (standard sea-level atmospheric pressure)
  • \(1\,\mathrm{bar} = 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • \(1\,\mathrm{torr} = 1\,\mathrm{mm\ of\ Hg} \approx 133\,\mathrm{Pa}\)

Mass density \(\rho = m/V\) (SI unit: \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) is a positive scalar. For water at \(4\,°\mathrm{C}\), \(\rho_{\mathrm{water}} = 1.0 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). The relative density (specific gravity) of a substance is the dimensionless ratio of its density to that of water at \(4\,°\mathrm{C}\).

2.3 Variation of Pressure with Depth and the Hydrostatic Paradox

Consider a vertical cylindrical element of an incompressible fluid of uniform density \(\rho\) at rest under gravity \(g\). Balancing the downward force at the top face (\(P_1 A\)), the weight of the fluid column (\(\rho A h g\)), and the upward force at the bottom face (\(P_2 A\)) located a vertical distance \(h\) below yields:

\[P_2 - P_1 = \rho g h\]

If the top face lies at the free surface open to atmospheric pressure \(P_a\), the absolute pressure \(P\) at depth \(h\) is:

\[P = P_a + \rho g h\]

The difference \(P - P_a = \rho g h\) is called the gauge pressure. Notice that \(P\) depends only on the vertical depth \(h\), fluid density \(\rho\), and \(g\)—it is completely independent of the cross-sectional area or shape of the vessel. Therefore, interconnected vessels of wildly different shapes filled with the same liquid settle to the exact same horizontal free-surface level, a result known as the hydrostatic paradox.

2.4 Measuring Pressure: Barometer and Open-Tube Manometer

A mercury barometer measures atmospheric pressure by inverting a long closed glass tube filled with mercury into a mercury trough. Ignoring the tiny vapour pressure of mercury in the evacuated space at the top, balancing pressure at the trough surface level gives \(P_a = \rho_{\mathrm{Hg}} g h_{\mathrm{Hg}}\). At sea level, \(h_{\mathrm{Hg}} \approx 76\,\mathrm{cm} = 760\,\mathrm{mm}\).

An open-tube manometer measures the gauge pressure of a confined gas. One arm connects to the gas chamber at pressure \(P\), while the other is open to the atmosphere at \(P_a\). Because pressure is identical at the same horizontal level within a continuous static liquid, the pressure difference is read directly from the column height difference \(h\).

Diagram 1: Principle of an open-tube manometer. Points A and B lie at the same horizontal level in the same continuous liquid, so \(P = P_a + \rho g h\).

2.5 Pascal's Law and Hydraulic Machines

Pascal's Law states two equivalent truths for a fluid at rest: (1) pressure is the same at all points at the same horizontal height, and (2) any external pressure change applied to an enclosed, incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every point of the fluid and the container walls.

In a hydraulic lift or hydraulic brake, a small force \(F_1\) applied to a small piston of area \(A_1\) creates an excess pressure \(\Delta P = F_1 / A_1\). This pressure acts on a larger piston of area \(A_2\), generating an amplified upward force \(F_2\):

\[\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2} \implies F_2 = \left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right) F_1\]

The ratio \(A_2/A_1\) is the mechanical advantage. However, energy is conserved: because the liquid is incompressible, the volume displaced at both pistons is identical (\(A_1 d_1 = A_2 d_2\)), so the large piston moves a proportionately smaller distance \(d_2 = (A_1/A_2) d_1\).

3. Fluid Dynamics: Continuity and Bernoulli's Principle

3.1 Streamline Flow and Equation of Continuity

Fluid flow is steady (streamline) if, at every fixed point in space, the velocity of each passing fluid particle remains constant in time. A streamline is a curve whose tangent at any point gives the fluid velocity direction at that point.

  • No intersection: Two streamlines can never cross; if they did, a fluid particle arriving at the intersection would have two simultaneous velocities, violating steady flow.
  • Turbulence: Above a limiting speed called the critical speed, steady flow breaks down into irregular, swirling turbulent flow (such as white-water rapids).

For an incompressible fluid (\(\rho = \text{constant}\)) in steady flow through a tube of varying cross-section, conservation of mass requires that the mass entering any section per second equals the mass leaving any other section per second. This gives the equation of continuity:

\[A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \quad \text{or} \quad A v = \text{constant}\]

The product \(Av\) represents the volume flow rate (volume flux). Where the pipe narrows (\(A\) decreases), streamlines crowd together and fluid speed \(v\) increases.

3.2 Bernoulli's Principle

Applying the work–energy theorem to an ideal fluid (incompressible, non-viscous, steady flow) moving through a pipe of varying area and elevation yields Bernoulli's equation:

\[P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant}\]

Along any streamline, the sum of static pressure (\(P\)), kinetic energy per unit volume (\(\frac{1}{2}\rho v^2\)), and gravitational potential energy per unit volume (\(\rho g h\)) remains constant. For a horizontal pipe (\(h_1 = h_2\)), higher speed implies lower pressure—the fluid accelerates into a constriction because the higher pressure behind pushes it forward.

Diagram 2: In steady horizontal flow, narrowing the cross-section increases velocity (\(A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2\)) and lowers static pressure (\(P_2 < P_1\)).

Limitations of Bernoulli's Equation

Bernoulli's equation strictly assumes zero viscosity (no energy loss to heat), incompressibility, and steady streamline flow. In real viscous fluids, internal friction converts mechanical energy into heat, causing downstream pressure to be lower than the ideal Bernoulli prediction. It cannot be applied to turbulent flows.

3.3 Key Applications: Torricelli's Law and Dynamic Lift

  • Speed of Efflux (Torricelli's Law): Consider a large tank of cross-section \(A_2\) filled with liquid to a height \(h\) above a small side hole of area \(A_1\) (\(A_2 \gg A_1\), so top surface speed \(v_2 \approx 0\)). If the air above the liquid is at pressure \(P\) and the hole opens to atmospheric pressure \(P_a\), Bernoulli's equation gives:
    \[v_1 = \sqrt{2gh + \frac{2(P - P_a)}{\rho}}\]
    When the tank is open to the atmosphere (\(P = P_a\)), this reduces to Torricelli's law: \(v_1 = \sqrt{2gh}\), identical to the speed of a body falling freely from rest through height \(h\).
  • Magnus Effect (Spinning Ball): A ball moving forward while spinning drags adjacent air around with it. On one side, the dragged air adds to the oncoming airflow (higher speed, lower pressure); on the opposite side, it opposes the airflow (lower speed, higher pressure). This pressure difference produces a sideways/upward dynamic lift called the Magnus effect, curving the ball's trajectory.
  • Aerofoil Lift: An aircraft wing is shaped and angled so that air streams faster over the curved upper surface than beneath the lower surface. By Bernoulli's principle, the pressure below exceeds the pressure above, generating upward lift that balances the aircraft's weight in level flight.

4. Viscosity and Stokes' Law

4.1 Internal Friction and Coefficient of Viscosity

Real fluids resist relative motion between adjacent layers through an internal friction called viscosity. In laminar flow between a fixed bottom plate and a top plate moving at speed \(v\) separated by distance \(l\), the fluid layer in contact with each plate shares that plate's velocity, creating a uniform velocity gradient \(v/l\).

Unlike solids, where shear stress depends on shear strain, in fluids the shear stress \(F/A\) is proportional to the rate of change of shear strain (strain rate) \(v/l\). The coefficient of viscosity \(\eta\) is defined as:

\[\eta = \frac{\text{Shearing stress}}{\text{Strain rate}} = \frac{F/A}{v/l} = \frac{F l}{v A}\]

Temperature dependence (high-yield NEET fact): As temperature increases, the viscosity of liquids decreases (thermal agitation weakens cohesive intermolecular forces), whereas the viscosity of gases increases (faster random molecular motion increases momentum transfer between adjacent layers).

4.2 Stokes' Law and Terminal Velocity

When a smooth sphere of radius \(a\) moves with velocity \(v\) through a viscous fluid of coefficient of viscosity \(\eta\), it experiences an opposing viscous drag force given by Stokes' law:

\[F_v = 6\pi \eta a v\]

As a sphere of density \(\rho\) falls from rest through a fluid of density \(\sigma\), gravity pulls it downward while buoyancy and viscous drag act upward. Initially it accelerates, increasing \(v\) and therefore increasing \(F_v\), until the upward forces exactly balance the weight. At that point, net force and acceleration become zero, and the sphere descends at a constant terminal velocity \(v_t\):

\[6\pi \eta a v_t + \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 \sigma g = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 \rho g \implies v_t = \frac{2 a^2 (\rho - \sigma) g}{9 \eta}\]

Notice the quadratic scaling: \(v_t \propto a^2\). Doubling the radius of a falling droplet quadruples its terminal velocity.

5. Surface Tension, Contact Angles, and Capillarity

5.1 Surface Energy and Surface Tension

A molecule deep inside a liquid is pulled equally in all directions by neighbouring molecules, giving it a strongly negative potential energy. A molecule at the free surface has liquid neighbours only on one side (below it), so it possesses higher potential energy—roughly half the binding energy is missing. Consequently, a liquid tends to minimize its surface area, and increasing the surface area by \(\Delta A\) requires work \(W = S\,\Delta A\).

Surface tension \(S\) is numerically equal to both the surface energy per unit area and the force per unit length acting tangentially in the plane of the interface. For a thin liquid film stretched on a wire frame of movable slider length \(l\), the film has two free surfaces (front and back), so the total upward/inward surface tension force is \(F = 2 S l\), giving \(S = F / (2l)\). Like viscosity, surface tension of liquids generally decreases with rising temperature.

5.2 Angle of Contact and Wetting

Near a solid wall, the liquid surface curves. The angle of contact \(\theta\) is the angle between the tangent to the liquid surface at the point of contact and the solid surface inside the liquid. Balancing the three interfacial tensions—liquid–air (\(S_{\mathrm{la}}\)), solid–air (\(S_{\mathrm{sa}}\)), and solid–liquid (\(S_{\mathrm{sl}}\))—along the solid surface gives Young's relation:

\[S_{\mathrm{la}} \cos\theta + S_{\mathrm{sl}} = S_{\mathrm{sa}} \implies \cos\theta = \frac{S_{\mathrm{sa}} - S_{\mathrm{sl}}}{S_{\mathrm{la}}}\]
  • Acute angle (\(\theta < 90°\)): Occurs when adhesive attraction between liquid and solid is strong (\(S_{\mathrm{sl}} < S_{\mathrm{sa}}\)), e.g., water on clean glass. The liquid spreads and wets the solid. Detergents lower \(S_{\mathrm{la}}\) and reduce \(\theta\), improving wetting.
  • Obtuse angle (\(\theta > 90°\)): Occurs when cohesive attraction within the liquid dominates (\(S_{\mathrm{sl}} > S_{\mathrm{sa}}\)), e.g., mercury on glass or water on a waxy lotus leaf. The liquid forms beads and does not wet the solid.

5.3 Excess Pressure in Drops, Cavities, and Bubbles

Because a curved interface tends to contract, the pressure on the concave (inner) side always exceeds the pressure on the convex (outer) side:

  • Liquid drop in air or air bubble inside a liquid (one liquid–gas interface): \(\Delta P = P_i - P_o = \dfrac{2S}{r}\).
  • Soap bubble in air (two liquid–gas interfaces—inner and outer): \(\Delta P = P_i - P_o = \dfrac{4S}{r}\).

5.4 Capillary Rise

When a narrow cylindrical tube of radius \(a\) is dipped into a wetting liquid (\(\theta < 90°\)), the meniscus is concave upward, so the liquid pressure just below the meniscus is less than atmospheric pressure by \(\Delta P = \frac{2S}{r_{\mathrm{meniscus}}} = \frac{2S\cos\theta}{a}\). Atmospheric pressure pushes the liquid up the tube until the hydrostatic column of height \(h\) restores equilibrium:

\[h \rho g = \frac{2S\cos\theta}{a} \implies h = \frac{2S\cos\theta}{a \rho g}\]

If \(\theta > 90°\) (like mercury in glass), \(\cos\theta\) is negative, the meniscus is convex upward, and the liquid level is depressed below the outside level.

Diagram 4: Capillary rise \(h\) for a wetting liquid (\(\theta < 90°\)) and the geometric relation \(r = a / \cos\theta\) between meniscus radius \(r\) and tube radius \(a\).

Liquid drop / Air bubble in liquid \(\Delta P = \dfrac{2S}{r}\) Only 1 free liquid–gas surface
Soap bubble in air \(\Delta P = \dfrac{4S}{r}\) 2 free surfaces (inner + outer)

6. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Immiscible Liquids in a U-Tube

A vertical U-tube of uniform bore contains mercury at the bottom. Into the left arm, a column of glycerine of height \(h_1 = 20\,\mathrm{cm}\) (assumed density \(\rho_1 = 1.26 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) is poured. Into the right arm, an unknown oil is poured until the mercury surfaces in both arms stand at the exact same horizontal level. If the oil column has height \(h_2 = 28\,\mathrm{cm}\), calculate the density \(\rho_2\) of the oil.

Solution: Since the mercury interfaces in both arms are at the same horizontal level and mercury is a continuous static fluid, the pressures at those two interfaces must be equal:

\[P_a + \rho_1 g h_1 = P_a + \rho_2 g h_2\] \[\rho_2 = \rho_1 \frac{h_1}{h_2} = (1.26 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}) \times \frac{20\,\mathrm{cm}}{28\,\mathrm{cm}} = 1.26 \times 10^3 \times \frac{5}{7} = 900\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\]
Worked Example 2: Hydraulic Workshop Jack

A hydraulic workshop jack has a small input piston of radius \(r_1 = 2.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) and a large output piston of radius \(r_2 = 12\,\mathrm{cm}\). Assume \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). (a) What downward force \(F_1\) on the small piston is needed to support a crate of mass \(M = 1800\,\mathrm{kg}\) on the large piston? (b) If the small piston is pushed down through \(d_1 = 18\,\mathrm{cm}\), how high does the crate rise?

Solution:

(a) By Pascal's law, \(\dfrac{F_1}{A_1} = \dfrac{F_2}{A_2}\), where \(F_2 = M g = 1800 \times 9.8 = 17\,640\,\mathrm{N}\):

\[F_1 = F_2 \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2 = 17\,640\,\mathrm{N} \times \left(\frac{2.0}{12}\right)^2 = \frac{17\,640}{36} = 490\,\mathrm{N}\]

(b) By volume conservation of the incompressible hydraulic fluid, \(A_1 d_1 = A_2 d_2\):

\[d_2 = d_1 \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2 = \frac{18\,\mathrm{cm}}{36} = 0.50\,\mathrm{cm}\]
Worked Example 3: Continuity and Bernoulli's Equation in a Horizontal Pipe

An ideal incompressible fluid of assumed density \(\rho = 1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) flows steadily through a horizontal pipe whose cross-sectional area narrows from \(A_1 = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) to \(A_2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2}\). At the wider section, the speed is \(v_1 = 1.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and the gauge pressure is \(P_1 = 24\,\mathrm{kPa}\). Find (a) the flow speed \(v_2\) and (b) the gauge pressure \(P_2\) at the narrower section.

Solution:

(a) From the equation of continuity, \(A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2\):

\[v_2 = \left(\frac{A_1}{A_2}\right) v_1 = \left(\frac{4.0 \times 10^{-3}}{1.0 \times 10^{-3}}\right) \times 1.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

(b) Since the pipe is horizontal (\(h_1 = h_2\)), Bernoulli's equation gives:

\[P_2 = P_1 - \frac{1}{2}\rho (v_2^2 - v_1^2) = 24\,000 - \frac{1}{2}(1000)(6.0^2 - 1.5^2)\] \[P_2 = 24\,000 - 500(36 - 2.25) = 24\,000 - 16\,875 = 7125\,\mathrm{Pa} \approx 7.13\,\mathrm{kPa}\]
Worked Example 4: Terminal Velocity in Castor Oil

A steel bearing of radius \(a = 1.5\,\mathrm{mm}\) (assumed density \(\rho = 7.8 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) falls through a tall column of castor oil (assumed density \(\sigma = 0.96 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) at a steady terminal velocity \(v_t = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\). Taking \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find the coefficient of viscosity \(\eta\) of the castor oil.

Solution: Rearranging the terminal velocity formula \(v_t = \dfrac{2 a^2 (\rho - \sigma) g}{9\eta}\):

\[\eta = \frac{2 a^2 (\rho - \sigma) g}{9 v_t} = \frac{2 \times (1.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m})^2 \times (7.8 - 0.96)\times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \times 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}}{9 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}\] \[\eta = \frac{2 \times 2.25 \times 10^{-6} \times 6.84 \times 10^3 \times 9.8}{0.36} \approx 0.84\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\]
Worked Example 5: Pressure Inside a Submerged Air Bubble

An air bubble of radius \(r = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) is formed at a depth of \(h = 50\,\mathrm{cm}\) below the surface of a detergent solution of assumed relative density \(1.10\) and surface tension \(S = 3.0 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\). Taking \(P_a = 1.01 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\) and \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), determine the absolute pressure inside the bubble.

Solution: Density of the solution is \(\rho = 1.10 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). The hydrostatic pressure just outside the bubble is:

\[P_o = P_a + \rho g h = 1.01 \times 10^5 + (1100)(10)(0.50) = 1.065 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\]

An air bubble inside a liquid has only one liquid–gas interface, so the excess pressure is \(\Delta P = \dfrac{2S}{r}\):

\[\Delta P = \frac{2 \times 3.0 \times 10^{-2}}{1.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 60\,\mathrm{Pa}\] \[P_i = P_o + \Delta P = 106\,500 + 60 = 106\,560\,\mathrm{Pa} \approx 1.066 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\]

7. NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are modeled on the NEET UG examination pattern (single-correct MCQs, statement-based, assertion–reason, and match-the-following). Attempt each question independently before checking the worked solution.

Practice Question 1 · Single-Correct MCQ

A diver descends in seawater of assumed density \(1.03 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and atmospheric pressure \(P_a = 1.01 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\), at what depth below the surface is the absolute pressure equal to \(3.07 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\)?

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(20\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(25\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(30\,\mathrm{m}\)
Correct Answer: (B) \(20\,\mathrm{m}\)

Worked Solution: Gauge pressure is \(P - P_a = \rho g h\). Thus, \(3.07 \times 10^5 - 1.01 \times 10^5 = 2.06 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\). Solving for \(h\): \(h = \dfrac{2.06 \times 10^5}{1.03 \times 10^3 \times 10} = 20\,\mathrm{m}\).

Practice Question 2 · Single-Correct MCQ

If a barometer is constructed using a non-volatile liquid of assumed density \(3.4 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) instead of mercury (density \(13.6 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)), what will be the height of the liquid column at standard atmospheric pressure (where mercury stands at \(76\,\mathrm{cm}\))?

  • (A) \(0.19\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(1.52\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(3.04\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(10.3\,\mathrm{m}\)
Correct Answer: (C) \(3.04\,\mathrm{m}\)

Worked Solution: Since \(P_a = \rho_1 g h_1 = \rho_2 g h_2\), we have \(h_2 = h_1 \dfrac{\rho_1}{\rho_2} = 0.76\,\mathrm{m} \times \dfrac{13.6 \times 10^3}{3.4 \times 10^3} = 0.76 \times 4 = 3.04\,\mathrm{m}\).

Practice Question 3 · Single-Correct MCQ

In a hydraulic press, the ratio of the diameter of the larger piston to that of the smaller piston is \(5 : 1\). If a force of \(80\,\mathrm{N}\) is applied to the smaller piston, the maximum load that can be balanced on the larger piston is:

  • (A) \(400\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(1600\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(2000\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(4000\,\mathrm{N}\)
Correct Answer: (C) \(2000\,\mathrm{N}\)

Worked Solution: By Pascal's law, \(F_2 = F_1 \left(\dfrac{A_2}{A_1}\right) = F_1 \left(\dfrac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2 = 80\,\mathrm{N} \times 5^2 = 80 \times 25 = 2000\,\mathrm{N}\).

Practice Question 4 · Single-Correct MCQ

Water flows steadily through a cylindrical garden hose of internal diameter \(2.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) at a speed of \(1.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). It exits through a nozzle having \(12\) identical circular holes, each of diameter \(2.0\,\mathrm{mm}\). What is the speed of ejection of water through each hole?

  • (A) \(5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)

Worked Solution: Using the equation of continuity, \(A_{\mathrm{hose}} v_1 = N A_{\mathrm{hole}} v_2 \implies \pi (10\,\mathrm{mm})^2 \times 1.2 = 12 \times \pi (1.0\,\mathrm{mm})^2 \times v_2\). Thus, \(v_2 = \dfrac{100 \times 1.2}{12} = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 5 · Single-Correct MCQ

Air (assumed density \(1.2\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) streams past the wings of a small aircraft of total wing area \(15\,\mathrm{m^2}\). If the air speed over the upper surface is \(60\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and over the lower surface is \(50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the net upward dynamic lift on the wings is:

  • (A) \(4.95\,\mathrm{kN}\)
  • (B) \(6.60\,\mathrm{kN}\)
  • (C) \(9.90\,\mathrm{kN}\)
  • (D) \(19.8\,\mathrm{kN}\)
Correct Answer: (C) \(9.90\,\mathrm{kN}\)

Worked Solution: Neglecting the small wing thickness, \(\Delta P = \frac{1}{2}\rho (v_{\mathrm{top}}^2 - v_{\mathrm{bot}}^2) = 0.5 \times 1.2 \times (60^2 - 50^2) = 0.6 \times 1100 = 660\,\mathrm{Pa}\). Lift \(F = \Delta P \times A = 660 \times 15 = 9900\,\mathrm{N} = 9.90\,\mathrm{kN}\).

Practice Question 6 · Single-Correct MCQ

A large open tank is filled with water to a total height \(H = 20\,\mathrm{m}\). A small orifice is punched in its vertical wall at a depth \(h = 5.0\,\mathrm{m}\) below the free surface. What is the speed of efflux of water from the orifice? (Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(17.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (A) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)

Worked Solution: By Torricelli's law, the efflux speed depends on the vertical depth \(h\) below the free surface: \(v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 5.0} = \sqrt{100} = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 7 · Single-Correct MCQ

Eight identical spherical raindrops fall through air with a steady terminal velocity of \(2.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\) each. If they coalesce to form a single large spherical drop, what will be its terminal velocity? (Neglect air density compared to water).

  • (A) \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(8.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(16\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(32\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B) \(8.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)

Worked Solution: Volume conservation gives \(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = 8 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \implies R = 2r\). Since terminal velocity \(v_t \propto r^2\), the new terminal velocity is \(v_t' = 2.0 \times \left(\dfrac{R}{r}\right)^2 = 2.0 \times 4 = 8.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 8 · Single-Correct MCQ

Water rises to a height of \(6.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) in a clean glass capillary tube of radius \(r\). If another clean glass capillary tube of radius \(1.5r\) is dipped in the same water at the same temperature, to what height will the water rise?

  • (A) \(3.0\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (B) \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (C) \(6.0\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (D) \(9.0\,\mathrm{cm}\)
Correct Answer: (B) \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\)

Worked Solution: Capillary rise \(h = \dfrac{2S\cos\theta}{a\rho g}\) is inversely proportional to the tube radius \(a\). Hence, \(h_2 = \dfrac{6.0\,\mathrm{cm}}{1.5} = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Practice Question 9 · Statement-Based MCQ

Read the two statements carefully and choose the correct option:
Statement I: With an increase in temperature, the viscosity of a liquid decreases, whereas the viscosity of a gas increases.
Statement II: In a liquid, higher temperature increases molecular mobility and weakens intermolecular cohesive forces, whereas in a gas, higher temperature increases random thermal velocity and momentum transfer between layers.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
  • (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  • (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
Correct Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.

Worked Solution: Liquid viscosity is governed by cohesive forces, which weaken as temperature rises. Gas viscosity arises from molecular collisions and momentum exchange across layers, which intensify as thermal speeds increase with temperature.

Practice Question 10 · Assertion–Reason MCQ

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as Assertion (A) and the other as Reason (R).
Assertion (A): A spinning cricket ball curves away from a simple parabolic path as it travels through air.
Reason (R): Due to viscous drag of the spinning surface, air is dragged along with the ball, creating unequal relative air speeds and hence unequal pressures on opposite sides of the ball.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

Worked Solution: This is the Magnus effect: asymmetric air speeds around the spinning ball create a pressure difference via Bernoulli's principle, producing a transverse dynamic lift that deflects the ball from a parabolic path.

Practice Question 12 · Single-Correct Numerical MCQ

What is the work done in blowing a spherical soap bubble from an initial radius of \(2.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) to a final radius of \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\), if the surface tension of the soap solution is \(S = 2.5 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\)? (Take \(\pi = 3.14\)).

  • (A) \(3.77 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(7.54 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(1.51 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(3.01 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J}\)
Correct Answer: (B) \(7.54 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}\)

Worked Solution: A soap bubble has two free surfaces, so \(\Delta A = 2 \times 4\pi (r_2^2 - r_1^2) = 8\pi (0.04^2 - 0.02^2) = 8\pi (0.0012) = 9.6\pi \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2}\). Work done \(W = S\,\Delta A = (2.5 \times 10^{-2}) \times 9.6 \times 3.14 \times 10^{-3} = 7.536 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 7.54 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}\).

8. Common Pitfalls and Final Revision Checklist

Top 5 NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Soap bubble vs. air bubble in liquid: A soap bubble in air has two surfaces (\(\Delta P = 4S/r\), \(\Delta A = 8\pi r^2\)), whereas a liquid drop in air or an air bubble inside a liquid has one surface (\(\Delta P = 2S/r\)).
  • Radius vs. diameter: Always halve given diameters before substituting into \(A = \pi r^2\), \(v_t \propto a^2\), or \(\Delta P \propto 1/r\).
  • Torricelli's depth: Use the vertical depth \(h\) measured from the free surface down to the hole, not the height measured from the tank floor.
  • Temperature effect on viscosity: Remember the opposite trends—\(\eta_{\mathrm{liquid}}\) decreases with temperature, while \(\eta_{\mathrm{gas}}\) increases with temperature.
  • Gauge vs. absolute pressure: Check whether a question asks for total (absolute) pressure \(P_a + \rho g h\) or excess (gauge) pressure \(\rho g h\).
Concise Final Recap

Static fluids exert normal forces with scalar pressure \(P = P_a + \rho g h\) and transmit external pressure changes undiminished (Pascal's law). In steady, ideal flow, mass conservation gives \(Av = \text{constant}\) and energy conservation gives Bernoulli's equation \(P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant}\). Real fluids exhibit internal friction (\(\eta\)), producing Stokes' drag \(6\pi\eta a v\) and terminal velocity \(v_t \propto a^2\). Finally, excess surface energy at liquid interfaces drives surface tension \(S\), wetting behavior via contact angle \(\theta\), excess pressure \(2S/r\) (drop) or \(4S/r\) (soap bubble), and capillary rise \(h = 2S\cos\theta / (a\rho g)\).
