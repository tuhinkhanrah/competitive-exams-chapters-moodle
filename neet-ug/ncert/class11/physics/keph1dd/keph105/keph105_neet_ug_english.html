Work, Energy, and Power

Core principles, graphical analysis, conservation laws, collisions, and exam-oriented practice

1. Learning Objectives & Syllabus Overview

In everyday speech, "work," "energy," and "power" carry broad, informal meanings. In physics, however, each term has a strict mathematical definition that connects force, displacement, and motion. Mastering this chapter is essential for NEET UG because energy and momentum methods often solve multi-step mechanics problems far faster than direct kinematic equations.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial
  • Computing the scalar (dot) product of vectors and interpreting it as geometric projection.
  • Evaluating work done by constant and position-dependent forces, both algebraically and from force–displacement graphs.
  • Applying the Work–Energy Theorem to single-body and multi-force systems.
  • Distinguishing conservative from non-conservative forces and using gravitational and elastic potential energy functions.
  • Solving problems using Conservation of Mechanical Energy, including vertical circular motion and spring–block systems.
  • Calculating average and instantaneous power, and analyzing constant-power motion.
  • Analyzing one-dimensional and two-dimensional elastic, inelastic, and completely inelastic collisions.

2. Mathematical Prerequisite: The Scalar (Dot) Product

Force and displacement are vector quantities, yet work is a scalar. To obtain a scalar from two vectors \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) inclined at an angle \(\theta\) to each other, we define their scalar product (or dot product) as:

\[ \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A B \cos\theta \]

Geometrically, \(B\cos\theta\) is the projection (component) of \(\mathbf{B}\) along \(\mathbf{A}\), and \(A\cos\theta\) is the projection of \(\mathbf{A}\) along \(\mathbf{B}\). Thus, \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}\) equals the magnitude of one vector multiplied by the component of the other vector along its direction.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Geometric interpretation of the scalar product \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A(B\cos\theta)\).

Key Algebraic Properties of the Dot Product

  • Commutative: \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{A}\).
  • Distributive: \(\mathbf{A} \cdot (\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{C}\).
  • Orthogonal Unit Vectors: For \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}, \hat{\mathbf{j}}, \hat{\mathbf{k}}\), we have \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{i}} = \hat{\mathbf{j}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{j}} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{k}} = 1\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{j}} = \hat{\mathbf{j}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{i}} = 0\).
  • Component Form: If \(\mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + A_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + A_z\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) and \(\mathbf{B} = B_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + B_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + B_z\hat{\mathbf{k}}\), then: \[ \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z \]
  • Perpendicularity Condition: Two non-zero vectors are perpendicular (\(\theta = 90°\)) if and only if \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\).

3. Mechanical Work: Constant and Variable Forces

3.1 Work Done by a Constant Force

When a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) acts on a particle undergoing a displacement \(\mathbf{d}\), the work \(W\) done by that force is:

\[ W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = F d \cos\theta \]

Notice that work is always specified by a particular force on a particular body over a displacement. Work is a scalar with dimensions \([\mathrm{ML}^2\mathrm{T}^{-2}]\) and SI unit joule (J), where \(1\,\mathrm{J} = 1\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\).

  • Positive Work (\(0° \le \theta < 90°\)): Force has a component along the displacement (for example, gravity on a falling stone).
  • Negative Work (\(90° < \theta \le 180°\)): Force opposes the displacement (for example, kinetic friction on a sliding block).
  • Zero Work: Occurs when (i) displacement \(d = 0\) (pushing a rigid wall), (ii) force \(F = 0\), or (iii) \(\mathbf{F} \perp \mathbf{d}\) (\(\theta = 90°\), such as centripetal force on a body in uniform circular motion or normal reaction on a flat horizontal floor).

3.2 Work Done by a Variable Force

Most forces in nature vary with position. For one-dimensional motion along the \(x\)-axis from \(x_i\) to \(x_f\), we divide the path into infinitesimal displacements \(dx\) over which \(F(x)\) is nearly constant. Summing these strips gives the definite integral:

\[ W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,dx \]

Graphically, the work done equals the signed area under the \(F\)–\(x\) curve: area above the displacement axis is positive work, and area below it is negative work.

Tutorial Diagram 2: Net work is the algebraic sum of signed areas between the \(F(x)\) graph and the \(x\)-axis.
Worked Example 1 (Variable Force & Graph)

Scenario (assumed values): A robotic cart moves along a straight track from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 12\,\mathrm{m}\) under a horizontal force \(F(x)\). For the first \(4\,\mathrm{m}\), \(F = +30\,\mathrm{N}\) is constant. From \(x = 4\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(x = 10\,\mathrm{m}\), \(F\) decreases linearly from \(+30\,\mathrm{N}\) to \(0\,\mathrm{N}\). From \(x = 10\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(x = 12\,\mathrm{m}\), \(F\) reverses and decreases linearly from \(0\,\mathrm{N}\) to \(-10\,\mathrm{N}\). Find the net work done on the cart.

Solution:

  • From \(x = 0\) to \(4\,\mathrm{m}\) (rectangle): \(W_1 = 30 \times 4 = +120\,\mathrm{J}\).
  • From \(x = 4\) to \(10\,\mathrm{m}\) (triangle): \(W_2 = \frac{1}{2} \times (10 - 4) \times 30 = +90\,\mathrm{J}\).
  • From \(x = 10\) to \(12\,\mathrm{m}\) (triangle below axis): \(W_3 = \frac{1}{2} \times (12 - 10) \times (-10) = -10\,\mathrm{J}\).

Total work done: \(W_{\text{net}} = W_1 + W_2 + W_3 = 120 + 90 - 10 = 200\,\mathrm{J}\).

4. Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem

The kinetic energy \(K\) of a particle of mass \(m\) moving with speed \(v\) is a positive scalar quantity:

\[ K = \frac{1}{2}m v^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m} \]

where \(p = mv\) is the magnitude of linear momentum. For NEET UG, the relation \(K = \frac{p^2}{2m}\) is especially useful for comparing kinetic energies of two bodies with equal momentum (\(K \propto 1/m\)) or finding percentage changes.

The Work–Energy (WE) Theorem

The work done by the net force (the vector sum of all forces—conservative, non-conservative, internal, and external) acting on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy:

\[ W_{\text{net}} = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_{\text{net}}\,dx = K_f - K_i = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 \]

Important Scope: The WE theorem is an integral form of Newton's Second Law. It holds for both constant and variable forces, in any inertial frame (and in non-inertial frames if pseudoforces are included in \(W_{\text{net}}\)).

Worked Example 2 (Work–Energy Theorem with Unknown Resistive Force)

Scenario (assumed values): A hailstone of mass \(2.0\,\mathrm{g}\) falls from rest from a cloud at height \(800\,\mathrm{m}\) and strikes the ground at \(40\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find the work done by air resistance during the fall.

Solution:

Convert mass to SI units: \(m = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}\).
Initial kinetic energy \(K_i = 0\). Final kinetic energy: \[ K_f = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{1}{2} (2.0 \times 10^{-3})(40)^2 = 1.6\,\mathrm{J} \] Work done by gravity: \[ W_g = mgh = (2.0 \times 10^{-3})(10)(800) = 16.0\,\mathrm{J} \] By the Work–Energy Theorem, \(\Delta K = W_g + W_{\text{air}}\): \[ 1.6 = 16.0 + W_{\text{air}} \implies W_{\text{air}} = -14.4\,\mathrm{J} \]

5. Conservative Forces, Potential Energy & Conservation of Mechanical Energy

5.1 Conservative vs. Non-Conservative Forces

A force is conservative if the work it does on a particle depends only on the initial and final positions, and is independent of the path taken. Equivalently, the work done by a conservative force over any closed loop is zero (\(\oint \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0\)). Examples include gravity, electrostatic force, and ideal spring force.

A force is non-conservative if the work done depends on the path (such as kinetic friction or air drag). Potential energy can be defined only for conservative forces.

5.2 Potential Energy Functions

For a one-dimensional conservative force \(F(x)\), the potential energy \(V(x)\) is defined such that the change in potential energy equals the negative of the work done by the conservative force:

\[ \Delta V = V_f - V_i = -\int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,dx \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad F(x) = -\frac{dV}{dx} \]
  • Gravitational Potential Energy (near Earth's surface, \(h \ll R_E\)): \(V(h) = mgh\), taking \(V = 0\) at the reference ground level.
  • Elastic Potential Energy of a Spring: By Hooke's Law, the restoring force is \(F_s = -kx\) (where \(k\) is the spring constant in \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) and \(x\) is extension or compression from natural length). The stored potential energy is: \[ V(x) = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 \]
Tutorial Diagram 3: Complementary parabolic variation of potential energy \(V(x)\) and kinetic energy \(K(x)\) for a spring–mass system.

5.3 Conservation of Total Mechanical Energy

Total mechanical energy is defined as \(E = K + V\). If only conservative forces do work on a system, mechanical energy is conserved:

\[ K_i + V_i = K_f + V_f \]

When non-conservative forces (such as friction) also do work \(W_{\text{nc}}\), the principle generalizes to:

\[ E_f - E_i = W_{\text{nc}} \]

5.4 Application: Vertical Circular Motion

Consider a bob of mass \(m\) tied to a light string of length \(L\) and given a horizontal speed \(v_A\) at the lowest point \(A\). Tension \(T\) is always perpendicular to the instantaneous displacement, so it does zero work. Only gravity does work, conserving mechanical energy.

Worked Example 3 (Spring Compression with Friction)

Scenario (assumed values): A \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) block slides on a rough horizontal floor (\(\mu_k = 0.25\)) at \(6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) toward a massless horizontal spring of stiffness \(k = 400\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find the maximum compression \(x_m\) of the spring.

Solution:

Using the Work–Energy Theorem from impact to maximum compression (\(v_f = 0\)): \[ \Delta K = W_{\text{spring}} + W_{\text{friction}} \] \[ 0 - \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = -\frac{1}{2}kx_m^2 - \mu_k m g x_m \] Substitute \(m = 4\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(v = 6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(k = 400\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\), \(\mu_k = 0.25\), \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\): \[ \frac{1}{2}(4)(36) = \frac{1}{2}(400)x_m^2 + (0.25)(4)(10)x_m \] \[ 72 = 200 x_m^2 + 10 x_m \implies 20 x_m^2 + x_m - 7.2 = 0 \] Solving the quadratic for the positive root: \[ x_m = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4(20)(7.2)}}{40} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{577}}{40} \approx \frac{-1 + 24.02}{40} \approx 0.58\,\mathrm{m} \]

6. Power: Rate of Doing Work

Power measures how fast work is done or energy is transferred. It is a scalar with dimensions \([\mathrm{ML}^2\mathrm{T}^{-3}]\) and SI unit watt (W), where \(1\,\mathrm{W} = 1\,\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}\). Another common unit is horsepower: \(1\,\mathrm{hp} = 746\,\mathrm{W}\).

\[ P_{\text{av}} = \frac{W}{t}, \qquad P_{\text{inst}} = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} \]
High-Yield NEET Proportionality Results
  • Constant Acceleration (\(a = \text{const}\), starting from rest): \(v = at\) and \(F = ma\), so \(P = Fv = ma^2 t\), meaning \(P \propto t\).
  • Constant Power (\(P = \text{const}\), starting from rest): Since \(P = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}mv^2\right)\), integrating gives \(\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = Pt\), so \(v \propto t^{1/2}\). Integrating \(v = \frac{dx}{dt}\) once more yields displacement \(x \propto t^{3/2}\).
Worked Example 4 (Power of a Mine Hoist)

Scenario (assumed values): A mine hoist raises an ore cage of total mass \(1200\,\mathrm{kg}\) vertically at a steady speed of \(2.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) against a constant guide-rail friction of \(3000\,\mathrm{N}\). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find the minimum power delivered by the motor.

Solution:

Since speed is constant, upward tension balances both weight and downward friction: \[ F = mg + f = (1200 \times 10) + 3000 = 15000\,\mathrm{N} \] Instantaneous power: \[ P = Fv = 15000 \times 2.5 = 37500\,\mathrm{W} = 37.5\,\mathrm{kW} \approx 50.3\,\mathrm{hp} \]

7. Collisions in One and Two Dimensions

During a collision, two bodies exert large mutual impulsive forces over a brief contact interval \(\Delta t\). By Newton's Third Law, \(\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21}\), so the net external impulse is negligible and total linear momentum is conserved in every collision (elastic or inelastic).

Collision Type Momentum Conserved? Kinetic Energy Conserved? Key Outcome
Elastic Yes (at every instant) Yes (initial \(K_i\) = final \(K_f\); not constant during deformation) Relative speed of separation equals relative speed of approach
Inelastic Yes No (\(K_f < K_i\)) Part of KE converts to heat, sound, or permanent deformation
Completely Inelastic Yes No (maximum KE loss) Colliding bodies stick together and move with a common velocity

7.1 One-Dimensional (Head-On) Collisions

Consider mass \(m_1\) moving at \(v_{1i}\) colliding head-on with stationary mass \(m_2\) (\(v_{2i} = 0\)):

  • Completely Inelastic: Common final velocity \(v_f = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}\), and kinetic energy lost is: \[ \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}\frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}^2 \]
  • Elastic: Solving momentum and kinetic energy conservation simultaneously gives: \[ v_{1f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}, \qquad v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i} \]

Special Elastic Cases: (i) If \(m_1 = m_2\), they exchange velocities (\(v_{1f} = 0\), \(v_{2f} = v_{1i}\)). (ii) If \(m_2 \gg m_1\) (ball hitting a wall), \(v_{1f} \approx -v_{1i}\) and \(v_{2f} \approx 0\). (iii) If \(m_1 \gg m_2\) (truck hitting a pebble), \(v_{1f} \approx v_{1i}\) and \(v_{2f} \approx 2v_{1i}\).

7.2 Two-Dimensional (Glancing) Collisions

When a moving mass \(m_1\) strikes a stationary mass \(m_2\) off-center, momentum conservation applies independently along the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes: \[ m_1 v_{1i} = m_1 v_{1f}\cos\theta_1 + m_2 v_{2f}\cos\theta_2 \] \[ 0 = m_1 v_{1f}\sin\theta_1 - m_2 v_{2f}\sin\theta_2 \]

Tutorial Diagram 5: Glancing collision in the \(x\)–\(y\) plane. For an elastic collision of equal masses (\(m_1 = m_2\)), \(\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 90°\).
Worked Example 5 (Energy Transfer in a Head-On Elastic Collision)

Scenario (assumed values): A fast particle of mass \(m\) collides head-on and elastically with a stationary target nucleus of mass \(3m\). What fraction of the incident particle's kinetic energy is transferred to the target?

Solution:

The speed of the target after collision is \(v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i} = \frac{2m}{m + 3m}v_{1i} = \frac{1}{2}v_{1i}\).
The kinetic energy gained by the target is: \[ K_{2f} = \frac{1}{2}(3m)\left(\frac{v_{1i}}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{3}{4} \left(\frac{1}{2}m v_{1i}^2\right) = \frac{3}{4} K_{1i} \] Thus, \(75\%\) of the incident kinetic energy is transferred to the target.

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are designed in the NEET UG format (single-correct MCQs, statement-based MCQs, and assertion–reason MCQs). Attempt each question before reading the worked solution.

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ
A force \(\mathbf{F} = (4\hat{\mathbf{i}} + c\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 2\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N}\) acts on a particle and displaces it by \(\mathbf{d} = (2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 7\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{m}\). If the work done by this force is zero, the value of \(c\) is:
  • (A) \(2\)
  • (B) \(-2\)
  • (C) \(6\)
  • (D) \(-6\)
Answer: (A) \(2\)
Work done \(W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = (4)(2) + (c)(3) + (-2)(7) = 0\).
Thus, \(8 + 3c - 14 = 0 \implies 3c = 6 \implies c = 2\).
Practice Question 2 • Single-Correct MCQ
Two bodies of masses \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) are moving with equal kinetic energies. What is the ratio of the magnitudes of their linear momenta?
  • (A) \(1 : 4\)
  • (B) \(1 : 2\)
  • (C) \(2 : 1\)
  • (D) \(4 : 1\)
Answer: (B) \(1 : 2\)
Using \(p = \sqrt{2mK}\), for equal kinetic energy \(K\), \(p \propto \sqrt{m}\).
Therefore, \(\frac{p_1}{p_2} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}} = \frac{1}{2}\).
Practice Question 3 • Single-Correct MCQ
A particle moves along the \(x\)-axis from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 3\,\mathrm{m}\) under a variable force \(F(x) = (3x^2 - 2x + 5)\,\mathrm{N}\). The work done by the force during this displacement is:
  • (A) \(27\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(33\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(36\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(42\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (B) \(33\,\mathrm{J}\)
\(W = \int_0^3 (3x^2 - 2x + 5)\,dx = \left[x^3 - x^2 + 5x\right]_0^3 = 27 - 9 + 15 = 33\,\mathrm{J}\).
Practice Question 4 • Single-Correct MCQ
A block of mass \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) is pushed up a rough inclined plane of angle \(30°\) with a constant speed of \(2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) over a distance of \(5\,\mathrm{m}\) along the incline. If the coefficient of kinetic friction is \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}\) and \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), the work done by the applied force parallel to the incline is:
  • (A) \(25\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(50\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(75\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(100\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (C) \(75\,\mathrm{J}\)
Since speed is constant, applied force \(F = mg\sin 30° + \mu_k mg\cos 30°\).
\(mg\sin 30° = 2 \times 10 \times 0.5 = 10\,\mathrm{N}\).
\(\mu_k mg\cos 30° = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}} \times 20 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 5\,\mathrm{N}\).
Thus \(F = 15\,\mathrm{N}\), and \(W = F \cdot d = 15 \times 5 = 75\,\mathrm{J}\).
Practice Question 5 • Single-Correct MCQ
A small stone of mass \(m\) tied to a light string of length \(L\) is whirled in a vertical circle. If it just completes the vertical loop, what is the ratio of its kinetic energy at the lowest point to its kinetic energy at the horizontal position?
  • (A) \(5 : 3\)
  • (B) \(3 : 1\)
  • (C) \(5 : 1\)
  • (D) \(\sqrt{5} : \sqrt{3}\)
Answer: (A) \(5 : 3\)
For just completing the circle, \(v_{\text{lowest}}^2 = 5gL\) and \(v_{\text{horizontal}}^2 = 3gL\).
Since \(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\), the ratio is \(\frac{K_{\text{lowest}}}{K_{\text{horizontal}}} = \frac{5gL}{3gL} = \frac{5}{3}\).
Practice Question 6 • Single-Correct MCQ
A body of mass \(m\) starts from rest and accelerates along a straight line under the action of a source delivering constant power \(P\). Its speed \(v\) at time \(t\) is proportional to:
  • (A) \(t^{-1/2}\)
  • (B) \(t^{1/2}\)
  • (C) \(t\)
  • (D) \(t^{3/2}\)
Answer: (B) \(t^{1/2}\)
Since power \(P = \frac{dK}{dt}\) is constant and \(K(0) = 0\), we have \(\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = Pt \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m}}\,t^{1/2}\).
Practice Question 7 • Single-Correct MCQ
A \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\) ball moving at \(8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) collides head-on with a stationary \(5\,\mathrm{kg}\) ball, and the two stick together after impact. The kinetic energy lost in the collision is:
  • (A) \(36\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(48\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(60\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(96\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (C) \(60\,\mathrm{J}\)
For a completely inelastic collision with \(v_{2i} = 0\): \[ \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}\frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3 \times 5}{3 + 5}\right)(8)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{15}{8}\right)(64) = 60\,\mathrm{J} \]
Practice Question 8 • Single-Correct MCQ
The potential energy of a particle moving in one dimension is given by \(V(x) = \frac{a}{x^2} - \frac{b}{x}\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are positive constants. The equilibrium position of the particle is at:
  • (A) \(x = \frac{a}{b}\)
  • (B) \(x = \frac{2a}{b}\)
  • (C) \(x = \frac{b}{2a}\)
  • (D) \(x = \frac{a}{2b}\)
Answer: (B) \(x = \frac{2a}{b}\)
At equilibrium, \(F = -\frac{dV}{dx} = 0\).
\(\frac{dV}{dx} = -\frac{2a}{x^3} + \frac{b}{x^2} = 0 \implies \frac{b}{x^2} = \frac{2a}{x^3} \implies x = \frac{2a}{b}\).
Practice Question 9 • Statement-Based MCQ
Consider the following two statements:
Statement I: Total linear momentum is conserved at every instant during an elastic collision.
Statement II: Total kinetic energy of the colliding system remains constant at every instant during an elastic collision.
Choose the correct option:
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
Because internal impulsive forces obey Newton's Third Law at every instant, total linear momentum is conserved throughout the collision. However, during the brief contact interval, part of the kinetic energy is temporarily stored as elastic deformation potential energy, so kinetic energy is equal before and after the collision, not at every instant during contact.
Practice Question 10 • Assertion–Reason MCQ
Assertion (A): When a body moves in a circular path with uniform speed, the work done by the centripetal force is zero.
Reason (R): The centripetal force is always perpendicular to the instantaneous displacement of the body.
Choose the correct option:
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
Infinitesimal work \(dW = \mathbf{F}_c \cdot d\mathbf{r} = F_c\,dr\cos 90° = 0\), because the radial centripetal force is perpendicular to the tangential displacement.

9. Common Pitfalls & Formula Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Mistakes to Avoid
  • Action–Reaction Work Misconception: By Newton's Third Law, \(\mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{21} = \mathbf{0}\), but the work done by these two forces need not sum to zero because the two bodies may undergo different displacements.
  • Kilowatt-hour vs. Kilowatt: \(\mathrm{kW}\) is a unit of power, whereas \(\mathrm{kWh}\) is a unit of energy (\(3.6 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J}\)).
  • Reference Level of Potential Energy: The zero of potential energy is arbitrary, but once chosen for a problem, it must be used consistently throughout.
  • Non-Conservative Forces: Never apply \(K_i + V_i = K_f + V_f\) when friction or air drag does work; use \(E_f - E_i = W_{\text{nc}}\) or the Work–Energy Theorem instead.
Quantity Core Formula SI Unit Dimensions
Work (\(W\)) \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}\) or \(\int F\,dx\) \(\mathrm{J}\) \([\mathrm{ML}^2\mathrm{T}^{-2}]\)
Kinetic Energy (\(K\)) \(\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}\) \(\mathrm{J}\) \([\mathrm{ML}^2\mathrm{T}^{-2}]\)
Potential Energy (\(V\)) \(mgh\) (gravity); \(\frac{1}{2}kx^2\) (spring) \(\mathrm{J}\) \([\mathrm{ML}^2\mathrm{T}^{-2}]\)
Power (\(P\)) \(\frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) \(\mathrm{W}\) \([\mathrm{ML}^2\mathrm{T}^{-3}]\)
Concise Final Recap

Work is the dot product of force and displacement; the net work done on any body equals its change in kinetic energy. Conservative forces allow us to define potential energy so that total mechanical energy \(K + V\) remains constant in the absence of dissipative forces. Power tracks the rate of energy transfer via \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}\), while collisions always conserve total linear momentum—with kinetic energy conserved overall only when the collision is elastic.
