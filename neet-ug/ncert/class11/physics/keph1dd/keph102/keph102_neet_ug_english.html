Motion in a Straight Line (Rectilinear Kinematics)

Concept Mastery, Graphical Analysis, Calculus Tools, and Exam-Oriented Practice

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Context

Kinematics is the branch of mechanics that describes how objects move in space and time without examining the forces that cause the motion. For NEET UG Physics, one-dimensional (rectilinear) motion is a high-yield foundation: questions routinely test sign conventions, the difference between path length and displacement, calculus-based instantaneous quantities, graph interpretation, free fall, stopping distances, and relative velocity.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Apply the point-object approximation and choose an unambiguous coordinate axis and origin.
  • Distinguish between scalar path quantities (distance, speed) and signed vector-like 1D quantities (displacement, velocity, acceleration).
  • Compute average and instantaneous velocity or acceleration using both limiting slopes and differential calculus.
  • Interpret \(x\text{-}t\), \(v\text{-}t\), and \(a\text{-}t\) graphs, and identify physically impossible graphs.
  • Solve uniformly accelerated motion, vertical free-fall, \(n\text{th}\)-second displacement, and 1D relative-motion problems accurately.

2. Frame of Reference, Path Length, and Displacement

2.1 Point-Object Approximation & Coordinate Axis

An object is said to be in motion if its position changes with time relative to a chosen reference frame. When the physical dimensions of a moving body are negligible compared to the total distance it covers during the time of interest (for instance, a train travelling between two cities), we model the body as a point object.

For motion along a straight line, we align a single coordinate axis (commonly the \(x\)-axis or \(y\)-axis) with the path and pick a convenient origin \(O\) (\(x = 0\)). By standard convention, positions to the right of \(O\) (or vertically upward) are positive, and positions to the left (or downward) are negative.

2.2 Path Length (Distance) vs. Displacement

  • Path length (distance, \(d\)): The total length of the actual trajectory traversed by the particle during a time interval. It is a scalar quantity, is always non-negative (\(d \ge 0\)), and never decreases with time.
  • Displacement (\(\Delta x\)): The net change in position from the initial coordinate \(x_1\) to the final coordinate \(x_2\): \[ \Delta x = x_2 - x_1 \] In one dimension, the algebraic sign (\(+\) or \(-\)) of \(\Delta x\) specifies its direction.
Tutorial Diagram 1: A particle moves from origin O (0 m) to P (+60 m) and reverses to Q (+20 m). The path length is 100 m, whereas the net displacement is +20 m.
Key Inequality (High-Yield NEET Concept)

Over any finite time interval, Path length \(\ge |\text{Displacement}|\). Equality holds if and only if the particle moves in a single fixed direction without reversing its motion.

3. Average and Instantaneous Velocity & Speed

3.1 Average Velocity vs. Average Speed

Over a time interval \(\Delta t = t_2 - t_1\):

\[ \text{Average Velocity: } \bar{v} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{Average Speed: } v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Path Length}}{\Delta t} \]

Because path length is always greater than or equal to the magnitude of displacement, it follows directly that:

\[ \text{Average Speed} \ge |\text{Average Velocity}| \]

If a particle returns to its starting point, its average velocity is zero, even though its average speed is strictly positive.

Two Classic NEET Shortcuts for Average Speed
  • Equal Time Intervals: If a particle travels for two equal time intervals \(t\) with speeds \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) in the same direction, the average speed is the arithmetic mean: \[ v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{v_1 + v_2}{2} \]
  • Equal Distances: If a particle covers two equal distances \(s\) with speeds \(v_1\) and \(v_2\), the average speed is the harmonic mean: \[ v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2 v_1 v_2}{v_1 + v_2} \]

3.2 Instantaneous Velocity and Instantaneous Speed

Average velocity smooths out variations within an interval. To know how fast—and in which direction—an object moves at a specific instant \(t\), we shrink \(\Delta t\) toward zero:

\[ v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

Geometrically, \(\bar{v}\) is the slope of the secant line connecting two points on the \(x\text{-}t\) curve, whereas \(v(t)\) is the slope of the tangent to the \(x\text{-}t\) curve at that instant.

Instantaneous speed is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity, \(|v(t)|\). Unlike average quantities, instantaneous speed is always equal to the magnitude of instantaneous velocity because over an infinitesimally small interval \(\mathrm{d}t \to 0\), a particle cannot reverse direction, so the infinitesimal path length equals \(|\mathrm{d}x|\).

4. Acceleration & Graphical Kinematics

4.1 Average and Instantaneous Acceleration

Acceleration measures how rapidly velocity changes with time (not with distance). Over a finite interval \(\Delta t\):

\[ \bar{a} = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} \qquad\text{and}\qquad a = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 x}{\mathrm{d}t^2} = v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} \]

The chain-rule identity \(a = v\,\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}\) is indispensable whenever acceleration or velocity is given as a function of position \(x\) instead of time \(t\).

Critical Conceptual Distinction: Negative Acceleration vs. Retardation
  • A particle speeds up when velocity \(v\) and acceleration \(a\) have the same sign (both positive or both negative).
  • A particle slows down (decelerates / retards) when \(v\) and \(a\) have opposite signs.
  • Therefore, \(a < 0\) does not automatically mean slowing down: a ball falling downward (with upward chosen as positive) has \(v < 0\) and \(a = -g < 0\), so its speed increases!
  • Zero instantaneous velocity does not imply zero acceleration: at the peak of a vertical throw, \(v = 0\) instantaneously, while \(a = -g \ne 0\).

4.2 Reading Motion Graphs

  • \(x\text{-}t\) graph: Slope \(= v\). Curvature (concavity) indicates acceleration: concave upward implies \(a > 0\); concave downward implies \(a < 0\); a straight line implies \(a = 0\) (uniform velocity).
  • \(v\text{-}t\) graph: Slope \(= a\). Signed area between the curve and the time axis gives displacement: \[ \Delta x = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v\,\mathrm{d}t \] To find total distance from a \(v\text{-}t\) graph that crosses the time axis, sum the absolute areas above and below the axis: \(d = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v|\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  • \(a\text{-}t\) graph: Area under the curve gives the change in velocity: \[ \Delta v = v_2 - v_1 = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a\,\mathrm{d}t \]
Tutorial Diagram 3: (A) Concavity of an x-t curve reveals the sign of acceleration. (B) On a v-t graph, crossing the time axis at t₁ marks a reversal of direction; displacement is signed area, while distance is total unsigned area.

4.3 Rules for Physically Valid 1D Motion Graphs

  • A particle cannot occupy two positions or possess two velocities at the same instant—so a vertical line drawn on an \(x\text{-}t\) or \(v\text{-}t\) graph can intersect the curve at most once (closed loops or backward-bending curves are impossible).
  • Speed and total path length can never be negative.
  • Total path length can never decrease with time (its slope must be \(\ge 0\)).
  • In realistic motion, \(x(t)\) and \(v(t)\) are smooth without abrupt jumps, since a sudden discontinuity in velocity would require infinite acceleration.

5. Uniformly Accelerated Motion, Calculus & Free Fall

5.1 The Kinematic Equations (Constant Acceleration Only)

When acceleration \(a\) is constant in both magnitude and direction, integrating \(a = \mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t\) and \(v = \mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t\) from \(t = 0\) (where \(x = x_0\) and \(v = v_0\)) yields the three fundamental kinematic equations (with \(\Delta x = x - x_0\)):

\[ \text{(1) } v = v_0 + at \qquad \text{(2) } \Delta x = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2 \qquad \text{(3) } v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x \]

Two additional relations are frequently tested in NEET UG:

\[ \text{Average Velocity (constant } a\text{): } \bar{v} = \frac{v_0 + v}{2} \implies \Delta x = \left(\frac{v_0 + v}{2}\right)t \] \[ \text{Displacement in the } n\text{th second: } s_n = x(n) - x(n - 1) = v_0 + \frac{a}{2}(2n - 1) \]

Note on non-uniform acceleration: If \(a\) varies with time, position, or velocity, the algebraic kinematic equations above are invalid; you must return to the exact calculus definitions \(v = \mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t\) and \(a = \mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t = v\,\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}x\) and integrate.

5.2 Free Fall Under Gravity

Near Earth's surface, when air resistance is neglected, every freely falling or vertically projected body experiences a constant downward acceleration of magnitude \(g \approx 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) (often approximated as \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) in numerical problems). Choosing vertically upward as positive gives \(a = -g\).

  • Symmetry of Vertical Throw: For a body projected upward with speed \(u\) from ground level:
    • Time to reach maximum height: \(t_{\text{up}} = \dfrac{u}{g}\); total time of flight: \(T = \dfrac{2u}{g}\).
    • Maximum height attained: \(H = \dfrac{u^2}{2g}\).
    • At any given height, upward speed equals downward speed.
  • Galileo's Law of Odd Numbers: A body released from rest (\(v_0 = 0\)) under constant acceleration covers distances in successive equal time intervals \(\tau\) in the ratio: \[ 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 : 9 : \dots \] because total distance grows as \(t^2\) (\(1\tau^2, 4\tau^2, 9\tau^2, \dots\)).
  • Stopping Distance (\(d_s\)): If a vehicle moving at \(v_0\) brakes with uniform deceleration \(-a\) until \(v = 0\), then \(0 = v_0^2 - 2ad_s\), giving: \[ d_s = \frac{v_0^2}{2a} \propto v_0^2 \] Doubling the initial speed quadruples the stopping distance for the same braking deceleration.
  • Reaction Time (\(t_r\)): If a ruler dropped from rest falls a vertical distance \(d\) before being caught, \(d = \frac{1}{2}g t_r^2\), so \(t_r = \sqrt{2d/g}\).
Tutorial Diagram 4: Choosing +y upward with y = 0 at the ground allows a single-step algebraic equation y = y₀ + v₀t - ½gt² to determine the total flight time without splitting the path.

6. Relative Velocity in One Dimension

Motion is always measured relative to a reference frame. If two objects \(A\) and \(B\) move along the same straight line with positions \(x_A(t)\) and \(x_B(t)\) measured from a common ground origin, the position of \(A\) as observed from \(B\) is:

\[ x_{AB} = x_A - x_B \]

Differentiating with respect to time gives the relative velocity and relative acceleration of \(A\) with respect to \(B\):

\[ v_{AB} = v_A - v_B \qquad\text{and}\qquad a_{AB} = a_A - a_B \]
  • Same direction: The relative speed is \(|v_A - v_B|\). If \(v_A = v_B\), their separation remains constant and their \(x\text{-}t\) graphs are parallel lines.
  • Opposite directions: If \(v_A = +u\) and \(v_B = -w\), then \(v_{AB} = u - (-w) = u + w\), so they approach or separate at the sum of their speeds.
  • Relative kinematic equations: If \(a_{AB}\) is constant, you may apply \(x_{AB} = (x_{AB})_0 + v_{AB,0} t + \frac{1}{2}a_{AB} t^2\) and \(v_{AB}^2 = v_{AB,0}^2 + 2a_{AB}\,\Delta x_{AB}\) directly in the frame of \(B\).

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Calculus Kinematics & Motion Reversal

Problem: Suppose the position of a particle moving along the \(x\)-axis is given by \(x(t) = 2t^3 - 9t^2 + 12t + 4\), where \(x\) is in metres and \(t \ge 0\) is in seconds (assumed values). Find (a) the instants when the particle is momentarily at rest, (b) its acceleration at \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\), and (c) the total distance travelled from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\).

Solution:

(a) Differentiating \(x(t)\) with respect to \(t\) gives velocity: \[ v(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = 6t^2 - 18t + 12 = 6(t^2 - 3t + 2) = 6(t - 1)(t - 2)\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \] Setting \(v(t) = 0\), the particle is momentarily at rest at \(t = 1\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\).

(b) Differentiating \(v(t)\) gives acceleration: \[ a(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = 12t - 18\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \] At \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\), \(a(2) = 12(2) - 18 = +6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

(c) Because \(v(t)\) changes sign at \(t = 1\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\), we compute positions at \(t = 0, 1, 2, 3\,\mathrm{s}\): \(x(0) = 4\,\mathrm{m}\), \(x(1) = 9\,\mathrm{m}\), \(x(2) = 8\,\mathrm{m}\), and \(x(3) = 13\,\mathrm{m}\). Total distance is the sum of segment lengths: \[ d = |9 - 4| + |8 - 9| + |13 - 8| = 5 + 1 + 5 = 11\,\mathrm{m} \] (By contrast, net displacement is \(13 - 4 = 9\,\mathrm{m}\).)

Worked Example 2: Average Speed & Stopping Distance

Problem: Assume a scooter travels the first half of a straight \(120\,\mathrm{m}\) track at \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and the second half at \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). (a) What is its average speed? (b) At the end of the track, it brakes uniformly and stops in \(9\,\mathrm{m}\). If it had entered the braking zone at \(30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) with the same deceleration, what would its stopping distance be?

Solution:

(a) Since the two segments have equal distances, the average speed is the harmonic mean: \[ v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2 v_1 v_2}{v_1 + v_2} = \frac{2 \times 10 \times 15}{10 + 15} = \frac{300}{25} = 12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

(b) For constant braking deceleration, \(d_s = \frac{v_0^2}{2a} \propto v_0^2\). Doubling the entry speed from \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) to \(30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) multiplies the stopping distance by \(2^2 = 4\): \[ d_s' = 4 \times 9\,\mathrm{m} = 36\,\mathrm{m} \]

Worked Example 3: Vertical Throw from an Elevated Platform

Problem: Suppose a stone is thrown vertically upward at \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) from the edge of a bridge \(50\,\mathrm{m}\) above a river. Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and neglecting air resistance, find the time taken for the stone to strike the water.

Solution:

Choose \(y = 0\) at the water surface and \(+y\) vertically upward. Then \(y_0 = +50\,\mathrm{m}\), \(v_0 = +15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(a = -10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and final position \(y = 0\). Using \(y = y_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\): \[ 0 = 50 + 15t - 5t^2 \implies t^2 - 3t - 10 = 0 \implies (t - 5)(t + 2) = 0 \] Since \(t > 0\), the stone hits the water at \(t = 5\,\mathrm{s}\).

Worked Example 4: Relative Motion in One Dimension

Problem: Assume Train A (\(120\,\mathrm{m}\) long) moves east at \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and Train B (\(180\,\mathrm{m}\) long) moves west on a parallel track at \(30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). How long do they take to completely cross each other?

Solution:

Since the trains move in opposite directions, the relative speed of A with respect to B is: \[ |v_{AB}| = 20 - (-30) = 50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \] To cross completely from nose-to-nose to tail-to-tail, the relative distance covered is the sum of their lengths, \(120 + 180 = 300\,\mathrm{m}\). Thus: \[ t = \frac{300\,\mathrm{m}}{50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}} = 6\,\mathrm{s} \]

9. NEET UG Practice Questions & Worked Solutions

The following original practice questions are modeled on the NEET UG syllabus and examination pattern (single-correct 4-option MCQs, including statement-based and assertion-reason items).

Practice Question 1 (Path Length vs. Displacement)

A particle moves along a straight line from \(x = -12\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(x = +18\,\mathrm{m}\) and then turns back to \(x = +6\,\mathrm{m}\), all in \(10\,\mathrm{s}\). The ratio of its average speed to the magnitude of its average velocity over this interval is:

  • (A) \(7 : 3\)
  • (B) \(3 : 7\)
  • (C) \(5 : 3\)
  • (D) \(1 : 1\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Total path length \(= |18 - (-12)| + |6 - 18| = 30 + 12 = 42\,\mathrm{m}\). Magnitude of net displacement \(= |6 - (-12)| = 18\,\mathrm{m}\). Since both average speed and \(|\bar{v}|\) divide by the same time \(10\,\mathrm{s}\), their ratio equals \(\frac{42}{18} = \frac{7}{3}\).

Practice Question 2 (Average Speed)

A cyclist covers the first one-third of a straight journey at \(10\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\), the second one-third at \(20\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\), and the final one-third at \(60\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\). The average speed for the entire journey is:

  • (A) \(30\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(20\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(18\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(24\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Let total distance be \(3d\). Total time \(T = \frac{d}{10} + \frac{d}{20} + \frac{d}{60} = d\left(\frac{6 + 3 + 1}{60}\right) = \frac{d}{6}\). Thus, \(v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{3d}{d/6} = 18\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 3 (Calculus: Position-Dependent Acceleration)

The time \(t\) and position \(x\) of a particle moving in a straight line are related by \(t = \alpha x^2 + \beta x\), where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are positive constants. The acceleration of the particle as a function of its velocity \(v\) is:

  • (A) \(-2\alpha v^2\)
  • (B) \(-2\alpha v^3\)
  • (C) \(2\alpha v^3\)
  • (D) \(-2\beta v^3\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Differentiating \(t = \alpha x^2 + \beta x\) with respect to \(x\) gives \(\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}x} = 2\alpha x + \beta\), so \(\frac{1}{v} = 2\alpha x + \beta\). Differentiating again with respect to \(t\): \(-\frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2\alpha \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2\alpha v \implies a = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = -2\alpha v^3\).

Practice Question 4 (Conceptual: Statement I & Statement II)

Read the two statements carefully and select the correct option:
Statement I: A particle in one-dimensional motion with positive acceleration must always be speeding up.
Statement II: A particle in one-dimensional motion can have zero velocity at an instant while having non-zero acceleration at that same instant.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
  • (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  • (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
Answer: (D)

Solution: Statement I is false because if \(v < 0\) and \(a > 0\), the velocity and acceleration have opposite signs, so the particle slows down. Statement II is true (for example, a ball at the highest point of a vertical throw has \(v = 0\) and \(a = -g \ne 0\)).

Practice Question 5 (Graphical Interpretation)

A particle starts from rest and moves along a straight line. Its acceleration-time (\(a\text{-}t\)) graph is a constant \(+4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\), and \(-2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) from \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\) to \(t = 9\,\mathrm{s}\). What is the velocity of the particle at \(t = 9\,\mathrm{s}\)?

  • (A) \(0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(24\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Change in velocity equals the signed area under the \(a\text{-}t\) graph: \(\Delta v = (+4)(3) + (-2)(9 - 3) = 12 - 12 = 0\). Since \(v_0 = 0\), \(v(9) = 0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 6 (Free Fall & \(n\text{th}\)-Second Distance)

A ball is dropped from rest from the top of a tall building. If it travels a distance \(h\) during the first \(2\,\mathrm{s}\) and a distance \(3h\) during the next \(2\,\mathrm{s}\), what is the ratio of the distance covered in the \(3\text{rd}\) second to the distance covered in the \(1\text{st}\) second?

  • (A) \(3 : 1\)
  • (B) \(5 : 1\)
  • (C) \(7 : 1\)
  • (D) \(9 : 1\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: By Galileo's law of odd numbers, for a body starting from rest under uniform acceleration, distances covered in successive \(1\,\mathrm{s}\) intervals (\(1\text{st}, 2\text{nd}, 3\text{rd}, \dots\)) are in the ratio \(1 : 3 : 5 : \dots\). Therefore, \(s_3 : s_1 = 5 : 1\). (Alternatively, \(s_n = \frac{1}{2}g(2n - 1)\), so \(s_3 / s_1 = 5 / 1\).)

Practice Question 7 (Vertical Projection & Ratio of Heights)

Two balls P and Q are thrown vertically upward from the ground with initial speeds in the ratio \(2 : 3\). Neglecting air resistance, the ratio of the maximum heights reached by P and Q is:

  • (A) \(2 : 3\)
  • (B) \(3 : 2\)
  • (C) \(4 : 9\)
  • (D) \(9 : 4\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Maximum height \(H = \frac{u^2}{2g} \propto u^2\). Thus, \(\frac{H_P}{H_Q} = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{9}\).

Practice Question 8 (Stopping Distance)

A car moving at \(54\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) is brought to rest by uniform braking over a distance of \(15\,\mathrm{m}\). If the same car is moving at \(108\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) and the same braking deceleration is applied, the minimum stopping distance will be:

  • (A) \(30\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(45\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(60\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(75\,\mathrm{m}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Stopping distance \(d_s = \frac{v_0^2}{2a} \propto v_0^2\). Increasing speed from \(54\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) to \(108\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) doubles \(v_0\), so \(d_s\) becomes \(2^2 \times 15\,\mathrm{m} = 60\,\mathrm{m}\).

Practice Question 9 (Assertion-Reason)

Assertion (A): The instantaneous speed of a particle is always equal to the magnitude of its instantaneous velocity.
Reason (R): Over an infinitesimally small time interval \(\Delta t \to 0\), the path length covered by a particle equals the magnitude of its displacement.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: As \(\Delta t \to 0\), the particle cannot reverse direction within the infinitesimal interval, so \(\Delta d = |\Delta x|\). Dividing by \(\Delta t\) gives \(|v| = \text{instantaneous speed}\). Thus, both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A.

Practice Question 10 (Relative Velocity in 1D)

Two cars X and Y start from the same point at \(t = 0\) and move in the same direction along a straight highway. Car X moves with a constant velocity of \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), while Car Y starts from rest with a uniform acceleration of \(4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). At what time \(t > 0\) does Car Y overtake Car X?

  • (A) \(5\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (C) \(15\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (D) \(20\,\mathrm{s}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: At overtaking, their positions are equal: \(x_X = x_Y \implies 20t = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(4)t^2 = 2t^2\). Since \(t > 0\), \(2t = 20 \implies t = 10\,\mathrm{s}\).

10. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Forgetting unit conversions: Always convert \(\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) to \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) by multiplying by \(\frac{5}{18}\) before using \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) or \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).
  • Using constant-\(a\) formulas when \(a\) varies: Never apply \(v = v_0 + at\) if \(a\) depends on \(t\), \(x\), or \(v\); integrate instead.
  • Confusing displacement with distance on a \(v\text{-}t\) graph: Always check whether the \(v\text{-}t\) curve crosses the time axis before computing total distance.
  • Mixing signs in vertical motion: Fix one positive direction at the start and assign signs to \(y_0\), \(v_0\), and \(a\) consistently.

NEET UG Revision Checklist

  • Can I distinguish \(\text{Distance} \ge |\text{Displacement}|\) and \(\text{Average Speed} \ge |\text{Average Velocity}|\)?
  • Do I know both \(a = \mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t\) and \(a = v\,\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}x\)?
  • Can I determine speeding up vs. slowing down purely from the relative signs of \(v\) and \(a\)?
  • Can I compute \(s_n = v_0 + \frac{a}{2}(2n-1)\) and apply the \(1:3:5:7\) odd-number rule for free fall from rest?
  • Can I solve 1D overtaking and crossing problems using \(v_{AB} = v_A - v_B\)?
Concise Final Recap
