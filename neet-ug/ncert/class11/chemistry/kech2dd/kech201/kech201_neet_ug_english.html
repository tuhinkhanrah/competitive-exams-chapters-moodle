Redox Reactions: Electron Transfer, Oxidation Numbers & Electrode Processes

A complete, self-contained tutorial covering classical and electronic concepts, oxidation number rules, reaction balancing, redox titrations, and standard electrode potentials.

1. Learning Objectives and Overview

Chemical transformations that power biological metabolism, fuel combustion, metal extraction, battery operation, and corrosion all share a common mechanistic thread: the simultaneous transfer or shift of electrons between interacting species. Such coupled processes are called redox reactions (short for reduction–oxidation reactions). Oxidation never occurs in isolation; every electron lost by one species must be accepted by another.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial

  • Distinguish oxidation, reduction, oxidants (oxidizing agents), and reductants (reducing agents) using classical, electronic, and oxidation-number frameworks.
  • Assign oxidation numbers accurately, including exceptions in peroxides, superoxides, metal hydrides, and oxygen–fluorine compounds, and resolve fractional oxidation states using molecular structures.
  • Classify redox reactions into combination, decomposition, displacement, and disproportionation processes.
  • Balance redox equations systematically in acidic and basic media via both the oxidation number method and the half-reaction (ion-electron) method.
  • Apply redox stoichiometry to volumetric titrations and predict reaction feasibility using standard electrode potentials (\(E^\circ\)) in galvanic cells.

2. Three Conceptual Frameworks of Redox Reactions

Chemistry evolved through three complementary ways of defining oxidation and reduction. Understanding how they connect prevents errors when analyzing unfamiliar reactions.

2.1 Classical Concept (Elemental Gain or Loss)

  • Oxidation: Addition of oxygen or a more electronegative element to a substance, or removal of hydrogen or a more electropositive element from a substance.
  • Reduction: Removal of oxygen or a more electronegative element from a substance, or addition of hydrogen or a more electropositive element to a substance.

For instance, when hydrogen sulfide reacts with chlorine gas, hydrogen is removed from \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) (oxidizing it to sulfur) while hydrogen is added to \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) (reducing it to \(\mathrm{HCl}\)):

\[\mathrm{H_2S(g) + Cl_2(g) \rightarrow 2HCl(g) + S(s)}\]

2.2 Electronic Concept (Electron Transfer)

For ionic reactions, electron movement is explicit. Each overall redox process can be split into two half-reactions—one showing electron release and the other electron capture:

  • Oxidation: Loss of electron(s) by a chemical species.
  • Reduction: Gain of electron(s) by a chemical species.
  • Reducing Agent (Reductant): The electron donor; it gets oxidized while reducing the partner species.
  • Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant): The electron acceptor; it gets reduced while oxidizing the partner species.

Consider the formation of solid sodium chloride from its elements:

\[\text{Oxidation half: } 2\mathrm{Na(s)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Na^+} + 2\mathrm{e^-}\] \[\text{Reduction half: } \mathrm{Cl_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Cl^-}\] \[\text{Overall: } 2\mathrm{Na(s) + Cl_2(g) \rightarrow 2NaCl(s)}\]

2.3 Oxidation Number Concept (Book-keeping for Covalent & Ionic Species)

In covalent reactions such as \(\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 4Cl_2(g) \rightarrow CCl_4(l) + 4HCl(g)}\), electrons are shared unequally rather than transferred completely. The oxidation number (or oxidation state) is a formal book-keeping charge assigned to an atom by pretending that every shared electron pair in a covalent bond belongs entirely to the more electronegative atom.

  • Oxidation: Increase in the oxidation number of an element.
  • Reduction: Decrease in the oxidation number of an element.

3. Competitive Electron Transfer and Activity Series

When a metal strip is immersed in an aqueous solution containing ions of another metal, the two metals compete for electrons. The metal with a stronger tendency to lose electrons donates them to the ions of the less active metal.

  • Zinc in Copper(II) Nitrate: Metallic zinc dissolves as \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)}\), the blue color of \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)}\) fades, and reddish-brown copper deposits on the strip. The equilibrium lies overwhelmingly toward the products: \[\mathrm{Zn(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)}\] Reversing the experiment—placing a copper strip in \(\mathrm{ZnSO_4(aq)}\)—produces no detectable reaction.
  • Copper in Silver Nitrate: Copper metal reduces \(\mathrm{Ag^+(aq)}\) to silvery metallic \(\mathrm{Ag(s)}\), turning the initially colorless solution blue as \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)}\) forms: \[\mathrm{Cu(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) \rightarrow Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2Ag(s)}\]
  • Intermediate Equilibria: Placing cobalt in \(\mathrm{NiSO_4(aq)}\) gives \(\mathrm{Co(s) + Ni^{2+}(aq) \rightleftharpoons Co^{2+}(aq) + Ni(s)}\), where moderate concentrations of both ions coexist at equilibrium.

Comparing these outcomes establishes the electron-releasing (reducing) order: \(\mathrm{Zn > Cu > Ag}\). By contrast, among halogens (Group 17), the oxidizing (electron-accepting) power decreases down the group: \(\mathrm{F_2 > Cl_2 > Br_2 > I_2}\).

4. Oxidation Number Rules, Stock Notation & Fractional States

4.1 Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers

  1. Free / Uncombined Elements: Every atom in an uncombined element (\(\mathrm{H_2, O_3, P_4, S_8, Na, Fe}\)) has an oxidation number of \(0\).
  2. Monatomic Ions: The oxidation number equals the ionic charge. Alkali metals (Group 1) are always \(+1\), alkaline earth metals (Group 2) are always \(+2\), and aluminium is \(+3\) in their compounds.
  3. Oxygen: Usually \(-2\), with three critical exceptions:
    • Peroxides (\(\mathrm{H_2O_2, Na_2O_2, BaO_2}\)) containing an \(\mathrm{-O-O-}\) linkage: oxygen is \(-1\).
    • Superoxides (\(\mathrm{KO_2, RbO_2}\)): each oxygen is \(-1/2\).
    • Oxygen fluorides: in \(\mathrm{OF_2}\), oxygen is \(+2\); in \(\mathrm{O_2F_2}\), oxygen is \(+1\) (because fluorine is more electronegative).
  4. Hydrogen: Usually \(+1\), except in binary metal hydrides (\(\mathrm{LiH, NaH, CaH_2}\)), where hydrogen is \(-1\).
  5. Halogens: Fluorine is always \(-1\) in all its compounds. Chlorine, bromine, and iodine are \(-1\) as halide ions, but show positive oxidation numbers when bonded to oxygen or a more electronegative halogen (as in oxoacids and oxoanions).
  6. Algebraic Sum Rule: In a neutral compound, the sum of oxidation numbers of all atoms is \(0\). In a polyatomic ion, the sum equals the net charge of the ion.

4.2 Stock Notation

In Stock notation, the oxidation state of a metal is written as a Roman numeral in parentheses immediately after the metal's symbol. For example, \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\) is written \(\mathrm{Cu_2(I)O}\), \(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}\) is \(\mathrm{Fe_2(III)O_3}\), and \(\mathrm{SnCl_4}\) is \(\mathrm{Sn(IV)Cl_4}\). This notation immediately distinguishes oxidized and reduced forms of the same metal.

4.3 Resolving Fractional Oxidation Numbers via Structure

Electrons are never transferred in fractions, so a fractional oxidation number obtained from a molecular formula is only an average across atoms of the same element occupying different chemical environments. Drawing the structural formula reveals the true integer oxidation states:

  • Carbon suboxide (\(\mathrm{C_3O_2}\)): Structure \(\mathrm{O=C=C^*=C=O}\). The two terminal carbons bonded to oxygen are each \(+2\), while the central carbon (\(\mathrm{C^*}\)) bonded only to two identical carbons is \(0\). Average \(= (+2 + 0 + 2)/3 = +4/3\).
  • Tribromooctaoxide (\(\mathrm{Br_3O_8}\)): Each of the two terminal \(\mathrm{Br}\) atoms is double-bonded to three oxygens (\(+6\)), while the central \(\mathrm{Br^*}\) is double-bonded to two oxygens (\(+4\)). Average \(= (+6 + 4 + 6)/3 = +16/3\).
  • Tetrathionate ion (\(\mathrm{S_4O_6^{2-}}\)): The two terminal sulfur atoms each bear three oxygens and one \(\mathrm{S-S}\) bond (\(+5\)), whereas the two inner sulfurs (\(\mathrm{S^*}\)) are bonded only to other sulfurs (\(0\)). Average \(= (+5 + 0 + 0 + 5)/4 = +2.5\).
  • Mixed Oxides (\(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4, Pb_3O_4}\)): \(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\) behaves as \(\mathrm{FeO \cdot Fe_2O_3}\) (one \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) and two \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\), average \(+8/3\)). Similarly, \(\mathrm{Pb_3O_4}\) is \(\mathrm{2PbO \cdot PbO_2}\) (two \(\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}\) and one \(\mathrm{Pb^{4+}}\)). With \(\mathrm{HCl}\), \(\mathrm{PbO_2}\) oxidizes \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) to \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) while \(\mathrm{PbO}\) undergoes acid–base neutralization; with \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) (itself an oxidant), \(\mathrm{PbO_2}\) remains passive and only \(\mathrm{PbO}\) reacts to form \(\mathrm{Pb(NO_3)_2}\).

5. Classification of Redox Reactions

Redox reactions fall into four major structural categories:

  1. Combination Reactions (\(\mathrm{A + B \rightarrow C}\)): At least one of the reactants must be in the elemental state. All combustion reactions involving \(\mathrm{O_2}\) belong here, such as \(\mathrm{3Mg(s) + N_2(g) \rightarrow Mg_3N_2(s)}\).
  2. Decomposition Reactions (\(\mathrm{C \rightarrow A + B}\)): Breakdown of a compound where at least one product is in the elemental state, such as \(\mathrm{2KClO_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2KCl(s) + 3O_2(g)}\).
    Exception check: Thermal decomposition of limestone, \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} CaO(s) + CO_2(g)}\), is not a redox reaction because no atom changes oxidation state (\(\mathrm{Ca}=+2, \mathrm{C}=+4, \mathrm{O}=-2\) throughout).
  3. Displacement Reactions (\(\mathrm{X + YZ \rightarrow XZ + Y}\)):
    • Metal displacement: A more reducing metal displaces a less active metal from its salt or oxide, e.g., \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3(s) + 2Al(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} Al_2O_3(s) + 2Cr(s)}\).
    • Non-metal displacement: Active alkali/alkaline earth metals (\(\mathrm{Na, Ca}\)) displace \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from cold water; \(\mathrm{Mg}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe}\) displace \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from steam; \(\mathrm{Zn, Mg, Fe}\) displace \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from dilute acids, whereas \(\mathrm{Ag}\) and \(\mathrm{Au}\) do not. Among halogens, \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) displaces \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) and \(\mathrm{I^-}\) from aqueous solutions (the basis of the laboratory Layer Test with \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\)). Fluorine is so reactive that it oxidizes water itself to \(\mathrm{O_2}\): \(\mathrm{2F_2(g) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 4HF(aq) + O_2(g)}\).
  4. Disproportionation Reactions: A single element in an intermediate oxidation state is simultaneously oxidized to a higher state and reduced to a lower state. The element must therefore be capable of existing in at least three oxidation states.
    • Example: \(\mathrm{2H_2O_2(aq) \rightarrow 2H_2O(l) + O_2(g)}\) (oxygen changes from \(-1\) to \(-2\) and \(0\)).
    • Example in alkali: \(\mathrm{Cl_2(g) + 2OH^-(aq) \rightarrow ClO^-(aq) + Cl^-(aq) + H_2O(l)}\) (chlorine changes from \(0\) to \(+1\) and \(-1\)).
    • Key NEET Exceptions: Fluorine (\(\mathrm{F_2}\)) cannot disproportionate because it has no positive oxidation state (with \(\mathrm{OH^-}\), it forms \(\mathrm{F^-}\) and \(\mathrm{OF_2}\)). Similarly, an element already in its maximum oxidation state (such as \(\mathrm{Cl}\) at \(+7\) in \(\mathrm{ClO_4^-}\)) or minimum oxidation state cannot disproportionate.

6. Balancing Redox Equations & Redox Titrations

6.1 Oxidation Number Method

  1. Write the skeletal ionic equation and assign oxidation numbers to all atoms.
  2. Identify the atoms undergoing oxidation and reduction; calculate the total increase and total decrease in oxidation number per formula unit.
  3. Multiply the oxidant and reductant by suitable integers so that total oxidation-number increase equals total decrease, and balance the oxidized/reduced atoms.
  4. Balance net ionic charge by adding \(\mathrm{H^+}\) (acidic medium) or \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) (basic medium) to the appropriate side.
  5. Balance hydrogen atoms by adding \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), then verify that oxygen atoms match on both sides.

6.2 Half-Reaction (Ion-Electron) Method

  1. Split the skeletal equation into an oxidation half-reaction and a reduction half-reaction.
  2. In each half-reaction, balance all atoms other than \(\mathrm{O}\) and \(\mathrm{H}\).
  3. Balance \(\mathrm{O}\) atoms by adding \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), and balance \(\mathrm{H}\) atoms by adding \(\mathrm{H^+}\). (If the medium is basic, neutralize every \(\mathrm{H^+}\) by adding an equal number of \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) ions to both sides, combining \(\mathrm{H^+ + OH^- \rightarrow H_2O}\).)
  4. Balance charge in each half-reaction by adding electrons (\(\mathrm{e^-}\)).
  5. Multiply the half-reactions so that electrons gained equal electrons lost, add them together, and cancel common terms.

6.3 Redox Titrations and Indicators

  • Self-Indicator (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)): Deep purple \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) acts as its own indicator in acidic titrations against \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) or \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\). The first persistent pale pink tinge at \(\sim 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) signals the endpoint.
  • External Redox Indicator (\(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\)): Dichromate is not a self-indicator; diphenylamine is added and turns intense blue immediately after the equivalence point when excess \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) oxidizes it.
  • Iodometric Titration (Starch + Thiosulfate): Oxidants such as \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) oxidize \(\mathrm{I^-}\) to \(\mathrm{I_2}\) (kept soluble as \(\mathrm{KI_3}\)): \[2\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq) + 4I^-(aq) \rightarrow Cu_2I_2(s) + I_2(aq)}\] The liberated \(\mathrm{I_2}\) forms an intense blue complex with starch and is titrated with standard sodium thiosulfate (\(\mathrm{Na_2S_2O_3}\)) until the blue color vanishes: \[\mathrm{I_2(aq) + 2S_2O_3^{2-}(aq) \rightarrow 2I^-(aq) + S_4O_6^{2-}(aq)}\]

7. Redox Reactions, Galvanic Cells & Electrode Potentials

When oxidation and reduction half-reactions are physically separated into two half-cells connected by an external wire and an internal salt bridge (a U-tube filled with \(\mathrm{KCl}\) or \(\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}\) in agar-agar), electron transfer occurs through the wire as usable electrical current. Each half-cell contains a redox couple—the oxidized and reduced forms of a species at an interface, written as \(\mathrm{M^{n+}/M}\) (oxidized form first).

7.1 Standard Electrode Potential (\(E^\circ\))

When all dissolved species are at unit concentration (\(1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)), gases are at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\), and temperature is \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the potential of a half-cell measured against the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (\(\mathrm{H^+/H_2}\), defined as \(E^\circ = 0.00\,\mathrm{V}\)) is its Standard Reduction Potential (\(E^\circ\)).

Interpreting \(E^\circ\) Values for NEET UG

  • More Positive \(E^\circ\): Greater tendency for the oxidized form to gain electrons (be reduced). Hence, the oxidized form is a stronger oxidizing agent (e.g., \(\mathrm{F_2}\) with \(E^\circ = +2.87\,\mathrm{V}\) is the strongest oxidant).
  • More Negative \(E^\circ\): Greater tendency for the reduced form to lose electrons (be oxidized). Hence, the reduced form is a stronger reducing agent than \(\mathrm{H_2}\) (e.g., \(\mathrm{Li}\) with \(E^\circ = -3.05\,\mathrm{V}\) is the strongest reductant in aqueous solution).
  • Feasibility Rule: A redox reaction \(\mathrm{Ox_1 + Red_2 \rightarrow Red_1 + Ox_2}\) is spontaneous under standard conditions if \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode (reduced)}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode (oxidized)}} > 0\).

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Oxidation Number with Peroxo Linkages

Problem: Determine the oxidation number of chromium in chromium(VI) peroxide, \(\mathrm{CrO_5}\), and sulfur in peroxomonosulfuric acid, \(\mathrm{H_2SO_5}\). Explain why the naive algebraic formula gives an impossible value.

Solution:

If all oxygens were assumed to be \(-2\), \(\mathrm{Cr}\) in \(\mathrm{CrO_5}\) would appear to be \(+10\) and \(\mathrm{S}\) in \(\mathrm{H_2SO_5}\) would appear to be \(+8\), both exceeding their maximum possible valence-electron counts (\(6\)).

  • In \(\mathrm{CrO_5}\) (butterfly structure), four oxygens form two \(\mathrm{-O-O-}\) peroxo linkages (each \(\mathrm{O} = -1\)) and one oxygen is a normal oxo ligand (\(=O\), \(\mathrm{O} = -2\)). Thus: \[x + 4(-1) + 1(-2) = 0 \implies x = +6\]
  • In \(\mathrm{H_2SO_5}\), two oxygens form one peroxo linkage (\(\mathrm{H-O-O-SO_2-OH}\), two \(\mathrm{O} = -1\)) and three oxygens are normal (\(-2\)). With \(\mathrm{H} = +1\): \[2(+1) + x + 2(-1) + 3(-2) = 0 \implies x = +6\]
Worked Example 2: Balancing in Acidic Medium (Ion-Electron Method)

Problem: Balance the oxidation of oxalate ion (\(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\)) by permanganate ion (\(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\)) in acidic solution to form \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

Solution:

  • Oxidation half: \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-} \rightarrow 2CO_2 + 2e^-}\) (C goes from \(+3\) to \(+4\); atoms and charge balanced).
  • Reduction half: \(\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 8H^+ + 5e^- \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 4H_2O}\) (Mn goes from \(+7\) to \(+2\)).
  • Equalize electrons: Multiply oxidation half by \(5\) and reduction half by \(2\) (LCM \(= 10\,\mathrm{e^-}\)), then add: \[2\mathrm{MnO_4^-(aq) + 5C_2O_4^{2-}(aq) + 16H^+(aq) \rightarrow 2Mn^{2+}(aq) + 10CO_2(g) + 8H_2O(l)}\]
Worked Example 3: Balancing in Basic Medium

Problem: Balance the reaction of \(\mathrm{Cr(OH)_3(s)}\) with hypochlorite ion (\(\mathrm{ClO^-}\)) in basic aqueous solution to yield \(\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}(aq)}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)}\).

Solution:

  • Oxidation half: \(\mathrm{Cr(OH)_3 \rightarrow CrO_4^{2-}}\). Add \(1\,\mathrm{H_2O}\) to left (\(\mathrm{O}\) balanced), \(5\,\mathrm{H^+}\) to right (\(\mathrm{H}\) balanced). Neutralize with \(5\,\mathrm{OH^-}\) on both sides: \[\mathrm{Cr(OH)_3 + 5OH^- \rightarrow CrO_4^{2-} + 4H_2O + 3e^-}\]
  • Reduction half: \(\mathrm{ClO^- + H_2O + 2e^- \rightarrow Cl^- + 2OH^-}\).
  • Equalize electrons: Multiply oxidation half by \(2\) and reduction half by \(3\) (\(6\,\mathrm{e^-}\) transferred), then add and cancel \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\): \[2\mathrm{Cr(OH)_3(s) + 3ClO^-(aq) + 4OH^-(aq) \rightarrow 2CrO_4^{2-}(aq) + 3Cl^-(aq) + 5H_2O(l)}\]
Worked Example 4: Redox Titration Stoichiometry (Hypothetical Sample)

Problem: (Assumed laboratory data) A \(25.0\,\mathrm{mL}\) aliquot of an acidic \(\mathrm{FeSO_4}\) solution requires \(20.0\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.020\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) solution to reach the pale-pink endpoint. Calculate the molarity of \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) in the solution.

Solution:

In acidic medium, \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) (\(n\)-factor \(= 5\)) oxidizes \(5\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) (\(n\)-factor \(= 1\)):

\[\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 5Fe^{2+} + 8H^+ \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 5Fe^{3+} + 4H_2O}\]

Moles of \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) used \(= 0.020\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} \times 0.0200\,\mathrm{L} = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol}\).

Moles of \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) oxidized \(= 5 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-4} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol}\).

Molarity of \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}} = \dfrac{2.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.0250\,\mathrm{L}} = 0.080\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\).

9. NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are modeled on the NEET UG examination pattern (single-correct MCQs, statement-based, assertion–reason, and matching). Attempt each question before reviewing its worked solution.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The oxidation numbers of the two nitrogen atoms in ammonium nitrate, \(\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}\), are respectively:

  • (A) \(+1\) and \(+1\)
  • (B) \(-3\) and \(+5\)
  • (C) \(-3\) and \(+3\)
  • (D) \(+3\) and \(-5\)
Answer: (B) Worked Solution: \(\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}\) is an ionic salt composed of \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\) and \(\mathrm{NO_3^-}\). In \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\), \(x + 4(+1) = +1 \implies x = -3\). In \(\mathrm{NO_3^-}\), \(y + 3(-2) = -1 \implies y = +5\). (Using the whole formula gives an average of \(+1\), which hides the two distinct states.)
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following species cannot undergo a disproportionation reaction?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{ClO_2^-}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{ClO_4^-}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\)
Answer: (C) Worked Solution: In \(\mathrm{ClO_4^-}\), chlorine is in its highest possible oxidation state (\(+7\)), so it can only be reduced and cannot be oxidized further. In \(\mathrm{ClO_2^-}\) (\(\mathrm{Cl}=+3\)), \(\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}\) (\(\mathrm{Mn}=+6\)), and \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) (\(\mathrm{O}=-1\)), the central element is in an intermediate oxidation state.
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the balanced ionic equation for the reaction in basic medium: \[a\,\mathrm{I^-(aq)} + b\,\mathrm{MnO_4^-(aq)} + c\,\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow d\,\mathrm{I_2(s)} + e\,\mathrm{MnO_2(s)} + f\,\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\] the stoichiometric coefficients \((a, b, c, f)\) are respectively:

  • (A) \(6, 2, 4, 8\)
  • (B) \(2, 2, 4, 4\)
  • (C) \(6, 2, 2, 4\)
  • (D) \(4, 2, 4, 8\)
Answer: (A) Worked Solution: Oxidation half: \(2\mathrm{I^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{I_2} + 2\mathrm{e^-}\). Reduction half (basic): \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} + 3\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{MnO_2} + 4\mathrm{OH^-}\). Multiplying the first by \(3\) and the second by \(2\) gives \(6\mathrm{I^-} + 2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 4\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{I_2} + 2\mathrm{MnO_2} + 8\mathrm{OH^-}\). Hence \(a=6, b=2, c=4, f=8\).
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following reactions is not a redox reaction?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{2NaH(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2Na(s) + H_2(g)}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} CaO(s) + CO_2(g)}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Cu_2O(s) + Cu_2S(s) \rightarrow 6Cu(s) + SO_2(g)}\) (unbalanced)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{2F_2(g) + 2OH^-(aq) \rightarrow 2F^-(aq) + OF_2(g) + H_2O(l)}\)
Answer: (B) Worked Solution: In \(\mathrm{CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2}\), the oxidation numbers remain unchanged (\(\mathrm{Ca}=+2, \mathrm{C}=+4, \mathrm{O}=-2\)). All other options involve changes in oxidation numbers.
Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Fluorine does not show a disproportionation reaction when treated with aqueous alkali.

Reason (R): Being the most electronegative element, fluorine cannot exhibit any positive oxidation state.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A) Worked Solution: Disproportionation requires an element to be simultaneously oxidized to a higher oxidation state and reduced to a lower one. Since elemental \(\mathrm{F_2}\) (\(0\)) cannot go above \(0\) (only to \(-1\)), it cannot disproportionate.
Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Given standard reduction potentials: \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{K^+/K}} = -2.93\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{Mg^{2+}/Mg}} = -2.36\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cr^{3+}/Cr}} = -0.74\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{Ag^+/Ag}} = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\). The increasing order of reducing power of these metals is:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{K < Mg < Cr < Ag}\)

  • (C) \(\mathrm{Cr < Mg < K < Ag}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Ag < Mg < Cr < K}\)
Answer: (B) Worked Solution: Lower (more negative) standard reduction potential corresponds to stronger reducing power of the metal. Ordering from highest \(E^\circ\) to lowest \(E^\circ\) gives increasing reducing power: \(\mathrm{Ag\ (+0.80\,V) < Cr\ (-0.74\,V) < Mg\ (-2.36\,V) < K\ (-2.93\,V)}\).

Statement I: In the reaction \(\mathrm{2S_2O_3^{2-}(aq) + I_2(s) \rightarrow S_4O_6^{2-}(aq) + 2I^-(aq)}\), thiosulfate is oxidized to tetrathionate.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Using \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}} = +0.77\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{I_2/I^-}} = +0.54\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}} = +0.34\,\mathrm{V}\), and \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{Ag^+/Ag}} = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\), which of the following reactions is not feasible under standard conditions?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{2Fe^{3+}(aq) + 2I^-(aq) \rightarrow 2Fe^{2+}(aq) + I_2(s)}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Cu(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) \rightarrow Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2Ag(s)}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{2Fe^{3+}(aq) + Cu(s) \rightarrow 2Fe^{2+}(aq) + Cu^{2+}(aq)}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Ag(s) + Fe^{3+}(aq) \rightarrow Ag^+(aq) + Fe^{2+}(aq)}\)
Answer: (D) Worked Solution: For (D), \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) is reduced (\(E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} = +0.77\,\mathrm{V}\)) and \(\mathrm{Ag}\) is oxidized (\(E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\)). Thus \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.77 - 0.80 = -0.03\,\mathrm{V} < 0\), making it non-spontaneous. All other reactions have \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0\).
Practice Question 9 (Numerical MCQ — Hypothetical Sample)

(Assumed laboratory data) How many moles of \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) are required to completely oxidize \(0.60\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{FeC_2O_4}\) (ferrous oxalate) in acidic medium to \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)?

  • (A) \(0.10\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (B) \(0.20\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (C) \(0.30\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (D) \(0.60\,\mathrm{mol}\)
Answer: (C) Worked Solution: In \(\mathrm{FeC_2O_4}\), both \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) (\(\rightarrow \mathrm{Fe^{3+}} + 1\mathrm{e^-}\)) and \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\) (\(\rightarrow 2\mathrm{CO_2} + 2\mathrm{e^-}\)) are oxidized, so \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{FeC_2O_4}\) releases \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{e^-}\) (\(n\)-factor \(= 3\)). Total electrons released by \(0.60\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(= 0.60 \times 3 = 1.80\,\mathrm{mol}\). Since \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) accepts \(6\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{e^-}\) (\(\mathrm{Cr}: +6 \rightarrow +3\)), moles of \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) needed \(= 1.80 / 6 = 0.30\,\mathrm{mol}\).

Match each species in Column I with the oxidation number of the highlighted central atom in Column II:

(p) \(\mathrm{K_2MnO_4}\) (Mn) (1) \(+3\)
(q) \(\mathrm{H_4P_2O_7}\) (P) (2) \(+6\)

  • (C) p–2, q–3, r–4, s–1
  • (D) p–2, q–1, r–3, s–4

10. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid

  • Forgetting Peroxide/Hydride Exceptions: Always check if oxygen is in a peroxide (\(-1\), e.g., \(\mathrm{BaO_2, CrO_5, H_2SO_5}\)) or bonded to fluorine (\(+1/+2\)), and whether hydrogen is bonded to a metal (\(-1\) in \(\mathrm{NaBH_4, LiAlH_4, CaH_2}\)).
  • Confusing Average vs. Individual Oxidation States: Fractional values (\(\mathrm{S_4O_6^{2-}}, \mathrm{Fe_3O_4}, \mathrm{C_3O_2}\)) are averages; exam questions often ask for the individual states of terminal vs. central atoms.
  • Misreading \(E^\circ\) Signs: A more negative \(E^\circ\) means a stronger reducing agent (metal), whereas a more positive \(E^\circ\) means a stronger oxidizing agent (oxidized ion/molecule).
  • Overlooking Dual-Oxidation Reductants: In \(\mathrm{FeC_2O_4}\) or \(\mathrm{Cu_2S}\), both parts of the formula may get oxidized (e.g., \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+} \rightarrow Fe^{3+}}\) and \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-} \rightarrow 2CO_2}\)), increasing the total \(n\)-factor.

11. Concise Final Recap

  • Oxidation and reduction always occur together: oxidation increases oxidation number (loss of \(\mathrm{e^-}\)) and reduction decreases it (gain of \(\mathrm{e^-}\)).
  • Disproportionation occurs only when an element sits in an intermediate oxidation state; \(\mathrm{F_2}\) and species at maximum/minimum oxidation states cannot disproportionate.
  • Both the oxidation number method and half-reaction method conserve mass (atoms) and net charge; in basic medium, use \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\).
  • In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the negative anode, reduction at the positive cathode, and a reaction is standard-spontaneous when \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} > 0\).
