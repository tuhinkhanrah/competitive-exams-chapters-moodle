Structure of Atom

A Complete Conceptual, Mathematical, and Exam-Oriented Tutorial on Atomic Models, Quantum Mechanics, and Electronic Configurations

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Overview

Understanding how sub-atomic particles are arranged inside an atom is the foundation of chemical periodicity, bonding, and spectroscopy. For NEET UG, this unit consistently tests quantitative fluency with Bohr's model, photoelectric calculations, de Broglie wavelengths, quantum number rules, node counting, and electronic configuration exceptions.

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Compare the properties of electrons, protons, and neutrons, and determine nuclear composition for neutral atoms and ions.
  • Contrast Thomson's and Rutherford's models and explain why classical electrodynamics failed to account for atomic stability.
  • Apply Planck's quantum hypothesis and Einstein's photoelectric equation to radiation–matter interactions.
  • Calculate orbital radii, energies, velocities, and spectral wavelengths for hydrogen and hydrogen-like one-electron ions using Bohr's model.
  • Interpret wave–particle duality (de Broglie relation) and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle as the bridge to quantum mechanics.
  • Use the four quantum numbers \((n, l, m_l, m_s)\), radial/angular node formulas, and filling rules (Aufbau, Pauli, Hund) to write ground-state electronic configurations, including half-filled and fully filled exceptions.

2. Sub-Atomic Architecture & Classical Atomic Models

2.1 Fundamental Sub-Atomic Particles

Gas-discharge tube experiments at low pressure and high voltage revealed that atoms are divisible. Cathode rays—streams of negatively charged particles moving from cathode to anode—showed identical charge-to-mass ratios regardless of the electrode material or gas, establishing the electron as a universal constituent of matter. J.J. Thomson measured the electron's charge-to-mass ratio using perpendicular electric and magnetic fields, while R.A. Millikan's oil-drop experiment demonstrated that electric charge is quantized in integral multiples of the elementary charge \(e\) (\(q = ne\)).

Modified discharge tubes produced positively charged canal rays whose mass depended on the gas used; the lightest positive ion from hydrogen was identified as the proton. Later, bombarding thin beryllium sheets with \(\alpha\)-particles (helium nuclei, \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}^{2+}\)) led James Chadwick to discover electrically neutral neutrons.

2.2 Atomic Number, Mass Number, Isotopes, and Isobars

  • Atomic Number (\(Z\)): Equal to the number of protons in the nucleus. In a neutral atom, \(Z\) also equals the number of electrons.
  • Mass Number (\(A\)): Total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons): \(A = Z + N\), where \(N\) is the neutron count. An atom is written as \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\).
  • Isotopes: Atoms of the same element (identical \(Z\), different \(A\) and \(N\)), such as protium (\(^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}\)), deuterium (\(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{D}\)), and tritium (\(^{3}_{1}\mathrm{T}\)), or \(^{35}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\) and \(^{37}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\). Because chemical reactivity depends on electrons, isotopes of an element exhibit nearly identical chemical behaviour.
  • Isobars: Atoms of different elements with the same mass number \(A\) but different atomic numbers \(Z\), such as \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N}\).

2.3 Evolution from Thomson to Rutherford

J.J. Thomson envisioned the atom as a uniform sphere of positive charge (radius \(\approx 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)) with electrons embedded inside like seeds in a watermelon. While it explained electrical neutrality, it failed when Ernest Rutherford, Hans Geiger, and Ernest Marsden directed high-energy \(\alpha\)-particles at an ultra-thin gold foil (\(\sim 100\,\mathrm{nm}\) thick).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Schematic of Rutherford's \(\alpha\)-particle scattering experiment demonstrating a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.

From these observations, Rutherford concluded that most of the atom is empty space, while its positive charge and nearly all its mass reside in a minuscule central nucleus (radius \(\approx 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\), compared to the atomic radius \(\approx 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)).

Why Rutherford's Planetary Model Failed

According to Maxwell's classical electromagnetic theory, any charged particle undergoing acceleration radiates energy. An electron moving in a circular orbit constantly changes direction and therefore accelerates. Consequently, it should continuously emit radiation, lose energy, and spiral into the nucleus within \(\sim 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}\) (or \(10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}\) in order-of-magnitude estimates). Real atoms are stable, and their spectra consist of sharp lines rather than a continuous smear—two facts classical physics could not explain.

3. Electromagnetic Radiation, Quantization & Photoelectric Effect

3.1 Wave Nature of Electromagnetic Radiation

Electromagnetic (EM) waves consist of mutually perpendicular oscillating electric and magnetic fields that propagate through vacuum at a constant speed \(c \approx 3.00 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). They are characterized by:

  • Wavelength (\(\lambda\)): Distance between adjacent crests or troughs (SI unit: \(\mathrm{m}\); common sub-units: \(1\,\mathrm{nm} = 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}\), \(1\,\mathrm{Å} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)).
  • Frequency (\(\nu\)): Number of wave cycles passing a point per second (SI unit: \(\mathrm{Hz}\) or \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)).
  • Wavenumber (\(\bar{\nu}\)): Number of wavelengths per unit length, \(\bar{\nu} = 1/\lambda\) (SI unit: \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\); commonly expressed in \(\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\)).
\[ c = \nu \lambda \qquad \text{and} \qquad \bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{\nu}{c} \]

Across the electromagnetic spectrum, frequency increases (and wavelength decreases) in the order: Radio waves < Microwaves < Infrared (IR) < Visible (\(400\text{–}750\,\mathrm{nm}\)) < Ultraviolet (UV) < X-rays < \(\gamma\)-rays.

3.2 Planck's Quantum Theory & Black-Body Radiation

Wave theory accounts for diffraction and interference, but it fails to explain black-body radiation (the temperature-dependent intensity vs. wavelength curve of an ideal absorber/emitter) and the photoelectric effect. In 1900, Max Planck proposed that energy is emitted or absorbed only in discrete packets called quanta (later termed photons for light). The energy of a single quantum is directly proportional to its frequency:

\[ E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \qquad \left(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\right) \]

3.3 Einstein's Explanation of the Photoelectric Effect

When light of frequency \(\nu\) above a metal-specific threshold frequency (\(\nu_0\)) strikes a clean metal surface in vacuum, electrons are ejected instantaneously with no time lag. Below \(\nu_0\), no electrons are emitted regardless of light intensity. Above \(\nu_0\), increasing intensity increases the number of photoelectrons (photocurrent), whereas increasing frequency increases their maximum kinetic energy.

Tutorial Diagram 2: Photoelectric effect setup and the linear variation of maximum photoelectron kinetic energy with incident frequency above \(\nu_0\).

Applying conservation of energy, Einstein showed that one photon transfers its energy \(h\nu\) to one electron. Part of this energy overcomes the metal's work function \(W_0 = h\nu_0\), and the remainder becomes the photoelectron's kinetic energy:

\[ h\nu = h\nu_0 + \frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\max}^2 \implies K_{\max} = h(\nu - \nu_0) = eV_s \]

Here \(V_s\) is the stopping potential required to halt the fastest photoelectrons. Light therefore exhibits dual behaviour: wave-like during propagation (interference, diffraction) and particle-like during energy exchange with matter (black-body radiation, photoelectric effect).

4. Atomic Spectra & Bohr's Model of Hydrogen-Like Species

4.1 Line Spectra and the Rydberg Equation

Unlike white light, which disperses into a continuous rainbow, excited gaseous atoms emit radiation only at discrete wavelengths, producing a sharp line emission spectrum unique to each element (an atomic fingerprint). Conversely, passing continuous light through cool gas yields a line absorption spectrum with dark lines at the exact same wavelengths. For atomic hydrogen, Johannes Rydberg generalized all spectral series into:

\[ \bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_{\mathrm{H}} \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right) \qquad \left(R_{\mathrm{H}} = 109,677\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}} = 1.09677 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\right) \]

where \(n_1 = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) and \(n_2 = n_1 + 1, n_1 + 2, \dots\)

4.2 Bohr's Postulates & Quantitative Relations

In 1913, Niels Bohr explained hydrogen's stability and line spectrum using four key postulates:

  1. Stationary States: An electron revolves around the nucleus only in certain fixed circular orbits of definite radius and energy without radiating energy.
  2. Quantization of Angular Momentum: Only orbits whose angular momentum is an integral multiple of \(h/(2\pi)\) are permitted: \[ m_e v r = n \frac{h}{2\pi} \qquad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots) \]
  3. Quantum Jumps: Energy is absorbed when an electron moves from a lower orbit to a higher orbit, and emitted when it drops from a higher orbit to a lower orbit.
  4. Bohr's Frequency Rule: The photon frequency equals \(\nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{E_{\text{high}} - E_{\text{low}}}{h}\).

For hydrogen (\(Z = 1\)) and one-electron hydrogen-like ions (\(\mathrm{He}^+\) with \(Z=2\), \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\) with \(Z=3\), \(\mathrm{Be}^{3+}\) with \(Z=4\)), the radius and energy of the \(n\)-th stationary state are:

\[ r_n = \frac{52.9\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{pm} = \frac{0.529\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{Å} \] \[ E_n = -2.18 \times 10^{-18} \left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right)\,\mathrm{J\,atom^{-1}} = -13.6 \left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right)\,\mathrm{eV\,atom^{-1}} \]

Meaning of the Negative Energy Sign: A free electron at rest infinitely far from the nucleus (\(n = \infty\)) is assigned zero energy (\(E_\infty = 0\)). When bound to the nucleus, energy is released, making \(E_n\) negative; the ground state (\(n = 1\)) has the most negative energy and highest stability. As \(Z\) increases, \(r_n\) shrinks and \(E_n\) becomes more negative (stronger nuclear binding), while electron velocity increases with \(Z\) and decreases with \(n\) (\(v_n \propto Z/n\)).

Tutorial Diagram 3: Energy-level transitions in atomic hydrogen showing the Lyman, Balmer, and Paschen emission series.

For a transition between \(n_i\) and \(n_f\) in a one-electron species of atomic number \(Z\):

\[ \Delta E = 2.18 \times 10^{-18} Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_i^2} - \frac{1}{n_f^2}\right)\,\mathrm{J} \qquad \bar{\nu} = 1.09677 \times 10^7 Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_i^2} - \frac{1}{n_f^2}\right)\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \]

Limitations of Bohr's Model: It cannot explain (i) multi-electron spectra (even \(\mathrm{He}\)), (ii) closely spaced doublet/fine-structure lines, (iii) splitting of spectral lines in magnetic fields (Zeeman effect) or electric fields (Stark effect), and (iv) the formation of chemical bonds.

5. Dual Nature of Matter & Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

5.1 de Broglie's Matter Waves

In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed that every moving particle has an associated wavelength inversely proportional to its momentum \(p = mv\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}} \]

For macroscopic objects (e.g., a \(0.1\,\mathrm{kg}\) ball), \(\lambda\) is unimaginably small (\(\sim 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{m}\)) and undetectable. For electrons, \(\lambda\) is comparable to atomic dimensions and is confirmed experimentally by electron diffraction (the operating principle of electron microscopes). Moreover, Bohr's quantization condition \(m_e v r = nh/(2\pi)\) is equivalent to \(2\pi r = n\lambda\), meaning an allowed orbit's circumference must fit an integral number of electron wavelengths.

5.2 Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

Werner Heisenberg (1927) proved that it is fundamentally impossible to simultaneously measure both the exact position and the exact momentum (or velocity) of a microscopic particle:

\[ \Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \Delta x \cdot \Delta v_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi m} \]

Because defining a fixed circular trajectory requires knowing both position and velocity simultaneously at every instant, Heisenberg's principle—together with wave–particle duality—renders Bohr's fixed-orbit picture physically invalid for electrons and demands a probabilistic quantum mechanical model.

6. Quantum Mechanical Model, Orbitals & Quantum Numbers

6.1 Schrödinger Wave Equation & Probability Density

Erwin Schrödinger (1926) formulated the time-independent wave equation \(\hat{H}\psi = E\psi\), where \(\hat{H}\) is the Hamiltonian operator, \(E\) is the total energy, and \(\psi\) is the wave function. For an electron in an atom, \(\psi\) is called an atomic orbital. While \(\psi\) itself has no direct physical meaning, Max Born showed that \(|\psi|^2\) at any point represents the probability density (probability per unit volume) of finding the electron there.

6.2 The Four Quantum Numbers

Quantum Number Symbol Permitted Values Physical Significance
Principal \(n\) \(1, 2, 3, 4, \dots\) (K, L, M, N) Determines shell, orbital size, and energy (solely determines energy in H-atom). Total orbitals in shell \(= n^2\); max electrons \(= 2n^2\).
Azimuthal (Orbital Angular Momentum) \(l\) \(0, 1, 2, \dots, (n-1)\) Identifies subshell (\(0=s, 1=p, 2=d, 3=f\)) and 3D shape. Number of subshells in shell \(n\) equals \(n\).
Magnetic Orbital \(m_l\) \(-l, \dots, 0, \dots, +l\) Specifies spatial orientation of the orbital. Total orbitals in a subshell \(= 2l + 1\) (\(s: 1, p: 3, d: 5, f: 7\)).
Electron Spin \(m_s\) \(+\frac{1}{2}\) (\(\uparrow\)) or \(-\frac{1}{2}\) (\(\downarrow\)) Specifies the two intrinsic spin orientations of an electron; explains spectral doublets/triplets.

6.3 Orbital Shapes & Node Counting

A boundary surface diagram encloses the region where the probability of finding the electron is high (typically 90%; a 100% boundary is impossible because \(|\psi|^2\) never drops strictly to zero at finite distance). Regions where \(\psi\) and \(|\psi|^2\) fall to zero are called nodes:

  • Radial (Spherical) Nodes: Number of spherical shells where probability density is zero \(= n - l - 1\).
  • Angular (Planar/Conical) Nodes: Number of nodal planes/surfaces passing through the nucleus \(= l\).
  • Total Nodes: \((n - l - 1) + l = n - 1\).

6.4 Orbital Energies: Hydrogen vs. Multi-Electron Atoms

In hydrogen and one-electron ions, orbital energy depends only on \(n\), so \(2s = 2p\), \(3s = 3p = 3d\) (degenerate orbitals). In multi-electron atoms, inner electrons partially screen the nuclear charge, creating an effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\text{eff}}\)). Within a shell, spherical \(s\)-electrons penetrate closest to the nucleus and shield better than \(p\), which shields better than \(d\). Thus, orbital energy depends on both \(n\) and \(l\), following the \((n+l)\) rule:

  • The orbital with the lower \((n+l)\) value has lower energy.
  • If two orbitals have the same \((n+l)\) value, the one with the lower \(n\) has lower energy (e.g., \(4s\) has \(n+l = 4+0 = 4\), which is lower than \(3d\) with \(3+2 = 5\); and \(3p\) with \(3+1=4\) is lower than \(4s\)).

7. Electronic Configuration Rules & Stability Exceptions

The ground-state distribution of electrons across orbitals is governed by three foundational principles:

  1. Aufbau Principle: Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy: \[ 1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p < 7s \dots \]
  2. Pauli Exclusion Principle: No two electrons in the same atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers \((n, l, m_l, m_s)\). Consequently, a single orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins (\(\uparrow\downarrow\)).
  3. Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity: Within degenerate orbitals of the same subshell (\(p, d, f\)), electron pairing does not occur until each orbital is singly occupied with parallel spins.
Why Half-Filled (\(d^5, f^7\)) and Fully Filled (\(d^{10}, f^{14}\)) Subshells Are Extra Stable

In elements like Chromium (\(Z = 24\)) and Copper (\(Z = 29\)), the \(4s\) and \(3d\) subshells are very close in energy. Shifting one \(4s\) electron into \(3d\) yields \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1\) for \(\mathrm{Cr}\) and \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1\) for \(\mathrm{Cu}\) instead of \(3d^4 4s^2\) and \(3d^9 4s^2\). This extra stability arises from:

  • Symmetrical Distribution: Uniform spatial occupancy reduces mutual shielding and increases nuclear attraction.
  • Maximum Exchange Energy: Parallel-spin electrons in degenerate orbitals can exchange positions, releasing stabilizing exchange energy. The number of pairwise exchanges is maximized when a subshell is half-filled (10 exchanges in \(d^5\)) or completely filled.

8. Worked Examples (Original Scenarios)

Worked Example 1: Sub-Atomic Composition of an Anion

Problem: A hypothetical anion \(Y^{2-}\) has a mass number of \(34\) and contains \(18\) electrons. Determine the number of protons and neutrons in \(Y^{2-}\), and write its complete nuclear symbol.

Solution: Since \(Y^{2-}\) carries a \(-2\) charge, it has \(2\) excess electrons compared to its neutral atom. Therefore, the number of protons is: \[ Z = 18 - 2 = 16 \] Atomic number \(16\) corresponds to sulfur (\(\mathrm{S}\)). The number of neutrons is: \[ N = A - Z = 34 - 16 = 18 \] Hence, the species is \(^{34}_{16}\mathrm{S}^{2-}\).

Worked Example 2: Photoelectric Kinetic Energy Calculation

Problem: Suppose a clean metal surface has an assumed work function \(W_0 = 2.00\,\mathrm{eV}\). When monochromatic light of wavelength \(400\,\mathrm{nm}\) strikes this surface, calculate the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected photoelectrons in electron-volts (\(\mathrm{eV}\)) and joules (\(\mathrm{J}\)).

Solution: First, compute the energy of one incident photon (\(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\), \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)): \[ E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s} \times 3.00 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{400 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}} = 4.970 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \] Converting to \(\mathrm{eV}\) (using \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\)): \[ E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{4.970 \times 10^{-19}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 3.10\,\mathrm{eV} \] By Einstein's photoelectric equation: \[ K_{\max} = E_{\text{photon}} - W_0 = 3.10\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.00\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.10\,\mathrm{eV} \] In joules: \(K_{\max} = 1.10 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.76 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\).

Worked Example 3: Bohr Radii and Energies in Hydrogen-Like Ions

Problem: Calculate the ratio of the radius of the \(3\text{rd}\) Bohr orbit of \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\) to the radius of the \(2\text{nd}\) Bohr orbit of \(\mathrm{He}^+\). Also find the energy of the electron in the \(3\text{rd}\) orbit of \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\).

Solution: Using \(r_n = \frac{52.9\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{pm}\): \[ r_3(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}) = 52.9 \times \frac{3^2}{3} = 3 \times 52.9\,\mathrm{pm} = 158.7\,\mathrm{pm} \] \[ r_2(\mathrm{He}^+) = 52.9 \times \frac{2^2}{2} = 2 \times 52.9\,\mathrm{pm} = 105.8\,\mathrm{pm} \] \[ \frac{r_3(\mathrm{Li}^{2+})}{r_2(\mathrm{He}^+)} = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5 \] For the energy of the \(3\text{rd}\) orbit of \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\) (\(Z = 3, n = 3\)): \[ E_3 = -2.18 \times 10^{-18} \left(\frac{3^2}{3^2}\right)\,\mathrm{J} = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J} \]

Worked Example 4: de Broglie Wavelength from Kinetic Energy

Problem: A hypothetical sub-atomic particle of mass \(2.0 \times 10^{-28}\,\mathrm{kg}\) moves with a kinetic energy of \(4.0 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{J}\). Calculate its de Broglie wavelength.

Solution: Momentum is related to kinetic energy by \(p = \sqrt{2mK}\): \[ p = \sqrt{2 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-28}\,\mathrm{kg}) \times (4.0 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{J})} = \sqrt{16 \times 10^{-52}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}} \] Using de Broglie's equation: \[ \lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}}{4.0 \times 10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}} = 1.66 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m} = 16.6\,\mathrm{nm} \]

Practice Question 1: A dipositive ion \(M^{2+}\) has a mass number of \(56\) and contains \(24\) electrons. What is the number of neutrons in the nucleus of \(M\)?
  • (A) \(24\)
  • (B) \(26\)
  • (C) \(30\)
  • (D) \(32\)
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: A dipositive ion \(M^{2+}\) has \(2\) fewer electrons than the neutral atom \(M\). Thus, atomic number \(Z = 24 + 2 = 26\). Number of neutrons \(N = A - Z = 56 - 26 = 30\).

Practice Question 2: A monochromatic light source of wavelength \(600\,\mathrm{nm}\) emits \(1.0 \times 10^{16}\) photons per second. What is the power of the source? (Given \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\), \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\))
  • (A) \(3.315\,\mathrm{mW}\)
  • (B) \(6.630\,\mathrm{mW}\)
  • (C) \(1.658\,\mathrm{mW}\)
  • (D) \(33.15\,\mathrm{mW}\)
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Energy of one photon: \[ E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{600 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.315 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \] Power = Energy emitted per second \(= (1.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}) \times (3.315 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}) = 3.315 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{W} = 3.315\,\mathrm{mW}\).

Practice Question 3: The threshold frequency \(\nu_0\) for a metal is \(6.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). When radiation of frequency \(\nu = 9.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) hits the metal, the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electrons is:
  • (A) \(1.99 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(3.98 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(5.96 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(9.94 \times 10^{-20}\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Using \(K_{\max} = h(\nu - \nu_0)\): \[ K_{\max} = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}) \times (9.0 - 6.0) \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = 1.988 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.99 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \]

Practice Question 4: Which transition in the \(\mathrm{He}^+\) spectrum produces a spectral line of the exact same wavelength as the \(n = 3 \to n = 2\) transition in the hydrogen atom?
  • (A) \(n = 4 \to n = 2\)
  • (B) \(n = 6 \to n = 4\)
  • (C) \(n = 3 \to n = 1\)
  • (D) \(n = 6 \to n = 2\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: For equal wavelengths, wavenumbers must be equal: \[ R_{\mathrm{H}}(1)^2\left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2}\right) = R_{\mathrm{H}}(2)^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right) \] Rewriting the right side: \(\frac{4}{n_1^2} - \frac{4}{n_2^2} = \frac{1}{(n_1/2)^2} - \frac{1}{(n_2/2)^2}\). Matching terms gives \(n_1/2 = 2 \Rightarrow n_1 = 4\) and \(n_2/2 = 3 \Rightarrow n_2 = 6\).

Practice Question 5: When an excited electron in a large sample of hydrogen atoms drops from the \(n = 5\) state to the ground state (\(n = 1\)), what is the maximum number of distinct emission lines observed?
  • (A) \(6\)
  • (B) \(10\)
  • (C) \(12\)
  • (D) \(15\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: The maximum number of emission lines from level \(n\) to \(n = 1\) is given by \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 10\).

Practice Question 6: If the kinetic energy of a moving electron is increased by a factor of \(9\), its de Broglie wavelength will:
  • (A) Increase by \(3\) times
  • (B) Decrease to one-third
  • (C) Increase by \(9\) times
  • (D) Decrease to one-ninth
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Since \(\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}}\), \(\lambda \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\). When \(K\) increases \(9\)-fold, \(\lambda' = \frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{9}} = \frac{\lambda}{3}\).

Practice Question 7: If the uncertainty in position of a particle of mass \(m\) equals the uncertainty in its momentum (\(\Delta x = \Delta p_x\)), the minimum uncertainty in its velocity (\(\Delta v_x\)) is:
  • (A) \(\frac{1}{2m}\sqrt{\frac{h}{\pi}}\)
  • (B) \(\frac{1}{m}\sqrt{\frac{h}{2\pi}}\)
  • (C) \(\sqrt{\frac{h}{4\pi m}}\)
  • (D) \(\frac{1}{2m}\sqrt{\frac{h}{2\pi}}\)
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: From Heisenberg's relation \(\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}\), setting \(\Delta x = \Delta p_x\) gives \((\Delta p_x)^2 \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}\), so \(\Delta p_x = m \Delta v_x \ge \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{h}{\pi}}\). Dividing by \(m\) yields \(\Delta v_x \ge \frac{1}{2m}\sqrt{\frac{h}{\pi}}\).

Practice Question 8: Given below are two statements:
Statement I: The set of quantum numbers \(n = 3, l = 3, m_l = -1, m_s = +\frac{1}{2}\) is not permissible for an electron in an atom.
Statement II: For a given principal quantum number \(n\), the azimuthal quantum number \(l\) can take values from \(0\) to \(n - 1\).
Choose the correct option:
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: For \(n = 3\), permitted \(l\) values are \(0, 1, 2\). Thus \(l = 3\) is impossible, making Statement I correct. Statement II states the exact rule for \(l\), so both statements are correct.

Practice Question 9: The number of radial nodes and angular nodes present in a \(4p\) orbital are, respectively:
  • (A) \(2\) and \(1\)
  • (B) \(1\) and \(2\)
  • (C) \(3\) and \(1\)
  • (D) \(2\) and \(2\)
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: For a \(4p\) orbital, \(n = 4\) and \(l = 1\). Radial nodes \(= n - l - 1 = 4 - 1 - 1 = 2\). Angular nodes \(= l = 1\).

Practice Question 10: Which of the following sequences correctly represents the increasing order of orbital energy in a multi-electron atom?
  • (A) \(3p < 3d < 4s < 4p\)
  • (B) \(3p < 4s < 3d < 4p\)
  • (C) \(4s < 3p < 3d < 4p\)
  • (D) \(3p < 4s < 4p < 3d\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Applying the \((n + l)\) rule: \(3p\,(3+1=4)\), \(4s\,(4+0=4)\), \(3d\,(3+2=5)\), \(4p\,(4+1=5)\). When \((n+l)\) is equal, lower \(n\) has lower energy: \(3p < 4s\) and \(3d < 4p\). Thus \(3p < 4s < 3d < 4p\).

10. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Mistakes to Avoid
  • Forgetting \(Z^2\) or \(Z\) in Hydrogen-Like Ions: Always include \(Z^2\) in \(E_n\) and \(\bar{\nu}\), and \(Z\) in the denominator of \(r_n\) for \(\mathrm{He}^+\), \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\), or \(\mathrm{Be}^{3+}\).
  • Confusing Radial Nodes with Total Nodes: Radial nodes \(= n - l - 1\); angular nodes \(= l\); total nodes \(= n - 1\).
  • Misapplying \((n+l)\) to Hydrogen: In one-electron species, \(2s = 2p\) and \(3s = 3p = 3d\). The \((n+l)\) splitting applies only to multi-electron atoms.
  • Sign Errors in \(\Delta E\) and \(K_{\max}\): In emission, \(\Delta E\) is negative; use \(|\Delta E|\) to compute photon frequency \(\nu\) and wavelength \(\lambda\). In photoelectric problems, verify \(\nu > \nu_0\) (or \(E_{\text{photon}} > W_0\)) before calculating \(K_{\max}\).
  • Unit Mismatches: Convert \(\mathrm{nm}\) (\(10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}\)) or \(\mathrm{Å}\) (\(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)) to metres when using \(h\) in \(\mathrm{J\,s}\) and \(c\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and convert \(\mathrm{eV}\) to \(\mathrm{J}\) via \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\).
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Charge quantization: \(q = ne\); \(e/m_e = 1.7588 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\).
  • Photon energy: \(E = h\nu = hc/\lambda\); Photoelectric: \(h\nu = h\nu_0 + K_{\max}\).
  • Bohr formulas: \(r_n = 0.529\,n^2/Z\,\mathrm{Å}\); \(E_n = -13.6\,Z^2/n^2\,\mathrm{eV}\); Max lines from \(n \to 1\) is \(n(n-1)/2\).
  • Matter waves & uncertainty: \(\lambda = h/p = h/\sqrt{2mK}\); \(\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge h/(4\pi)\).
  • Quantum numbers: \(l \in [0, n-1]\), \(m_l \in [-l, +l]\), \(m_s = \pm 1/2\); \(\mathrm{Cr}\) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1\), \(\mathrm{Cu}\) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1\).

11. Concise Final Recap

Modern atomic theory progressed from Dalton's indivisible sphere to Thomson's positive pudding, Rutherford's nuclear atom, and Bohr's quantized circular orbits—before culminating in the Schrödinger quantum mechanical model. While Bohr's equations accurately describe one-electron spectra, wave–particle duality and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle replace fixed trajectories with three-dimensional probability orbitals defined by \((n, l, m_l)\) and spin \(m_s\). Filling these orbitals via the \((n+l)\) rule, Pauli exclusion principle, and Hund's rule explains both the periodic table and the exceptional stability of half-filled and completely filled subshells.
