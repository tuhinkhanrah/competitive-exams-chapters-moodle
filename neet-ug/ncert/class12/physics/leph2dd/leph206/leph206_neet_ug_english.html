Semiconductor Electronics: Materials, Devices and Simple Circuits

Energy bands, intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors, p-n junction physics, diode characteristics, and rectification circuits

Learning Objectives & Scope

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish metals, semiconductors, and insulators using resistivity ranges and energy band diagrams.
  • Explain charge transport in intrinsic semiconductors through thermally generated electrons and holes.
  • Analyze n-type and p-type extrinsic semiconductors, dopant energy levels, electrical neutrality, and the equilibrium relation \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\).

1. From Vacuum Valves to Solid-State Semiconductors

Electronic circuits rely on devices that regulate the number and direction of moving charge carriers. Prior to the invention of the transistor in 1948, this control was achieved using vacuum tubes (valves) such as vacuum diodes, triodes, tetrodes, and pentodes. In those devices, a heated cathode emitted electrons that travelled through an evacuated space toward an anode—a design that required high operating voltages (~100 V), consumed large power, and resulted in bulky, short-lived hardware.

Modern solid-state semiconductor devices control charge carriers directly inside a crystalline solid without requiring external heating or a vacuum chamber. Consequently, semiconductor components are compact, operate at low voltages, consume minimal power, and offer high reliability.

Classification by Electrical Resistivity and Conductivity

Solids can be grouped broadly by their room-temperature resistivity (\(\rho\)) and electrical conductivity (\(\sigma = 1/\rho\)). Semiconductors occupy the intermediate regime between metals and insulators, and their conductivity can be tuned dramatically by heat, light, or trace impurities.

Metals (Conductors) \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^{-8}\) \(10^{2}\) to \(10^{8}\) Copper, Aluminium, Tin (Sn)
Semiconductors \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{6}\) \(10^{5}\) to \(10^{-6}\) Elemental: Si, Ge

Energy Band Theory of Solids

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy sharp, discrete energy levels. When \(N\) atoms pack closely into a crystal lattice, outer electron orbitals overlap and each electron experiences a slightly different local electric environment. The discrete atomic levels broaden into continuous bands of allowed energies:

  • Valence Band: The energy band occupied by valence electrons; its highest energy edge is denoted \(E_V\).
  • Conduction Band: The next higher allowed energy band where electrons are free to move through the crystal; its lowest energy edge is denoted \(E_C\).
  • Forbidden Energy Band Gap (\(E_g\)): The energy separation \(E_g = E_C - E_V\) containing no allowed electron states.

In a crystal of Silicon or Germanium containing \(N\) atoms, each atom contributes 4 valence electrons (\(4N\) total) out of 8 available outer states (\(2s + 6p\), giving \(8N\) states). At the equilibrium interatomic spacing of the crystal, these \(8N\) states split into a completely filled valence band of \(4N\) states and a completely empty conduction band of \(4N\) states at \(T = 0\,\mathrm{K}\).

Group-14 Trend in Band Gaps (High-Yield NEET Fact)

Carbon (diamond), Silicon, Germanium, and Tin all crystallize with four valence electrons per atom, yet their band gaps decrease down the group as the valence shell moves farther from the nucleus (\(n = 2, 3, 4\)):

Diamond (C): \(E_g = 5.4\,\mathrm{eV}\) (insulator) > Silicon (Si): \(E_g = 1.1\,\mathrm{eV}\) (semiconductor) > Germanium (Ge): \(E_g = 0.7\,\mathrm{eV}\) (semiconductor) > Tin (Sn): \(E_g \approx 0\,\mathrm{eV}\) (metal).

2. Intrinsic Semiconductors

A chemically pure semiconductor crystal free of impurities is called an intrinsic semiconductor. In Si or Ge, each atom forms four covalent bonds with its nearest neighbours in a tetrahedral diamond-like lattice (lattice spacing \(a = 5.43\,\mathrm{Å}\) for Si and \(5.66\,\mathrm{Å}\) for Ge, compared to \(3.56\,\mathrm{Å}\) for diamond).

At absolute zero (\(T = 0\,\mathrm{K}\)), every covalent bond is intact and the conduction band is completely empty, so an intrinsic semiconductor behaves as a perfect insulator. At \(T > 0\,\mathrm{K}\), thermal energy breaks a small fraction of covalent bonds, promoting electrons into the conduction band and leaving behind electron vacancies called holes in the valence band.

  • Carrier Equality: Every thermally broken bond produces one free electron and one hole, so the electron concentration \(n_e\) equals the hole concentration \(n_h\): \[n_e = n_h = n_i\] where \(n_i\) is the intrinsic carrier concentration.
  • Hole Conduction Mechanism: A hole acts as an apparent mobile particle with effective positive charge \(+q\). In reality, a bound valence electron from a neighbouring covalent bond hops into the vacancy, shifting the vacancy in the opposite direction.
  • Total Current: Under an applied electric field, free electrons drift opposite to the field and holes move along the field. Both contribute to conventional current in the same direction: \[I = I_e + I_h\]
  • Dynamic Equilibrium: Thermal generation of electron–hole pairs occurs simultaneously with recombination (an electron colliding with and filling a hole). In thermal equilibrium, the rate of generation equals the rate of recombination.

3. Extrinsic Semiconductors & Doping

Because intrinsic semiconductors have very low conductivity at room temperature, practical devices use extrinsic semiconductors formed by doping—deliberately adding controlled trace impurities (a few parts per million, ppm) whose atomic size closely matches that of the host Si or Ge atom so the crystal lattice is not distorted.

Dopant Type Pentavalent (Group 15): P, As, Sb (Donors) Trivalent (Group 13): B, Al, In (Acceptors)
Bonding Effect 4 electrons form covalent bonds; 5th electron is weakly bound and easily freed 3 electrons form bonds; 4th bond has a vacancy (hole) ready to accept an electron
Impurity Energy Level Donor level \(E_D\) lies just below \(E_C\) (\(\approx 0.01\,\mathrm{eV}\) in Ge, \(0.05\,\mathrm{eV}\) in Si) Acceptor level \(E_A\) lies just above \(E_V\) (\(\approx 0.01\text{–}0.05\,\mathrm{eV}\))

Mass-Action Law and Overall Charge Neutrality

Adding majority carriers increases the rate at which minority carriers meet and recombine with them, suppressing the minority carrier concentration below \(n_i\). In thermal equilibrium, both intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors obey the mass-action law:

\[n_e \, n_h = n_i^2\]

Crucially, an n-type or p-type crystal remains electrically neutral overall because every mobile carrier donated or accepted is balanced by an equal and opposite fixed ionized dopant core in the crystal lattice.

Worked Example 1: Carrier Concentrations in Doped Silicon (Original Numerical)

Problem: Suppose a pure silicon sample has an atomic density of \(5.0 \times 10^{28}\,\mathrm{atoms\cdot m^{-3}}\) and an intrinsic carrier concentration \(n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) at room temperature. If it is doped with indium (trivalent) at a hypothetical concentration of \(2\,\mathrm{ppm}\) (2 dopant atoms per \(10^6\) silicon atoms), find the approximate electron and hole concentrations.

Solution:

At \(2\,\mathrm{ppm}\), the acceptor concentration \(N_A\) is:

\[N_A = \frac{2}{10^6} \times 5.0 \times 10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} = 1.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\]

Since \(N_A \gg n_i\), almost all holes come from ionized acceptor atoms: \(n_h \approx N_A = 1.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\).

Using \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\):

\[n_e = \frac{n_i^2}{n_h} = \frac{(1.5 \times 10^{16})^2}{1.0 \times 10^{23}} = \frac{2.25 \times 10^{32}}{1.0 \times 10^{23}} = 2.25 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\]

Thus, \(n_h \approx 1.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) (majority) and \(n_e \approx 2.25 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) (minority).

4. The p-n Junction & Semiconductor Diode

A p-n junction cannot be formed by pressing two separate p-type and n-type slabs together, because surface roughness far exceeds the interatomic spacing (~2–3 Å) and breaks crystal continuity. Instead, one side of a single semiconductor wafer is doped to convert part of it from p-type to n-type (or vice versa), creating a continuous metallurgical junction.

Diffusion, Drift, Depletion Region, and Barrier Potential

  1. Diffusion: Due to the steep concentration gradient, holes diffuse from p to n and electrons diffuse from n to p, creating a diffusion current from p to n.
  2. Space-Charge (Depletion) Region: Diffusing electrons leave behind immobile positively charged donor ions on the n-side; diffusing holes leave behind immobile negatively charged acceptor ions on the p-side. This region (~0.1 µm wide) is depleted of mobile carriers and is called the depletion region.
  3. Drift & Equilibrium: The immobile ions set up an internal electric field \(\vec{E}\) pointing from the n-side to the p-side. This field sweeps minority carriers back across the junction (drift current, opposite to diffusion). At equilibrium, drift current equals diffusion current, giving zero net current and a built-in barrier potential \(V_0\) (with the n-side at higher potential than the p-side).

Forward Bias vs. Reverse Bias

Because the depletion region lacks mobile charge carriers, its resistance is much higher than that of the p and n bulk regions. Consequently, almost the entire external bias voltage \(V\) drops across the depletion region.

Feature Forward Bias (p to +, n to −) Reverse Bias (p to −, n to +)
Effective Barrier Height Decreases to \((V_0 - V)\) Increases to \((V_0 + V)\)
Depletion Layer Width Decreases (narrows) Increases (widens)
Dominant Mechanism Majority carrier diffusion & minority carrier injection across junction Drift of minority carriers swept by the junction field
Current Magnitude Large, in milliamperes (\(\mathrm{mA}\)); grows exponentially above cut-in voltage Very small, in microamperes (\(\mathrm{µA}\)); nearly voltage-independent up to \(V_{br}\)
Effective Resistance Low (typically tens of \(\mathrm{Ω}\)) Very high (typically \(\mathrm{MΩ}\))

V–I Characteristics, Threshold Voltage, and Dynamic Resistance

  • Threshold (Cut-in) Voltage: In forward bias, current remains negligible until the applied voltage exceeds a material-dependent threshold: approximately \(0.2\,\mathrm{V}\) for Germanium and \(0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) for Silicon.
  • Reverse Breakdown Voltage (\(V_{br}\)): In reverse bias, the small reverse saturation current is limited by minority carrier concentration, not bias voltage, until \(V = V_{br}\), where reverse current rises sharply. Exceeding the rated current in either bias destroys the junction by overheating.
  • Dynamic Resistance (\(r_d\)): For a non-linear diode curve, AC or dynamic resistance at an operating point is defined as: \[r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}\]
Worked Example 2: Computing Forward Dynamic Resistance (Original Numerical)

Problem: In a laboratory test on a silicon diode (hypothetical data), the forward current increases from \(12\,\mathrm{mA}\) at \(0.72\,\mathrm{V}\) to \(28\,\mathrm{mA}\) at \(0.80\,\mathrm{V}\). Calculate the dynamic resistance of the diode in this operating range.

Solution:

Change in voltage: \(\Delta V = 0.80\,\mathrm{V} - 0.72\,\mathrm{V} = 0.08\,\mathrm{V}\).

Change in current: \(\Delta I = 28\,\mathrm{mA} - 12\,\mathrm{mA} = 16\,\mathrm{mA} = 16 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A}\).

Using \(r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}\):

\[r_d = \frac{0.08\,\mathrm{V}}{16 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A}} = 5\,\mathrm{Ω}\]

5. Junction Diode as a Rectifier & Capacitor Filter

Because a p-n junction diode conducts easily in forward bias and blocks current in reverse bias, it converts bidirectional alternating current (AC) into unidirectional pulsating direct current (DC)—a process called rectification.

Half-Wave vs. Centre-Tap Full-Wave Rectifier

  • Half-Wave Rectifier: Uses a single diode in series with load resistor \(R_L\) across a transformer secondary. Only the positive half-cycles forward-bias the diode and reach \(R_L\); negative half-cycles are blocked. For an AC input of frequency \(f\), the output ripple frequency is \(f\).
  • Centre-Tap Full-Wave Rectifier: Uses two diodes (\(D_1\) and \(D_2\)) connected to the opposite ends \(A\) and \(B\) of a centre-tapped transformer secondary, with \(R_L\) connected between the common cathodes and the centre tap. Since voltages at \(A\) and \(B\) are \(180^\circ\) out of phase, \(D_1\) conducts during the positive half-cycle and \(D_2\) conducts during the negative half-cycle. Current flows through \(R_L\) in the same direction during both halves, doubling the output ripple frequency to \(2f\).
Tutorial Diagram 4: Centre-tap full-wave rectifier schematic and comparison of unfiltered vs. capacitor-filtered output waveforms.

Role of the Capacitor Input Filter

Pulsating DC from a rectifier still contains an AC ripple component. Connecting a large capacitor \(C\) in parallel across the load \(R_L\) smooths the output:

  • As the rectified voltage rises toward its peak, the capacitor charges rapidly to the peak voltage.
  • When the rectified pulse drops, the diode becomes reverse-biased and the capacitor discharges slowly through \(R_L\), keeping the output voltage close to the peak value until the next pulse recharges it.
  • The discharge rate varies inversely with the time constant \(\tau = C R_L\). Using a large capacitance \(C\) makes \(\tau\) large, minimizing ripple and yielding a nearly steady DC voltage.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Three Group-14 elements \(X, Y,\) and \(Z\) have four valence electrons each, with \(X\) being a diamond-lattice insulator, \(Y\) a commonly used semiconductor with \(E_g = 1.1\,\mathrm{eV}\), and \(Z\) a semiconductor with \(E_g = 0.7\,\mathrm{eV}\). Which ordering of their energy band gaps is correct?

  • (A) \(E_g(Z) < E_g(Y) < E_g(X)\)
  • (B) \(E_g(X) < E_g(Y) < E_g(Z)\)
  • (C) \(E_g(Y) < E_g(Z) < E_g(X)\)
  • (D) \(E_g(X) = E_g(Y) = E_g(Z)\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: Here \(X\) is Carbon (diamond, \(E_g = 5.4\,\mathrm{eV}\)), \(Y\) is Silicon (\(E_g = 1.1\,\mathrm{eV}\)), and \(Z\) is Germanium (\(E_g = 0.7\,\mathrm{eV}\)). As valence electrons occupy higher principal shells (\(n = 2 \to 3 \to 4\)), they are less tightly bound, so \(E_g(Z) < E_g(Y) < E_g(X)\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A pure germanium sample has an intrinsic carrier concentration \(n_i = 2.0 \times 10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). After doping with pentavalent antimony, the conduction electron concentration becomes \(4.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). What is the approximate hole concentration at the same temperature?

  • (A) \(1.0 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)
  • (B) \(2.0 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)
  • (C) \(4.0 \times 10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)
  • (D) \(8.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: Applying the mass-action law \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\): \[n_h = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^{19})^2}{4.0 \times 10^{23}} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{38}}{4.0 \times 10^{23}} = 1.0 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\]

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When a p-n junction diode is forward biased with an external voltage \(V\) less than the built-in barrier potential \(V_0\), what happens to the depletion region width \(W\) and effective barrier height?

  • (A) \(W\) increases and effective barrier height becomes \(V_0 + V\)
  • (B) \(W\) decreases and effective barrier height becomes \(V_0 - V\)
  • (C) \(W\) remains unchanged and effective barrier height becomes \(V_0 - V\)
  • (D) \(W\) decreases and effective barrier height becomes \(V_0 + V\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: In forward bias, the applied voltage opposes the built-in potential \(V_0\), reducing the effective barrier height to \(V_0 - V\) and narrowing the depletion width \(W\).

Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): An n-type silicon crystal carries a net negative electrostatic charge.
Reason (R): In an n-type semiconductor, the concentration of free electrons is much greater than the concentration of holes.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Correct Answer: (D)

Solution: Assertion (A) is false because an n-type crystal is electrically neutral overall; each donated conduction electron is balanced by an immobile positively charged donor ion core. Reason (R) is true because \(n_e \gg n_h\) in n-type material.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

An AC mains signal of frequency \(60\,\mathrm{Hz}\) is fed to (i) a half-wave rectifier and (ii) a centre-tap full-wave rectifier. What are the fundamental ripple frequencies of the output waveforms in cases (i) and (ii), respectively?

  • (A) \(60\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(60\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (B) \(30\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(60\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (C) \(60\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(120\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (D) \(120\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(60\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: A half-wave rectifier passes one pulse per input cycle, so \(f_{\text{out}} = f = 60\,\mathrm{Hz}\). A full-wave rectifier passes one pulse for every half-cycle, so \(f_{\text{out}} = 2f = 120\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Why is a large-value capacitor connected in parallel with the load resistor \(R_L\) at the output of a full-wave rectifier?

  • (A) To increase the AC ripple frequency
  • (B) To make the time constant \(C R_L\) large so the output voltage decays slowly between peaks
  • (C) To protect the diodes from forward-bias overheating
  • (D) To convert the centre-tap circuit into a half-wave rectifier
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: A large capacitance \(C\) gives a large discharge time constant \(\tau = C R_L\), so the capacitor discharges very little through \(R_L\) between consecutive peaks and maintains a nearly steady DC output close to the peak voltage.

7. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist
  • Neutrality Misconception: Never confuse majority carrier sign with net crystal charge. Both n-type and p-type crystals are electrically neutral.
  • Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Current: In intrinsic semiconductors, \(I = I_e + I_h\) with \(n_e = n_h\). In extrinsic semiconductors, \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\) still holds, and current is dominated by majority carriers.
  • Bias Polarity Check: Forward bias connects p to higher potential and n to lower potential (current in \(\mathrm{mA}\)); reverse bias reverses this (current in \(\mathrm{µA}\)).
  • Cut-In Values: Remember \(\approx 0.2\,\mathrm{V}\) for Ge and \(\approx 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) for Si.
  • Rectifier Frequency: Half-wave output frequency equals input frequency \(f\); full-wave output frequency equals \(2f\).
8. Concise Final Recap

Semiconductors possess a small forbidden gap (\(E_g < 3\,\mathrm{eV}\)) and conduct via both electrons and holes. Doping with pentavalent donors (n-type) or trivalent acceptors (p-type) tunes carrier density while obeying \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\). A p-n junction forms a depletion barrier that narrows under forward bias and widens under reverse bias, enabling unidirectional conduction used in half-wave, full-wave, and capacitor-filtered rectifiers.
