Atomic Nuclei, Binding Energy & Nuclear Reactions

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide covering nuclear structure, mass–energy equivalence, binding energy curves, the strong nuclear force, radioactivity, fission, and thermonuclear fusion.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Roadmap

In NEET UG Physics, questions from nuclear physics test both conceptual precision and rapid numerical estimation. Almost all calculations rely on conservation laws (charge, nucleon number, and mass–energy) and scaling relations such as the dependence of nuclear radius on mass number.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Define atomic mass unit (\(\mathrm{u}\)) and compute weighted average atomic masses from isotopic abundances.
  • Distinguish between isotopes, isobars, and isotones using proton number (\(Z\)), neutron number (\(N\)), and mass number (\(A\)).
  • Apply \(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\) to compare nuclear radii and explain why nuclear matter density is independent of \(A\).
  • Compute mass defect (\(\Delta M\)), nuclear binding energy (\(E_b\)), and binding energy per nucleon (\(E_{bn}\)) using \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\).
  • Interpret the binding energy per nucleon curve to explain nuclear stability, fission, and fusion.
  • Describe the properties of the strong nuclear force and interpret the nucleon–nucleon potential energy curve.
  • Calculate the \(Q\)-value of nuclear reactions and analyze energy generation in fission reactors and stellar fusion.

2. Nuclear Architecture, Mass Units & Nuclide Classification

2.1 Scale of the Nucleus

Scattering experiments using alpha particles demonstrated that nearly all of an atom's mass (more than 99.9%) and its entire positive charge are packed into a central core called the nucleus. While a typical atom has a radius on the order of \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\) (1 Å), the nuclear radius is smaller by a factor of roughly \(10^4\), around \(10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\). Consequently, the nuclear volume is only about \(10^{-12}\) times the atomic volume, leaving most of the atom as empty space.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Relative scale of the atom and its nucleus. Protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons) are densely packed in a tiny central volume.

2.2 Atomic Mass Unit (\(\mathrm{u}\))

Because the kilogram is inconveniently large for subatomic particles, atomic and nuclear masses are expressed in the atomic mass unit (\(\mathrm{u}\)), defined as exactly one-twelfth of the mass of a single unbound, neutral carbon-12 (\(^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}\)) atom:

\[ 1\,\mathrm{u} = \frac{\text{mass of one } {}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}\text{ atom}}{12} = 1.660539 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg} \]

2.3 Constituents of the Nucleus & Discovery of the Neutron

A nucleus consists of two types of particles, collectively termed nucleons:

  • Protons: Positively charged particles carrying charge \(+e\) (\(+1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)) and mass \(m_p = 1.00727\,\mathrm{u} = 1.67262 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\). An electrically neutral atom with \(Z\) electrons must have exactly \(Z\) protons in its nucleus.
  • Neutrons: Electrically neutral particles discovered by James Chadwick in 1932 when he bombarded beryllium nuclei with alpha particles and observed penetrating neutral radiation capable of knocking protons out of light targets (such as hydrogen-rich paraffin, helium, carbon, and nitrogen). By applying conservation of energy and momentum, Chadwick showed the neutral radiation could not be photons and consisted of neutral particles with mass nearly equal to that of a proton: \(m_n = 1.00866\,\mathrm{u} = 1.6749 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\).
Free Neutron vs. Bound Neutron

A free proton is stable, whereas a free neutron is unstable: it undergoes beta-minus decay into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino with a mean life of about \(1000\,\mathrm{s}\). Inside a stable nucleus, however, neutrons are stabilized by the nuclear binding environment.

2.4 Nuclide Notation & Classification

A specific nuclear species (nuclide) is denoted as \({}^{A}_{Z}X\), where \(X\) is the chemical symbol, \(Z\) is the atomic number (proton count), \(N\) is the neutron number, and \(A = Z + N\) is the mass number (total nucleon count).

Most naturally occurring elements exist as mixtures of isotopes. The observed atomic mass of an element is the weighted average of its isotopic masses according to their fractional natural abundances.

3. Nuclear Size & Constant Nuclear Matter Density

High-energy electron scattering experiments probe the charge distribution of nuclei and establish that a nucleus of mass number \(A\) behaves approximately as a sphere of radius \(R\):

\[ R = R_0 A^{1/3}, \quad \text{where } R_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.2\,\mathrm{fm} \]

Note that \(1\,\mathrm{fm}\) (femtometre) \(= 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\). Because the volume of a sphere is \(V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A\), the nuclear volume is directly proportional to the mass number \(A\).

Since the mass of a nucleus is approximately \(A \times m_{\text{nucleon}}\) (where \(m_{\text{nucleon}} \approx 1\,\mathrm{u} \approx 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\)), the nuclear mass density \(\rho\) is:

\[ \rho = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}} \approx \frac{A \, m_{\text{nucleon}}}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A} = \frac{3 \, m_{\text{nucleon}}}{4\pi R_0^3} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \]
High-Yield NEET Insight: Density Independence

The mass number \(A\) cancels out completely. Therefore, nuclear matter density is constant and independent of \(A\) for all nuclei—analogous to an incompressible liquid drop. This density (\(\sim 10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) is about \(10^{14}\) times greater than that of water (\(10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) and is comparable to the density of neutron stars.

4. Mass–Energy Equivalence, Mass Defect & Binding Energy

4.1 Einstein's Mass–Energy Relation

Special relativity unifies the conservation of mass and energy into a single law. A mass \(m\) possesses an equivalent energy \(E\):

\[ E = m c^2 \quad (c \approx 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}) \]

Converting \(1\,\mathrm{u}\) (\(1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) into energy using \(c = 2.9979 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) yields a fundamental conversion factor used throughout nuclear physics:

\[ 1\,\mathrm{u} \equiv 1.4924 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{J} = 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2 \]

4.2 Mass Defect (\(\Delta M\)) and Binding Energy (\(E_b\))

Experimentally, the actual mass \(M\) of a bound nucleus is always less than the sum of the masses of its \(Z\) free protons and \((A - Z)\) free neutrons. This missing mass is the mass defect:

\[ \Delta M = \left[ Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n \right] - M_{\text{nucleus}} \]

When tables provide atomic masses \(M_{\text{atom}}\) and the mass of a hydrogen atom \(m_H = m_p + m_e\), the \(Z\) electron masses cancel out automatically (neglecting tiny electronic binding energies):

\[ \Delta M = \left[ Z m_H + (A - Z) m_n \right] - M_{\text{atom}} \]

The energy equivalent of the mass defect is the nuclear binding energy \(E_b = \Delta M c^2\). It represents the energy released when free nucleons assemble into a nucleus, or equivalently, the minimum work required to completely separate the nucleus into its constituent free protons and neutrons.

4.3 Binding Energy per Nucleon (\(E_{bn}\)) & Stability Curve

A nucleus with a larger total \(E_b\) is not necessarily more stable than a lighter nucleus; stability is governed by the binding energy per nucleon:

\[ E_{bn} = \frac{E_b}{A} \]
Tutorial Diagram 2: Variation of binding energy per nucleon (\(E_{bn}\)) with mass number (\(A\)). Nuclei in the middle mass range are most tightly bound, with a peak at \({}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}\).

Four crucial conclusions emerge from the binding energy curve:

  1. Plateau Stability (\(30 < A < 170\)): \(E_{bn}\) is nearly constant around \(8.0\,\mathrm{MeV}\) to \(8.75\,\mathrm{MeV}\) (peaking at \(8.75\,\mathrm{MeV}\) for \(A = 56\)). This constancy proves that the nuclear force is short-ranged and saturates: a nucleon inside a large nucleus interacts only with its immediate neighbors, not with all \(A-1\) nucleons.
  2. Light Nuclei Peaks: Sharp local maxima appear at even–even light nuclei such as \({}^{4}\mathrm{He}\), \({}^{12}\mathrm{C}\), and \({}^{16}\mathrm{O}\), indicating extra stability (nuclear shell effects).
  3. Nuclear Fission (\(A > 170\)): Heavy nuclei like \({}^{238}\mathrm{U}\) (\(E_{bn} \approx 7.6\,\mathrm{MeV}\)) are less tightly bound than intermediate-mass fragments (\(A \approx 120\), \(E_{bn} \approx 8.5\,\mathrm{MeV}\)). Splitting a heavy nucleus into two medium nuclei increases total binding energy and releases energy.
  4. Nuclear Fusion (\(A < 30\)): Very light nuclei (such as \({}^{2}\mathrm{H}\)) have low \(E_{bn}\). Combining two light nuclei into a heavier nucleus (such as \({}^{4}\mathrm{He}\)) moves up the curve, increasing binding energy and releasing substantial energy.

5. The Strong Nuclear Force

Within the tiny nuclear volume (\(\sim 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\)), protons experience enormous electrostatic repulsion. To keep the nucleus bound, there must be an attractive strong nuclear force with the following experimental characteristics:

  • Strength: It is the strongest fundamental force in nature—much stronger than both electrostatic (Coulomb) repulsion and gravitational attraction.
  • Short Range & Saturation: It drops rapidly to zero beyond a separation of a few femtometres (\(\sim 2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{fm}\)). Because each nucleon interacts only with nearby neighbors, binding energy per nucleon saturates in medium and heavy nuclei.
  • Charge Independence: The nuclear force between neutron–neutron (\(n\text{–}n\)), proton–neutron (\(p\text{–}n\)), and proton–proton (\(p\text{–}p\)) pairs is approximately identical.
  • Distance Dependence (Attractive vs. Repulsive Core): As shown in the nucleon–nucleon potential energy plot below, the potential energy reaches a minimum at \(r_0 \approx 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\). For \(r > 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\), the force is attractive; for \(r < 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\), the potential rises sharply, meaning the force becomes strongly repulsive—preventing the nucleus from collapsing into a point.

6. Radioactivity & Nuclear Energy Reactions

6.1 Nuclear Instability & Radioactivity

Nuclear stability depends on the neutron-to-proton (\(N/Z\)) ratio. Light stable nuclei have \(N/Z \approx 1\), whereas heavy stable nuclei require more neutrons (\(N/Z \approx 1.5\)) to provide extra attractive nuclear binding without increasing electrostatic repulsion. Unstable nuclei undergo spontaneous radioactive decay in three principal modes:

  • \(\alpha\)-decay: Emission of a helium nucleus (\({}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\)), reducing \(A\) by 4 and \(Z\) by 2.
  • \(\beta\)-decay: Emission of an electron (\(e^{-}\)) or positron (\(e^{+}\), the antiparticle of the electron with identical mass and opposite charge).
  • \(\gamma\)-decay: Emission of high-energy electromagnetic photons (hundreds of \(\mathrm{keV}\) or higher) as an excited nucleus drops to a lower energy state.

6.2 \(Q\)-Value of a Nuclear Process

For any nuclear reaction or decay \(A + b \rightarrow C + d\), the \(Q\)-value measures the net kinetic energy released (or absorbed):

\[ Q = \text{KE}_{\text{final}} - \text{KE}_{\text{initial}} = \left( \sum m_{\text{initial}} - \sum m_{\text{final}} \right) c^2 \]

Equivalently, in terms of binding energies, \(Q = \sum E_{b,\text{final}} - \sum E_{b,\text{initial}}\). If \(Q > 0\), the process is exothermic (energy is released); if \(Q < 0\), it is endothermic (threshold energy must be supplied).

6.3 Nuclear Fission

When a heavy nucleus such as \({}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) absorbs a slow neutron, it forms an unstable compound nucleus \({}^{236}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) that splits into two intermediate-mass fragments plus 2 to 4 neutrons, for example:

\[ {}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{236}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{144}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{89}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \]

Each fission of \({}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) releases approximately \(200\,\mathrm{MeV}\) of energy—roughly a million times more energy per unit mass than chemical combustion (for instance, fission of \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) of uranium yields \(\sim 10^{14}\,\mathrm{J}\), compared to \(\sim 10^7\,\mathrm{J}\) from burning \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) of coal). Controlled fission powers nuclear reactors, while uncontrolled fission occurs in atomic weapons.

6.4 Nuclear Fusion & Stellar Energy

In nuclear fusion, two light nuclei combine to form a heavier, more tightly bound nucleus. However, both nuclei are positively charged and must overcome a Coulomb potential barrier (about \(400\,\mathrm{keV}\) for two protons) before the short-range nuclear force can bind them. Achieving this by heating the fuel to extreme temperatures (\(\sim 10^7\text{–}10^9\,\mathrm{K}\)) is called thermonuclear fusion.

In the core of the Sun (at \(T \approx 1.5 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{K}\)), fusion proceeds via the proton–proton (\(p\text{–}p\)) cycle, whose net effect is the conversion of four hydrogen nuclei into one helium-4 nucleus:

\[ 4\,{}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H} + 2\,e^{-} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + 2\,\nu + 6\,\gamma + 26.7\,\mathrm{MeV} \]

In controlled fusion reactors on Earth, fuel heated to \(\sim 10^8\,\mathrm{K}\) becomes a fully ionized plasma that must be confined without touching material walls (e.g., via magnetic confinement).

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Scaling of Nuclear Radius and Density

Problem: Consider two hypothetical nuclei with mass numbers \(A_1 = 27\) and \(A_2 = 125\). Determine the ratio of (a) their nuclear radii and (b) their nuclear matter densities.

Solution:

(a) Using \(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\):

\[ \frac{R_1}{R_2} = \left(\frac{A_1}{A_2}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{27}{125}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6 \]

(b) Nuclear matter density is independent of mass number \(A\) because both mass and volume scale linearly with \(A\). Therefore, \(\rho_1 : \rho_2 = 1 : 1\).

Worked Example 2: Average Atomic Mass from Isotopes

Problem: Suppose a naturally occurring element \(X\) (assumed values) consists of two stable isotopes: \({}^{63}X\) with atomic mass \(62.93\,\mathrm{u}\) (70% abundance) and \({}^{65}X\) with atomic mass \(64.93\,\mathrm{u}\) (30% abundance). Calculate the average atomic mass of \(X\).

Solution:

\[ M_{\text{avg}} = \frac{(70 \times 62.93) + (30 \times 64.93)}{100} = 44.051 + 19.479 = 63.53\,\mathrm{u} \]
Worked Example 3: Mass Defect and Binding Energy per Nucleon

Problem: Using the standard atomic masses \(m({}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}) = 1.007825\,\mathrm{u}\), \(m_n = 1.008665\,\mathrm{u}\), and \(m({}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}) = 12.000000\,\mathrm{u}\), find the binding energy per nucleon of \({}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) in \(\mathrm{MeV}\).

Solution: A \({}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) atom has \(Z = 6\) protons (paired with 6 electrons in atomic mass) and \(N = 12 - 6 = 6\) neutrons.

\[ \Delta M = [6\,m({}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}) + 6\,m_n] - m({}^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}) \] \[ \Delta M = [6(1.007825) + 6(1.008665)] - 12.000000 = 12.09894 - 12.00000 = 0.09894\,\mathrm{u} \] \[ E_b = 0.09894 \times 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 92.16\,\mathrm{MeV} \] \[ E_{bn} = \frac{E_b}{12} = \frac{92.16}{12} = 7.68\,\mathrm{MeV/nucleon} \]
Worked Example 4: Fission Fuel Consumption Rate in a Reactor

Problem: Assume a nuclear reactor operates at a steady thermal power output of \(320\,\mathrm{MW}\) using \({}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\), and each fission releases \(200\,\mathrm{MeV}\) of energy. Calculate the mass of \({}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) consumed per second. (Take \(N_A = 6.023 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\).)

Solution:

Energy released per fission in joules:

\[ E_1 = 200 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{eV} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J/eV} = 3.2 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J} \]

Number of fissions per second for \(P = 320\,\mathrm{MW} = 3.2 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{J/s}\):

\[ n = \frac{P}{E_1} = \frac{3.2 \times 10^8}{3.2 \times 10^{-11}} = 10^{19}\,\mathrm{fissions/s} \]

Mass of \(10^{19}\) atoms of \({}^{235}\mathrm{U}\):

\[ m = \frac{10^{19}}{6.023 \times 10^{23}} \times 235\,\mathrm{g} \approx 3.90 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{g} = 3.90\,\mathrm{mg/s} \]

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two stable nuclei have mass numbers in the ratio \(8 : 27\). What is the ratio of their nuclear matter densities and nuclear radii, respectively?

  • (A) \(8 : 27\) and \(2 : 3\)
  • (B) \(1 : 1\) and \(2 : 3\)
  • (C) \(1 : 1\) and \(4 : 9\)
  • (D) \(2 : 3\) and \(1 : 1\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Nuclear matter density is independent of mass number \(A\), so \(\rho_1 : \rho_2 = 1 : 1\). Since \(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\), the ratio of radii is \(R_1/R_2 = (8/27)^{1/3} = 2/3\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following pairs of nuclides represents a pair of isotones?

  • (A) \({}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) and \({}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \({}^{39}_{19}\mathrm{K}\) and \({}^{40}_{20}\mathrm{Ca}\)
  • (C) \({}^{35}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\) and \({}^{37}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\)
  • (D) \({}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) and \({}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Isotones have the same neutron number \(N = A - Z\). For \({}^{39}_{19}\mathrm{K}\), \(N = 39 - 19 = 20\). For \({}^{40}_{20}\mathrm{Ca}\), \(N = 40 - 20 = 20\). Option (A) shows isobars; options (C) and (D) show isotopes.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If the binding energy per nucleon of a \({}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\) nucleus is \(7.07\,\mathrm{MeV}\) and that of a deuteron (\({}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}\)) is \(1.11\,\mathrm{MeV}\), how much energy is released when two deuterons fuse to form one \({}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\) nucleus?

  • (A) \(5.96\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (B) \(11.92\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (C) \(23.84\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (D) \(28.28\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Total initial binding energy of two deuterons \(= 2 \times (2 \times 1.11) = 4.44\,\mathrm{MeV}\). Total final binding energy of \({}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\) \(= 4 \times 7.07 = 28.28\,\mathrm{MeV}\). Energy released \(Q = E_{b,\text{final}} - E_{b,\text{initial}} = 28.28 - 4.44 = 23.84\,\mathrm{MeV}\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which statement accurately describes the strong nuclear force between two nucleons separated by distance \(r\)?

  • (A) It remains purely attractive down to \(r = 0\).
  • (B) It acts more strongly between two protons than between two neutrons at the same distance.
  • (C) It is strongly repulsive for \(r < 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\) and attractive for \(r > 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\) up to a few femtometres.
  • (D) It obeys an inverse-square law identical in form to Coulomb's law.
Answer: (C)

Solution: The nucleon–nucleon potential energy has a minimum near \(r_0 \approx 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\). For \(r < 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\), the potential rises steeply (strong repulsion); for \(r > 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\), it is attractive and short-ranged, vanishing beyond a few femtometres. It is also charge-independent.

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): The binding energy per nucleon is nearly constant (around \(8.0\text{–}8.75\,\mathrm{MeV}\)) for nuclei with mass numbers in the range \(30 < A < 170\).
Reason (R): The strong nuclear force is short-ranged and exhibits saturation, so each nucleon interacts only with its immediate neighbors.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Because the nuclear force has a short range of a few femtometres, adding more nucleons to a medium-sized nucleus does not increase the number of neighbors interacting with an interior nucleon. This saturation directly causes \(E_{bn}\) to stay nearly constant in the \(30 < A < 170\) range.

Practice Question 6 (Two-Statement MCQ)

Statement I: A free neutron is unstable and decays into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino with a mean life of about \(1000\,\mathrm{s}\).
Statement II: Heavy stable nuclei contain more neutrons than protons (\(N > Z\)) to increase attractive nuclear binding without adding electrostatic repulsion.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Both statements are experimentally established facts. A free neutron undergoes beta decay with \(\tau \approx 1000\,\mathrm{s}\), and heavy nuclei require an \(N/Z\) ratio approaching \(3:2\) to counterbalance proton–proton Coulomb repulsion.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a hypothetical nuclear reaction \(A + B \rightarrow C + D\), the total rest mass of the reactants is \(20.015\,\mathrm{u}\) and that of the products is \(20.005\,\mathrm{u}\). Taking \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\), the reaction is:

  • (A) Exothermic, releasing \(9.315\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (B) Endothermic, absorbing \(9.315\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (C) Exothermic, releasing \(0.010\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (D) Endothermic, absorbing \(18.63\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: \(Q = (m_{\text{initial}} - m_{\text{final}})c^2 = (20.015 - 20.005)\,\mathrm{u} \times 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/\mathrm{u} = +0.010 \times 931.5 = +9.315\,\mathrm{MeV}\). Since \(Q > 0\), the reaction is exothermic.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two protons must overcome a Coulomb potential barrier of roughly \(400\,\mathrm{keV}\) to undergo fusion. Using \(\frac{3}{2} k_B T = K\) (with \(k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\)), the temperature \(T\) required for average protons to overcome this barrier is closest to:

  • (A) \(3 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{K}\)
  • (B) \(3 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{K}\)
  • (C) \(1.5 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{K}\)
  • (D) \(3 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{K}\)
Answer: (D)

Solution: \(K = 400 \times 10^3 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 6.4 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{J}\). Thus, \(T = \frac{2K}{3k_B} = \frac{2 \times 6.4 \times 10^{-14}}{3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23}} \approx 3.09 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{K}\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a fission reactor, each fission of a \({}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) nucleus releases \(200\,\mathrm{MeV}\) of energy. If \(3.0 \times 10^{16}\) fissions take place every second, what is the thermal power output of the reactor?

  • (A) \(320\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (B) \(640\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (C) \(960\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (D) \(1.92\,\mathrm{MW}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Energy per fission \(= 200 \times 10^6 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.2 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J}\). Power \(P = (3.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}) \times (3.2 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J}) = 9.6 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{W} = 960\,\mathrm{kW}\).

10. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent NEET Mistakes
  • Forgetting electron masses in \(\Delta M\): If a problem gives atomic masses, use \(m({}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H})\) (not bare proton mass \(m_p\)) so the \(Z\) electron masses cancel cleanly.
  • Confusing total \(E_b\) with \(E_{bn}\): Always check whether a question gives binding energy total or binding energy per nucleon. Multiply \(E_{bn}\) by \(A\) before computing \(Q = E_{b,\text{final}} - E_{b,\text{initial}}\).
  • Sign of \(Q\)-value in terms of binding energy: In terms of masses, \(Q = (m_i - m_f)c^2\); in terms of binding energies, \(Q = E_{b,f} - E_{b,i}\) (final minus initial, because tighter binding means lower mass).
  • Assuming nuclear density scales with \(A\): Nuclear density is constant for all nuclei (\(\rho_1 / \rho_2 = 1\)), whereas atomic density varies widely across elements.
Quick Revision Checklist
  • \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg} = 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\).
  • \(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\) with \(R_0 = 1.2\,\mathrm{fm}\); nuclear density \(\approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\).
  • Peak of \(E_{bn}\) curve is at \({}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}\) (\(\approx 8.75\,\mathrm{MeV}\)); plateau lies in \(30 < A < 170\).
  • Strong nuclear force: strongest force, charge-independent, short-ranged, saturating, repulsive for \(r < 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\).
  • Fission (\(\sim 200\,\mathrm{MeV}\) per \({}^{235}\mathrm{U}\)) and Fusion (\(26.7\,\mathrm{MeV}\) per \(p\text{–}p\) cycle) both release energy by increasing \(E_{bn}\).

11. Concise Final Recap

Atomic nuclei pack nearly all atomic mass into a femtometre-scale core of protons and neutrons held at constant density by the short-range, charge-independent strong nuclear force. Because bound nuclei have slightly less mass than their separated nucleons, Einstein's mass–energy equivalence governs both nuclear stability—mapped by the binding energy per nucleon curve—and the enormous energy released when heavy nuclei undergo fission or light nuclei undergo thermonuclear fusion.
