NEET UG Physics • Core Electromagnetism

Moving Charges and Magnetism

A complete, self-contained tutorial covering magnetic forces, particle trajectories, Biot–Savart law, Ampere's circuital law, magnetic dipoles, and moving coil galvanometers.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Scope

Electric charges at rest generate only electric fields, whereas charges in motion—whether free beams or steady electric currents—create magnetic fields in surrounding space and experience velocity-dependent magnetic forces. For NEET UG, this topic is a high-weightage bridge between electrostatics and electromagnetic induction, testing both directional rules and rapid formula-based problem solving.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Compute the Lorentz force on moving charges and current-carrying conductors, and determine directions using right-hand vector rules.
  • Analyze circular and helical trajectories of charged particles in uniform magnetic fields (radius, cyclotron frequency, and pitch).
  • Apply the Biot–Savart law and Ampere's circuital law to find magnetic fields of straight wires, circular arcs, loops, and solenoids.
  • Calculate the magnetic force between parallel currents and the torque on a current loop treated as a magnetic dipole.
  • Convert a moving coil galvanometer into an ammeter or a voltmeter and evaluate current and voltage sensitivities.

2. Magnetic Force & Motion in a Magnetic Field

2.1 Vector Convention & The Lorentz Force

To represent three-dimensional vectors on a two-dimensional page, physics uses a standard arrow-tip convention: a dot inside a circle (⊙) denotes a vector pointing out of the page toward the viewer, while a cross inside a circle (⊗) denotes a vector pointing into the page away from the viewer.

When a point charge \(q\) moves with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) through a region containing both an electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) and a magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), the total electromagnetic force acting on it is the Lorentz force:

Lorentz Force Law
\[\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{electric}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{magnetic}} = q\mathbf{E} + q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\]

Magnitude of magnetic force: \(F_m = |q|\,v\,B\,\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\).

  • Zero-force conditions: The magnetic force vanishes if the charge is stationary (\(v = 0\)) or if it moves parallel (\(\theta = 0^\circ\)) or antiparallel (\(\theta = 180^\circ\)) to \(\mathbf{B}\).
  • Direction: By the right-hand rule for cross products, curl the fingers of your right hand from \(\mathbf{v}\) toward \(\mathbf{B}\) through the smaller angle; your thumb points in the direction of \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\). A positive charge experiences force along \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\), whereas a negative charge is pushed in the opposite direction.
  • SI Unit of \(\mathbf{B}\): The tesla (\(\mathrm{T}\)), defined as \(1\,\mathrm{T} = 1\,\mathrm{N\cdot s/(C\cdot m)}\). A commonly used smaller non-SI unit is the gauss (\(\mathrm{G}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{G} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}\).
  • Work-Energy Property: Because \(\mathbf{F}_m\) is always perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\), the instantaneous power \(\mathbf{F}_m \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\). Therefore, a static magnetic field does zero work on a moving charge; it can change the direction of momentum but never the speed or kinetic energy.

2.2 Particle Trajectories in a Uniform Magnetic Field

Depending on the launch angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf{v}\) and a uniform field \(\mathbf{B}\), a charged particle traces one of three paths:

  • Case 1 (\(\theta = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\)): \(\mathbf{F}_m = 0\), so the particle continues in a straight line at constant velocity.
  • Case 2 (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)): The magnetic force \(qvB\) acts purely as a centripetal force perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\), producing uniform circular motion: \[r = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB} = \frac{\sqrt{2mK}}{qB}\] where \(p\) is momentum and \(K\) is kinetic energy. The angular frequency \(\omega\) and cyclotron frequency \(\nu_c\) are: \[\omega = \frac{qB}{m}, \qquad \nu_c = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}\] Notice that \(\nu_c\) and \(T\) are completely independent of the particle's speed, radius, and kinetic energy (in the non-relativistic regime).
  • Case 3 (\(0^\circ < \theta < 90^\circ\)): Resolve velocity into \(v_\perp = v\sin\theta\) (perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\)) and \(v_\parallel = v\cos\theta\) (parallel to \(\mathbf{B}\)). The perpendicular component drives circular motion of radius \(r = mv_\perp/(qB)\), while \(v_\parallel\) remains unaffected and carries the particle steadily along \(\mathbf{B}\). The combined path is a helix with pitch: \[p_{\text{pitch}} = v_\parallel T = \frac{2\pi m v \cos\theta}{qB}\]

2.3 Magnetic Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A straight conducting rod of length \(l\) and cross-sectional area \(A\) carrying steady current \(I\) contains mobile charge carriers moving with drift velocity \(\mathbf{v}_d\). Summing the magnetic force over all carriers in the rod yields:

Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor
\[\mathbf{F} = I(\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{B}) \quad \text{(straight conductor in uniform }\mathbf{B}\text{)}\]

Magnitude: \(F = I l B \sin\theta\), where \(\mathbf{l}\) points in the direction of conventional current \(I\). For a curved wire in a uniform \(\mathbf{B}\), replace \(\mathbf{l}\) with the effective displacement vector \(\mathbf{l}_{\text{eff}}\) joining the start and end points of the wire; thus, the net magnetic force on any closed current loop in a uniform magnetic field is zero.

3. Sources of Magnetic Field: Biot–Savart & Ampere's Laws

3.1 The Biot–Savart Law

An infinitesimal current element \(I\,d\mathbf{l}\) produces an elementary magnetic field \(d\mathbf{B}\) at a displacement \(\mathbf{r}\) from the element given by:

Biot–Savart Law
\[d\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\,(d\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{r})}{r^3}, \qquad |d\mathbf{B}| = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\,dl\,\sin\theta}{r^2}\]

Here \(\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m\,A^{-1}}\) is the permeability of free space, so \(\mu_0/(4\pi) = 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m\,A^{-1}}\). Note the fundamental link to the speed of light \(c\) in vacuum: \(\mu_0 \varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2\).

Feature Electrostatics (Coulomb's Law) Magnetostatics (Biot–Savart Law)
Source of field Scalar source: electric charge \(q\) Vector source: current element \(I\,d\mathbf{l}\)
Distance dependence Inverse-square (\(1/r^2\)), long-range Inverse-square (\(1/r^2\)), long-range
Direction of field Along the displacement vector \(\mathbf{r}\) Perpendicular to the plane of \(d\mathbf{l}\) and \(\mathbf{r}\)
Angle dependence Isotropic (no \(\sin\theta\) factor) Proportional to \(\sin\theta\) (zero along axis of \(d\mathbf{l}\))

3.2 Magnetic Field of Circular Loops and Arcs

Integrating the Biot–Savart law around a circular loop of radius \(R\) carrying steady current \(I\) cancels all components perpendicular to the loop's axis by symmetry, leaving only the axial field at distance \(x\) from the centre:

Circular Coil & Arc Formulas
\[B_{\text{axis}} = \frac{\mu_0 N I R^2}{2(x^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \text{At centre } (x = 0): \quad B_0 = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2R}\]

For a planar circular arc subtending an angle \(\alpha\) (in radians) at its centre of curvature: \(B_{\text{arc}} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I \alpha}{4\pi R}\).

3.3 Ampere's Circuital Law & High-Symmetry Applications

For steady currents, Ampere's circuital law relates the line integral of \(\mathbf{B}\) around any closed Amperian loop \(C\) to the net current \(I_{\text{enc}}\) piercing the surface bounded by \(C\):

Ampere's Circuital Law
\[\oint_C \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}\]

When symmetry makes \(\mathbf{B}\) tangential and constant in magnitude along a path of length \(L\), this simplifies to \(BL = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}\):

  • Long Straight Thin Wire: At perpendicular distance \(r\), \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\). Magnetic field lines form concentric circles around the wire, directed by curling the right-hand fingers while the thumb points along the current.
  • Thick Cylindrical Wire of Radius \(a\) (uniform current \(I\)):
    • Outside (\(r \ge a\)): \(I_{\text{enc}} = I\), so \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\) (\(B \propto 1/r\)).
    • Inside (\(r < a\)): \(I_{\text{enc}} = I \left(\dfrac{r^2}{a^2}\right)\), so \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi a^2}\) (\(B \propto r\)).
    • The field peaks right at the surface (\(r = a\)) with \(B_{\max} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\).
  • Long Solenoid: For a tightly wound helical coil of length much greater than its radius with \(n = N/L\) turns per unit length, the interior field is uniform and axial while the exterior field is negligible: \[B_{\text{inside}} = \mu_0 n I\]

4. Parallel Currents, Magnetic Dipoles & Galvanometers

4.1 Force Between Two Parallel Conductors

Two long, straight, parallel wires separated by distance \(d\) and carrying steady currents \(I_1\) and \(I_2\) exert equal and opposite magnetic forces on each other. The force per unit length \(f = F/L\) on either wire is:

Force per Unit Length Between Parallel Currents
\[f = \frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}\]

Direction Rule: Parallel currents (same direction) attract; antiparallel currents (opposite directions) repel. This directly defines the SI unit ampere: \(1\,\mathrm{A}\) is the steady current in each of two infinitely long parallel wires \(1\,\mathrm{m}\) apart in vacuum that produces a force of \(2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N/m}\).

4.2 Torque on a Current Loop & Magnetic Dipole Moment

A planar loop of \(N\) closely wound turns, area \(A\), and current \(I\) behaves as a magnetic dipole with magnetic dipole moment:

Magnetic Moment & Torque in a Uniform Field
\[\mathbf{m} = N I \mathbf{A} \quad (\text{SI unit: }\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}\text{ or }\mathrm{J/T}), \qquad \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{B}, \qquad \tau = N I A B \sin\theta\]

Here \(\theta\) is the angle between the area vector \(\mathbf{A}\) (normal to the coil plane, found by the right-hand thumb rule) and \(\mathbf{B}\).

  • Equilibrium: Torque is zero when \(\theta = 0^\circ\) (stable equilibrium, \(\mathbf{m} \parallel \mathbf{B}\)) and when \(\theta = 180^\circ\) (unstable equilibrium, \(\mathbf{m}\) antiparallel to \(\mathbf{B}\)).
  • Far-field Dipole Analogy: At large distances \(x \gg R\) from a circular loop, the axial field is \(B_{\text{axial}} = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{2m}{x^3}\) and the equatorial (in-plane) field is \(B_{\text{eq}} = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{m}{x^3}\), mirroring the electric dipole with \(\mathbf{p}_e \to \mathbf{m}\) and \(1/\varepsilon_0 \to \mu_0\). However, unlike electric charges, isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist.

4.3 The Moving Coil Galvanometer (MCG)

A moving coil galvanometer suspends a rectangular coil of \(N\) turns and area \(A\) in a uniform radial magnetic field (created by concave pole pieces and a soft-iron core). Because the field is radial, the plane of the coil is always parallel to \(\mathbf{B}\) (\(\theta = 90^\circ\), \(\sin\theta = 1\)), giving a linear deflecting torque \(\tau = NIAB\). A spring of torsional constant \(k\) provides a restoring torque \(k\phi\), so at equilibrium:

Galvanometer Sensitivities & Conversions
\[\phi = \left(\frac{NAB}{k}\right) I \implies \text{Current Sensitivity } S_I = \frac{\phi}{I} = \frac{NAB}{k}, \qquad \text{Voltage Sensitivity } S_V = \frac{\phi}{V} = \frac{NAB}{k R_G}\]

Key NEET Insight: Increasing \(N\) increases \(S_I\), but if the wire length increases proportionally, the coil resistance \(R_G\) also increases in the same ratio, leaving \(S_V\) unchanged.

  • Conversion to Ammeter (connected in series): Attach a very small shunt resistance \(r_s\) in parallel with the galvanometer of resistance \(R_G\) and full-scale deflection current \(I_g\): \[r_s = \frac{I_g R_G}{I - I_g} \qquad (\text{for desired range } 0 \text{ to } I)\]
  • Conversion to Voltmeter (connected in parallel): Attach a very large resistance \(R\) in series with the galvanometer: \[R = \frac{V}{I_g} - R_G \qquad (\text{for desired range } 0 \text{ to } V)\]

6. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Helical Motion of a Proton

Problem (Assumed values): A proton (\(q = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), \(m = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) is injected into a uniform magnetic field \(B = 0.50\,\mathrm{T}\) with speed \(v = 4.0 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}\) at an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the field direction. Find the radius of the helix and its pitch.

Solution:

Resolve the velocity into components perpendicular and parallel to \(\mathbf{B}\):

\[v_\perp = v \sin 30^\circ = (4.0 \times 10^6) \times 0.5 = 2.0 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}\] \[v_\parallel = v \cos 30^\circ = (4.0 \times 10^6) \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 3.464 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m/s}\]

The radius of the circular projection is:

\[r = \frac{m v_\perp}{q B} = \frac{1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times 2.0 \times 10^6}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.50} = 4.175 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 4.18\,\mathrm{cm}\]

The time period of one revolution is:

\[T = \frac{2\pi m}{q B} = \frac{2\pi \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.50} \approx 1.312 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{s}\]

Hence, the pitch is \(p_{\text{pitch}} = v_\parallel T = (3.464 \times 10^6)(1.312 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 0.454\,\mathrm{m} = 45.4\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Worked Example 2: Magnetic Field of a Quarter-Circle Arc

Problem (Assumed values): A long straight wire is bent into a \(90^\circ\) circular arc of radius \(R = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) with two straight radial leads pointing directly away from the centre of curvature. If it carries a steady current \(I = 8.0\,\mathrm{A}\), determine the magnitude of the magnetic field at the centre of curvature.

Solution:

For every element \(d\mathbf{l}\) along the two straight radial segments, \(d\mathbf{l}\) is collinear with the displacement vector \(\mathbf{r}\) to the centre, so \(d\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{r} = 0\). Thus, the straight leads contribute zero field at the centre.

For the quarter-circle arc, the subtended angle is \(\alpha = \pi/2\,\mathrm{rad}\) and \(R = 0.04\,\mathrm{m}\):

\[B = \frac{\mu_0 I \alpha}{4\pi R} = \frac{10^{-7} \times 8.0 \times (\pi/2)}{0.04} = \pi \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 3.14 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}\]
Worked Example 3: Magnetic Levitation of a Current-Carrying Rod

Problem (Assumed values): A horizontal metal rod of mass \(60\,\mathrm{g}\) and length \(0.50\,\mathrm{m}\) hangs from two light vertical insulating strings in a uniform horizontal magnetic field \(B = 0.40\,\mathrm{T}\) directed perpendicular to the rod. What current must flow through the rod so that the tension in both strings drops to zero? (Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) as a standard assumption.)

Solution:

For zero tension, the upward magnetic force \(I l B\) must exactly balance the downward weight \(mg\):

\[I l B = m g \implies I = \frac{m g}{l B} = \frac{0.060 \times 10}{0.50 \times 0.40} = \frac{0.60}{0.20} = 3.0\,\mathrm{A}\]

The current must be directed such that \(\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{B}\) points vertically upward.

Worked Example 4: Converting a Galvanometer to an Ammeter and a Voltmeter

Problem (Assumed values): A moving coil galvanometer has coil resistance \(R_G = 50\,\Omega\) and shows full-scale deflection at a current \(I_g = 2.0\,\mathrm{mA}\). How can it be converted into (a) an ammeter of range \(0\text{–}2.0\,\mathrm{A}\), and (b) a voltmeter of range \(0\text{–}10\,\mathrm{V}\)?

Solution:

(a) Ammeter conversion: Connect a shunt resistor \(r_s\) in parallel:

\[r_s = \frac{I_g R_G}{I - I_g} = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times 50}{2.0 - 0.002} = \frac{0.10}{1.998} \approx 0.050\,\Omega\]

(b) Voltmeter conversion: Connect a high resistor \(R\) in series:

\[R = \frac{V}{I_g} - R_G = \frac{10}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} - 50 = 5000 - 50 = 4950\,\Omega\]

7. NEET UG Practice Questions & Worked Solutions

The following are original practice questions aligned to the NEET UG examination pattern.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

A proton and an alpha particle enter a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the field direction with the same kinetic energy. What is the ratio of the radius of the proton's circular path to that of the alpha particle (\(r_p : r_\alpha\))?

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(2 : 1\)
  • (C) \(1 : 1\)
  • (D) \(1 : 4\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: The radius in terms of kinetic energy \(K\) is \(r = \dfrac{\sqrt{2mK}}{qB}\). For a proton, mass is \(m_p\) and charge is \(e\); for an alpha particle, \(m_\alpha = 4m_p\) and \(q_\alpha = 2e\). Since \(K\) and \(B\) are identical: \[\frac{r_p}{r_\alpha} = \frac{\sqrt{m_p}/e}{\sqrt{4m_p}/(2e)} = \frac{1}{2/2} = 1 : 1\]

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

A long, straight cylindrical wire of radius \(a\) carries a steady current \(I\) uniformly distributed across its cross-section. At what distances from the axis of the wire is the magnetic field equal to half of its maximum value?

  • (A) \(a/4\) and \(4a\)
  • (B) \(a/2\) and \(2a\)
  • (C) \(a/\sqrt{2}\) and \(\sqrt{2}a\)
  • (D) \(a/2\) and \(3a/2\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: The maximum field occurs at the surface \(r = a\): \(B_{\max} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\). Half of this value is \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a}\). Inside (\(r < a\)): \(\dfrac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi a^2} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a} \implies r = \dfrac{a}{2}\). Outside (\(r > a\)): \(\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a} \implies r = 2a\).

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

The magnetic field at the centre of a circular current-carrying loop of radius \(R\) is \(B_0\). What is the magnitude of the magnetic field on its axis at a distance \(x = \sqrt{3}R\) from the centre?

  • (A) \(B_0 / 4\)
  • (B) \(B_0 / 8\)
  • (C) \(B_0 / 16\)
  • (D) \(B_0 / (2\sqrt{2})\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Using the axial field formula with \(x = \sqrt{3}R\): \[B(x) = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2(R^2 + 3R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2(4R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R \cdot 8} = \frac{B_0}{8}\]

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Two long, parallel straight wires separated by \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) in air carry steady currents of \(4.0\,\mathrm{A}\) and \(6.0\,\mathrm{A}\) in opposite directions. What is the magnetic force per unit length acting between them?

  • (A) \(4.8 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N/m}\), attractive
  • (B) \(4.8 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N/m}\), repulsive
  • (C) \(2.4 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N/m}\), repulsive
  • (D) \(9.6 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N/m}\), attractive
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Antiparallel currents repel each other. With \(d = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}\): \[f = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d} = \frac{2 \times 10^{-7} \times 4.0 \times 6.0}{0.10} = 4.8 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N/m} \text{ (repulsive)}\]

Practice Question 5 Single-Correct MCQ

A closely wound long solenoid of length \(0.40\,\mathrm{m}\) has \(800\) turns and carries a steady current of \(2.5\,\mathrm{A}\). What is the magnitude of the magnetic field inside the solenoid near its centre?

  • (A) \(3.14 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}\)
  • (B) \(6.28 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}\)
  • (C) \(1.26 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{T}\)
  • (D) \(2.51 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Turn density \(n = \dfrac{N}{L} = \dfrac{800}{0.40} = 2000\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\). Thus: \[B = \mu_0 n I = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 2000 \times 2.5 = 2\pi \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 6.28 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}\]

Practice Question 6 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): A uniform, time-independent magnetic field cannot alter the kinetic energy of a moving charged particle.
Reason (R): The magnetic force \(q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\) is always perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity \(\mathbf{v}\), so the instantaneous power delivered by the field is zero.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) Both A and R are false.
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Since \(\mathbf{F}_m \perp \mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{F}_m \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\), meaning zero work is done and by the work-energy theorem, kinetic energy remains constant.

Practice Question 7 Statement-Based MCQ

Statement I: Doubling the number of turns \(N\) of a moving coil galvanometer (keeping coil area, magnetic field, wire gauge, and spring constant fixed) doubles its current sensitivity.
Statement II: Doubling the number of turns \(N\) under the same conditions also doubles its voltage sensitivity.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Current sensitivity \(S_I = NAB/k\) doubles when \(N \to 2N\). However, doubling the turns doubles the wire length and hence doubles the coil resistance \(R_G \to 2R_G\). Therefore, voltage sensitivity \(S_V = \dfrac{NAB}{k R_G}\) remains unchanged.

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

A rectangular coil of \(50\) turns and area \(2.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) carries a current of \(4.0\,\mathrm{A}\) in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.25\,\mathrm{T}\). If the plane of the coil makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the magnetic field, what is the magnitude of the torque on the coil?

  • (A) \(0.050\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  • (B) \(0.087\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  • (C) \(0.100\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  • (D) \(0.025\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: The angle \(\theta\) in \(\tau = N I A B \sin\theta\) is between the normal to the coil and \(\mathbf{B}\). Since the plane makes \(30^\circ\) with \(\mathbf{B}\), \(\theta = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ\): \[\tau = 50 \times 4.0 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times 0.25 \times \sin 60^\circ = 0.10 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.0866\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} \approx 0.087\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\]

Practice Question 9 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following gives the correct dimensional formula for the permeability of free space \(\mu_0\)?

  • (A) \([\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}\,A^{-2}}]\)
  • (B) \([\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}\,A^{-1}}]\)
  • (C) \([\mathrm{M\,T^{-2}\,A^{-1}}]\)
  • (D) \([\mathrm{M^{-1}\,L^{-3}\,T^4\,A^2}]\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: From \(F/L = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}\), we get \([\mu_0] = \dfrac{[F]}{[I]^2} = \dfrac{[\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]}{[\mathrm{A^2}]} = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}\,A^{-2}}]\).

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

A positively charged particle moves along the \(+x\) axis through a region with a uniform magnetic field \(B = 0.15\,\mathrm{T}\) along the \(+y\) axis and a uniform electric field \(E = 3.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{V/m}\) along the \(-z\) axis. At what speed will the particle pass through the region completely undeflected?

  • (A) \(4.5 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{m/s}\)
  • (B) \(2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\)
  • (C) \(5.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\)
  • (D) \(2.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{m/s}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: The magnetic force \(q(v\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times B\hat{\mathbf{j}}) = qvB\,\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) points along \(+z\), while the electric force \(q(-E\hat{\mathbf{k}})\) points along \(-z\). For zero net force: \[qvB = qE \implies v = \frac{E}{B} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^4}{0.15} = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\]

8. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Avoid These Frequent Exam Mistakes:
  • Angle Trap in Torque: In \(\tau = NIAB\sin\theta\), \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{B}\) and the normal (area vector), NOT the angle between \(\mathbf{B}\) and the plane of the coil.
  • Opposite Sign Rule for Parallel Wires: Unlike electric charges (where like charges repel), like (parallel) currents attract and antiparallel currents repel.
  • Galvanometer Sensitivity Fallacy: Increasing \(N\) raises current sensitivity, but voltage sensitivity stays constant if wire resistance increases in proportion to \(N\).
  • Negative Charge Direction: Always reverse the direction obtained from \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\) when the moving particle is an electron or any negatively charged ion.
Quick Revision Checklist:
  • Can you write \(r\), \(\nu_c\), and pitch from memory and express \(r\) in terms of \(K\) or accelerating potential \(V\)?
  • Do you remember \(B \propto r\) inside and \(B \propto 1/r\) outside a thick uniform current-carrying wire?
  • Can you instantly compute shunt \(r_s\) for an ammeter and series \(R\) for a voltmeter?

9. Concise Final Recap

Moving charges both create magnetic fields (governed by the Biot–Savart and Ampere's laws) and respond to external magnetic fields via the velocity-dependent Lorentz force \(q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\). Because magnetic forces do no work, they bend trajectories into circles or helices without changing speed. Current loops act as elementary magnetic dipoles experiencing torque \(\mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{B}\), forming the operating principle behind the moving coil galvanometer and its conversion into practical ammeters and voltmeters.
