NEET UG Biology Class XII • Human Physiology & Reproduction

Human Reproduction: Complete Concept & Assessment Tutorial

Anatomy, Gametogenesis, Hormonal Regulation, Fertilisation, Embryonic Development, and Parturition

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Roadmap

Humans are sexually reproducing, viviparous mammals. Successful reproduction depends on a tightly coordinated sequence of anatomical structures, cellular divisions, and neuroendocrine feedback loops that begin after puberty. For NEET UG, questions frequently test exact ductal sequences, ploidy transitions during gametogenesis, hormonal peaks in the menstrual cycle, and gestational milestones.

Core Learning Objectives
  • Trace the structural organization and secretory roles of the male and female reproductive tracts and mammary glands.
  • Contrast spermatogenesis and oogenesis in terms of onset, meiotic arrests, cytoplasmic distribution, and chromosomal ploidy.
  • Interpret the 28/29-day menstrual cycle by linking pituitary gonadotropins (LH, FSH) with ovarian hormones (estrogen, progesterone) and uterine endometrial changes.
  • Explain the cellular events of fertilisation, paternal sex determination, cleavage, blastocyst implantation, and placental endocrinology.
  • Identify trimester-wise fetal milestones and the positive-feedback mechanism of parturition and lactation.
Chronological Sequence of Human Reproductive Events:
Gametogenesis → Insemination → Fertilisation → Cleavage & Blastocyst Formation → Implantation → Gestation → Parturition & Lactation

2. Functional Anatomy of the Male Reproductive System

The male reproductive system resides in the pelvic region and comprises a pair of primary sex organs (testes), accessory ducts, accessory glands, and the external genitalia (penis).

2.1 Testes, Scrotal Thermoregulation, and Seminiferous Tubules

  • Scrotum: The testes are suspended outside the abdominal cavity within a pouch called the scrotum. This external position maintains testicular temperature 2–2.5 °C lower than normal internal body temperature, a strict thermal requirement for normal spermatogenesis.
  • Testicular Lobules: Each adult testis is oval (4–5 cm long, 2–3 cm wide), enclosed by a dense fibrous covering, and partitioned internally into approximately 250 compartments called testicular lobules.
  • Seminiferous Tubules: Each lobule houses 1 to 3 highly coiled seminiferous tubules, the sites of sperm production. Their inner lining contains two distinct cell populations:
    • Male germ cells (Spermatogonia): Undergo mitotic proliferation and meiotic divisions to form spermatozoa.
    • Sertoli cells: Tall somatic nurse cells that provide nutrition and structural support to developing germ cells.
  • Interstitial Spaces: The regions outside the seminiferous tubules contain small blood vessels, immunologically competent cells, and interstitial cells (Leydig cells), which synthesize and secrete testicular hormones called androgens (chiefly testosterone).

2.2 Male Accessory Ducts and Glands

Sperms produced in the seminiferous tubules are stored, matured, and transported outward through a sequential ductal network:

Seminiferous tubules → Rete testis → Vasa efferentia → Epididymis → Vas deferens → Ejaculatory duct → Urethra → Urethral meatus
  • Epididymis & Vas Deferens: Vasa efferentia exit the testis and open into the epididymis along its posterior surface. The epididymis continues as the vas deferens, which ascends into the abdomen, loops over the urinary bladder, and receives a duct from the seminal vesicle to form the ejaculatory duct opening into the urethra.
  • Male Accessory Glands: Comprise paired seminal vesicles, a single prostate gland, and paired bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands. Their combined secretions form seminal plasma, which is rich in fructose (energy substrate for sperm), calcium, and specific enzymes. Bulbourethral secretions additionally lubricate the penis.
  • Semen & External Genitalia: Seminal plasma plus spermatozoa constitutes semen. The penis contains specialized erectile tissue that facilitates insemination; its enlarged tip, the glans penis, is covered by a loose skin fold called the foreskin.

3. Functional Anatomy of the Female Reproductive System & Mammary Glands

The female reproductive system includes a pair of ovaries, a pair of oviducts (fallopian tubes), a single uterus, the cervix, the vagina, external genitalia, and a pair of mammary glands, all integrated for ovulation, fertilisation, pregnancy, childbirth, and infant nourishment.

3.1 Ovaries, Oviducts, Uterus, and External Genitalia

  • Ovaries: Primary female sex organs (2–4 cm long) located on each side of the lower abdomen and anchored to the pelvic wall and uterus by ligaments. A thin epithelium encloses the ovarian stroma, which is divided into a peripheral cortex and an inner medulla.
  • Oviducts (Fallopian Tubes): Each tube is 10–12 cm long and has three anatomical regions from ovary to uterus:
    1. Infundibulum: Funnel-shaped region near the ovary bearing finger-like fimbriae that sweep in the ovulated oocyte.
    2. Ampulla: Wider central region; serves as the anatomical site of fertilisation.
    3. Isthmus: Narrow-lumen segment that joins the uterus.
  • Uterus (Womb): Single, inverted-pear-shaped organ opening into the vagina through a narrow cervix. The cervical canal together with the vagina forms the birth canal. The uterine wall has three layers:
    • Perimetrium: Outer thin membranous covering.
    • Myometrium: Middle thick layer of smooth muscle responsible for vigorous contractions during childbirth.
    • Endometrium: Inner glandular lining that undergoes cyclical proliferation and shedding during the menstrual cycle.
  • External Genitalia: Include the mons pubis (fatty cushion with pubic hair), labia majora (outer fleshy folds), labia minora (inner paired folds), clitoris (tiny finger-like erectile structure at the upper junction of labia minora above the urethral opening), and hymen (membrane partially covering the vaginal opening).
High-Yield Clinical Concept: Significance of the Hymen

Although the hymen is often torn during first coitus, it can also rupture due to a sudden fall, jolt, insertion of a vaginal tampon, or vigorous sports (such as cycling or horseback riding). Conversely, it may persist even after coitus. Therefore, the presence or absence of the hymen is not a reliable indicator of virginity or sexual experience.

3.2 Mammary Gland Ductal Hierarchy

Each paired mammary gland contains glandular tissue and variable adipose tissue. The glandular tissue is partitioned into 15–20 mammary lobes containing clusters of milk-secreting cells called alveoli. Milk flows outward in a strict order:

Mammary alveoli (lumen) → Mammary tubules → Mammary duct → Mammary ampulla → Lactiferous duct → Nipple

4. Gametogenesis: Spermatogenesis vs. Oogenesis

4.1 Endocrine Initiation & Spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis begins at puberty due to a marked surge in hypothalamic gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH). GnRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to secrete two gonadotropins:

  • Luteinising Hormone (LH): Targets Leydig cells to stimulate androgen synthesis and secretion; androgens drive spermatogenesis.
  • Follicle Stimulating Hormone (FSH): Targets Sertoli cells to stimulate secretion of factors required for spermiogenesis.

Inside the seminiferous tubules, diploid spermatogonia (\(2n = 46\)) multiply by mitosis. Some differentiate into primary spermatocytes (\(2n = 46\)), which complete Meiosis I (reduction division) to yield two equal haploid secondary spermatocytes (\(n = 23\)). Each secondary spermatocyte undergoes Meiosis II to produce two equal haploid spermatids (\(n = 23\)), giving four spermatids per primary spermatocyte. Spermatids remodel into spermatozoa by spermiogenesis. Finally, sperm heads embed in Sertoli cells and are released into the tubule lumen by spermiation.

4.2 Structure of a Mature Sperm & Fertility Thresholds

  • Components (enveloped by a plasma membrane):
    • Head: Contains an elongated haploid nucleus capped anteriorly by the acrosome, which is packed with hydrolytic enzymes for penetrating the ovum.
    • Neck: Connects head to middle piece.
    • Middle piece: Houses numerous spirally arranged mitochondria generating ATP for flagellar movement.
    • Tail: Provides motility essential for reaching the ampulla.
  • Normal Fertility Benchmarks: A human male ejaculates roughly 200–300 million sperms per coitus. For normal fertility, at least 60% must display normal morphology and at least 40% must show vigorous motility.

4.3 Oogenesis & Follicular Development

Oogenesis begins during embryonic (fetal) life when a couple of million oogonia form in each fetal ovary; no new oogonia are added after birth. Oogonia enter Meiosis I and arrest at Prophase-I as primary oocytes. Each primary oocyte becomes surrounded by granulosa cells to form a primary follicle. Massive follicular degeneration (atresia) occurs from birth to puberty, leaving only 60,000–80,000 primary follicles per ovary at puberty.

  • Follicular Progression: Primary follicle → Secondary follicle (more granulosa layers + new theca) → Tertiary follicle (characterized by a fluid-filled cavity called the antrum, and differentiation of theca into inner theca interna and outer theca externa).
  • Completion of Meiosis I: Within the tertiary follicle, the primary oocyte grows and completes an unequal first meiotic division, forming a large haploid secondary oocyte (retaining bulk of nutrient-rich cytoplasm) and a tiny first polar body.
  • Graafian Follicle & Ovulation: The tertiary follicle matures into the Graafian follicle, and the secondary oocyte develops a glycoprotein coat called the zona pellucida. At ovulation, the Graafian follicle ruptures to release the secondary oocyte.
Feature Spermatogenesis Oogenesis
Onset & Duration Begins at puberty; continues even in old age. Initiated in fetal life; ceases around age 50 (menopause).
Meiotic Arrests Continuous; no prolonged arrest. Arrested at Prophase-I (birth to puberty) and Metaphase-II (until fertilisation).
Meiotic Cytokinesis Equal divisions yielding 4 equal spermatids per primary spermatocyte. Unequal divisions yielding 1 large ovum + tiny polar bodies.
Differentiation Step Requires spermiogenesis (spermatid → spermatozoon). No post-meiotic morphological metamorphosis like flagellation.

5. Hormonal Orchestration of the Menstrual Cycle

The menstrual cycle is the reproductive cycle of female primates, starting at puberty (menarche), repeating every 28–29 days on average, and ceasing around 50 years of age (menopause). Each cycle coordinates ovarian events with uterine endometrial preparation:

  1. Menstrual Phase (Days 1–5): Occurs if fertilisation does not take place. Falling progesterone causes breakdown of the endometrial lining and its blood vessels, producing menstrual flow. (Absence of menstruation may indicate pregnancy, though stress or poor health can also cause it.)
  2. Follicular (Proliferative) Phase (Days 6–13): Gradual rise in pituitary FSH and LH stimulates primary follicles to mature into a Graafian follicle. Growing follicles secrete estrogen, which drives regeneration (proliferation) of the uterine endometrium.
  3. Ovulatory Phase (Around Day 14): Estrogen peaks and triggers a rapid mid-cycle surge of LH (LH surge) along with an FSH peak. The LH surge induces rupture of the Graafian follicle and release of the secondary oocyte (ovulation).
  4. Luteal (Secretory) Phase (Days 15–28): The ruptured follicle transforms into the corpus luteum, which secretes large amounts of progesterone. Progesterone maintains a thick, vascular, secretory endometrium essential for implantation. If fertilisation fails, the corpus luteum degenerates, progesterone drops, and menstruation begins a new cycle.

6. Fertilisation, Sex Determination, Cleavage, and Implantation

  • Site & Timing: Semen deposited in the vagina during coitus travels through the cervix and uterus to the ampullary region of the fallopian tube. Fertilisation occurs only when the ovum and sperms arrive simultaneously at the ampulla.
  • Block to Polyspermy & Meiosis II Completion: Contact of a sperm with the zona pellucida induces membrane changes that block entry of additional sperms. Acrosomal enzymes help the sperm enter the ovum cytoplasm through the zona pellucida and plasma membrane. This entry triggers the secondary oocyte to complete its unequal second meiotic division, producing a second polar body and a haploid ovum (ootid). Fusion of the male and female haploid pronuclei forms a diploid zygote (\(2n = 46\)).
  • Chromosomal Sex Determination: All human ova carry an X sex chromosome, whereas 50% of sperms carry X and 50% carry Y. Thus, an XX zygote develops into a female and an XY zygote into a male—meaning the father's gamete determines the biological sex of the child.
  • Cleavage & Blastocyst Implantation: As the zygote moves through the isthmus toward the uterus, rapid mitotic divisions (cleavage) form 2, 4, 8, and 16 daughter cells called blastomeres. The 8–16 celled solid embryo is a morula, which transforms into a hollow blastocyst in the uterus. The blastocyst has:
    • An outer trophoblast layer (attaches to the endometrium and later forms extra-embryonic membranes/placenta).
    • An inner cell mass (differentiates into the embryo proper and contains pluripotent stem cells).
    Rapid division of uterine cells covers and embeds the blastocyst in the endometrium—a process called implantation, establishing pregnancy.

7. Pregnancy, Placental Endocrinology, Parturition & Lactation

7.1 Placenta & Pregnancy Hormones

After implantation, finger-like chorionic villi on the trophoblast interdigitate with vascularized uterine tissue to form the placenta, connected to the embryo via the umbilical cord. The placenta supplies O₂ and nutrients, removes CO₂ and metabolic wastes, and acts as a temporary endocrine gland.

  • Hormones produced ONLY during pregnancy: hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) and hPL (human placental lactogen) from the placenta, plus relaxin secreted by the ovary in the later phase of pregnancy.
  • Other elevated maternal hormones: Estrogens, progestogens, cortisol, prolactin, and thyroxine increase several-fold to support fetal growth and maternal metabolic adaptations.

7.2 Germ Layers & Gestational Milestones

Immediately after implantation, the inner cell mass differentiates into an outer ectoderm and inner endoderm, with a mesoderm appearing between them. These three germ layers give rise to all adult tissues and organs. Human gestation lasts approximately 9 months:

Gestational Age Key Developmental Milestones
End of 1st Month Embryonic heart is formed (first sign of growing fetus heard via stethoscope).
End of 2nd Month Fetus develops limbs and digits.
End of 12 Weeks (1st Trimester) Most major organ systems are formed; limbs and external genital organs are well-developed.
During 5th Month First fetal movements and appearance of hair on the head.
End of 24 Weeks (2nd Trimester) Body covered with fine hair, eyelids separate, and eyelashes are formed.
End of 9 Months Fetus is fully developed and ready for delivery.

7.3 Parturition & Lactation

  • Parturition (Childbirth): Induced by a complex neuroendocrine mechanism involving cortisol, estrogens, and oxytocin. Signals from the fully developed fetus and placenta trigger mild uterine contractions called the foetal ejection reflex. This stimulates maternal posterior pituitary release of oxytocin, which drives stronger myometrial contractions; stronger contractions in turn stimulate further oxytocin release (a positive-feedback loop) until the baby and placenta are expelled.
  • Lactation & Colostrum: Mammary glands differentiate during pregnancy and secrete milk after birth. Milk produced during the initial few days is called colostrum, which is rich in antibodies (essential for passive immunity and resistance in the newborn).

8. Worked Examples (Exam Problem-Solving Strategies)

Worked Example 1: Quantitative Gametogenesis & Ploidy Ratio

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Suppose a researcher examines 120 primary spermatocytes undergoing complete spermatogenesis and 120 primary oocytes undergoing complete oogenesis (assuming all reach fertilisation). Calculate the total number of functional gametes and total polar bodies formed.

Solution:

  • Each primary spermatocyte (\(2n\)) yields 4 functional haploid spermatozoa (\(n\)). Thus, \(120 \times 4 = 480\) spermatozoa.
  • Each primary oocyte (\(2n\)) yields 1 functional haploid ovum (\(n\)) and up to 2 polar bodies (first polar body from Meiosis I, second polar body from Meiosis II; note that the first polar body may or may not divide further).
  • Therefore, 120 primary oocytes yield 120 functional ova (and 240 polar bodies if the first polar body does not divide).
  • Ratio of functional male to female gametes produced per primary gametocyte = \(4 : 1\).
Worked Example 2: Semen Analysis & Fertility Benchmarks

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): A semen sample from an adult male contains 250 million spermatozoa in a single ejaculate. Microscopic analysis shows that 160 million have normal shape and size, and of those, 90 million exhibit vigorous motility. Does this sample meet the standard fertility criteria?

Solution:

  • Total sperm count = \(250\text{ million}\), which lies within the normal range (\(200\text{–}300\text{ million}\)).
  • Percentage with normal morphology = \(\frac{160}{250} \times 100\% = 64\%\) (exceeds the \(\ge 60\%\) threshold).
  • Percentage with vigorous motility out of total sperms = \(\frac{90}{250} \times 100\% = 36\%\) (below the \(\ge 40\%\) threshold of total ejaculated sperms; even relative to normal-shaped sperms, \(90/160 = 56.25\%\), the standard criterion applies to at least 40% of the total normal-shaped population/ejaculate). Because overall vigorous motility is below 40% of the total ejaculate, the sample falls short on motility.

9. NEET UG Practice Questions with Answers & Worked Solutions

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ (Male Anatomy)
Which of the following represents the correct sequential pathway of sperm transport from the site of production to the exterior?
  • (A) Seminiferous tubules → Vasa efferentia → Rete testis → Epididymis → Vas deferens → Urethra
  • (B) Seminiferous tubules → Rete testis → Vasa efferentia → Epididymis → Vas deferens → Urethra
  • (C) Seminiferous tubules → Rete testis → Epididymis → Vasa efferentia → Vas deferens → Urethra
  • (D) Seminiferous tubules → Epididymis → Rete testis → Vasa efferentia → Ejaculatory duct → Urethra
Correct Answer: (B) Sperms produced in the seminiferous tubules open into the rete testis, which leads into the vasa efferentia. The vasa efferentia leave the testis and open into the epididymis, which continues into the vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, and finally the urethra.
Practice Question 2 • Single-Correct MCQ (Endocrinology of Spermatogenesis)
Match the hormone with its primary target cell in the human testis and its physiological effect:
  • (A) LH acts on Sertoli cells to stimulate androgen secretion.
  • (B) FSH acts on Leydig cells to stimulate spermiogenesis.
  • (C) LH acts on Leydig cells to stimulate androgen synthesis, while FSH acts on Sertoli cells to aid spermiogenesis.
  • (D) GnRH acts directly on spermatogonia to trigger Meiosis I.
Correct Answer: (C) Hypothalamic GnRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release LH and FSH. LH acts on Leydig (interstitial) cells to stimulate androgen secretion, whereas FSH acts on Sertoli cells to secrete factors that assist spermiogenesis.
Practice Question 3 • Statement-Based MCQ (Oogenesis)
Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: No new oogonia are formed or added in a human female ovary after birth.
Statement II: The primary oocyte completes its first meiotic division inside the primary follicle before puberty.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
Correct Answer: (B) Statement I is correct: oogonia are formed only during fetal development. Statement II is incorrect: the primary oocyte remains arrested in Prophase-I until puberty and completes Meiosis I only within the tertiary follicle, not the primary follicle.
Practice Question 4 • Single-Correct MCQ (Menstrual Cycle)
During a normal 28-day menstrual cycle, the rapid surge in Luteinising Hormone (LH) around day 14 directly causes:
  • (A) Breakdown of the endometrial lining and menstrual bleeding.
  • (B) Rupture of the Graafian follicle and release of the secondary oocyte.
  • (C) Transformation of primary follicles into secondary follicles.
  • (D) Degeneration of the corpus luteum.
Correct Answer: (B) The mid-cycle LH surge (around day 14) induces rupture of the mature Graafian follicle, resulting in ovulation (release of the secondary oocyte).
Practice Question 5 • Assertion-Reason MCQ (Fertilisation)
Assertion (A): Every copulation does not necessarily lead to fertilisation and pregnancy.
Reason (R): Fertilisation can occur only if the ovum and sperms are transported simultaneously to the ampullary region of the fallopian tube.
  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Correct Answer: (A) Both statements are factually accurate, and the requirement for simultaneous arrival of viable sperms and the ovum at the ampullary region directly explains why not all copulations result in fertilisation.
Practice Question 6 • Single-Correct MCQ (Embryonic Cleavage)
Which of the following statements accurately distinguishes a morula from a blastocyst?
  • (A) A morula is a 2-celled stage, whereas a blastocyst has 4 blastomeres.
  • (B) A morula is an 8–16 celled embryo, whereas a blastocyst has an outer trophoblast and an inner cell mass.
  • (C) A morula implants directly into the myometrium, whereas a blastocyst remains in the ampulla.
  • (D) A morula possesses an antrum, whereas a blastocyst lacks any cavity.
Correct Answer: (B) Cleavage of the zygote produces an 8–16 celled morula. As it enters the uterus, it transforms into a blastocyst characterized by an outer trophoblast layer (which attaches to the endometrium) and an inner cell mass (which forms the embryo).
Practice Question 7 • Single-Correct MCQ (Placental Endocrinology)
Which group of hormones is produced in women exclusively during pregnancy?
  • (A) Estrogen, progesterone, and prolactin
  • (B) hCG, hPL, and relaxin
  • (C) Oxytocin, cortisol, and thyroxine
  • (D) LH, FSH, and progesterone
Correct Answer: (B) Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and human placental lactogen (hPL) are secreted by the placenta, and relaxin is secreted by the ovary in later pregnancy. These three hormones are produced only during pregnancy.

2. End of 2nd month — (q) Heart is formed
3. During 5th month — (r) Limbs and digits develop

Practice Question 9 • Single-Correct MCQ (Parturition & Lactation)
The "foetal ejection reflex" during parturition triggers the release of which hormone from the maternal pituitary to intensify uterine contractions?
  • (A) Prolactin
  • (B) Progesterone
  • (C) Oxytocin
  • (D) Relaxin
Correct Answer: (C) Mild uterine contractions induced by the fully developed fetus and placenta trigger maternal pituitary release of oxytocin, creating a positive-feedback loop that drives vigorous myometrial contractions for delivery.
Practice Question 10 • Single-Correct MCQ (Mammary Ducts)
In the human mammary gland, milk secreted by the alveoli reaches the nipple in which exact sequence?
  • (A) Alveoli → Mammary duct → Mammary tubules → Lactiferous duct → Mammary ampulla
  • (B) Alveoli → Mammary tubules → Mammary duct → Mammary ampulla → Lactiferous duct
  • (C) Alveoli → Lactiferous duct → Mammary ampulla → Mammary duct → Mammary tubules
  • (D) Alveoli → Mammary ampulla → Mammary tubules → Mammary duct → Lactiferous duct
Correct Answer: (B) Alveolar lumens open into mammary tubules, which join to form a mammary duct. Several mammary ducts merge into a wider mammary ampulla, which connects to a lactiferous duct opening at the nipple.

10. Common Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Confusing Sertoli and Leydig cells: Sertoli cells are inside seminiferous tubules (nutrition, FSH-regulated); Leydig cells are outside in interstitial spaces (androgens, LH-regulated).
  • Misidentifying the ploidy of polar bodies & secondary oocytes: Both the secondary oocyte and the first polar body are haploid (\(n = 23\)), even though Meiosis I is unequal in cytoplasm.
  • Wrong site of fertilisation: Fertilisation occurs in the ampulla (or ampullary region) of the fallopian tube, not in the uterus or infundibulum.
  • Confusing Spermiogenesis vs. Spermiation: Spermiogenesis is the differentiation of spermatids into spermatozoa; spermiation is the release of spermatozoa from Sertoli cells into the seminiferous tubule lumen.
Final NEET UG Revision Checklist
  • Scrotal temperature is 2–2.5 °C below core body temperature; ~250 testicular lobules per testis.
  • Seminal plasma contains fructose, Ca²⁺, and enzymes; normal fertility needs 200–300 million sperms, ≥60% normal shape/size, and ≥40% vigorous motility.
  • At puberty, 60,000–80,000 primary follicles remain per ovary; tertiary follicle is marked by the antrum.
  • Mid-cycle LH surge (day 14) causes ovulation; corpus luteum secretes progesterone to maintain the endometrium.
  • Colostrum provides essential antibodies to the newborn; oxytocin drives parturition via positive feedback.
