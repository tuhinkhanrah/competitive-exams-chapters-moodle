Principles of Inheritance and Variation

Comprehensive conceptual guide, quantitative genetics methods, pedigree logic, and NEET UG practice assessment.

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Overview

Genetics investigates two complementary biological phenomena: inheritance (the faithful transmission of traits from parents to offspring) and variation (the degree by which progeny differ from their parents and from each other). For the NEET UG examination, this unit demands both conceptual precision—such as distinguishing incomplete dominance from co-dominance or qualitative from quantitative globin defects—and rapid problem-solving fluency using probability, Punnett squares, linkage maps, and pedigree trees.

Core Competencies Targeted
  • Apply Mendel's Laws of Dominance, Segregation, and Independent Assortment to monohybrid, dihybrid, and multi-locus crosses.
  • Distinguish classical dominance from incomplete dominance, co-dominance, multiple allelism, pleiotropy, and polygenic inheritance.
  • Connect meiotic chromosome behaviour to the Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance, linkage, and recombination-based gene mapping.
  • Compare chromosomal (XX–XY, XX–XO, ZZ–ZW) and haplodiploid sex-determination systems.
  • Diagnose Mendelian and chromosomal disorders from molecular mechanisms, karyotypes, and family pedigrees.

2. Mendelian Genetics and Single-Gene Inheritance

2.1 Experimental Foundations

Between 1856 and 1863, Gregor Mendel conducted controlled hybridisation experiments on garden pea (Pisum sativum). Unlike earlier breeders who lacked quantitative records, Mendel used large sample sizes, tracked traits across successive generations, and applied statistical logic to reveal discrete rules of heredity.

Mendel began with 14 true-breeding pea lines—varieties that produced uniform offspring over multiple generations of self-pollination—arranged as 7 pairs differing in a single contrasting character.

2.2 Key Genetic Terminology

  • Gene (Mendel's "factor"): A discrete unit of heredity carrying information for a trait.
  • Alleles: Alternative forms of the same gene located at the same locus on homologous chromosomes (e.g., T and t for stem height).
  • Homozygous vs. Heterozygous: An organism with identical alleles (TT or tt) is homozygous; one with dissimilar alleles (Tt) is heterozygous.
  • Genotype vs. Phenotype: Genotype is the genetic constitution (TT, Tt, tt), whereas phenotype is the observable physical or biochemical expression (tall or dwarf).

2.3 Monohybrid Cross, Laws of Inheritance, and Test Cross

When Mendel crossed a true-breeding tall plant (TT) with a true-breeding dwarf plant (tt), all first filial (F1) hybrids (Tt) were tall, showing no intermediate blending. Self-pollination of F1 plants produced F2 progeny in a 3 : 1 phenotypic ratio (3/4 tall : 1/4 dwarf) and a 1 : 2 : 1 genotypic ratio (1/4 TT : 1/2 Tt : 1/4 tt).

Mathematically, random union of gametes carrying T and t at equal frequency (1/2 each) follows the binomial expansion:

\[ \left(\frac{1}{2}T + \frac{1}{2}t\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}TT + \frac{1}{2}Tt + \frac{1}{4}tt \]
  • Law of Dominance: Characters are controlled by discrete paired factors; in a dissimilar pair, one factor (dominant) masks the expression of the other (recessive) in the F1 heterozygote.
  • Law of Segregation: Alleles do not blend; during gamete formation (meiosis), the two alleles of a pair separate cleanly so each gamete receives only one allele with 50% probability.
  • Test Cross: Because TT and Tt share the same tall phenotype, an individual displaying a dominant phenotype is crossed with a homozygous recessive parent (tt) to determine its genotype. If all progeny show the dominant trait, the tested parent is homozygous (TT); if progeny split in a 1 : 1 ratio, the tested parent is heterozygous (Tt).

3. Deviations from Classical Dominance and Pleiotropy

3.1 Molecular Basis of Dominance

Dominance is not an intrinsic property of an allele in isolation; it reflects how gene products function to produce a phenotype. Consider a gene encoding an enzyme that converts substrate S to product P. A modified (mutant) allele may produce: (i) a normal or slightly less efficient enzyme (functionally equivalent, still dominant), (ii) a non-functional enzyme, or (iii) no enzyme at all. In cases (ii) and (iii), the phenotype depends solely on the unmodified functional allele, making the unmodified allele dominant and the loss-of-function allele recessive.

3.2 Incomplete Dominance vs. Co-dominance

  • Incomplete Dominance: Neither allele completely masks the other, so the F1 heterozygote displays an intermediate phenotype. In snapdragon (Antirrhinum sp.), crossing true-breeding red (RR) and white (rr) plants yields pink F1 (Rr) flowers. Selfing F1 produces F2 in a 1 Red (RR) : 2 Pink (Rr) : 1 White (rr) ratio—making the phenotypic and genotypic ratios identical (1 : 2 : 1).
  • Co-dominance and Multiple Alleles: Both alleles express simultaneously and independently in the heterozygote. The human ABO blood group is governed by gene I, which has three alleles in the population: \(I^A\), \(I^B\), and \(i\) (an example of multiple allelism, though any diploid individual carries only two alleles). Alleles \(I^A\) and \(I^B\) produce distinct surface sugar polymers on red blood cells and are completely dominant over \(i\) (which produces no sugar). When \(I^A\) and \(I^B\) occur together (\(I^AI^B\)), both sugars are expressed, yielding blood group AB.

Notice that 3 alleles generate \(\frac{n(n+1)}{2} = \frac{3 \times 4}{2} = 6\) genotypes and 4 phenotypes.

3.3 Pleiotropy and Context-Dependent Dominance

A pleiotropic gene influences multiple seemingly unrelated phenotypes, usually because its product participates in a shared metabolic pathway. For example:

  • Phenylketonuria (PKU): Mutation in the single gene encoding phenylalanine hydroxylase causes mental retardation as well as reduced hair and skin pigmentation.
  • Starch synthesis in pea seeds: Controlled by one gene with alleles B and b. Homozygous BB seeds produce large starch grains and are round; bb seeds produce small starch grains and become wrinkled upon drying. Heterozygous Bb seeds are round (so B is dominant for seed shape), yet their starch grains are intermediate in size (incomplete dominance at the starch-grain level). Thus, dominance depends on which phenotype is observed.

4. Two-Gene Inheritance, Chromosomal Theory, and Linkage

4.1 Dihybrid Cross and Independent Assortment

When Mendel crossed pea plants differing in two traits—round yellow seeds (RRYY) × wrinkled green seeds (rryy)—all F1 seeds were round and yellow (RrYy). Selfing the F1 produced four phenotypic classes in F2 in a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio. Mathematically, this is the product of two independent monohybrid ratios:

\[ (3\text{ Round} : 1\text{ Wrinkled}) \times (3\text{ Yellow} : 1\text{ Green}) = 9\text{ Round Yellow} : 3\text{ Round Green} : 3\text{ Wrinkled Yellow} : 1\text{ Wrinkled Green} \]

This led to the Law of Independent Assortment: when two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, the segregation of one pair of alleles is independent of the other pair. For an individual heterozygous at \(n\) independently assorting loci, the number of distinct gamete types is \(2^n\), and the number of F2 genotypes upon selfing is \(3^n\) (for \(n = 2\): 4 gamete types and 9 genotypes in a \(1:2:1:2:4:2:1:2:1\) ratio).

4.2 Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance

Mendel's 1865 paper remained unrecognized until 1900, when de Vries, Correns, and von Tschermak independently rediscovered his laws. In 1902, Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri noted that chromosomes—like Mendelian factors—occur in homologous pairs, segregate during meiosis so each gamete receives one member of each pair, and assort independently during Metaphase I / Anaphase I alignment. Sutton unified chromosomal behaviour with Mendelian principles as the Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance.

4.3 Linkage, Recombination, and Gene Mapping

Thomas Hunt Morgan experimentally verified the chromosomal theory using the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, chosen for its short (~2-week) life cycle, large progeny numbers, simple synthetic diet, clear sexual dimorphism, and easily visible hereditary variations.

In dihybrid crosses involving sex-linked genes on the X chromosome, Morgan observed that F2 ratios deviated sharply from 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, with parental combinations far exceeding non-parental combinations. He defined:

  • Linkage: The physical association of two or more genes located on the same chromosome, causing them to be inherited together.
  • Recombination: The generation of non-parental gene combinations via crossing over during meiosis.

Morgan found that linkage strength varies with physical distance: yellow (y) and white (w) are tightly linked (98.7% parental, only 1.3% recombination), whereas white (w) and miniature wing (m) are loosely linked (62.8% parental, 37.2% recombination). Morgan's student Alfred Sturtevant used recombination frequency as a measure of distance between genes to construct the first genetic linkage maps (1% recombination = 1 map unit or centimorgan, cM).

5. Polygenic Inheritance and Sex Determination

5.1 Polygenic Inheritance

Traits showing continuous variation across a gradient—such as human height or human skin colour—are polygenic traits controlled by three or more genes and influenced by the environment. Each dominant allele contributes an equal, additive increment to the phenotype. Modeling human skin colour with three loci (A, B, C), AABBCC produces the darkest pigmentation, aabbcc produces the lightest, and AaBbCc (three dominant and three recessive alleles) produces an intermediate tone.

5.2 Chromosomal and Haplodiploid Sex Determination

Sex chromosomes (allosomes) differ between sexes, whereas autosomes are identical in both sexes. Tracing back to Henking's 1891 discovery of the "X body" in insect spermatogenesis, four major genetic mechanisms are tested in NEET UG:

Mechanism Female Male Heterogametic Sex Examples

In humans (22 pairs of autosomes + 1 pair of sex chromosomes), every ovum carries an X chromosome, while 50% of sperm carry X and 50% carry Y. Therefore, the sperm's genetic makeup determines the sex of the child, with an equal 50% probability of a boy or girl in each pregnancy. In honey bees, fertilised eggs develop into diploid females (queen or worker, \(2n = 32\)), whereas unfertilised eggs develop via parthenogenesis into haploid males (drones, \(n = 16\)). Drones produce sperm by mitosis, have no father and cannot have sons, but have a grandfather and can have grandsons.

6. Mutation, Pedigree Analysis, and Genetic Disorders

6.1 Mutations

Mutation is an alteration of DNA sequences that modifies genotype and phenotype. Loss (deletion) or gain (insertion/duplication) of DNA segments alters chromosomes (chromosomal aberrations, frequently seen in cancer cells). A change in a single base pair is a point mutation (e.g., sickle-cell anaemia), whereas insertions or deletions of base pairs cause frameshift mutations. Physical or chemical agents that induce mutations (such as UV radiation) are called mutagens.

6.2 Pedigree Analysis

Because controlled matings cannot be performed in humans, pedigree analysis traces a trait across multiple generations of a family tree using standardized symbols.

6.3 Mendelian Disorders (Single-Gene Mutations)

Disorder Inheritance Molecular / Genetic Basis & Key Features
Colour Blindness X-linked recessive Defect in red or green cone cells of the eye; affects ~8% of males vs. ~0.4% of females. Carrier mother transmits to 50% of sons.
Haemophilia X-linked recessive Defect in a single protein of the blood-clotting cascade, causing prolonged bleeding from minor cuts. Transmitted from unaffected carrier female to sons; affected females are extremely rare.
Sickle-cell Anaemia Autosomal recessive Point mutation in β-globin gene (GAG → GUG) substituting Glutamic acid (Glu) with Valine (Val) at the 6th position. Polymerisation under low O2 tension deforms RBCs into sickle shapes (qualitative defect).
Phenylketonuria (PKU) Autosomal recessive Lack of enzyme converting phenylalanine to tyrosine; phenylalanine accumulates as phenylpyruvic acid, causing mental retardation and urinary excretion.
Thalassemia Autosomal recessive Reduced synthesis rate of α-globin (controlled by HBA1 and HBA2 on chromosome 16; 4 alleles) or β-globin (controlled by HBB on chromosome 11; 2 alleles). A quantitative defect.

6.4 Chromosomal Disorders

Chromosomal disorders arise from aneuploidy (gain or loss of one or more chromosomes due to failure of chromatid segregation during cell division) or polyploidy (gain of a whole chromosome set due to failure of cytokinesis after telophase, common in plants).

  • Down's Syndrome (47, +21): Trisomy of chromosome 21. Features include short stature, small round head, furrowed tongue, partially open mouth, broad palm with characteristic palm crease, congenital heart defects, and retarded physical, psychomotor, and mental development.
  • Klinefelter's Syndrome (47, XXY): Extra X chromosome in a male. Overall masculine development with feminine features such as breast development (gynaecomastia); individuals are sterile.
  • Turner's Syndrome (45, XO): Monosomy of X chromosome in a female. Short stature, rudimentary ovaries (sterile), and underdeveloped secondary sexual characters.

7. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 • Multi-Locus Cross Probability (Assumed Scenario)

Problem: Suppose a botanist crosses two garden pea plants with genotypes \(AaBbCc \times AaBbcc\), where all three loci assort independently. Assuming complete dominance at each locus, what fraction of the progeny will have the genotype \(AABbcc\)?

Solution:

  1. Treat each independently assorting gene separately:
    • Cross \(Aa \times Aa\): Probability of \(AA = \frac{1}{4}\).
    • Cross \(Bb \times Bb\): Probability of \(Bb = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}\).
    • Cross \(Cc \times cc\): Probability of \(cc = \frac{1}{2}\).
  2. Multiply the independent probabilities: \[ P(AABbcc) = \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{16} \]

Answer: \(\frac{1}{16}\) (or 6.25%).

Worked Example 2 • ABO Blood Group Inheritance (Assumed Scenario)

Problem: In a hypothetical family, a father has blood group A and a mother has blood group B. Their first child has blood group O. Determine the genotypes of the parents and the probability that their second child will have blood group AB.

Solution:

  1. A child with blood group O has genotype \(ii\) and must receive one \(i\) allele from each parent.
  2. Since the father is blood group A and carries \(i\), his genotype is \(I^Ai\). Since the mother is blood group B and carries \(i\), her genotype is \(I^Bi\).
  3. Crossing \(I^Ai \times I^Bi\) yields four equally likely genotypes: \(I^AI^B\) (AB), \(I^Ai\) (A), \(I^Bi\) (B), and \(ii\) (O).

Answer: Parental genotypes are \(I^Ai\) and \(I^Bi\); the probability of the second child having blood group AB is \(\frac{1}{4}\) (25%).

Worked Example 3 • Linkage and Recombination Frequency (Assumed Scenario)

Problem: In a hypothetical test cross of a dihybrid insect heterozygous for two X-linked genes P and Q (\(P^+Q^+ / pq\)), 1,000 progeny are scored: 440 are \(P^+Q^+\), 440 are \(pq\), 60 are \(P^+q\), and 60 are \(pQ^+\). Calculate the recombination frequency and map distance between P and Q.

Solution:

  1. Identify recombinant progeny (non-parental combinations): \(60 + 60 = 120\).
  2. Total progeny = \(440 + 440 + 60 + 60 = 1000\).
  3. Recombination frequency: \[ \text{RF} = \frac{120}{1000} \times 100\% = 12\% \]

Answer: 12% recombination frequency, corresponding to a genetic distance of 12 map units (centimorgans).

Worked Example 4 • X-Linked Recessive Pedigree Risk (Assumed Scenario)

Problem: A phenotypically normal couple has a son affected with haemophilia (an X-linked recessive disorder). What is the probability that their next child will be an affected son?

Solution:

  1. An affected son has genotype \(X^hY\) and inherits his X chromosome from his mother. Since the mother is phenotypically normal, she must be a carrier (\(X^HX^h\)).
  2. The normal father has genotype \(X^HY\).
  3. From \(X^HX^h \times X^HY\), the possible offspring genotypes are \(X^HX^H\) (normal daughter), \(X^HX^h\) (carrier daughter), \(X^HY\) (normal son), and \(X^hY\) (affected son), each with probability \(\frac{1}{4}\).

Answer: \(\frac{1}{4}\) (25% of all pregnancies; note that among sons specifically, it is \(\frac{1}{2}\)).

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Solve the following original practice questions under exam conditions, then verify your reasoning against the worked solutions.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A diploid plant is heterozygous at 3 independently assorting loci (AaBbCc) and homozygous at 2 loci (DDee). How many genetically distinct types of gametes can it produce?

  • (A) 4
  • (B) 8
  • (C) 16
  • (D) 32
Answer: (B) 8

Worked Solution: Only heterozygous loci contribute to gamete variety. Using \(2^n\) where \(n\) is the number of heterozygous loci (\(n = 3\) for Aa, Bb, and Cc; DD and ee each produce only one allele type), the number of gamete types is \(2^3 = 8\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In snapdragon plants, red flower colour (RR) shows incomplete dominance over white flower colour (rr). If a pink-flowered plant is crossed with a white-flowered plant, what will be the phenotypic ratio of the progeny?

  • (A) 3 Red : 1 White
  • (B) 1 Red : 2 Pink : 1 White
  • (C) 1 Pink : 1 White
  • (D) All Pink
Answer: (C) 1 Pink : 1 White

Worked Solution: Crossing pink (Rr) with white (rr) yields gametes R and r from the pink parent and only r from the white parent. Progeny genotypes are 50% Rr (Pink) and 50% rr (White), giving a 1 : 1 ratio.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which statement accurately distinguishes thalassemia from sickle-cell anaemia?

  • (A) Thalassemia is X-linked recessive, whereas sickle-cell anaemia is autosomal recessive.
  • (B) Thalassemia is a quantitative problem of synthesising too few globin chains, whereas sickle-cell anaemia is a qualitative problem of synthesising an incorrectly functioning globin.
  • (C) Both disorders result from a single base substitution changing glutamic acid to valine.
  • (D) β-thalassemia is controlled by four alleles on chromosome 16.
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Both are autosomal recessive blood disorders. Sickle-cell anaemia is a qualitative defect (Glu → Val at position 6 of β-globin), whereas thalassemia is a quantitative defect (reduced synthesis of α-globin controlled by HBA1/HBA2 on chromosome 16 or β-globin controlled by HBB on chromosome 11).

Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): In birds, the female is the heterogametic sex.
Reason (R): Female birds possess one Z and one W chromosome, producing two types of ova with respect to sex chromosomes.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Birds exhibit ZZ–ZW sex determination, where males are homogametic (ZZ) and females are heterogametic (ZW), producing Z-bearing and W-bearing ova in equal proportions. Thus, (R) directly explains (A).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a test cross involving pea plants, 50% of the offspring show violet flowers and 50% show white flowers. Given that violet (V) is dominant over white (v), what is the genotype of the violet-flowered parent?

  • (A) VV
  • (B) Vv
  • (C) vv
  • (D) Cannot be determined
Answer: (B) Vv

Worked Solution: A test cross mates the dominant-phenotype parent with a homozygous recessive parent (vv). Obtaining a 1 : 1 ratio (50% violet Vv : 50% white vv) proves the tested violet parent produced both V and v gametes and is therefore heterozygous (Vv).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following is an example of a pleiotropic gene in humans?

  • (A) Gene I controlling ABO blood groups
  • (B) Genes A, B, and C controlling skin colour
  • (C) Gene encoding phenylalanine hydroxylase in phenylketonuria
  • (D) Gene for red-green colour blindness on the X chromosome
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: A pleiotropic gene produces multiple phenotypic effects from a single gene mutation. In PKU, mutation of the phenylalanine hydroxylase gene causes mental retardation as well as reduced hair and skin pigmentation. ABO blood group illustrates multiple alleles/co-dominance, while skin colour illustrates polygenic inheritance.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In Morgan's Drosophila experiments, two X-linked genes A and B showed 8% recombination, whereas genes A and C showed 28% recombination. Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Genes A and C are more tightly linked than genes A and B.
  • (B) Genes A and B are located closer together on the chromosome than genes A and C.
  • (C) Genes A and B assort completely independently.
  • (D) Crossing over occurs more frequently between A and B than between A and C.
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Recombination frequency is directly proportional to physical distance between linked genes and inversely proportional to linkage strength. Since 8% < 28%, genes A and B are closer together (8 map units) and more tightly linked than A and C (28 map units).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two phenotypically normal parents have three children: one unaffected son, one unaffected daughter, and one daughter affected with an inherited metabolic disorder. What is the most likely mode of inheritance of this disorder?

  • (A) Autosomal dominant
  • (B) Autosomal recessive
  • (C) X-linked dominant
  • (D) Y-linked
Answer: (B) Autosomal recessive

Worked Solution: Unaffected parents having an affected child rules out dominant inheritance (trait skips a generation, so it is recessive; both parents are heterozygous carriers). Because a daughter is affected while her father is phenotypically normal, it cannot be X-linked recessive (an affected X-linked recessive daughter requires an affected father) or Y-linked. Hence, it is autosomal recessive.

9. Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Confusing Incomplete Dominance and Co-dominance: Incomplete dominance gives an intermediate phenotype (pink = between red and white), whereas co-dominance expresses both parental phenotypes simultaneously (AB blood group has both A and B sugars).
  • Polygenic vs. Pleiotropic: Polygenic = many genes → 1 trait (e.g., skin colour); Pleiotropic = 1 gene → many traits (e.g., PKU).
  • Thalassemia Chromosome Numbers: α-thalassemia is on chromosome 16 (2 linked genes HBA1, HBA2 = 4 alleles); β-thalassemia is on chromosome 11 (1 gene HBB = 2 alleles).
  • Sickle-cell Codon Change: mRNA codon changes from GAG → GUG (Glu → Val at position 6 of β-globin), not GUG → GAG.
  • Gamete Formation in Honey Bee Drones: Drones are haploid and form sperm by mitosis, while queens are diploid and form eggs by meiosis.

10. Concise Final Recap

Mendel established that traits are governed by paired discrete factors (genes) that segregate cleanly during gamete formation (Law of Segregation) and assort independently when on separate chromosomes (Law of Independent Assortment). Extensions such as incomplete dominance, co-dominance, multiple alleles, pleiotropy, and polygenic inheritance explain richer phenotypic patterns without violating allelic segregation. Sutton and Boveri linked genes to chromosomes, and Morgan's Drosophila crosses revealed linkage and recombination-based genetic mapping. Finally, understanding sex-determination systems, pedigree logic, and the molecular/chromosomal basis of Mendelian and aneuploid disorders equips you to solve both conceptual and quantitative NEET UG genetics questions with confidence.
