Human Health and Disease

Comprehensive Conceptual Guide, Diagnostic & Immunological Mechanisms, and NEET UG Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

Understanding human health requires connecting microbiology, immunology, oncology, and neuropharmacology. For NEET UG, this topic is a high-weightage scoring unit that tests precise pathogen–vector–symptom associations, the cellular basis of immunity, retroviral replication, tumor biology, and the physiological effects of psychoactive substances.

Core Competencies Targeted
  • Concept of Health & Disease: Differentiate the modern biopsychosocial definition of health from historical humoral theories, and classify diseases into infectious and non-infectious categories.
  • Human Pathogens & Life Cycles: Identify causative agents, transmission routes, target organs, diagnostic tests, and preventive strategies for major bacterial, viral, protozoan, helminthic, and fungal infections.
  • Immunity & Lymphoid Architecture: Distinguish innate barriers from adaptive (humoral and cell-mediated) responses, active vs. passive immunisation, allergies, auto-immunity, and primary vs. secondary lymphoid organs.
  • HIV/AIDS & Cancer: Trace retroviral replication in macrophages and helper T-cells, and contrast benign vs. malignant tumors, carcinogens, diagnostic imaging, and cancer therapies.
  • Substance Abuse: Map plant sources, receptor targets, and systemic effects of opioids, cannabinoids, coca alkaloids, hallucinogens, tobacco, alcohol, and anabolic steroids.

2. Defining Health and Factors Affecting Well-Being

Health is not merely the absence of physical illness or good muscular fitness. It is defined as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being. A healthy population exhibits higher work efficiency, greater economic productivity, increased life expectancy, and reduced infant and maternal mortality.

Historically, early Greek physicians such as Hippocrates and practitioners of the Indian Ayurveda system proposed the ‘good humor’ hypothesis, suggesting that health depended on a balance of bodily fluids (‘humors’), and that excess ‘blackbile’ caused hot personalities and fevers. This purely speculative model was overturned when William Harvey experimentally demonstrated blood circulation and thermometer measurements confirmed normal body temperatures in individuals thought to possess blackbile.

Modern biology recognizes that the mind communicates with the immune system via the neural and endocrine systems; consequently, mental stress directly influences immune competence. Overall human health is governed by three primary determinants:

  • Genetic disorders: Congenital deficiencies or inherited defects present from birth.
  • Infections: Invasion and multiplication of disease-causing organisms (pathogens).
  • Lifestyle: Nutritional quality of food and water, rest, physical exercise (including yoga), and personal habits.

When one or more organs or organ systems malfunction—producing observable signs and symptoms—the body is in a state of disease. Diseases are broadly divided into infectious diseases (easily transmitted from person to person, e.g., typhoid, AIDS) and non-infectious diseases (not transmitted between individuals, e.g., cancer, diabetes).

3. Common Infectious Diseases in Humans

Organisms across diverse taxonomic groups—bacteria, viruses, protozoans, helminths, and fungi—act as pathogens. To survive, pathogens must adapt to the host's internal microenvironment; for instance, enteric pathogens must withstand gastric acid (low pH) and digestive enzymes in the gastrointestinal tract.

3.2 Malaria and the Two-Host Life Cycle of Plasmodium

Malaria is caused by the protozoan parasite Plasmodium. Different species—P. vivax, P. malaria, and P. falciparum—produce distinct clinical forms. Among these, malignant malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum is the most severe and can be fatal if untreated.

Plasmodium requires two hosts to complete its life cycle: humans (where asexual reproduction occurs) and the female Anopheles mosquito (which acts as both the definitive host for sexual reproduction and the transmitting vector):

  1. Infection of Human Host: When an infected female Anopheles mosquito bites a human, it injects sporozoites (the infectious stage) along with saliva.
  2. Hepatic Phase: Sporozoites travel through the bloodstream to the liver, where they multiply asexually inside liver cells (hepatocytes) and rupture them.
  3. Erythrocytic Phase & Haemozoin Release: Parasites released from the liver invade red blood cells (RBCs) and multiply asexually, causing periodic rupture of RBCs. Ruptured RBCs release a toxic pigment called haemozoin, which triggers the characteristic chill and high fever recurring every 3 to 4 days.
  4. Gametocyte Formation: Some parasites within RBCs differentiate into sexual stages—male and female gametocytes.
  5. Mosquito Phase: When another female Anopheles mosquito takes a blood meal from the infected human, it ingests the gametocytes. Fertilization and further development occur inside the mosquito's gut. Mature infective sporozoites eventually migrate to and are stored in the mosquito's salivary glands, ready for transmission.

3.3 Prevention and Public Health Control

  • Personal & Public Hygiene (Water/Food-borne diseases): Clean drinking water, washed fruits/vegetables, proper waste and excreta disposal, periodic cleaning/disinfection of water reservoirs and tanks (crucial for typhoid, amoebiasis, ascariasis).
  • Air-borne Precautions: Avoiding close contact with infected persons or their belongings (pneumonia, common cold).
  • Vector Control (Malaria, Filariasis, Dengue, Chikungunya): Eliminate stagnant water, clean household coolers regularly, use mosquito nets and wire-mesh screens, spray insecticides in ditches/swamps, and introduce larvivorous fishes such as Gambusia in ponds. Note that dengue and chikungunya are transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes.
  • Vaccines & Antibiotics: Immunisation has completely eradicated smallpox and controlled polio, diphtheria, pneumonia, and tetanus.

4. Immunity, Vaccination, Allergies, and Lymphoid Organs

Immunity is the overall capacity of the host's immune system to recognize and defend against disease-causing agents. It operates through two major branches: Innate Immunity and Acquired Immunity.

4.1 Innate Immunity (Non-Specific, Present from Birth)

Innate immunity provides immediate, non-specific defence via four barrier types:

  1. Physical barriers: Skin prevents microbial entry; mucus coating of the epithelium lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital tracts traps microbes.
  2. Physiological barriers: Gastric acid (low pH in stomach), saliva in the mouth, and tears from eyes inhibit microbial growth.
  3. Cellular barriers: Leukocytes in blood—specifically polymorpho-nuclear leukocytes (PMNL-neutrophils), monocytes, and natural killer (NK) cells (a type of lymphocyte)—along with tissue macrophages phagocytose and destroy microbes.
  4. Cytokine barriers: Virus-infected cells secrete protective proteins called interferons, which shield neighbouring non-infected cells from further viral infection.

4.2 Acquired Immunity (Pathogen-Specific, Memory-Based)

Acquired immunity is highly specific to particular pathogens and exhibits immunological memory:

  • Primary response: Occurs upon first encounter with a pathogen; it is of low intensity and slow to develop.
  • Secondary (anamnestic) response: Occurs upon subsequent encounter with the same pathogen; it is rapid and highly intensified due to memory B- and T-lymphocytes retained from the first exposure.
Tutorial Diagram 2: Comparison of primary and secondary (anamnestic) antibody responses illustrating immunological memory.

Acquired immunity is executed by two lymphocyte populations in blood:

  • Humoral Immune Response (Antibody-Mediated): B-lymphocytes produce an army of specialized proteins called antibodies (immunoglobulins: IgA, IgM, IgE, IgG) into the blood to neutralize pathogens. T-lymphocytes do not secrete antibodies themselves, but helper T-cells assist B-cells in producing them.
  • Cell-Mediated Immune Response (CMI): Mediated directly by T-lymphocytes. CMI enables the body to distinguish ‘self’ from ‘non-self’ and is primarily responsible for organ/graft rejection. Therefore, tissue matching and blood group matching are mandatory before organ transplantation, and recipients must take lifelong immunosuppressants.

Structure of an Antibody Molecule

Each antibody monomer consists of four polypeptide chains: two shorter light (L) chains and two longer heavy (H) chains, represented structurally as \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{L}_2\). The chains are linked together by inter-chain and intra-chain disulfide bonds (\(\text{–S–S–}\)), forming a Y-shaped molecule with two identical antigen-binding sites at the N-terminal tips.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Schematic representation of an \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{L}_2\) antibody molecule showing two heavy chains, two light chains, disulfide linkages, and two antigen-binding sites.

4.3 Active vs. Passive Immunity and Vaccination

Feature Active Immunity Passive Immunity
Mechanism Host is exposed to antigens (living/dead microbes or proteins); host's own immune system produces antibodies. Ready-made (preformed) antibodies are directly administered to protect the host.
Speed & Memory Slow to develop full response; generates immunological memory (memory B- and T-cells). Provides immediate protection; no immunological memory is generated.
Examples Natural infection; deliberate immunisation with vaccines (e.g., recombinant Hepatitis B vaccine produced in yeast). Colostrum (yellowish initial breast milk rich in IgA); maternal antibodies crossing the placenta to the foetus; antitoxin for tetanus and antivenom for snakebite.

Vaccination relies on immunological memory: introducing antigenic proteins or inactivated/weakened pathogens stimulates memory B- and T-cell formation without causing full-blown disease. Modern recombinant DNA technology enables large-scale synthesis of antigenic polypeptides in bacteria or yeast (such as the Hepatitis B vaccine).

4.4 Allergies and Auto-Immunity

  • Allergy: An exaggerated immune response to environmental antigens called allergens (e.g., dust mites, pollen, animal dander). It is mediated by IgE antibodies, which trigger mast cells to release histamine and serotonin, causing sneezing, watery eyes, running nose, and breathing difficulty. Symptoms are rapidly relieved by antihistamines, adrenaline, and steroids.
  • Auto-immunity: Memory-based acquired immunity evolved alongside self/non-self discrimination. Occasionally, due to genetic or unknown factors, the immune system attacks self-cells, damaging body tissues (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis).

4.5 Lymphoid Organs of the Human Immune System

  • Primary Lymphoid Organs: Sites where immature lymphocytes originate and/or differentiate into antigen-sensitive lymphocytes.
    • Bone marrow: Main lymphoid organ where all blood cells, including lymphocytes, are produced.
    • Thymus: Lobed organ near the heart beneath the breastbone; large at birth, progressively shrinks with age, and becomes very small by puberty. Both bone marrow and thymus provide microenvironments for T-lymphocyte development and maturation.
  • Secondary Lymphoid Organs: Sites where mature lymphocytes interact with antigens and proliferate into effector cells. Examples include spleen (large bean-shaped blood filter trapping blood-borne microbes; also a major reservoir of erythrocytes), lymph nodes (trap antigens from lymph/tissue fluid), tonsils, Peyer's patches of the small intestine, and appendix.
  • MALT (Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue): Lymphoid tissue lining the respiratory, digestive, and urogenital tracts; constitutes approximately 50% of total human lymphoid tissue.

5. AIDS and Cancer: Cellular Mechanisms and Diagnostics

5.1 AIDS (Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome)

First reported in 1981, AIDS is an acquired (non-congenital) syndrome caused by the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), an enveloped retrovirus containing an RNA genome.

Routes of Transmission: (a) sexual contact with an infected person, (b) transfusion of contaminated blood/blood products, (c) sharing infected needles (intravenous drug users), and (d) from infected mother to child across the placenta. HIV does not spread by mere touch or casual physical contact—it spreads strictly through body fluids. The incubation period between infection and symptom onset typically spans 5–10 years.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Mechanism of retroviral (HIV) replication; macrophages survive while continuously producing virions ("HIV factory").

Pathogenesis: HIV enters macrophages, where its RNA genome is converted into viral DNA by reverse transcriptase. The viral DNA integrates into the host genome and directs virion assembly. Macrophages continue to survive and act as an HIV factory. Simultaneously, HIV infects helper T-lymphocytes (\(\mathrm{T_H}\)), causing a progressive decline in \(\mathrm{T_H}\) count. The resulting immunodeficiency leads to bouts of fever, diarrhoea, weight loss, and opportunistic infections by Mycobacterium, viruses, fungi, and parasites like Toxoplasma.

Diagnosis, Treatment & Prevention: Diagnosed widely via ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immuno-Sorbent Assay). Anti-retroviral drugs only prolong life and cannot cure AIDS. Prevention—spearheaded in India by NACO (National AIDS Control Organisation) and WHO—focuses on blood-bank screening, disposable needles/syringes, safe sex, condom distribution, and de-addiction.

5.2 Cancer: Loss of Regulation and Clinical Management

Normal cells exhibit contact inhibition, halting division upon contact with neighbouring cells. Cancer cells lose contact inhibition and divide uncontrollably to form cellular masses called tumors:

  • Benign tumors: Remain confined to their site of origin, do not spread, and cause minimal damage.
  • Malignant tumors: Rapidly proliferating neoplastic cells that invade and damage surrounding tissues and starve normal cells by competing for nutrients. Cells sloughed from malignant tumors travel via blood to distant sites and establish secondary tumors—a feared property called metastasis.

Carcinogens: Agents inducing neoplastic transformation include physical agents (ionising X-rays, gamma rays; non-ionising UV rays causing DNA damage), chemical agents (tobacco smoke carcinogens causing lung cancer), and biological agents (oncogenic viruses carrying viral oncogenes). Normal cells also possess cellular oncogenes (c-onc) or proto-oncogenes that can trigger cancer if inappropriately activated.

Detection & Treatment:

  • Detection: Biopsy and histopathological tissue examination; blood/bone marrow counts for leukemias; Radiography (X-rays) and CT (Computed Tomography, 3D X-ray imaging); MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging, using strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiation); cancer-specific antibodies; and molecular genetic screening for inherited susceptibility.
  • Treatment: Combination of surgery, radiotherapy (lethal irradiation of tumor while sparing normal tissue), chemotherapy (drugs with side effects like hair loss, anemia), and immunotherapy using biological response modifiers such as \(\alpha\)-interferon to activate the immune system against tumor cells.

6. Drugs and Alcohol Abuse

Adolescence (ages 12–18 years) is a vulnerable transitional phase marked by curiosity, peer pressure, academic stress, and media influence. 6.1 Addiction, Dependence, and Performance-Enhancing Abuse

  • Addiction: Psychological attachment to temporary euphoria/well-being. Repeated exposure increases receptor tolerance, demanding higher doses and trapping the user in a self-destructive cycle.
  • Dependence & Withdrawal Syndrome: Tendency of the body to exhibit unpleasant physical symptoms—anxiety, shakiness, nausea, and sweating—when regular intake is abruptly stopped.
  • Severe Systemic Risks: Overdose can cause coma and death via respiratory failure, heart failure, or cerebral haemorrhage. Intravenous users sharing needles risk AIDS and Hepatitis B. Chronic alcohol/drug abuse damages the nervous system and liver (cirrhosis) and harms the foetus during pregnancy.
  • Anabolic Steroid Abuse in Sports: Sportspersons sometimes misuse narcotic analgesics, anabolic steroids, diuretics, and hormones.
    • Side effects in females: Masculinisation, increased aggressiveness, mood swings, depression, abnormal menstrual cycles, excessive facial/body hair, clitoral enlargement, deepening of voice.
    • Side effects in males: Acne, aggressiveness, mood swings, depression, reduced testicle size, decreased sperm production, kidney/liver dysfunction, breast enlargement, premature baldness, prostate enlargement.
    • In adolescents (both sexes): Severe acne and premature closure of growth centres of long bones, causing stunted growth.

7. Worked Examples (Original NEET-Style Reasoning)

Worked Example 1: Evaluating Immunological Memory in Vaccination

Scenario (Hypothetical): A researcher administers a purified recombinant viral polypeptide antigen (Dose 1) to a healthy volunteer on Day 0, followed by a booster dose of the same antigen (Dose 2) on Day 28. On Day 35, serum antibody titres are measured to be 40-fold higher than the peak measured on Day 14. Identify the immune cell populations responsible for this 40-fold surge and classify the type of immunity conferred.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Dose 1 triggers a primary immune response, which is slow and of low intensity (peaking modestly around Day 14), while generating antigen-specific memory B- and T-lymphocytes.
  2. Dose 2 triggers a secondary (anamnestic) response, in which memory B-cells rapidly proliferate and differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells with help from memory helper T-cells, producing the 40-fold surge by Day 35.
  3. Because the host's own immune system synthesized the antibodies upon antigenic stimulation, this represents artificially acquired active immunity.
Worked Example 2: Distinguishing Diagnostic & Therapeutic Modalities

Scenario (Hypothetical): A patient presents with a painless, rapidly enlarging mass in the lung, and subsequent imaging reveals secondary nodules in the liver. A clinician orders an MRI instead of repeated CT scans for soft-tissue evaluation, followed by a biopsy and \(\alpha\)-interferon administration. Explain the biological rationale for each clinical choice.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Secondary nodules in the liver: Indicate metastasis, confirming that the primary lung tumor is malignant rather than benign.
  2. Choice of MRI over CT: CT uses X-rays (ionising radiation), whereas MRI uses strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiation to detect pathological and physiological changes in living tissue safely.
  3. Biopsy: Thin stained sections of suspected tissue are examined histopathologically under a microscope to confirm neoplastic cellular morphology.
  4. \(\alpha\)-interferon: Acts as a biological response modifier (immunotherapy) that activates the patient's immune system to recognize and destroy tumor cells that otherwise evade immune surveillance.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following pairs correctly matches a human disease with its diagnostic test and causative pathogen?

  • (A) Amoebiasis — Widal test — Entamoeba histolytica
  • (B) Typhoid — Widal test — Salmonella typhi
  • (C) AIDS — Biopsy — Human Immunodeficiency Virus
  • (D) Pneumonia — ELISA — Streptococcus pneumoniae
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Typhoid fever is caused by the bacterium Salmonella typhi and is confirmed by the Widal test. AIDS is diagnosed using ELISA, whereas biopsy/histopathology is used for cancer detection.
Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

During the life cycle of Plasmodium falciparum, the toxic substance responsible for recurring chills and high fever every 3 to 4 days is released from:

  • (A) Ruptured hepatocytes when sporozoites multiply
  • (B) Salivary glands of the female Anopheles mosquito
  • (C) Ruptured red blood cells (RBCs) as haemozoin

Answer: (C)
Explanation: After multiplying asexually in the liver, Plasmodium attacks RBCs. Rupture of RBCs releases the toxic pigment haemozoin, which induces chills and high fever recurring every 3–4 days.

9. Common NEET UG Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Common Cold vs. Pneumonia: Rhino viruses infect the nose and respiratory passage only—never the alveoli. Alveolar fluid accumulation occurs in bacterial pneumonia (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae).
  • Plasmodium Infectious Stages: Sporozoites are infectious to humans (injected by mosquito); gametocytes are taken up by the mosquito from human blood. Do not swap these two stages!
  • CT vs. MRI Radiation: CT uses X-rays (ionising), whereas MRI uses strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiations.
  • Heroin vs. Morphine: Heroin (smack) is diacetylmorphine, obtained by acetylation of morphine (extracted from Papaver somniferum latex).
  • Colostrum Antibody Class: Colostrum is rich in IgA (not IgE; IgE is for allergies).

Quick Revision Checklist

  • ✓ Widal test = Typhoid (Salmonella typhi); ELISA = AIDS (HIV); Biopsy = Cancer.
  • ✓ Gambusia fish = Biological control of mosquito larvae (prevents malaria, filariasis, dengue, chikungunya).
  • ✓ MALT = 50% of human lymphoid tissue; Thymus shrinks by puberty.
  • ✓ Antibody formula = \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{L}_2\) (2 heavy + 2 light chains joined by disulfide bonds).
  • ✓ \(\alpha\)-interferon = Biological response modifier used in cancer immunotherapy.
  • ✓ Cannabis sativa = Cannabinoids; Erythroxylum coca = Cocaine; Papaver somniferum = Opioids; Datura / Atropa belladonna = Hallucinogens.

10. Concise Final Recap

Human health is maintained by a dynamic interplay between lifestyle, environment, and the neuro-endocrine-immune axis. While infectious diseases are countered by hygiene, vector control, antibiotics, and memory-based vaccination, chronic conditions such as AIDS and cancer require early detection (ELISA, biopsy, MRI) and targeted intervention. Finally, safeguarding adolescents from the addictive cycle of opioids, cannabinoids, cocaine, tobacco, and steroids depends on education, supportive parenting, and timely medical counselling.
