NEET UG • Inorganic Chemistry

The d- and f-Block Elements

A complete, standalone exam-oriented tutorial covering electronic structure, periodic trends, redox stability, oxoanions, lanthanoids, actinoids, and NEET UG practice assessments.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Roadmap

In NEET UG Chemistry, the d- and f-block elements test both conceptual reasoning (why trends deviate at Cr, Mn, Cu, Zn, or Ce/Eu) and factual command over redox reactions of dichromate and permanganate. Mastering this unit equips you to:

  • Identify the periodic position and IUPAC definition of transition elements versus non-transition d-block elements.
  • Write ground-state and ionic electronic configurations across the 3d, 4d, and 5d series, including exchange-energy exceptions.
  • Rationalise trends in melting point, enthalpy of atomisation, atomic/ionic radii, ionisation enthalpy, and standard electrode potentials (\(E^\circ\)).
  • Predict magnetic moments, aqueous ion colours, catalytic roles, interstitial behaviour, and alloy formation.
  • Explain the preparation, pH equilibria, structures, and oxidising reactions of \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) and \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\).
  • Compare the 4f (lanthanoid) and 5f (actinoid) series in terms of contraction, oxidation states, and chemical reactivity.

2. Position, IUPAC Definition & Electronic Configurations

The d-block spans Groups 3 to 12 in the middle of the periodic table, bridging the electropositive s-block and electronegative p-block. Electrons progressively enter the penultimate \((n-1)d\) subshell across four rows:

  • 3d series (First transition series): \(\mathrm{Sc}\) (\(Z=21\)) to \(\mathrm{Zn}\) (\(Z=30\)).
  • 4d series (Second transition series): \(\mathrm{Y}\) (\(Z=39\)) to \(\mathrm{Cd}\) (\(Z=48\)).
  • 5d series (Third transition series): \(\mathrm{La}\) (\(Z=57\)) and \(\mathrm{Hf}\) (\(Z=72\)) to \(\mathrm{Hg}\) (\(Z=80\)).
  • 6d series (Fourth transition series): \(\mathrm{Ac}\) (\(Z=89\)) and \(\mathrm{Rf}\) (\(Z=104\)) to \(\mathrm{Cn}\) (\(Z=112\)).
IUPAC Definition of a Transition Element

A transition metal is an element that possesses an incompletely filled d subshell either in its neutral ground state or in any of its common oxidation states.

  • Why Group 12 (\(\mathrm{Zn}\), \(\mathrm{Cd}\), \(\mathrm{Hg}\), \(\mathrm{Cn}\)) are NOT transition elements: They have a full \((n-1)d^{10}ns^2\) configuration in the ground state and retain \(d^{10}\) in their stable \(+2\) oxidation state.
  • Why Coinage Metals (\(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Ag}\), \(\mathrm{Au}\)) ARE transition elements: Although their neutral atoms have \(d^{10}s^1\), their common ions such as \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) (\(3d^9\)), \(\mathrm{Ag^{2+}}\) (\(4d^9\)), and \(\mathrm{Au^{3+}}\) (\(5d^8\)) have partially filled d orbitals.
  • Why Scandium (\(\mathrm{Sc}\)) IS a transition element: Its common \(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}\) ion is \(3d^0\), but its neutral atom has \(3d^1 4s^2\) (an incomplete d subshell).

General Configuration & Key Exceptions

The general valence configuration of d-block elements is \((n-1)d^{1\text{–}10}ns^{1\text{–}2}\). Because the energy difference between \((n-1)d\) and \(ns\) orbitals is very small, extra stability associated with half-filled (\(d^5\)) and completely filled (\(d^{10}\)) subshells (driven by symmetrical distribution and maximum exchange energy) causes notable ground-state exceptions:

  • 3d series: \(\mathrm{Cr}\) (\(Z=24\)) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1\) (not \(3d^4 4s^2\)); \(\mathrm{Cu}\) (\(Z=29\)) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1\) (not \(3d^9 4s^2\)).
  • 4d series: \(\mathrm{Nb}\) (\(4d^4 5s^1\)), \(\mathrm{Mo}\) (\(4d^5 5s^1\)), \(\mathrm{Tc}\) (\(4d^5 5s^2\) or \(4d^6 5s^1\) depending on convention—note \(\mathrm{Ru}=4d^7 5s^1\), \(\mathrm{Rh}=4d^8 5s^1\), \(\mathrm{Ag}=4d^{10} 5s^1\)), and uniquely Palladium (\(\mathrm{Pd}\), \(Z=46\)) with \([\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10} 5s^0\).
  • 5d series: \(\mathrm{Pt}\) (\(Z=78\)) is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14} 5d^9 6s^1\); \(\mathrm{Au}\) (\(Z=79\)) is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14} 5d^{10} 6s^1\).

Crucial rule for ionisation: When a transition metal forms a cation, electrons are always removed first from the outermost \(ns\) orbital, and only then from \((n-1)d\). Thus, \(\mathrm{Fe}\) (\([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6 4s^2\)) forms \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) (\([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6\)) and \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) (\([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5\)).

3. General Physical & Atomic Trends of 3d Elements

3.1 Metallic Bonding, Melting Points & Enthalpies of Atomisation

Except for \(\mathrm{Zn}\), \(\mathrm{Cd}\), \(\mathrm{Hg}\), and \(\mathrm{Mn}\), transition metals adopt typical close-packed metallic lattices (hcp, ccp, or bcc), exhibit high tensile strength, ductility, malleability, and high thermal/electrical conductivity. Their high melting points and high enthalpies of atomisation (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}H^\circ\)) arise because both \(ns\) and unpaired \((n-1)d\) electrons participate in strong interatomic metallic bonding.

  • Mid-series peak: Melting points peak around Group 6 (\(\mathrm{Cr}\), \(\mathrm{Mo}\), \(\mathrm{W}\)) where each atom can contribute the maximum number of unpaired d electrons to metallic bonding.
  • Anomalous dip at \(\mathrm{Mn}\) and \(\mathrm{Tc}\): Although \(\mathrm{Mn}\) has five unpaired 3d electrons, its \(3d^5 4s^2\) subshell is half-filled and tightly held, reducing delocalisation into the metallic lattice. Consequently, \(\mathrm{Mn}\) has an unexpectedly low melting point and low \(\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}H^\circ\) (\(281\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  • Lowest values at Group 12: \(\mathrm{Zn}\) (\(126\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), \(\mathrm{Cd}\), and \(\mathrm{Hg}\) (liquid at room temperature) lack unpaired d electrons, so only \(ns\) electrons contribute to weak metallic bonding.
  • Heavier series (4d, 5d) vs. 3d: 4d and 5d metals have higher \(\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}H^\circ\) than 3d metals, explaining why metal–metal bonding is much more common in compounds of heavy transition metals.

3.2 Atomic & Ionic Radii and Lanthanoid Contraction

  • Horizontal trend: Across a given series (left to right), nuclear charge increases by \(+1\) per step while the incoming electron enters an inner \((n-1)d\) orbital. Because d electrons shield poorly, effective nuclear charge rises, causing a gradual decrease in atomic and ionic radii. Near the end (\(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Zn}\)), inter-electronic repulsion in filled \(d^{10}\) orbitals slightly expands the radius.
  • Vertical trend & Lanthanoid Contraction: Atomic radii increase from the 3d to the 4d series, as expected for an extra shell. However, the 4d and 5d series have virtually identical radii (for example, \(\mathrm{Zr} = 160\;\mathrm{pm}\) and \(\mathrm{Hf} = 159\;\mathrm{pm}\)). This occurs because the 14 elements of the 4f series (lanthanoids) intervene before the 5d series. Very poor shielding by 4f electrons causes a steady size decrease (lanthanoid contraction) that cancels the expected shell-expansion effect. Consequently, 4d/5d pairs like \(\mathrm{Zr}\)/\(\mathrm{Hf}\) and \(\mathrm{Nb}\)/\(\mathrm{Ta}\) share nearly identical chemical properties and occur together in nature.
  • Density: Decreasing metallic radius combined with increasing atomic mass drives a steep rise in density from \(\mathrm{Sc}\) (\(3.43\;\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)) to \(\mathrm{Cu}\) (\(8.9\;\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)).

3.3 Ionisation Enthalpies (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H\))

Ionisation enthalpies increase across the 3d series, but far less steeply than in main-group periods because inner 3d electrons partially screen the outer 4s electrons. Key NEET-tested irregularities:

  • Second Ionisation Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_2\)): Unusually high for \(\mathrm{Cr}\) (removing an electron from stable \(3d^5\) of \(\mathrm{Cr}^+\)) and \(\mathrm{Cu}\) (removing an electron from stable \(3d^{10}\) of \(\mathrm{Cu}^+\)). Thus, \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_2(\mathrm{Cr}) > \Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_2(\mathrm{Mn})\) and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_2(\mathrm{Cu}) > \Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_2(\mathrm{Zn})\).
  • Third Ionisation Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_3\)): Much higher for \(\mathrm{Mn}\) (disrupting stable \(3d^5\) of \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\)) than for \(\mathrm{Fe}\) (converting \(3d^6\) of \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) into stable \(3d^5\) of \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\)).

4. Oxidation States, Electrode Potentials & Redox Stability

Transition metals display variable oxidation states differing by unity (e.g., \(\mathrm{V^{II}}, \mathrm{V^{III}}, \mathrm{V^{IV}}, \mathrm{V^{V}}\)) because \((n-1)d\) and \(ns\) electrons have comparable energies. In contrast, p-block oxidation states typically differ by two units.

  • Maximum oxidation state: Increases from \(+3\) in \(\mathrm{Sc}\) to \(+7\) in \(\mathrm{Mn}\) (equal to the sum of \(4s + 3d\) electrons), seen in \(\mathrm{TiO_2}\), \(\mathrm{VO_2^+}\), \(\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}\), and \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\). Beyond \(\mathrm{Mn}\), pairing of d electrons limits higher states, making \(+2\) and \(+3\) dominant (\(\mathrm{Fe^{II,III}}\), \(\mathrm{Co^{II,III}}\), \(\mathrm{Ni^{II}}\), \(\mathrm{Cu^{I,II}}\), \(\mathrm{Zn^{II}}\)).
  • Group trend reversal vs. p-block: In d-block groups, heavier elements favour higher oxidation states. For instance, in Group 6, \(\mathrm{Mo(VI)}\) and \(\mathrm{W(VI)}\) are far more stable than \(\mathrm{Cr(VI)}\), which is why \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) is a strong oxidant while \(\mathrm{MoO_3}\) and \(\mathrm{WO_3}\) are not.
  • Zero oxidation state: Achieved with \(\pi\)-acceptor ligands such as \(\mathrm{CO}\) in \([\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}]\) and \([\mathrm{Fe(CO)_5}]\).

4.1 Trends in Standard Electrode Potentials (\(E^\circ\))

The \(E^\circ(\mathrm{M^{2+}/M})\) value reflects the net enthalpy of three steps: atomisation (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}H^\circ\)), first and second ionisation (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_1 + \Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_2\)), and hydration (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}}H^\circ\)).

  • Why Copper has a positive \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}) = +0.34\;\mathrm{V}\): The high energy required to atomise and ionise \(\mathrm{Cu}\) to \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) is not compensated by its hydration enthalpy. Hence, \(\mathrm{Cu}\) cannot displace \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from dilute non-oxidising acids; it dissolves only in oxidising acids (\(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) or hot conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)).
  • More negative \(E^\circ\) at \(\mathrm{Mn}\), \(\mathrm{Ni}\), and \(\mathrm{Zn}\): \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) (\(3d^5\)) and \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\) (\(3d^{10}\)) gain extra electronic stability, whereas \(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}\) has an exceptionally high negative \(\Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}}H^\circ\) (\(-2121\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{M^{3+}/M^{2+}}\) redox couples:
    • \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}/Mn^{2+}}\) (\(+1.57\;\mathrm{V}\)) and \(\mathrm{Co^{3+}/Co^{2+}}\) (\(+1.97\;\mathrm{V}\)) are strongly positive: \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Co^{3+}}\) are powerful oxidising agents in aqueous acid.
    • \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}/Cr^{2+}}\) (\(-0.41\;\mathrm{V}\)) is negative: \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) (\(d^4\)) is a strong reducing agent because oxidising to \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) (\(d^3\)) yields a stable half-filled \(t_{2g}^3\) configuration in aqueous octahedral complexes. Even though both \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) are \(d^4\), \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) is reducing (\(d^4 \to d^3\)) while \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) is oxidising (\(d^4 \to d^5\)).

4.2 Disproportionation & Stabilisation by Oxygen and Fluorine

  • Disproportionation of \(\mathrm{Cu^+}\): In aqueous solution, \(\mathrm{Cu^+}\) is unstable and disproportionates: \[2\mathrm{Cu^+}(\mathrm{aq}) \longrightarrow \mathrm{Cu^{2+}}(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{Cu}(\mathrm{s})\] This occurs because the much more negative hydration enthalpy of \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) overcompensates for the high second ionisation enthalpy of \(\mathrm{Cu}\). Likewise, \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) oxidises \(\mathrm{I^-}\) to \(\mathrm{I_2}\) (\(2\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + 4\mathrm{I^-} \to \mathrm{Cu_2I_2} + \mathrm{I_2}\)), so \(\mathrm{CuI_2}\) does not exist.
  • Why \(\mathrm{O}\) and \(\mathrm{F}\) stabilise highest oxidation states: Small size and high electronegativity. Oxygen surpasses fluorine (\(\mathrm{Mn_2O_7}\) reaches \(+7\), whereas the highest fluoride is \(\mathrm{MnF_4}\)) because oxygen can form \(p\pi\text{–}d\pi\) multiple bonds with metals.
  • Oxide acid–base trend: As oxidation number increases, covalent character and acidity increase: \(\mathrm{CrO}\) (basic) \(\to\) \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}\) (amphoteric) \(\to\) \(\mathrm{CrO_3}\) (acidic); \(\mathrm{V_2O_3}\) (basic) \(\to\) \(\mathrm{V_2O_4}\) (less basic) \(\to\) \(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\) (amphoteric, mainly acidic).

5. Characteristic Properties of Transition Metals

5.1 Magnetic Behaviour (Spin-Only Formula)

For first-row (3d) transition metal ions, orbital angular momentum is quenched by the surrounding ligand field. Thus, magnetic moment depends only on the number of unpaired electrons (\(n\)):

\[\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\;\mathrm{BM}\]

Here \(\mathrm{BM}\) stands for Bohr Magneton. Ions with \(n = 0\) (\(d^0\) or \(d^{10}\)) are diamagnetic; ions with \(n \ge 1\) are paramagnetic. Iron, cobalt, and nickel exhibit ferromagnetism in the metallic state.

5.2 Coloured Ions, Complexation, Catalysis, Interstitials & Alloys

  • Coloured ions: Partially filled d orbitals (\(d^1\text{–}d^9\)) allow d–d electronic transitions by absorbing visible light; the observed colour is complementary to the absorbed wavelength. Ions with \(d^0\) (\(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}, \mathrm{Ti^{4+}}\)) and \(d^{10}\) (\(\mathrm{Cu^+}, \mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\)) are colourless. Important exception: \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) (purple) and \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) (orange) have \(d^0\) metal centres; their intense colour arises from ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT).
  • Complex formation: Favoured by small ionic size, high nuclear charge, and availability of vacant \((n-1)d\) orbitals.
  • Catalytic activity: Transition metals adopt multiple oxidation states and provide surface sites for reactant adsorption, lowering activation energy. Key exam examples: \(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\) (Contact process for \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)), finely divided \(\mathrm{Fe}\) (Haber process for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)), \(\mathrm{Ni}\) (hydrogenation), \(\mathrm{TiCl_4 + Al(CH_3)_3}\) (Ziegler–Natta polymerisation), \(\mathrm{PdCl_2}\) (Wacker process), and \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) (catalysing \(2\mathrm{I^-} + \mathrm{S_2O_8^{2-}} \to \mathrm{I_2} + 2\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\)).
  • Interstitial compounds: Formed when small non-metal atoms (\(\mathrm{H}, \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{N}, \mathrm{B}\)) occupy interstitial voids of the metal lattice (e.g., \(\mathrm{TiC}\), \(\mathrm{Mn_4N}\), \(\mathrm{Fe_3H}\), \(\mathrm{VH_{0.56}}\)). They are non-stoichiometric, have higher melting points than the pure metals, are extremely hard, retain metallic conductivity, and are chemically inert.
  • Alloys: Metals with atomic radii within about \(15\%\) form homogeneous substitutional solid solutions (e.g., ferrous steels with \(\mathrm{Cr}, \mathrm{V}, \mathrm{W}, \mathrm{Mn}\); brass \(\mathrm{Cu\text{–}Zn}\); bronze \(\mathrm{Cu\text{–}Sn}\)).

6. Important Compounds: \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) & \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)

6.1 Potassium Dichromate (\(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\))

Preparation from chromite ore (\(\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4}\)):

  1. Roasting / Alkaline oxidative fusion: \[4\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4} + 8\mathrm{Na_2CO_3} + 7\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow 8\mathrm{Na_2CrO_4} + 2\mathrm{Fe_2O_3} + 8\mathrm{CO_2}\]
  2. Acidification of yellow sodium chromate: \[2\mathrm{Na_2CrO_4} + 2\mathrm{H^+} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Na_2Cr_2O_7} + 2\mathrm{Na^+} + \mathrm{H_2O}\]
  3. Conversion to potassium salt: \(\mathrm{Na_2Cr_2O_7}\) is treated with \(\mathrm{KCl}\); less soluble orange \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) crystallises out: \[\mathrm{Na_2Cr_2O_7} + 2\mathrm{KCl} \longrightarrow \mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7} + 2\mathrm{NaCl}\]

pH-dependent equilibrium (no change in \(+6\) oxidation state):

\[2\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}\;(\text{yellow}) + 2\mathrm{H^+} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\;(\text{orange}) + \mathrm{H_2O}\]

Oxidising action in acidic medium (\(E^\circ = +1.33\;\mathrm{V}\), \(n\text{-factor} = 6\)):

\[\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}} + 14\mathrm{H^+} + 6\mathrm{e^-} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{Cr^{3+}} + 7\mathrm{H_2O}\]

It oxidises \(\mathrm{I^-} \to \mathrm{I_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2S} \to \mathrm{S}\), \(\mathrm{Sn^{2+}} \to \mathrm{Sn^{4+}}\), and \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}} \to \mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\).

6.2 Potassium Permanganate (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\))

Preparation from pyrolusite ore (\(\mathrm{MnO_2}\)):

  1. Alkaline oxidative fusion: \(\mathrm{MnO_2}\) fused with \(\mathrm{KOH}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) (or \(\mathrm{KNO_3}\)) forms dark green potassium manganate: \[2\mathrm{MnO_2} + 4\mathrm{KOH} + \mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{K_2MnO_4} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\]
  2. Conversion to permanganate: Either by acid/neutral disproportionation (\(3\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}} + 4\mathrm{H^+} \to 2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + \mathrm{MnO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\)) or commercially by electrolytic oxidation in alkaline solution.
  3. Laboratory method: Oxidation of \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) by peroxodisulphate: \[2\mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + 5\mathrm{S_2O_8^{2-}} + 8\mathrm{H_2O} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 10\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}} + 16\mathrm{H^+}\]

Thermal decomposition at \(513\;\mathrm{K}\): \(2\mathrm{KMnO_4} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{K_2MnO_4} + \mathrm{MnO_2} + \mathrm{O_2}\).

Permanganate oxidises \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) ions to \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) gas, consuming extra \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) and ruining volumetric accuracy. Always acidify \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) titrations with dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\).

7. The f-Block: Lanthanoids & Actinoids

7.1 The Lanthanoids (4f Series: \(\mathrm{Ce}\), \(Z=58\) to \(\mathrm{Lu}\), \(Z=71\))

Lanthanum (\(\mathrm{La}\), \(Z=57\), \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,5d^1 6s^2\)) is studied alongside the 14 lanthanoids (\(\mathrm{Ln}\)). All \(\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}\) ions have the clean configuration \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^n\) (\(n = 0\text{ to }14\)). In neutral atoms, \(5d^1\) occupancy appears only in \(\mathrm{La}\) (\(4f^0 5d^1 6s^2\)), \(\mathrm{Ce}\) (\(4f^1 5d^1 6s^2\)), \(\mathrm{Gd}\) (\(4f^7 5d^1 6s^2\)), and \(\mathrm{Lu}\) (\(4f^{14} 5d^1 6s^2\)).

  • Lanthanoid Contraction: Across \(\mathrm{La^{3+}}\) (\(106\;\mathrm{pm}\)) to \(\mathrm{Lu^{3+}}\) (\(85\;\mathrm{pm}\)), ionic radii decrease steadily due to poor shielding by 4f electrons. Consequently, basic strength of \(\mathrm{Ln(OH)_3}\) decreases from \(\mathrm{La(OH)_3}\) (most basic) to \(\mathrm{Lu(OH)_3}\) (least basic).
  • Oxidation States: \(+3\) is universally dominant. Exceptions arise from empty (\(f^0\)), half-filled (\(f^7\)), or filled (\(f^{14}\)) stability:
    • \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\) (\(4f^0\), \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}/Ce^{3+}}) = +1.74\;\mathrm{V}\)) and \(\mathrm{Tb^{4+}}\) (\(4f^7\)) are strong oxidising agents.
    • \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\) (\(4f^7\)) and \(\mathrm{Yb^{2+}}\) (\(4f^{14}\)) are strong reducing agents.
  • Colour & Magnetism: \(\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}\) ions with unpaired f electrons show narrow absorption bands and paramagnetism; \(\mathrm{La^{3+}}\) (\(f^0\)), \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\) (\(f^0\)), \(\mathrm{Yb^{2+}}\) (\(f^{14}\)), and \(\mathrm{Lu^{3+}}\) (\(f^{14}\)) are colourless and diamagnetic.
  • Mischmetall: An alloy of ~\(95\%\) lanthanoid metals and ~\(5\%\) \(\mathrm{Fe}\) (with traces of \(\mathrm{S, C, Ca, Al}\)), used in Mg-based alloys for bullets, shells, and lighter flints.

7.2 The Actinoids (5f Series: \(\mathrm{Th}\), \(Z=90\) to \(\mathrm{Lr}\), \(Z=103\))

Actinoids are all radioactive and exhibit much richer oxidation-state chemistry than lanthanoids because \(5f\), \(6d\), and \(7s\) energy levels lie very close together. Notably, \(\mathrm{Th}\) (\(Z=90\)) has \([\mathrm{Rn}]\,6d^2 7s^2\) (no 5f electron in ground state), while \(\mathrm{Am}\) (\(5f^7 7s^2\)) and \(\mathrm{Cm}\) (\(5f^7 6d^1 7s^2\)) reflect half-filled \(5f^7\) stability.

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Magnetic Moment & Electronic Configuration

Problem: A hypothetical aqueous coordination study isolates a hydrated divalent transition-metal ion \(\mathrm{M^{2+}(aq)}\) whose metal atom has atomic number \(Z = 28\). Calculate its spin-only magnetic moment and predict whether the solution is coloured.

Solution:

  1. For \(Z = 28\) (\(\mathrm{Ni}\)), the neutral ground-state configuration is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^8 4s^2\).
  2. Removing two 4s electrons gives \(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}\): \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^8\).
  3. Applying Hund's rule across five 3d orbitals gives three paired sets and \(n = 2\) unpaired electrons.
  4. Spin-only magnetic moment: \[\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = \sqrt{2(2+2)} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83\;\mathrm{BM}\]
  5. Because the 3d subshell is partially filled (\(3d^8\)), d–d transitions occur in the visible region, so \(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}(aq)}\) is coloured (green).
Worked Example 2: Stoichiometry of \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) in Different Media

Problem: In a laboratory exercise, an assumed \(0.15\;\mathrm{mol}\) sample of \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) is used to oxidise iodide ions (\(\mathrm{I^-}\)) in (a) dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) medium and (b) neutral/faintly alkaline medium. Calculate the moles of \(\mathrm{I_2}\) or \(\mathrm{IO_3^-}\) produced in each case.

Solution:

  • Case (a) — Acidic medium: \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) (\(n\)-factor \(= 5\)) oxidises \(\mathrm{I^-}\) to \(\mathrm{I_2}\) (\(n\)-factor \(= 2\) per \(\mathrm{I_2}\)): \[2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 10\mathrm{I^-} + 16\mathrm{H^+} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + 5\mathrm{I_2} + 8\mathrm{H_2O}\] Moles of \(\mathrm{I_2} = \frac{5}{2} \times 0.15 = 0.375\;\mathrm{mol}\).
  • Case (b) — Neutral/faintly alkaline medium: \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) (\(n\)-factor \(= 3\)) oxidises \(\mathrm{I^-}\) to \(\mathrm{IO_3^-}\) (\(n\)-factor \(= 6\)): \[2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + \mathrm{I^-} + \mathrm{H_2O} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{MnO_2} + \mathrm{IO_3^-} + 2\mathrm{OH^-}\] Moles of \(\mathrm{IO_3^-} = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.15 = 0.075\;\mathrm{mol}\).
Worked Example 3: Identifying Lanthanoid Ions from 4f Stability

Problem: Among \(\mathrm{La^{3+}}\), \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\), \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\), and \(\mathrm{Gd^{3+}}\), identify which species acts as a strong oxidising agent and which acts as a strong reducing agent, justifying via electronic configurations.

Solution:

  • \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\) (\(Z=58\)) has configuration \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^0\). Though stabilised by the noble-gas core, it readily accepts one electron to revert to the stable lanthanoid \(+3\) state (\(\mathrm{Ce^{3+}}\), \(4f^1\)), making \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\) a strong oxidising agent (\(E^\circ = +1.74\;\mathrm{V}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\) (\(Z=63\)) has configuration \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^7\) (half-filled). Despite this stability, it readily loses an electron to form the common \(+3\) state (\(\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}\), \(4f^6\)), making \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\) a strong reducing agent.

Practice Question 1 Single Correct MCQ

Which of the following elements has an electronic configuration of \([\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10} 5s^0\) in its ground state?

  • (A) Silver (\(\mathrm{Ag}\), \(Z=47\))
  • (B) Cadmium (\(\mathrm{Cd}\), \(Z=48\))
  • (C) Palladium (\(\mathrm{Pd}\), \(Z=46\))
  • (D) Rhodium (\(\mathrm{Rh}\), \(Z=45\))

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Palladium (\(Z=46\)) is the unique 4d element in which both 5s electrons shift into the 4d subshell to achieve a completely filled \([\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10} 5s^0\) configuration. \(\mathrm{Ag}\) is \(4d^{10}5s^1\), \(\mathrm{Cd}\) is \(4d^{10}5s^2\), and \(\mathrm{Rh}\) is \(4d^8 5s^1\).

Practice Question 2 Single Correct MCQ

Which of the following 3d transition elements exhibits the lowest enthalpy of atomisation?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Sc}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Mn}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Cu}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Zn}\)

Answer: (D)

Explanation: Zinc (\(3d^{10} 4s^2\)) has no unpaired 3d electrons available for interatomic metallic bonding, giving it the lowest enthalpy of atomisation in the 3d series (\(126\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)). (\(\mathrm{Mn}\) has a dip relative to its neighbours, but its value is \(281\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), still much higher than \(\mathrm{Zn}\)).

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Zirconium (\(\mathrm{Zr}\)) and Hafnium (\(\mathrm{Hf}\)) have almost identical atomic radii and very similar chemical properties.
Reason (R): The filling of 4f orbitals prior to the 5d series causes lanthanoid contraction due to poor shielding by 4f electrons.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.

Answer: (A)

Explanation: Poor shielding of nuclear charge by the 14 intervening 4f electrons produces lanthanoid contraction, making \(\mathrm{Zr}\) (\(160\;\mathrm{pm}\)) and \(\mathrm{Hf}\) (\(159\;\mathrm{pm}\)) nearly identical in size and chemical behaviour.

Practice Question 4 Single Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding the third ionisation enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_3\)) of 3d elements is correct?

  • (A) \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_3\) of \(\mathrm{Fe}\) is greater than that of \(\mathrm{Mn}\).
  • (B) \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_3\) of \(\mathrm{Mn}\) is greater than that of \(\mathrm{Fe}\).
  • (C) \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_3\) of \(\mathrm{Cr}\) is greater than that of \(\mathrm{Mn}\).
  • (D) \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_3\) of \(\mathrm{Cu}\) is lower than that of \(\mathrm{Fe}\).

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Removing the third electron from \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) (\(3d^5\)) disrupts a stable half-filled \(d^5\) subshell, requiring very high energy. In contrast, removing the third electron from \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) (\(3d^6\)) produces the stable \(3d^5\) configuration of \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\), so \(\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_3(\mathrm{Mn}) > \Delta_{\mathrm{i}}H_3(\mathrm{Fe})\).

Practice Question 5 Statement I & II

Statement I: In aqueous solution, \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) acts as a strong reducing agent whereas \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) acts as a strong oxidising agent.
Statement II: Both \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) have a \(3d^4\) electronic configuration.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (A)

Explanation: Both \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) are \(3d^4\). \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) oxidises to \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) (\(d^3\), stable half-filled \(t_{2g}^3\) in water), making it strongly reducing. \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) reduces to \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) (\(d^5\), half-filled d subshell), making it strongly oxidising.

Practice Question 6 Single Correct MCQ

Which of the following pairs of aqueous ions are both colourless and diamagnetic?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Ti^{3+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\)

Practice Question 7 Numerical / Calculation MCQ

The spin-only magnetic moment of a metal ion \(\mathrm{M^{3+}}\) is \(3.87\;\mathrm{BM}\). Which of the following could be the metal \(\mathrm{M}\)?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Fe}\) (\(Z=26\))
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Mn}\) (\(Z=25\))
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Cr}\) (\(Z=24\))
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Co}\) (\(Z=27\))

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Using \(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = 3.87\;\mathrm{BM}\), we get \(n(n+2) \approx 15\), so \(n = 3\) unpaired electrons. Checking the \(+3\) ions: \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) is \(3d^3\) (\(n = 3\)), \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) is \(3d^4\) (\(n = 4\)), \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) is \(3d^5\) (\(n = 5\)), and \(\mathrm{Co^{3+}}\) is \(3d^6\) (\(n = 4\)). Thus, \(\mathrm{M} = \mathrm{Cr}\).

Practice Question 11 Single Correct MCQ

Which of the following sets of lanthanoid hydroxides is arranged in the correct decreasing order of basicity?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Lu(OH)_3 > Gd(OH)_3 > La(OH)_3}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{La(OH)_3 > Gd(OH)_3 > Lu(OH)_3}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Gd(OH)_3 > La(OH)_3 > Lu(OH)_3}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{La(OH)_3 > Lu(OH)_3 > Gd(OH)_3}\)

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Due to lanthanoid contraction, the ionic radius of \(\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}\) decreases from \(\mathrm{La^{3+}}\) to \(\mathrm{Lu^{3+}}\). Smaller cation size increases covalent character in the \(\mathrm{Ln\text{–}OH}\) bond (Fajans' rules), making it harder to release \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) ions. Hence basicity decreases from \(\mathrm{La(OH)_3}\) to \(\mathrm{Lu(OH)_3}\).

Practice Question 12 Statement I & II

Statement I: Actinoids exhibit a wider range of oxidation states than lanthanoids.
Statement II: The \(5f\), \(6d\), and \(7s\) orbitals in actinoids have comparable energies, and actinoid contraction is greater from element to element than lanthanoid contraction.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (A)

10. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

  • Pitfall 1 — Confusing d-block with transition metals: \(\mathrm{Zn}, \mathrm{Cd}, \mathrm{Hg}\) are d-block elements, NOT transition elements, because they have \(d^{10}\) in both ground and \(+2\) states.
  • Pitfall 2 — Ionisation order: Always remove \(ns\) electrons before \((n-1)d\) electrons when writing cation configurations.
  • Pitfall 3 — Colour of \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) and \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\): Both have \(d^0\) metal centres; their colour is due to charge transfer (LMCT), NOT d–d transition.
  • Pitfall 4 — \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) vs. \(\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}\): \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) is purple and diamagnetic (\(d^0\)); \(\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}\) is green and paramagnetic (\(d^1\), \(1.73\;\mathrm{BM}\)).
  • Pitfall 5 — Acidifying \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\): Never use \(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\); always use dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\).
  • Pitfall 6 — Lanthanoid +2/+4 exceptions: Remember \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}, \mathrm{Tb^{4+}}\) (oxidants) and \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}, \mathrm{Yb^{2+}}\) (reductants).

11. Concise Final Recap

Transition elements possess incomplete \((n-1)d\) subshells in their neutral or common ionic states, giving rise to high melting points, variable oxidation states, paramagnetism (\(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\;\mathrm{BM}\)), coloured ions, complexes, catalysis, interstitial compounds, and alloys. Poor shielding by 4f electrons drives lanthanoid contraction, equalising 4d and 5d radii (\(\mathrm{Zr}\approx\mathrm{Hf}\)) and decreasing \(\mathrm{Ln(OH)_3}\) basicity. Potassium dichromate and potassium permanganate serve as benchmark oxidants whose products depend sharply on solution pH, while actinoids extend inner-transition behaviour with radioactivity, steeper 5f contraction, and oxidation states up to \(+7\).
