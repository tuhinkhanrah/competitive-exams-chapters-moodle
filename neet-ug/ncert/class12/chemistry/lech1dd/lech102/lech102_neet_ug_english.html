Electrochemistry: Concepts, Formulas & NEET UG Practice

A self-contained exam-oriented tutorial covering galvanic and electrolytic cells, Nernst equation, electrolytic conductance, Kohlrausch law, Faraday's laws, batteries, fuel cells, and corrosion.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Roadmap

Electrochemistry links chemical reactions with electrical energy. Either a spontaneous redox reaction generates electricity (as in galvanic cells and batteries), or an external electrical source drives a non-spontaneous chemical change (as in electrolysis). For NEET UG, questions frequently test sign conventions, Nernst equation calculations, conductance trends on dilution, Kohlrausch law, Faraday's laws, and commercial cell reactions.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between galvanic and electrolytic cells, including how an opposing external voltage reverses cell operation.
  • Use standard reduction potentials (\(E^\circ\)) and the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) to predict spontaneity, oxidizing/reducing strength, and cell emf.
  • Apply the Nernst equation to find non-standard cell potentials, equilibrium constants (\(K_c\)), and Gibbs free energy changes (\(\Delta_r G^\circ\)).
  • Calculate resistivity (\(\rho\)), conductivity (\(\kappa\)), cell constant (\(G^*\)), and molar conductivity (\(\Lambda_m\)), and explain their variation with dilution.
  • Apply Kohlrausch law to find limiting molar conductivity (\(\Lambda_m^\circ\)), degree of dissociation (\(\alpha\)), and dissociation constants (\(K_a, K_b\)) of weak electrolytes.
  • Predict electrolysis products and solve quantitative problems using Faraday's laws.
  • Identify electrode reactions and applications of primary cells, secondary batteries, fuel cells, and electrochemical corrosion.

2. Electrochemical Cells: Galvanic vs. Electrolytic

An electrochemical cell consists of two electronic conductors (electrodes) dipping into ionic conductors (electrolytes). Depending on whether the net redox reaction is spontaneous or driven by an external power supply, cells fall into two classes:

Property Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell Electrolytic Cell
Energy conversion Chemical energy → Electrical energy Electrical energy → Chemical energy
Reaction spontaneity Spontaneous (\(\Delta_r G < 0\), \(E_{\mathrm{cell}} > 0\)) Non-spontaneous (\(\Delta_r G > 0\), driven by \(E_{\mathrm{ext}}\))
Anode (oxidation) Negative (−) electrode Positive (+) electrode
Cathode (reduction) Positive (+) electrode Negative (−) electrode
Salt bridge Required when half-cells use separate electrolytes Usually absent (electrodes share one electrolyte)

Anatomy and Functioning of a Daniell Cell

In a Daniell cell, a zinc rod dips into \(\mathrm{ZnSO_4(aq)}\) and a copper rod dips into \(\mathrm{CuSO_4(aq)}\). Oxidation occurs at the zinc anode while reduction occurs at the copper cathode:

  • Anode (oxidation): \(\mathrm{Zn(s) \rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-}\)
  • Cathode (reduction): \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \rightarrow Cu(s)}\)
  • Overall cell reaction: \(\mathrm{Zn(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)}\)

At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) and unit molar concentrations (\(1\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}\)), the standard cell potential is \(1.10\,\mathrm{V}\). Electrons flow externally from the zinc anode to the copper cathode, while conventional current flows from copper to zinc.

Role of the Salt Bridge & IUPAC Cell Notation

A salt bridge (an inverted U-tube filled with an inert electrolyte such as \(\mathrm{KCl}\) or \(\mathrm{KNO_3}\) in agar-agar gel) completes the internal circuit by allowing ion migration while preventing the two solutions from mixing, thus maintaining electrical neutrality in both half-cells. (Note: \(\mathrm{KCl}\) is avoided if \(\mathrm{Ag^+}\), \(\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}\), or \(\mathrm{Hg_2^{2+}}\) ions are present because insoluble chlorides precipitate.)

By IUPAC convention, the anode is written on the left and the cathode on the right. A single vertical line (\(|\)) denotes a phase boundary, and a double vertical line (\(\parallel\)) denotes the salt bridge:

\(\mathrm{Zn(s) \mid Zn^{2+}(aq) \parallel Cu^{2+}(aq) \mid Cu(s)}\)

Effect of an Opposing External Potential (\(E_{\mathrm{ext}}\))

Applying a variable external voltage \(E_{\mathrm{ext}}\) opposite to the Daniell cell potential (\(1.10\,\mathrm{V}\)) produces three distinct regimes:

  • When \(E_{\mathrm{ext}} < 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\): The cell continues to act as a galvanic cell. Electrons flow from Zn to Cu; Zn dissolves at the anode and Cu deposits at the cathode.
  • When \(E_{\mathrm{ext}} = 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\): The opposing potential balances the cell emf. Current drops to zero (\(I = 0\)) and no chemical reaction occurs.
  • When \(E_{\mathrm{ext}} > 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\): The cell reverses and acts as an electrolytic cell. Electrons flow from Cu to Zn; Cu dissolves at the anode (now positive) and Zn deposits at the cathode (now negative).

3. Electrode Potential, SHE & Electrochemical Series

At an electrode–electrolyte interface, a potential difference develops due to the competition between metal atoms entering solution as ions and ions depositing onto the metal. Because a single half-cell potential cannot be measured in isolation, potentials are measured relative to a universal reference: the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE).

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

The SHE consists of a platinum foil coated with finely divided platinum black, immersed in an acidic solution of \(1.00\,\mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{H^+(aq)}\) with pure \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) bubbled at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure. By convention, its standard potential is assigned exactly \(0.00\,\mathrm{V}\) at all temperatures:

\[\mathrm{Pt(s) \mid H_2(g, 1\,bar) \mid H^+(aq, 1\,M)} \qquad \mathrm{H^+(aq) + e^- \rightleftharpoons \frac{1}{2}H_2(g)} \qquad E^\circ = 0.00\,\mathrm{V}\]

Standard Cell Potential & Electrochemical Series

According to IUPAC convention, standard electrode potentials are reported as standard reduction potentials (\(E^\circ\)). For any galvanic cell constructed with the anode on the left and cathode on the right:

\[E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ_{\mathrm{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\mathrm{anode}} = E^\circ_{\mathrm{right}} - E^\circ_{\mathrm{left}}\]

Arranging redox couples in decreasing order of \(E^\circ\) gives the electrochemical series:

  • Higher (more positive) \(E^\circ\): Greater tendency for the oxidized form to gain electrons → stronger oxidizing agent (e.g., \(\mathrm{F_2}\) with \(E^\circ = +2.87\,\mathrm{V}\) is the strongest oxidizing agent).
  • Lower (more negative) \(E^\circ\): Greater tendency for the reduced form to lose electrons → stronger reducing agent (e.g., \(\mathrm{Li}\) with \(E^\circ = -3.05\,\mathrm{V}\) is the strongest reducing agent in aqueous solution).
  • Metal displacement: A metal with a lower \(E^\circ\) can displace a metal with a higher \(E^\circ\) from its aqueous salt solution. Metals with \(E^\circ < 0\) liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from dilute acids, whereas metals like \(\mathrm{Cu}\) (\(E^\circ = +0.34\,\mathrm{V}\)) and \(\mathrm{Ag}\) (\(E^\circ = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\)) do not dissolve in \(\mathrm{HCl}\).

4. Nernst Equation & Cell Thermodynamics

When species are not at unit concentration (or \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure for gases), electrode and cell potentials depend on concentration according to the Nernst equation. For a general cell reaction \(a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \xrightarrow{n\mathrm{e^-}} c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}\):

\[E_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} - \frac{RT}{nF} \ln Q = E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} - \frac{2.303RT}{nF} \log \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c[\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a[\mathrm{B}]^b}\]

Here \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\), \(F = 96487\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\) (often approximated as \(96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\)), \(n\) is the number of moles of electrons transferred in the balanced equation, and pure solids or pure liquids are taken as unity in \(Q\). At \(T = 298\,\mathrm{K}\), \(\frac{2.303RT}{F} \approx 0.059\,\mathrm{V}\), so:

\[E_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} - \frac{0.059}{n} \log Q \qquad (\text{at } 298\,\mathrm{K})\]

Equilibrium Constant (\(K_c\)) and Gibbs Free Energy (\(\Delta_r G\))

As a galvanic cell discharges, reactant ion concentrations fall and product ion concentrations rise until equilibrium is reached, at which point \(E_{\mathrm{cell}} = 0\) and \(Q = K_c\). Furthermore, the maximum reversible electrical work obtained from a galvanic cell equals the decrease in Gibbs free energy:

\[\Delta_r G = -nFE_{\mathrm{cell}} \qquad \Delta_r G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = -RT \ln K_c\] \[E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = \frac{0.059}{n} \log K_c \qquad (\text{at } 298\,\mathrm{K})\]
Crucial NEET Concept: Intensive vs. Extensive Properties

\(E_{\mathrm{cell}}\) is an intensive property: multiplying a cell equation by 2 does not double \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}\) or \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}}\). However, \(\Delta_r G\) is an extensive property: multiplying the equation by 2 doubles \(n\), which doubles \(\Delta_r G\). Always match \(n\) to the exact stoichiometric equation written.

Worked Example 1: Non-Standard Cell Potential & \(\Delta_r G^\circ\)

Consider a hypothetical galvanic cell at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \(\mathrm{Cd(s) \mid Cd^{2+}(0.020\,M) \parallel Ag^+(0.20\,M) \mid Ag(s)}\). Given \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cd^{2+}/Cd}) = -0.40\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Ag^+/Ag}) = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\), calculate \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}\) and \(\Delta_r G^\circ\).

Solution:

1. Balanced cell reaction: \(\mathrm{Cd(s) + 2Ag^+(aq) \rightarrow Cd^{2+}(aq) + 2Ag(s)}\), so \(n = 2\).

2. Standard cell potential: \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ_{\mathrm{Ag^+/Ag}} - E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cd^{2+}/Cd}} = 0.80 - (-0.40) = 1.20\,\mathrm{V}\).

3. Reaction quotient: \(Q = \frac{[\mathrm{Cd^{2+}}]}{[\mathrm{Ag^+}]^2} = \frac{0.020}{(0.20)^2} = \frac{0.020}{0.040} = 0.50\).

4. Nernst equation at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \[E_{\mathrm{cell}} = 1.20 - \frac{0.059}{2}\log(0.50) = 1.20 - 0.0295 \times (-0.3010) = 1.20 + 0.0089 \approx 1.21\,\mathrm{V}\]

5. Standard Gibbs energy: \(\Delta_r G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = -2 \times 96487 \times 1.20 = -231568.8\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx -231.57\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Worked Example 2: Equilibrium Constant from \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}}\)

For the reaction \(\mathrm{Zn(s) + Ni^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq) + Ni(s)}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), calculate \(K_c\) using \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}/Zn}) = -0.76\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}/Ni}) = -0.25\,\mathrm{V}\).

Solution:

\(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = -0.25 - (-0.76) = 0.51\,\mathrm{V}\), with \(n = 2\).

\[\log K_c = \frac{n E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}}}{0.059} = \frac{2 \times 0.51}{0.059} \approx 17.29 \implies K_c = 10^{17.29} \approx 1.95 \times 10^{17}\]

5. Conductance of Electrolytic Solutions & Kohlrausch Law

Electricity flows through metals by electronic conductance (movement of electrons without chemical change; decreases as temperature increases due to lattice vibrations). In aqueous solutions or melts, electricity flows by ionic (electrolytic) conductance (movement of ions; increases as temperature increases because viscosity decreases and ion mobility/dissociation increases).

Key Conductance Quantities & Measurement

For an electrolyte column of length \(l\) and cross-sectional area \(A\) confined between two platinized platinum electrodes in a conductivity cell:

\[R = \rho \frac{l}{A} \qquad G = \frac{1}{R} = \kappa \frac{A}{l} \qquad G^* = \frac{l}{A} = R\kappa \qquad \kappa = \frac{G^*}{R}\]
  • Resistivity (\(\rho\)): SI unit \(\Omega\,\mathrm{m}\) (\(1\,\Omega\,\mathrm{m} = 100\,\Omega\,\mathrm{cm}\)).
  • Conductance (\(G\)): SI unit siemens (\(\mathrm{S} = \Omega^{-1}\)).
  • Conductivity (\(\kappa\)): Conductance of a unit volume of solution; SI unit \(\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}} = 100\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\)).
  • Cell constant (\(G^* = l/A\)): Determined experimentally using a standard \(\mathrm{KCl}\) solution of known conductivity rather than direct geometric measurement.
  • Wheatstone bridge with AC: Alternating current (audio frequency) is used instead of DC to prevent electrolysis and polarization from altering solution composition.

Molar Conductivity (\(\Lambda_m\)) and Dilution Effects

Molar conductivity is the conductance of the volume \(V\) of solution containing \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of electrolyte placed between electrodes \(1\,\mathrm{unit}\) apart:

\[\Lambda_m = \frac{\kappa}{c} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \Lambda_m\,(\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}) = \frac{\kappa\,(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}) \times 1000\,(\mathrm{cm^3\,L^{-1}})}{\text{Molarity}\,(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})}\]

Note the unit conversion: \(1\,\mathrm{S\,m^2\,mol^{-1}} = 10^4\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\).

  • Effect of dilution on \(\kappa\): Conductivity \(\kappa\) always decreases on dilution (for both strong and weak electrolytes) because the number of current-carrying ions per unit volume decreases.
  • Effect of dilution on \(\Lambda_m\): Molar conductivity \(\Lambda_m = \kappa V\) always increases on dilution because the increase in total volume \(V\) more than compensates for the decrease in \(\kappa\).
  • Strong electrolytes: Completely dissociated; \(\Lambda_m\) increases slowly on dilution as interionic attractions weaken, following \(\Lambda_m = \Lambda_m^\circ - A c^{1/2}\). Extrapolating the straight line to \(c^{1/2} \rightarrow 0\) gives \(\Lambda_m^\circ\) (limiting molar conductivity).
  • Weak electrolytes: Partially dissociated; \(\Lambda_m\) rises steeply at low concentrations because the degree of dissociation (\(\alpha\)) increases sharply. Extrapolation to \(c^{1/2} = 0\) is impossible, so \(\Lambda_m^\circ\) is found via Kohlrausch law.

Kohlrausch Law of Independent Migration of Ions

At infinite dilution, each ion migrates independently without interionic hindrance. Thus, \(\Lambda_m^\circ\) of an electrolyte is the sum of the individual limiting molar conductivities of its cations (\(\lambda_+^\circ\)) and anions (\(\lambda_-^\circ\)):

\[\Lambda_m^\circ = \nu_+ \lambda_+^\circ + \nu_- \lambda_-^\circ \qquad \alpha = \frac{\Lambda_m}{\Lambda_m^\circ} \qquad K_a = \frac{c\alpha^2}{1 - \alpha} = \frac{c\Lambda_m^2}{\Lambda_m^\circ(\Lambda_m^\circ - \Lambda_m)}\]
Worked Example 3: Cell Constant, Conductivity & Molar Conductivity

A conductivity cell filled with \(0.05\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{KCl}\) (\(\kappa = 0.0067\,\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\)) shows a resistance of \(120\,\Omega\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). When filled with \(0.02\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (assumed sample), its resistance is \(350\,\Omega\). Find \(\kappa\) and \(\Lambda_m\) of the \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) solution.

Solution:

1. Cell constant: \(G^* = R_{\mathrm{KCl}} \cdot \kappa_{\mathrm{KCl}} = 120\,\Omega \times 0.0067\,\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}} = 0.804\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\).

2. Conductivity of \(\mathrm{NaCl}\): \(\kappa = \frac{G^*}{R_{\mathrm{NaCl}}} = \frac{0.804}{350} \approx 2.297 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\).

3. Molar conductivity: \(\Lambda_m = \frac{2.297 \times 10^{-3} \times 1000}{0.02} = 114.86\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\).

Worked Example 4: Kohlrausch Law & Base Dissociation Constant

Using illustrative values at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \(\Lambda_m^\circ(\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2}) = 523.2\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\Lambda_m^\circ(\mathrm{BaCl_2}) = 280.0\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\), and \(\Lambda_m^\circ(\mathrm{NH_4Cl}) = 129.8\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\). Calculate \(\Lambda_m^\circ(\mathrm{NH_4OH})\). If \(0.01\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{NH_4OH}\) has \(\Lambda_m = 10.06\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\), find \(K_b\).

Solution:

1. \(\Lambda_m^\circ(\mathrm{NH_4OH}) = \Lambda_m^\circ(\mathrm{NH_4Cl}) + \frac{1}{2}\Lambda_m^\circ(\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2}) - \frac{1}{2}\Lambda_m^\circ(\mathrm{BaCl_2}) = 129.8 + 261.6 - 140.0 = 251.4\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\).

2. Degree of dissociation: \(\alpha = \frac{10.06}{251.4} = 0.040\).

3. Dissociation constant: \(K_b = \frac{0.01 \times (0.040)^2}{1 - 0.040} = \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-5}}{0.96} \approx 1.67 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\).

6. Electrolysis & Faraday's Laws

In an electrolytic cell, ions migrate toward oppositely charged electrodes. Which species actually discharges depends on standard electrode potentials, concentration (Nernst effect), electrode reactivity (inert Pt/Au vs. reactive Cu/Ag), and kinetic barriers known as overpotential.

Electrolyte & Electrodes Cathode Product Anode Product Key Governing Factor
Molten \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (inert Pt) \(\mathrm{Na(l)}\) \(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)}\) Only \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) present
Aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (inert Pt) \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) (+ \(\mathrm{OH^-}\)) \(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)}\) \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) reduces more easily than \(\mathrm{Na^+}\); \(\mathrm{O_2}\) has high overpotential
Dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) (inert Pt) \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) \(\mathrm{O_2(g)}\) \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) oxidation (\(1.23\,\mathrm{V}\)) favored over \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) (\(1.96\,\mathrm{V}\))
Concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) (inert Pt) \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) \(\mathrm{S_2O_8^{2-}(aq)}\) High \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) concentration favors peroxodisulfate formation
Aqueous \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) (Cu electrodes) \(\mathrm{Cu(s)}\) deposited \(\mathrm{Cu(s)}\) dissolves as \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) Reactive Cu anode oxidizes more easily than \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) or \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\)

Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

  • First Law: The mass (\(m\)) of substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity (\(Q = It\)) passed: \[m = ZIt = \frac{M \cdot I \cdot t}{nF} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{eq}} \cdot Q}{96500}\] where \(M\) is molar mass, \(n\) is electrons per formula unit, and \(E_{\mathrm{eq}} = M/n\) is equivalent mass.
  • Second Law: When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes connected in series, the masses liberated are proportional to their chemical equivalent weights (\(m_1 / m_2 = E_{\mathrm{eq},1} / E_{\mathrm{eq},2}\)).
Worked Example 5: Quantitative Electrolysis

A steady current of \(2.5\,\mathrm{A}\) is passed through molten \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) (with cryolite) for \(1930\,\mathrm{s}\). Calculate the mass of aluminium deposited at the cathode (molar mass of \(\mathrm{Al} = 27\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), \(F = 96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

1. Charge passed: \(Q = I \cdot t = 2.5\,\mathrm{A} \times 1930\,\mathrm{s} = 4825\,\mathrm{C}\).

2. Faradays passed: \(\frac{4825}{96500} = 0.05\,\mathrm{F}\).

3. Since \(\mathrm{Al^{3+} + 3e^- \rightarrow Al(s)}\), \(n = 3\), so equivalent mass \(= \frac{27}{3} = 9\,\mathrm{g\,eq^{-1}}\). Mass deposited \(= 0.05 \times 9 = 0.45\,\mathrm{g}\).

Hydrogen–Oxygen Fuel Cell

Fuel cells convert the energy of combustion of fuels (\(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\)) directly into electricity with ~\(70\%\) efficiency (compared to ~\(40\%\) for thermal power plants) and zero pollution. In an alkaline \(\mathrm{H_2–O_2}\) fuel cell with porous carbon electrodes containing Pt/Pd catalysts and concentrated \(\mathrm{NaOH(aq)}\):

  • Anode: \(2\mathrm{H_2(g) + 4OH^-(aq) \rightarrow 4H_2O(l) + 4e^-}\)
  • Cathode: \(\mathrm{O_2(g) + 2H_2O(l) + 4e^- \rightarrow 4OH^-(aq)}\)
  • Overall: \(2\mathrm{H_2(g) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2H_2O(l)}\)

Corrosion of Iron (Rusting)

Corrosion is an electrochemical process. On an iron surface exposed to moist air containing dissolved \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (forming \(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\), which supplies \(\mathrm{H^+}\)), one region acts as an anode and another as a cathode:

  • Anode: \(2\mathrm{Fe(s) \rightarrow 2Fe^{2+}(aq) + 4e^-}\) (\(E^\circ = -0.44\,\mathrm{V}\))
  • Cathode: \(\mathrm{O_2(g) + 4H^+(aq) + 4e^- \rightarrow 2H_2O(l)}\) (\(E^\circ = +1.23\,\mathrm{V}\))
  • Overall: \(2\mathrm{Fe(s) + O_2(g) + 4H^+(aq) \rightarrow 2Fe^{2+}(aq) + 2H_2O(l)}\) (\(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = +1.67\,\mathrm{V}\))

Atmospheric oxygen further oxidizes \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) to \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\), precipitating hydrated ferric oxide (rust, \(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O}\)). Prevention methods include barrier coatings (paint, bisphenol), metallic coatings (Sn, Zn), and sacrificial protection using more reactive metals like \(\mathrm{Mg}\) or \(\mathrm{Zn}\).

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All questions below are original practice questions designed to match the NEET UG examination pattern and difficulty.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When an opposing external potential \(E_{\mathrm{ext}} > 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\) is applied to a standard Daniell cell (\(\mathrm{Zn \mid Zn^{2+}(1\,M) \parallel Cu^{2+}(1\,M) \mid Cu}\)), which of the following occurs?

  • (A) Electrons flow from Zn to Cu and Cu deposits at the cathode.
  • (B) Electrons flow from Cu to Zn and Zn deposits at the cathode.
  • (C) No current flows through the cell and the reaction stops.
  • (D) Conventional current flows from Cu to Zn through the external wire.
Answer: (B)
Explanation: When \(E_{\mathrm{ext}} > 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\), the Daniell cell reverses and operates as an electrolytic cell. Electrons are forced from the Cu electrode (now anode, oxidizing \(\mathrm{Cu \rightarrow Cu^{2+} + 2e^-}\)) to the Zn electrode (now cathode, reducing \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Zn}\)).
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Given the standard reduction potentials at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \(E^\circ(\mathrm{A^{2+}/A}) = -2.36\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ(\mathrm{B^{2+}/B}) = -0.44\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ(\mathrm{C^+/C}) = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\), and \(E^\circ(\mathrm{D^{3+}/D}) = -1.66\,\mathrm{V}\). The correct order of increasing reducing power of these metals is:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{A < D < B < C}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{C < B < D < A}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{C < D < B < A}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{B < C < D < A}\)
Answer: (B)
Explanation: A lower (more negative) standard reduction potential indicates a stronger tendency to undergo oxidation, hence stronger reducing power. Ordering \(E^\circ\) from highest to lowest (\(+0.80 > -0.44 > -1.66 > -2.36\,\mathrm{V}\)) gives increasing reducing power: \(\mathrm{C < B < D < A}\).

What is the reduction potential of a hydrogen electrode at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) in contact with an aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{pH} = 4\) when \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) is bubbled at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure?

  • (A) \(-0.236\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(+0.236\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(-0.118\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(-0.472\,\mathrm{V}\)
Answer: (A)
Explanation: For \(\mathrm{H^+(aq) + e^- \rightarrow \frac{1}{2}H_2(g)}\), \(E^\circ = 0.00\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(n = 1\). At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \[E = E^\circ - \frac{0.059}{1}\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{H_2}}^{1/2}}{[\mathrm{H^+}]} = 0 - 0.059\log\left(\frac{1}{10^{-4}}\right) = -0.059 \times 4 = -0.236\,\mathrm{V}\]
Practice Question 4 (Numerical MCQ)

For the cell reaction \(2\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq) + 2I^-(aq) \rightarrow 2Fe^{2+}(aq) + I_2(s)}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = 0.236\,\mathrm{V}\). Taking \(F = 96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\), what is \(\Delta_r G^\circ\) for the reaction as written?

  • (A) \(-22.77\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(-45.55\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(+45.55\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(-91.10\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)
Explanation: In the balanced equation, \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of electrons are transferred (\(n = 2\)). Thus: \[\Delta_r G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = -2 \times 96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}} \times 0.236\,\mathrm{V} = -45548\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx -45.55\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]
Practice Question 5 (Conceptual MCQ)

Upon diluting an aqueous solution of a weak electrolyte such as acetic acid, how do its conductivity (\(\kappa\)) and molar conductivity (\(\Lambda_m\)) change?

  • (A) Both \(\kappa\) and \(\Lambda_m\) increase.
  • (B) Both \(\kappa\) and \(\Lambda_m\) decrease.
  • (C) \(\kappa\) increases, whereas \(\Lambda_m\) decreases.
  • (D) \(\kappa\) decreases, whereas \(\Lambda_m\) increases steeply.
Answer: (D)
Explanation: On dilution, the number of ions per unit volume decreases, so conductivity \(\kappa\) always decreases. Meanwhile, the degree of dissociation (\(\alpha\)) of a weak electrolyte increases sharply, causing \(\Lambda_m = \kappa V\) to increase steeply.
Practice Question 6 (Numerical MCQ)

The conductivity of a \(0.01\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) aqueous electrolyte solution at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(1.41 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\). Its molar conductivity is:

  • (D) \(0.141\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)

Practice Question 9 (Numerical MCQ)

Two electrolytic cells containing aqueous \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\) and \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) solutions are connected in series. If \(2.16\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{Ag}\) (atomic mass \(108\,\mathrm{u}\)) is deposited in the first cell, what mass of \(\mathrm{Cu}\) (atomic mass \(63.5\,\mathrm{u}\)) is deposited in the second cell?

  • (A) \(0.3175\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (B) \(0.635\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (C) \(1.270\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (D) \(2.540\,\mathrm{g}\)
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Equivalent mass of \(\mathrm{Ag} = 108/1 = 108\); equivalent mass of \(\mathrm{Cu} = 63.5/2 = 31.75\). By Faraday's second law: \[m_{\mathrm{Cu}} = m_{\mathrm{Ag}} \times \frac{E_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{E_{\mathrm{Ag}}} = 2.16 \times \frac{31.75}{108} = 0.02 \times 31.75 = 0.635\,\mathrm{g}\]
Practice Question 10 (Conceptual MCQ)

During the electrolysis of aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) solution using inert platinum electrodes, \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) gas is evolved at the anode instead of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) gas, even though \(E^\circ(\mathrm{O_2/H_2O}) = +1.23\,\mathrm{V}\) is lower than \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cl_2/Cl^-}) = +1.36\,\mathrm{V}\). This occurs because:

Practice Question 12 (Assertion & Reason)

Reason (R): Zinc has a more negative standard reduction potential than iron and acts as a sacrificial anode, oxidizing in preference to iron.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.

9. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Anode/Cathode polarity swap: In a galvanic cell, anode is negative; in an electrolytic cell, anode is positive. Oxidation always occurs at the anode in both.
  • Wrong \(n\) in Nernst or \(\Delta_r G^\circ\): Always balance the full redox equation first so that electrons cancel; never use \(n = 1\) when \(2\) or \(6\) electrons are transferred.
  • Confusing \(\kappa\) and \(\Lambda_m\) on dilution: \(\kappa\) decreases on dilution, whereas \(\Lambda_m\) increases on dilution.
  • Unit mismatch in \(\Lambda_m\): When \(\kappa\) is in \(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\) and \(c\) is in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\), you must multiply by \(1000\,\mathrm{cm^3\,L^{-1}}\).
Final Recap

Master the core equations: \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ_{\mathrm{R}} - E^\circ_{\mathrm{L}}\), \(E_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} - \frac{0.059}{n}\log Q\) (at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)), \(\Delta_r G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = -RT\ln K_c\), \(\kappa = G^*/R\), \(\Lambda_m = \frac{\kappa \times 1000}{M}\), \(\Lambda_m^\circ = \nu_+\lambda_+^\circ + \nu_-\lambda_-^\circ\), and \(m = \frac{MIt}{nF}\). With these relationships and clear sign conventions, Electrochemistry becomes one of the highest-scoring chapters in NEET UG Physical Chemistry.
