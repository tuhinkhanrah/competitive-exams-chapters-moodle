Solutions: Concentration, Vapour Pressure, and Colligative Properties

A complete, exam-oriented study guide with original worked examples, vector diagrams, and NEET UG practice assessment.

1. Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

In physical and biological systems, pure substances are rare; most chemical reactions and physiological processes occur in homogeneous mixtures called solutions. For NEET UG, this chapter tests both conceptual clarity (molecular interactions, Raoult's law deviations, and osmosis) and rapid numerical problem-solving (concentration conversions, colligative properties, and the van't Hoff factor).

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Classify binary solutions by physical state and convert fluently between mass percentage, mole fraction, molarity, and molality.
  • Apply Henry's law to gas-liquid equilibria and explain pressure and temperature effects on solubility.
  • Calculate total and vapour-phase compositions of volatile liquid mixtures using Raoult's law and Dalton's law.
  • Distinguish ideal solutions from non-ideal solutions showing positive or negative deviations, including azeotrope formation.
  • Use the four colligative properties to determine solute molar masses and apply the van't Hoff factor (\(i\)) for dissociating or associating solutes.

2. Types of Solutions and Concentration Terms

A solution is a single-phase, homogeneous mixture of two or more chemically non-reacting substances. In a binary solution (two components), the component present in the larger amount—and which generally determines the physical state of the final mixture—is the solvent, while the minor component is the solute.

Solution Class Solute State Solvent State Representative Examples
Gaseous Gas Gas Mixture of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{N_2}\) gases (dry air)
Gaseous Liquid Gas Chloroform vapour mixed with nitrogen gas
Gaseous Solid Gas Camphor sublimed in nitrogen gas
Liquid Gas Liquid Dissolved oxygen in water; carbonated beverages
Liquid Liquid Liquid Ethanol dissolved in water
Liquid Solid Liquid Glucose or sodium chloride dissolved in water
Solid Gas Solid Hydrogen occluded in palladium metal
Solid Liquid Solid Mercury amalgamated with sodium
Solid Solid Solid Copper dissolved in gold (alloys)

Quantitative Ways to Express Concentration

Qualitative labels such as "dilute" or "concentrated" are imprecise. Quantitative composition is expressed using the following standard definitions (denoting solvent by subscript \(1\) and solute by subscript \(2\), or components by \(A, B, \dots\)):

  • Mass percentage (\(\%\ \mathrm{w/w}\)): \(\dfrac{\text{Mass of component}}{\text{Total mass of solution}} \times 100\). Widely used in industrial formulations.
  • Volume percentage (\(\%\ \mathrm{V/V}\)): \(\dfrac{\text{Volume of component}}{\text{Total volume of solution}} \times 100\). Used for liquid–liquid mixtures such as antifreeze (ethylene glycol in water).
  • Mass by volume percentage (\(\%\ \mathrm{w/V}\)): Mass of solute (in \(\mathrm{g}\)) dissolved in \(100\ \mathrm{mL}\) of solution. Common in pharmacy and medicine.
  • Parts per million (\(\mathrm{ppm}\)): \(\dfrac{\text{Number of parts of component}}{\text{Total parts of all components}} \times 10^6\). Ideal for trace pollutants or dissolved \(\mathrm{O_2}\) in water (often expressed in \(\mathrm{µg\ mL^{-1}}\) for dilute aqueous samples).
  • Mole fraction (\(x_i\)): \(x_i = \dfrac{n_i}{\sum n_j}\), where \(\sum x_i = 1\). Dimensionless and central to vapour pressure laws.
  • Molarity (\(M\)): Moles of solute per litre (\(\mathrm{dm^3}\)) of solution: \[ M = \frac{n_2}{V_{\text{solution (L)}}} = \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times V_{\text{solution (mL)}}} \]
  • Molality (\(m\)): Moles of solute per kilogram of solvent: \[ m = \frac{n_2}{w_{1\text{ (kg)}}} = \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times w_{1\text{ (g)}}} \]
High-Yield NEET Distinction — Temperature Dependence: Volume changes with temperature due to thermal expansion, whereas mass and moles do not. Therefore, molarity (\(M\)), \(\%\ \mathrm{V/V}\), and \(\%\ \mathrm{w/V}\) depend on temperature, whereas molality (\(m\)), mole fraction (\(x\)), \(\%\ \mathrm{w/w}\), and \(\mathrm{ppm}\) (by mass) are temperature-independent.
Useful Interconversion (Molarity \(M\), Molality \(m\), and Solution Density \(d\) in \(\mathrm{g\ mL^{-1}}\)): \[ m = \frac{1000\,M}{1000\,d - M \cdot M_2} \] where \(M_2\) is the molar mass of the solute in \(\mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\).
Worked Example 1: Concentration Interconversion

An aqueous solution of glucose (\(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\), molar mass \(= 180\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)) is prepared as \(15\%\ \mathrm{(w/w)}\). Assuming the density of this solution at \(298\ \mathrm{K}\) is \(1.06\ \mathrm{g\ mL^{-1}}\), calculate (a) its molality and (b) its molarity.

Solution:

Consider \(100\ \mathrm{g}\) of the solution as a convenient basis. It contains \(15\ \mathrm{g}\) of glucose (\(w_2\)) and \(85\ \mathrm{g}\) of water (\(w_1 = 0.085\ \mathrm{kg}\)).

\[ n_2 = \frac{15\ \mathrm{g}}{180\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}} = 0.0833\ \mathrm{mol} \]

(a) Molality:

\[ m = \frac{0.0833\ \mathrm{mol}}{0.085\ \mathrm{kg}} = 0.980\ \mathrm{mol\ kg^{-1}} \]

(b) Molarity: Volume of \(100\ \mathrm{g}\) of solution is \(V = \dfrac{100\ \mathrm{g}}{1.06\ \mathrm{g\ mL^{-1}}} = 94.34\ \mathrm{mL} = 0.09434\ \mathrm{L}\).

\[ M = \frac{0.0833\ \mathrm{mol}}{0.09434\ \mathrm{L}} = 0.883\ \mathrm{mol\ L^{-1}} \]

3. Solubility Equilibria and Henry's Law

Solubility is the maximum quantity of a solute that can dissolve in a fixed amount of solvent at a specified temperature and pressure. A solution in dynamic equilibrium with undissolved solute is a saturated solution; if more solute can still dissolve, it is unsaturated.

Solid-in-Liquid Solubility

Dissolution follows the principle of "like dissolves like": polar or ionic solids (such as \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) or sucrose) dissolve in polar solvents like water, whereas non-polar solids (such as naphthalene or anthracene) dissolve in non-polar solvents like benzene.

  • Effect of Temperature: Controlled by Le Chatelier's principle on the equilibrium \(\text{Solute} + \text{Solvent} \rightleftharpoons \text{Solution}\). If dissolution is endothermic (\(\Delta_{\text{sol}} H > 0\)), solubility increases with temperature. If exothermic (\(\Delta_{\text{sol}} H < 0\)), solubility decreases as temperature rises.
  • Effect of Pressure: Negligible, because solids and liquids are nearly incompressible.

Gas-in-Liquid Solubility and Henry's Law

Unlike solids, the solubility of gases in liquids depends strongly on both pressure and temperature. Because gas dissolution is analogous to condensation, it is generally exothermic (\(\Delta_{\text{sol}} H < 0\)); hence, gas solubility decreases with increasing temperature (explaining why aquatic organisms thrive better in colder waters).

Henry's Law: At a constant temperature, the partial pressure (\(p\)) of a gas in the vapour phase above a liquid is directly proportional to its mole fraction (\(x\)) in the solution:

\[ p = K_{\mathrm{H}}\,x \]

Here \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) is Henry's law constant (units of pressure, e.g., \(\mathrm{bar}\) or \(\mathrm{kbar}\)), which depends on the nature of both gas and solvent and increases with temperature. At a given pressure \(p\), a higher \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) corresponds to a lower gas solubility (\(x = p / K_{\mathrm{H}}\)).

Biological and Practical Applications

  • Carbonated Beverages: Bottles are sealed under high \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) pressure to maximize dissolved gas concentration.
  • Scuba Diving ("Bends"): Breathing compressed air underwater increases dissolved \(\mathrm{N_2}\) in blood. Rapid ascent lowers pressure, releasing \(\mathrm{N_2}\) bubbles that block capillaries (decompression sickness). To prevent this, diving tanks use air diluted with helium (which has a very low solubility/high \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) in blood).
  • High-Altitude Anoxia: Lower partial pressure of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) at high altitudes reduces blood oxygen levels, causing weakness and impaired cognition.
Worked Example 2: Applying Henry's Law

At \(298\ \mathrm{K}\), assume Henry's law constant for oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)) in water is \(4.0 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{bar}\). If \(\mathrm{O_2}\) exerts a partial pressure of \(0.20\ \mathrm{bar}\) above \(1.0\ \mathrm{L}\) of water (containing \(55.5\ \mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)), how many millimoles of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) dissolve in the water?

Solution:
\[ x_{\mathrm{O_2}} = \frac{p_{\mathrm{O_2}}}{K_{\mathrm{H}}} = \frac{0.20\ \mathrm{bar}}{4.0 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{bar}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \]

Since \(n_{\mathrm{O_2}} \ll 55.5\ \mathrm{mol}\), we approximate \(x_{\mathrm{O_2}} \approx \dfrac{n_{\mathrm{O_2}}}{55.5}\):

\[ n_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 55.5\ \mathrm{mol} = 2.775 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol} = 0.278\ \mathrm{mmol} \]

4. Vapour Pressure, Raoult's Law, and Ideal vs. Non-Ideal Solutions

Volatile Liquid–Liquid Solutions

In a closed container holding two volatile liquids \(1\) and \(2\), both evaporate until vapour–liquid equilibrium is reached. According to Raoult's Law, at a given temperature, the partial vapour pressure of each volatile component is directly proportional to its mole fraction in the liquid phase:

\[ p_1 = p_1^0\,x_1, \qquad p_2 = p_2^0\,x_2 \] \[ p_{\text{total}} = p_1 + p_2 = x_1 p_1^0 + x_2 p_2^0 = p_1^0 + (p_2^0 - p_1^0)x_2 \]

Here \(p_1^0\) and \(p_2^0\) are the vapour pressures of pure components \(1\) and \(2\) at the same temperature. By Dalton's law of partial pressures, the mole fraction \(y_i\) of component \(i\) in the vapour phase is:

\[ y_1 = \frac{p_1}{p_{\text{total}}}, \qquad y_2 = \frac{p_2}{p_{\text{total}}} \]

Crucial Principle: At equilibrium, the vapour phase is always enriched in the more volatile component (the one with the higher pure vapour pressure \(p^0\)).

Notice that Raoult's law (\(p_1 = p_1^0 x_1\)) is mathematically identical to Henry's law (\(p = K_{\mathrm{H}} x\)) when \(K_{\mathrm{H}} = p_1^0\). Thus, Raoult's law can be viewed as a special case of Henry's law.

Solutions of Non-Volatile Solids in Volatile Liquids

When a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a volatile solvent, solute particles occupy a fraction of the liquid surface, reducing the number of solvent molecules escaping into the vapour phase. Consequently, the vapour pressure of the solution (\(p_1\)) is lower than that of the pure solvent (\(p_1^0\)) and equals \(p_1 = p_1^0 x_1\).

Ideal and Non-Ideal Solutions

An ideal solution obeys Raoult's law over the entire composition range because \(A\text{–}B\) intermolecular attractions match \(A\text{–}A\) and \(B\text{–}B\) attractions. Real mixtures often deviate positively or negatively:

Property Ideal Solution Positive Deviation Negative Deviation
Intermolecular Forces \(F_{A-B} \approx F_{A-A}, F_{B-B}\) \(F_{A-B} < F_{A-A}, F_{B-B}\) \(F_{A-B} > F_{A-A}, F_{B-B}\)
Vapour Pressure \(p_i = p_i^0 x_i\) \(p_i > p_i^0 x_i\) (higher) \(p_i < p_i^0 x_i\) (lower)
Enthalpy of Mixing \(\Delta_{\text{mix}} H = 0\) \(\Delta_{\text{mix}} H > 0\) (endothermic) \(\Delta_{\text{mix}} H < 0\) (exothermic)
Volume of Mixing \(\Delta_{\text{mix}} V = 0\) \(\Delta_{\text{mix}} V > 0\) (expansion) \(\Delta_{\text{mix}} V < 0\) (contraction)
Azeotrope Formed None (separable by fractional distillation) Minimum boiling azeotrope (for large deviation) Maximum boiling azeotrope (for large deviation)
Classic Examples Benzene + toluene; \(n\)-hexane + \(n\)-heptane; bromoethane + chloroethane Ethanol + acetone; \(\mathrm{CS_2}\) + acetone; ethanol + water Chloroform + acetone; phenol + aniline; \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) + water

Azeotropes are constant-boiling binary mixtures that have identical compositions in the liquid and vapour phases, making complete separation by fractional distillation impossible. Examples include \(95\%\ \mathrm{(V/V)}\) ethanol–water (minimum boiling azeotrope) and \(68\%\ \mathrm{(w/w)}\) \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\)–water (maximum boiling azeotrope, boiling at \(393.5\ \mathrm{K}\)).

Worked Example 3: Raoult's Law and Vapour Composition

Two hypothetical volatile liquids \(A\) and \(B\) form an ideal solution at \(300\ \mathrm{K}\) with pure vapour pressures \(p_A^0 = 120\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}\) and \(p_B^0 = 360\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}\). If \(2.0\ \mathrm{mol}\) of \(A\) and \(3.0\ \mathrm{mol}\) of \(B\) are mixed, calculate (a) the total vapour pressure of the solution and (b) the mole fraction of \(B\) in the vapour phase.

Solution:

Liquid-phase mole fractions:

\[ x_A = \frac{2.0}{2.0 + 3.0} = 0.40, \qquad x_B = \frac{3.0}{2.0 + 3.0} = 0.60 \]

(a) Partial and total pressures:

\[ p_A = 0.40 \times 120 = 48\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}, \qquad p_B = 0.60 \times 360 = 216\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg} \] \[ p_{\text{total}} = 48 + 216 = 264\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg} \]

(b) Vapour-phase mole fraction of \(B\):

\[ y_B = \frac{p_B}{p_{\text{total}}} = \frac{216}{264} = \frac{9}{11} \approx 0.818 \]

Notice that \(y_B (0.818) > x_B (0.60)\) because \(B\) is the more volatile component.

5. Colligative Properties and Molar Mass Determination

Colligative properties are properties of dilute solutions that depend strictly on the number of solute particles relative to solvent particles, and not on the chemical identity of the solute. All four colligative properties stem from the lowering of solvent vapour pressure caused by a non-volatile solute.

5.1 Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure (RLVP)

For a non-volatile solute in a volatile solvent, \(p_1 = p_1^0 x_1\). Since \(x_2 = 1 - x_1\), the lowering of vapour pressure is \(\Delta p_1 = p_1^0 - p_1 = p_1^0 x_2\). Dividing by \(p_1^0\) gives the relative lowering of vapour pressure:

\[ \frac{p_1^0 - p_1}{p_1^0} = x_2 = \frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2} \approx \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{w_2 \cdot M_1}{M_2 \cdot w_1} \quad (\text{for dilute solutions, } n_2 \ll n_1) \]

5.2 Elevation of Boiling Point (\(\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}\))

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals external atmospheric pressure (\(1.013\ \mathrm{bar}\)). Because a non-volatile solute lowers the vapour pressure, the solution must be heated to a higher temperature \(T_{\mathrm{b}} > T_{\mathrm{b}}^0\) to boil.

\[ \Delta T_{\mathrm{b}} = T_{\mathrm{b}} - T_{\mathrm{b}}^0 = K_{\mathrm{b}}\,m = \frac{K_{\mathrm{b}} \times 1000 \times w_2}{M_2 \times w_1} \]

Here \(K_{\mathrm{b}}\) is the ebullioscopic constant (molal boiling point elevation constant, unit: \(\mathrm{K\ kg\ mol^{-1}}\); for water, \(K_{\mathrm{b}} = 0.52\ \mathrm{K\ kg\ mol^{-1}}\)).

5.3 Depression of Freezing Point (\(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\))

At the freezing point, the liquid and solid phases share the same vapour pressure. Lowering the liquid's vapour pressure shifts the intersection with the solid solvent's vapour pressure curve to a lower temperature \(T_{\mathrm{f}} < T_{\mathrm{f}}^0\):

\[ \Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} = T_{\mathrm{f}}^0 - T_{\mathrm{f}} = K_{\mathrm{f}}\,m = \frac{K_{\mathrm{f}} \times 1000 \times w_2}{M_2 \times w_1} \]

Here \(K_{\mathrm{f}}\) is the cryoscopic constant (molal freezing point depression constant; for water, \(K_{\mathrm{f}} = 1.86\ \mathrm{K\ kg\ mol^{-1}}\)). Both \(K_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(K_{\mathrm{f}}\) depend solely on the solvent and are related to its molar enthalpies of vapourisation (\(\Delta_{\text{vap}} H\)) and fusion (\(\Delta_{\text{fus}} H\)):

\[ K_{\mathrm{b}} = \frac{R\,M_1\,(T_{\mathrm{b}}^0)^2}{1000\,\Delta_{\text{vap}} H}, \qquad K_{\mathrm{f}} = \frac{R\,M_1\,(T_{\mathrm{f}}^0)^2}{1000\,\Delta_{\text{fus}} H} \]

5.4 Osmosis, Osmotic Pressure (\(\Pi\)), and Reverse Osmosis

Osmosis is the spontaneous net flow of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane (SPM) from pure solvent (or a dilute solution) into a more concentrated solution. The excess hydrostatic pressure that must be applied to the solution side just to stop this solvent flow is the osmotic pressure (\(\Pi\)):

\[ \Pi = C\,R\,T = \frac{n_2}{V}\,R\,T = \frac{w_2\,R\,T}{M_2\,V} \]

where \(C\) is molarity (\(\mathrm{mol\ L^{-1}}\)), \(V\) is solution volume in litres, and \(R = 0.083\ \mathrm{L\ bar\ K^{-1}\ mol^{-1}}\) (or \(0.0821\ \mathrm{L\ atm\ K^{-1}\ mol^{-1}}\)).

  • Why \(\Pi\) is preferred for macromolecules (proteins, polymers): It produces a large, easily measurable pressure even for very dilute solutions, and measurements are made at room temperature, preventing thermal denaturation of biomolecules.
  • Tonicity: Two solutions with equal osmotic pressure at the same temperature are isotonic (e.g., \(0.9\%\ \mathrm{w/V}\) aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), normal saline, is isotonic with human blood cells). A solution with higher osmotic pressure is hypertonic (cells placed in it lose water and shrink/crenate); one with lower osmotic pressure is hypotonic (cells absorb water and swell).
  • Reverse Osmosis (RO): Applying an external pressure \(P > \Pi\) on the concentrated solution side reverses solvent flow, forcing pure water out through an SPM (such as cellulose acetate) for seawater desalination.
Worked Example 4: Molar Mass from Freezing Point Depression

An assumed mass of \(4.5\ \mathrm{g}\) of a non-volatile, non-electrolyte organic solid is dissolved in \(125\ \mathrm{g}\) of water. The solution freezes at \(272.25\ \mathrm{K}\) (pure water freezes at \(273.15\ \mathrm{K}\); \(K_{\mathrm{f}} = 1.86\ \mathrm{K\ kg\ mol^{-1}}\)). Determine the molar mass of the solute.

Solution:
\[ \Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} = 273.15\ \mathrm{K} - 272.25\ \mathrm{K} = 0.90\ \mathrm{K} \] \[ M_2 = \frac{1000 \times K_{\mathrm{f}} \times w_2}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} \times w_1} = \frac{1000 \times 1.86 \times 4.5}{0.90 \times 125} = \frac{8370}{112.5} = 74.4\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}} \]
Worked Example 5: Molar Mass from Osmotic Pressure

A \(500\ \mathrm{mL}\) aqueous solution containing \(2.50\ \mathrm{g}\) of a synthetic polymer exhibits an osmotic pressure of \(2.49 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{bar}\) at \(300\ \mathrm{K}\). Taking \(R = 0.083\ \mathrm{L\ bar\ K^{-1}\ mol^{-1}}\), calculate the molar mass of the polymer.

Solution:

Given \(V = 500\ \mathrm{mL} = 0.500\ \mathrm{L}\):

\[ M_2 = \frac{w_2\,R\,T}{\Pi\,V} = \frac{2.50 \times 0.083 \times 300}{2.49 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.500} = 50{,}000\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}} \]

6. Abnormal Molar Masses and the van't Hoff Factor (\(i\))

When a solute dissociates into ions in solution (e.g., \(\mathrm{KCl}\) or \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\) in water), the total number of particles increases, making the observed colligative property larger and the calculated molar mass smaller than the true value. Conversely, when a solute associates (e.g., ethanoic acid or benzoic acid dimerizing via hydrogen bonding in low-dielectric solvents like benzene), the particle count drops, reducing the colligative property and yielding a higher apparent molar mass.

To account for this, van't Hoff introduced the factor \(i\):

\[ i = \frac{\text{Observed colligative property}}{\text{Calculated colligative property (no association/dissociation)}} = \frac{M_{\text{normal}}}{M_{\text{abnormal}}} = \frac{\text{Total moles of particles at equilibrium}}{\text{Initial moles of solute}} \]
  • For dissociation (\(A_xB_y \rightarrow n\text{ ions}\), degree of dissociation \(\alpha\)): \[ i = 1 + (n - 1)\alpha \quad \implies \quad \alpha = \frac{i - 1}{n - 1} \] Here \(i > 1\) (for complete dissociation of \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(i \to 2\); for \(\mathrm{K_2SO_4}\), \(i \to 3\)).
  • For association (\(n\,A \rightarrow A_n\), degree of association \(\alpha\)): \[ i = 1 + \left(\frac{1}{n} - 1\right)\alpha \quad \implies \quad \alpha = \frac{1 - i}{1 - \frac{1}{n}} \] Here \(i < 1\) (for complete dimerization, \(n = 2, \alpha = 1 \implies i = 0.5\)).
Modified Colligative Property Equations: \[ \frac{p_1^0 - p_1}{p_1^0} = i\,\frac{n_2}{n_1}, \qquad \Delta T_{\mathrm{b}} = i\,K_{\mathrm{b}}\,m, \qquad \Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} = i\,K_{\mathrm{f}}\,m, \qquad \Pi = i\,C\,R\,T \]
Worked Example 6: Degree of Dissociation from Freezing Point

A \(0.10\ \mathrm{m}\) aqueous solution of a weak monobasic acid \(\mathrm{HA}\) freezes at \(-0.2046^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Given \(K_{\mathrm{f}}\) of water \(= 1.86\ \mathrm{K\ kg\ mol^{-1}}\), calculate the van't Hoff factor \(i\) and the percentage dissociation of \(\mathrm{HA}\).

Solution:

Observed depression: \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} = 0 - (-0.2046) = 0.2046\ \mathrm{K}\).

\[ i = \frac{\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}}{K_{\mathrm{f}}\,m} = \frac{0.2046}{1.86 \times 0.10} = \frac{0.2046}{0.186} = 1.10 \]

For \(\mathrm{HA} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{A^-}\), \(n = 2\). Therefore:

\[ \alpha = \frac{i - 1}{n - 1} = \frac{1.10 - 1}{2 - 1} = 0.10 \quad (10\%\text{ dissociated}) \]

Practice Question 1

Which of the following concentration units is independent of temperature?

  • (A) Molarity
  • (B) Mass by volume percentage
  • (C) Molality
  • (D) Volume percentage
Answer: (C)

Solution: Molality is defined as moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. Since neither mass nor number of moles varies with temperature, molality is temperature-independent. All volume-based units (molarity, \(\%\ \mathrm{w/V}\), \(\%\ \mathrm{V/V}\)) change with temperature.

Practice Question 2

An aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) (molar mass \(= 40\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)) is \(20\%\ \mathrm{(w/w)}\) and has a density of \(1.20\ \mathrm{g\ mL^{-1}}\). What is the molarity of this solution?

  • (A) \(4.0\ \mathrm{M}\)
  • (B) \(5.0\ \mathrm{M}\)
  • (C) \(6.0\ \mathrm{M}\)
  • (D) \(6.25\ \mathrm{M}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: In \(100\ \mathrm{g}\) of solution, mass of \(\mathrm{NaOH} = 20\ \mathrm{g}\) (\(0.5\ \mathrm{mol}\)) and volume \(= \dfrac{100}{1.20}\ \mathrm{mL} = \dfrac{1}{12}\ \mathrm{L}\). Thus, \(M = \dfrac{0.5}{1/12} = 6.0\ \mathrm{M}\).

Practice Question 3

At \(298\ \mathrm{K}\), Henry's law constants \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) in water for four gases \(P, Q, R,\) and \(S\) are \(0.5\ \mathrm{kbar}\), \(2.0\ \mathrm{kbar}\), \(35\ \mathrm{kbar}\), and \(80\ \mathrm{kbar}\), respectively. At identical partial pressure, which gas is most soluble in water?

  • (A) \(P\)
  • (B) \(Q\)
  • (C) \(R\)
  • (D) \(S\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: By Henry's law, \(x = \dfrac{p}{K_{\mathrm{H}}}\). At constant \(p\), solubility (\(x\)) is inversely proportional to \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\). Gas \(P\) has the smallest \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) (\(0.5\ \mathrm{kbar}\)), so it is the most soluble.

Practice Question 4

Liquids \(X\) and \(Y\) form an ideal solution at \(310\ \mathrm{K}\) with pure vapour pressures \(p_X^0 = 100\ \mathrm{torr}\) and \(p_Y^0 = 300\ \mathrm{torr}\). For an equimolar liquid mixture of \(X\) and \(Y\), what is the mole fraction of \(X\) in the vapour phase?

  • (A) \(0.25\)
  • (B) \(0.50\)
  • (C) \(0.75\)
  • (D) \(0.33\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: For an equimolar mixture, \(x_X = x_Y = 0.5\). Partial pressures: \(p_X = 0.5 \times 100 = 50\ \mathrm{torr}\) and \(p_Y = 0.5 \times 300 = 150\ \mathrm{torr}\). Total pressure \(p_{\text{total}} = 200\ \mathrm{torr}\). Hence, \(y_X = \dfrac{50}{200} = 0.25\).

Practice Question 5

Which of the following binary mixtures exhibits negative deviation from Raoult's law and forms a maximum boiling azeotrope?

  • (A) Ethanol and acetone
  • (B) Benzene and toluene
  • (C) Chloroform and acetone
  • (D) \(n\)-Hexane and \(n\)-heptane
Answer: (C)

Solution: Chloroform (\(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\)) forms intermolecular hydrogen bonds with the carbonyl oxygen of acetone, making \(A\text{–}B\) attractions stronger than \(A\text{–}A\) or \(B\text{–}B\) attractions. This lowers escaping tendency, producing negative deviation and a maximum boiling azeotrope.

Practice Question 6

At \(298\ \mathrm{K}\), the vapour pressure of pure water is \(24.0\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}\). When \(18\ \mathrm{g}\) of glucose (molar mass \(= 180\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)) is dissolved in \(178.2\ \mathrm{g}\) of water (molar mass \(= 18\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)), the vapour pressure of the resulting solution is:

  • (A) \(23.76\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}\)
  • (B) \(23.24\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}\)
  • (C) \(24.24\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}\)
  • (D) \(23.50\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Moles of glucose \(n_2 = \dfrac{18}{180} = 0.1\ \mathrm{mol}\); moles of water \(n_1 = \dfrac{178.2}{18} = 9.9\ \mathrm{mol}\). Mole fraction of water \(x_1 = \dfrac{9.9}{9.9 + 0.1} = 0.99\). Therefore, \(p_1 = p_1^0\,x_1 = 24.0 \times 0.99 = 23.76\ \mathrm{mm\ Hg}\).

Practice Question 7

Assuming complete dissociation of electrolytes, which of the following \(0.01\ \mathrm{m}\) aqueous solutions will have the highest boiling point?

  • (A) Urea
  • (B) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{MgCl_2}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Al_2(SO_4)_3}\)
Answer: (D)

Solution: Boiling point elevation is \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}} = i\,K_{\mathrm{b}}\,m\). For equal molality, the solution with the largest van't Hoff factor \(i\) has the highest boiling point. Values of \(i\) are: urea \(= 1\), \(\mathrm{NaCl} = 2\), \(\mathrm{MgCl_2} = 3\), and \(\mathrm{Al_2(SO_4)_3} = 5\).

Practice Question 8

A \(5\%\ \mathrm{(w/V)}\) aqueous solution of cane sugar (molar mass \(= 342\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)) is isotonic with a \(1\%\ \mathrm{(w/V)}\) aqueous solution of an unknown non-electrolyte solute \(Z\) at the same temperature. What is the molar mass of \(Z\)?

  • (A) \(34.2\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(68.4\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(171.0\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(136.8\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Isotonic solutions at the same temperature have equal molarities: \(C_{\text{sugar}} = C_Z\). In \(100\ \mathrm{mL}\) (\(0.1\ \mathrm{L}\)) of solution, \(\dfrac{5}{342 \times 0.1} = \dfrac{1}{M_Z \times 0.1} \implies M_Z = \dfrac{342}{5} = 68.4\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 9

Benzoic acid dimerizes in benzene to the extent of \(80\%\). What is the van't Hoff factor (\(i\)) of benzoic acid in this solution?

  • (A) \(0.20\)
  • (B) \(0.60\)
  • (C) \(0.80\)
  • (D) \(1.40\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: For dimerization (\(n = 2\)) with \(\alpha = 0.80\): \(i = 1 + \left(\dfrac{1}{2} - 1\right)\alpha = 1 - \dfrac{0.80}{2} = 0.60\).

Practice Question 10 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Human red blood cells shrink when placed in a \(1.5\%\ \mathrm{(w/V)}\) aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) solution.
Reason (R): A \(1.5\%\ \mathrm{(w/V)}\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) solution is hypertonic compared to the intracellular fluid of red blood cells (which is isotonic with \(0.9\%\ \mathrm{w/V}\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)), causing water to move out of the cells by osmosis.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Normal saline is \(0.9\%\ \mathrm{(w/V)}\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\). Because \(1.5\%\ \mathrm{(w/V)}\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) has a higher osmotic pressure (hypertonic), water flows out of the cells across the cell membrane, causing them to shrink.

8. Common Pitfalls and Final Revision Checklist

Avoid These Frequent NEET Mistakes:
  • Mass of solvent vs. mass of solution: Molality (\(m\)) uses kilograms of solvent in the denominator, not total solution mass.
  • Liquid vs. vapour mole fraction: Do not confuse \(x_i\) (liquid phase) with \(y_i\) (vapour phase); always use \(p_i = p_i^0 x_i\) first, then \(y_i = p_i / p_{\text{total}}\).
  • Forgetting the van't Hoff factor (\(i\)): Always check whether a solute is a non-electrolyte (\(i = 1\)), strong electrolyte, weak electrolyte, or associating species before computing \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\), or \(\Pi\).
  • Temperature units: In \(\Pi = C R T\), temperature \(T\) must always be in Kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\)), and \(R\) must match the pressure and volume units.

Concise Final Recap

Solutions are governed by composition and intermolecular interactions. Mass-based concentration units (\(m, x, \%\ \mathrm{w/w}\)) remain invariant with temperature, while Henry's law (\(p = K_{\mathrm{H}} x\)) and Raoult's law (\(p_i = p_i^0 x_i\)) quantify vapour–liquid equilibria. Deviations from ideality arise from unequal \(A\text{–}B\) versus \(A\text{–}A/B\text{–}B\) forces and can produce constant-boiling azeotropes. Finally, the four colligative properties—RLVP, \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}\), \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\), and \(\Pi\)—depend solely on the effective particle concentration, scaled by the van't Hoff factor \(i\) whenever solutes dissociate or associate.
