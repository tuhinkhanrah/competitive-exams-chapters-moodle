NEET UG Organic Chemistry

Amines and Arenediazonium Salts

A complete, self-contained tutorial covering structure, nomenclature, physical trends, synthetic routes, basicity equilibria, chemical tests, electrophilic substitution of aniline, and diazonium salt transformations.

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Scope

Nitrogen-containing organic bases—amines—and their aromatic diazonium derivatives are among the highest-yield functional groups in the NEET UG syllabus. Questions frequently test subtle trends in aqueous basicity, selective synthetic conversions (carbon-chain ascent and descent), chemical tests to distinguish \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) amines, and the synthetic versatility of benzenediazonium chloride.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Describe the \(sp^3\) hybridized, pyramidal geometry of amines and classify them as \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), or \(3^\circ\).
  • Assign both common and IUPAC names to aliphatic, heterocyclic-free aryl, and polyfunctional amines.
  • Predict boiling point and water-solubility trends using intermolecular hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic surface area.
  • Select appropriate synthetic routes (nitro reduction, ammonolysis, nitrile/amide reduction, Gabriel phthalimide synthesis, and Hoffmann bromamide degradation) and identify their structural scope.
  • Explain basicity orders in both gas and aqueous phases by balancing inductive (\(+I\)), solvation, steric, and resonance effects.
  • Differentiate \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) amines using Hinsberg's reagent, carbylamine reaction, and nitrous acid.
  • Control electrophilic aromatic substitution of aniline via acetylation and utilize arenediazonium salts to synthesize haloarenes, cyanides, phenols, nitroarenes, and azo dyes.

2. Structure, Classification, and Nomenclature

2.1 Biological and Industrial Significance

Amines are organic derivatives of ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)) formed by replacing one, two, or all three hydrogen atoms with alkyl (\(\mathrm{R-}\)) or aryl (\(\mathrm{Ar-}\)) groups. Naturally, amino groups appear in proteins, vitamins, alkaloids, and hormones. Clinically important examples include adrenaline and ephedrine (both contain a secondary amino group and are used to elevate blood pressure), Novocain (a synthetic amino compound used as a dental anaesthetic), and Benadryl (an antihistamine containing a tertiary amino group). Quaternary ammonium salts serve as surfactants, while arenediazonium salts act as key intermediates for medicines, synthetic fibres, and azo dyes.

2.2 Orbital Hybridization and Geometry

In amines, the trivalent nitrogen atom forms three \(\sigma\)-bonds (to carbon or hydrogen) and retains one unshared pair of electrons in its fourth valence orbital. Consequently, nitrogen is \(sp^3\) hybridized with a tetrahedral electron-pair arrangement and a pyramidal molecular geometry. Because lone-pair–bond-pair repulsion exceeds bond-pair–bond-pair repulsion, the \(\mathrm{C-N-E}\) bond angle (where \(\mathrm{E}\) is \(\mathrm{C}\) or \(\mathrm{H}\)) is compressed slightly below the ideal tetrahedral value of \(109.5^\circ\). For instance, in trimethylamine, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}\), the \(\mathrm{C-N-C}\) angle is \(108^\circ\).

2.3 Classification of Amines

Unlike alcohols and alkyl halides (whose degree depends on the carbon bearing the functional group), the degree of an amine is determined solely by the number of carbon groups (alkyl or aryl) directly bonded to the nitrogen atom:

  • Primary (\(1^\circ\)) amines (\(\mathrm{R-NH_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Ar-NH_2}\)): One hydrogen of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) is replaced. Note that tert-butylamine, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-NH_2}\), is a \(1^\circ\) amine because nitrogen is attached to only one carbon atom.
  • Secondary (\(2^\circ\)) amines (\(\mathrm{R-NH-R'}\) or \(\mathrm{Ar-NH-R}\)): Two hydrogens of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) are replaced.
  • Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) amines (\(\mathrm{R_3N}\) or \(\mathrm{RNR'R''}\)): All three hydrogens of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) are replaced; no \(\mathrm{N-H}\) bond remains.

Secondary and tertiary amines are termed simple amines when all attached alkyl/aryl groups are identical, and mixed amines when at least two groups differ.

2.4 Common and IUPAC Nomenclature

In the common system, aliphatic amines are named as alkylamine (one word), using prefixes di- or tri- when identical groups repeat. In the IUPAC system, primary amines are named as alkanamines by replacing the terminal -e of the parent alkane with -amine. When multiple \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) groups are present, the terminal -e of the alkane is retained (e.g., ethane-1,2-diamine). For \(2^\circ\) and \(3^\circ\) amines, the longest carbon chain forms the parent alkanamine, and remaining substituents on nitrogen are cited with the locant \(N-\). For arylamines, replacing the -e of the arene with -amine gives benzenamine (though aniline is also an accepted IUPAC name).

3. Physical Properties and Hydrogen Bonding

3.1 Physical State, Odour, and Solubility

  • State and Odour: Lower aliphatic amines (e.g., methanamine, ethanamine) are gases with a characteristic fishy odour. Primary amines with three or more carbons are liquids, and higher homologues are solids. Pure arylamines such as aniline are colourless, but darken upon storage due to atmospheric oxidation.
  • Solubility: Lower aliphatic amines dissolve readily in water because they form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. As molar mass increases, the hydrophobic alkyl portion grows and aqueous solubility drops sharply; higher amines and aniline are essentially insoluble in water. All amines dissolve well in organic solvents such as alcohols, ethers, and benzene.
  • Amines vs. Alcohols: Oxygen (electronegativity \(3.5\)) is more electronegative than nitrogen (electronegativity \(3.0\)). Therefore, \(\mathrm{O-H}\) bonds are more polar than \(\mathrm{N-H}\) bonds, making alcohols more soluble in water and higher-boiling than amines of comparable molar mass.

3.2 Boiling Point Order of Isomeric Amines

Among isomeric amines of identical molar mass, boiling points follow the order:

\[\text{Primary } (1^\circ) > \text{Secondary } (2^\circ) > \text{Tertiary } (3^\circ)\]

Primary amines possess two \(\mathrm{N-H}\) hydrogens and a lone pair, enabling extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Secondary amines have only one \(\mathrm{N-H}\) hydrogen, resulting in weaker association. Tertiary amines lack any \(\mathrm{N-H}\) bond and cannot form intermolecular hydrogen bonds with one another; their boiling points are governed only by weaker dipole–dipole and van der Waals forces. For example, among \(\mathrm{C_4H_{11}N}\) isomers (molar mass \(73\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)), butan-1-amine boils at \(350.8\,\mathrm{K}\), N-ethylethanamine at \(329.3\,\mathrm{K}\), and N,N-dimethylethanamine at \(310.5\,\mathrm{K}\) (compared to \(390.3\,\mathrm{K}\) for butan-1-ol and \(300.8\,\mathrm{K}\) for 2-methylbutane).

5. Basic Character of Amines (High-Yield NEET Topic)

5.1 Quantitative Measure: \(K_b\) and \(\mathrm{p}K_b\)

Because nitrogen carries an unshared electron pair, amines act as Lewis bases and Brønsted–Lowry proton acceptors. They react with mineral acids to form water-soluble ammonium salts, which regenerate the free amine upon treatment with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\)—a property used to separate amines from water-insoluble neutral organic compounds.

\[ \mathrm{R-NH_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{R-\overset{+}{N}H_3} + \mathrm{OH^-} \qquad K_b = \frac{[\mathrm{R-\overset{+}{N}H_3}][\mathrm{OH^-}]}{[\mathrm{R-NH_2}]} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_b = -\log K_b \]

Rule: A larger \(K_b\) or a smaller \(\mathrm{p}K_b\) corresponds to a stronger base. For reference, \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) has \(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.75\).

5.2 Gas Phase vs. Aqueous Phase Basicity of Aliphatic Amines

In the gas phase (absence of solvent), basicity is governed exclusively by the electron-releasing inductive (\(+I\)) effect of alkyl groups, which increases electron density on nitrogen and disperses the positive charge of the resulting ammonium ion:

\[ \text{Gas phase: } \quad 3^\circ\text{ amine} > 2^\circ\text{ amine} > 1^\circ\text{ amine} > \mathrm{NH_3} \]

In aqueous solution, three competing factors operate simultaneously:

  1. Inductive (\(+I\)) effect: Favours \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\).
  2. Solvation (hydration) of the conjugate acid: Protonated \(1^\circ\) amines (\(\mathrm{R\overset{+}{N}H_3}\)) form three H-bonds with water, \(2^\circ\) (\(\mathrm{R_2\overset{+}{N}H_2}\)) form two, and \(3^\circ\) (\(\mathrm{R_3\overset{+}{N}H}\)) form only one. Thus, solvation stability follows \(1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ\).
  3. Steric hindrance: Bulky alkyl groups impede water molecules from approaching the protonated nitrogen.
Tutorial Diagram 3: Solvation stabilization order of alkylammonium cations in water (1° > 2° > 3°).

Combining \(+I\), solvation, and steric effects yields two famous aqueous basicity orders that are frequently tested:

Memorize These Exact Aqueous Basicity Orders
  • Methyl-substituted amines (water): \( (\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{NH} \;(\mathrm{p}K_b = 3.27) > \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} \;(3.38) > (\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{N} \;(4.22) > \mathrm{NH_3} \;(4.75) \)
  • Ethyl-substituted amines (water): \( (\mathrm{C_2H_5})_2\mathrm{NH} \;(\mathrm{p}K_b = 3.00) > (\mathrm{C_2H_5})_3\mathrm{N} \;(3.25) > \mathrm{C_2H_5NH_2} \;(3.29) > \mathrm{NH_3} \;(4.75) \)

5.3 Arylamines vs. Ammonia and Substituent Effects

Aniline (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 9.38\)) is a dramatically weaker base than ammonia (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.75\)) and aliphatic amines. In aniline, the lone pair on nitrogen is delocalized into the benzene ring across five resonance structures (placing negative charge at ortho and para carbons). When aniline accepts a proton to form the anilinium ion (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5\overset{+}{N}H_3}\)), the lone pair is tied up in an \(\mathrm{N-H}\) bond, leaving only two Kekulé resonance structures. Because aniline is far more resonance-stabilized than the anilinium ion, protonation is thermodynamically unfavourable.

By contrast, benzylamine (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2NH_2}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.70\)) has an insulating \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) spacer that prevents lone-pair conjugation with the ring, making it nearly as basic as ammonia and much stronger than aniline.

  • Electron-donating groups (EDG) such as \(-\mathrm{OCH_3}\) and \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\) (especially at para) increase electron density on nitrogen and increase basicity (e.g., p-toluidine > aniline).
  • Electron-withdrawing groups (EWG) such as \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\), \(-\mathrm{SO_3H}\), \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), and halogens (\(-\mathrm{X}\)) pull electron density away and decrease basicity (e.g., p-nitroaniline < aniline).

6. Chemical Reactions and Distinguishing Tests

6.1 Acylation and Benzoylation

Primary and secondary amines (both aliphatic and aromatic) undergo nucleophilic acyl substitution with acid chlorides, acid anhydrides, or esters to form amides. A stronger base such as pyridine is added to scavenge the \(\mathrm{HCl}\) by-product and drive equilibrium to the right. Tertiary amines lack an \(\mathrm{N-H}\) hydrogen and do not undergo acylation. Reaction with benzoyl chloride (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5COCl}\)) is termed benzoylation.

\[ \mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2} + \mathrm{(CH_3CO)_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{Pyridine}} \mathrm{C_6H_5NHCOCH_3}\text{ (Acetanilide)} + \mathrm{CH_3COOH} \]

6.3 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) of Aniline

Because the \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) group donates its lone pair strongly by resonance, it is an intensely activating, ortho/para-directing group. This high reactivity causes three classic synthetic complications:

  1. Polyhalogenation on Direct Bromination: Treating aniline with \(\mathrm{Br_2/H_2O}\) at room temperature immediately precipitates white 2,4,6-tribromoaniline. To obtain monosubstituted 4-bromoaniline, the \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) group is first protected by acetylation with \(\mathrm{(CH_3CO)_2O}\)/pyridine to form acetanilide (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5NHCOCH_3}\)). In acetanilide, the nitrogen lone pair is partially delocalized into the carbonyl oxygen, moderating ring activation; bromination with \(\mathrm{Br_2/CH_3COOH}\) followed by hydrolysis (\(\mathrm{H^+}\) or \(\mathrm{OH^-}\)) yields 4-bromoaniline as the major product.
  2. Unexpected meta-Substitution on Direct Nitration: Direct nitration of aniline with \(\mathrm{HNO_3/H_2SO_4}\) at \(288\,\mathrm{K}\) gives tarry oxidation products along with 51% p-nitroaniline, 47% m-nitroaniline, and 2% o-nitroaniline. In strongly acidic media, aniline is protonated to the anilinium ion (\(-\overset{+}{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{H_3}\)), which is strongly deactivating and meta-directing. Protecting \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) as acetanilide prior to nitration and then hydrolysing gives p-nitroaniline cleanly.
  3. Sulphonation and Zwitterion Formation: Heating aniline with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) at \(453\text{–}473\,\mathrm{K}\) converts anilinium hydrogensulphate into p-aminobenzenesulphonic acid (sulphanilic acid), which exists predominantly as a dipolar zwitterion (\(\mathrm{H_3\overset{+}{N}-C_6H_4-SO_3^-}\)).
Why Aniline Fails Friedel–Crafts Reactions

Aniline does not undergo Friedel–Crafts alkylation or acylation. The Lewis acid catalyst \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) coordinates with the basic \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) lone pair to form an insoluble salt (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5\overset{+}{N}H_2\text{–}\overset{-}{A}lCl_3}\)). The resulting positive charge on nitrogen strongly deactivates the benzene ring toward electrophilic attack.

7. Arenediazonium Salts and Their Synthetic Mastery

7.1 Diazotisation and Stability

Primary aromatic amines react with nitrous acid (generated in situ from \(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + 2HCl}\)) at ice-cold temperatures (\(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\)) to form arenediazonium salts (\(\mathrm{Ar-\overset{+}{N}\equiv N\,Cl^-}\)), a process called diazotisation:

\[ \mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2} + \mathrm{NaNO_2} + 2\,\mathrm{HCl} \xrightarrow{273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}} \mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^-} + \mathrm{NaCl} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} \]

Unlike alkyldiazonium salts (which decompose instantaneously even at low temperatures), arenediazonium ions are stabilized for short periods in cold aqueous solution by resonance delocalization of the positive charge into the aromatic ring. Benzenediazonium chloride is water-soluble and decomposes if warmed or dried, whereas benzenediazonium fluoroborate (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+BF_4^-}\)) is water-insoluble and stable at room temperature.

7.2 Reactions Involving Displacement of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) vs. Retention of Diazo Group

Because \(\mathrm{N_2}\) is an exceptional leaving group, arenediazonium salts enable the introduction of substituents (\(-\mathrm{F}\), \(-\mathrm{I}\), \(-\mathrm{CN}\), \(-\mathrm{OH}\), \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\)) that cannot be placed on a benzene ring by direct substitution. Conversely, under mild conditions with electron-rich aromatics, the \(-\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{N}-\) linkage is retained in azo coupling (an electrophilic aromatic substitution).

Azo Coupling Details: Benzenediazonium chloride couples at the para-position of phenol in weakly alkaline medium (\(\mathrm{OH^-}\)) to give p-hydroxyazobenzene (an orange dye), and with aniline in weakly acidic medium (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) to give p-aminoazobenzene (a yellow dye). Note that Sandmeyer reactions generally give better yields than Gattermann reactions.

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Predicting Aqueous Basicity Order

Problem: Arrange the following four compounds in increasing order of their basic strength in aqueous solution: p-nitroaniline, benzylamine, N-ethylethanamine, and ammonia.

Solution:

  • p-Nitroaniline is an arylamine whose nitrogen lone pair is delocalized into the benzene ring and further withdrawn by the strongly electron-withdrawing \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) group (\(-M, -I\)). It is the weakest base.
  • Ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.75\)) lacks any electron-releasing alkyl group.
  • Benzylamine (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2NH_2}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.70\)) has an \(sp^3\) \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) spacer preventing resonance with the ring; the phenyl ring exerts a mild \(-I\) effect, making benzylamine slightly stronger than \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) but weaker than simple aliphatic amines.
  • N-Ethylethanamine (\(\mathrm{(C_2H_5)_2NH}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_b = 3.00\)) is a \(2^\circ\) aliphatic amine enjoying both \(+I\) donation from two ethyl groups and good hydration of its conjugate acid.

Answer: p-Nitroaniline < Ammonia < Benzylamine < N-Ethylethanamine.

Worked Example 2: Multi-Step Conversion Using Diazonium Chemistry

Problem: Suggest a clean synthetic route to convert 3-nitrotoluene into 3-bromotoluene without forming ortho/para isomer mixtures.

Solution: Direct bromination of toluene gives a mixture of o- and p-bromotoluene, not the meta isomer. Instead, use the nitro group as a handle to install bromine via a diazonium intermediate:

  1. Reduce 3-nitrotoluene with \(\mathrm{Sn/HCl}\) (or \(\mathrm{Fe/HCl}\)) to obtain 3-methylaniline (m-toluidine).
  2. Diazotize 3-methylaniline using \(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + HCl}\) at \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\) to form 3-methylbenzenediazonium chloride.
  3. Treat the cold diazonium salt with \(\mathrm{Cu_2Br_2/HBr}\) (Sandmeyer reaction) to replace the \(-\mathrm{N_2^+Cl^-}\) group cleanly with \(-\mathrm{Br}\), yielding 3-bromotoluene.
Worked Example 3: Identifying Unknown Amines via Chemical Tests

Problem: Three isomeric amines \(\mathbf{X}\), \(\mathbf{Y}\), and \(\mathbf{Z}\) share the molecular formula \(\mathrm{C_3H_9N}\). Compound \(\mathbf{X}\) evolves a foul-smelling gas on warming with \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) and alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\). Compound \(\mathbf{Y}\) reacts with benzenesulphonyl chloride to give a solid insoluble in aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\). Compound \(\mathbf{Z}\) does not react with benzenesulphonyl chloride at all. Identify \(\mathbf{X}\), \(\mathbf{Y}\), and \(\mathbf{Z}\).

Solution:

  • A positive carbylamine test (\(\mathrm{CHCl_3/KOH}\)) identifies \(\mathbf{X}\) as a \(1^\circ\) amine: either propan-1-amine (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2NH_2}\)) or propan-2-amine (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHNH_2}\)).
  • An alkali-insoluble sulphonamide with Hinsberg's reagent identifies \(\mathbf{Y}\) as a \(2^\circ\) amine: N-methylethanamine (\(\mathrm{CH_3NHCH_2CH_3}\)).
  • No reaction with Hinsberg's reagent identifies \(\mathbf{Z}\) as a \(3^\circ\) amine: N,N-dimethylmethanamine (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}\)).
Worked Example 4: Chain-Ascent vs. Chain-Descent Synthesis

Problem: Starting from 1-bromobutane (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Br}\)), how would you prepare (a) pentan-1-amine and (b) propan-1-amine?

Solution:

  • (a) Ascent (+1 C): Treat 1-bromobutane with ethanolic \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) to form pentanenitrile (\(\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_3CN}\)), then reduce with \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) (or \(\mathrm{H_2/Ni}\)) to obtain pentan-1-amine.
  • (b) Descent (–1 C): Convert 1-bromobutane to butan-1-ol with aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\), oxidize to butanoic acid (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4/H^+}\)), heat with \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) to form butanamide (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CONH_2}\)), and apply Hoffmann bromamide degradation (\(\mathrm{Br_2 + 4NaOH}\)) to obtain propan-1-amine.

  • (C) Propan-1-amine

Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: Gabriel phthalimide synthesis proceeds via an \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) nucleophilic attack of the phthalimide anion on an alkyl or benzyl halide (\(\mathrm{R-X}\) or \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2X}\)), followed by alkaline hydrolysis. p-Toluidine (\(\mathrm{4-CH_3C_6H_4NH_2}\)) is a primary arylamine; preparing it would require an aryl halide (\(\mathrm{4-CH_3C_6H_4X}\)), which does not undergo nucleophilic substitution under these conditions due to partial double-bond character of the \(\mathrm{C-X}\) bond and ring electron density.

  • (A) \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{N} > (\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{NH} > \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} > \mathrm{NH_3}\)
  • (B) \((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{NH} > \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} > (\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{N} > \mathrm{NH_3}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{CH_3NH_2} > (\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{NH} > (\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{N} > \mathrm{NH_3}\)
  • (D) \((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{NH} > (\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{N} > \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} > \mathrm{NH_3}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: In aqueous solution, methylamines are governed by a balance of \(+I\) effect and hydration of the conjugate ammonium ion. Although \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{N}\) has three \(+I\) methyl groups, its conjugate acid \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3\overset{+}{N}H}\) forms only one H-bond with water and suffers steric hindrance to solvation, making it less basic than \(\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}\) (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 3.38\)) and \((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{NH}\) (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 3.27\)). Thus, the order is \((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{NH} > \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} > (\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{N} > \mathrm{NH_3}\).

Practice Question 3 (Assertion–Reason): Given below are two statements:

  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Even though \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) is strongly ortho/para-directing, the strongly acidic nitrating mixture protonates a large fraction of aniline to anilinium ion (\(\mathrm{-NH_3^+}\)). Because \(-\mathrm{NH_3^+}\) lacks a lone pair and withdraws electrons strongly, it directs incoming \(\mathrm{NO_2^+}\) electrophiles to the meta position, producing ~47% m-nitroaniline alongside 51% p-nitroaniline.

10. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Misclassifying tert-butylamine: \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-NH_2}\) is a \(1^\circ\) amine (nitrogen is bonded to only one carbon), even though the carbon is tertiary.
  • Confusing methyl vs. ethyl aqueous basicity: Remember \(\mathrm{2^\circ > 1^\circ > 3^\circ}\) for methylamines, but \(\mathrm{2^\circ > 3^\circ > 1^\circ}\) for ethylamines in water.
  • Forgetting the carbon count in Hoffmann bromamide vs. nitrile reduction: Hoffmann bromamide loses one carbon; \(\mathrm{R-X} \to \mathrm{R-CN} \to \mathrm{R-CH_2NH_2}\) gains one carbon.
  • Using Gabriel phthalimide for aniline: Gabriel synthesis works only for \(1^\circ\) aliphatic/benzylic amines, never for arylamines.
  • Mixing up azo-coupling pH conditions: Phenol couples in weakly alkaline medium (forming phenoxide ion, orange dye), whereas aniline couples in weakly acidic medium (yellow dye).
Quick Revision Checklist
  • [ ] Hybridization & angle: \(sp^3\) pyramidal, \(\sim 108^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}\).
  • [ ] Boiling point order: Alcohols > \(1^\circ\) amines > \(2^\circ\) amines > \(3^\circ\) amines > Alkanes (comparable molar mass).
  • [ ] Gas-phase basicity: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \mathrm{NH_3}\).
  • [ ] Aniline \(\mathrm{p}K_b\) (\(9.38\)) >> Benzylamine \(\mathrm{p}K_b\) (\(4.70\)) due to resonance delocalization in aniline.
  • [ ] Distinguishing tests: Carbylamine (\(1^\circ\) only), Hinsberg (\(1^\circ\) alkali-soluble, \(2^\circ\) alkali-insoluble, \(3^\circ\) no reaction), \(\mathrm{HNO_2}\) (\(1^\circ\) aliphatic gives \(\mathrm{N_2}\); \(1^\circ\) aromatic gives diazonium salt at \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\)).
  • [ ] Protecting aniline: Acetylation to acetanilide controls bromination and nitration to give para-substituted products cleanly.

11. Concise Final Recap

Amines are pyramidal \(sp^3\)-hybridized Lewis bases whose physical and chemical behaviour is dictated by the nitrogen lone pair and the number of \(\mathrm{N-H}\) hydrogens. Intermolecular H-bonding sets the boiling point order \(1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ\), while aqueous basicity reflects a delicate balance of \(+I\) donation, cation solvation, and steric hindrance—and drops sharply in arylamines due to ring resonance. Pure \(1^\circ\) aliphatic amines are best accessed via Gabriel phthalimide synthesis, nitrile/amide reduction, or Hoffmann bromamide degradation (one carbon shorter). Chemical differentiation relies on Hinsberg's reagent and the carbylamine test. Finally, controlling aniline's hyper-reactivity via acetylation and exploiting cold arenediazonium salts (\(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\)) unlocks reliable syntheses of haloarenes, phenols, nitriles, arenes, and brightly coloured azo dyes.
