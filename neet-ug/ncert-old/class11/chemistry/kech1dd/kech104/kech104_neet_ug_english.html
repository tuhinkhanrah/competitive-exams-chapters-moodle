Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

A complete, exam-oriented guide to electronic bonding theories, molecular geometry, orbital overlap, hybridisation, molecular orbital theory, and hydrogen bonding.

Learning Objectives and Exam Scope

Chemical bonding explains why isolated atoms—with the notable exception of noble gases—combine into stable molecules and ionic lattices, why only definite stoichiometries form, and why molecules adopt specific three-dimensional shapes. For NEET UG, this chapter is a high-yield pillar because geometry, polarity, bond order, and magnetic behaviour are tested both directly and across coordination chemistry and organic mechanisms.

Key Competencies You Will Master

  • Distinguish electrovalent (ionic) and covalent bonding using the Kössel–Lewis electronic approach, formal charge, and octet-rule exceptions.
  • Evaluate lattice stability and partial covalent character in ionic solids using Fajans' rules.
  • Correlate bond length, bond angle, bond enthalpy, bond order, resonance, and dipole moment.
  • Predict molecular shapes and bond-angle distortions with VSEPR theory.
  • Explain directional covalent bonding through Valence Bond Theory (\(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) overlap) and orbital hybridisation (\(sp\), \(sp^2\), \(sp^3\), \(sp^3d\), \(sp^3d^2\)).
  • Construct Molecular Orbital (MO) configurations for second-period homonuclear diatomics to determine bond order, relative stability, and paramagnetism.
  • Classify intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding and its impact on physical properties.

1. Electronic Theory of Valence: Kössel–Lewis Approach

Every chemical system tends toward a state of lower potential energy. Forming a chemical bond—the attractive force holding atoms or ions together—releases energy and stabilizes the resulting species. In 1916, W. Kössel and G. N. Lewis independently linked chemical reactivity to the electronic inertness of noble gases, whose valence shell (except helium's \(1s^2\) duplet) holds a stable octet configuration (\(ns^2np^6\)).

Lewis Symbols, Octet Rule, and Covalent Bonding

Only valence-shell electrons participate in bonding; inner-shell electrons remain tightly bound in the atomic kernel. A Lewis symbol places dots around an element's chemical symbol equal to its valence electrons. The group valence of a main-group element equals either the number of valence dots or \(8 - (\text{number of valence dots})\).

According to the octet rule, atoms attain a noble-gas valence configuration either by complete electron transfer (forming an electrovalent or ionic bond) or by sharing electron pairs (forming a covalent bond, named by Langmuir in 1919):

  • Single covalent bond: Sharing one electron pair between two atoms (for example, \(\mathrm{Cl-Cl}\) in \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) or \(\mathrm{O-H}\) in \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)).
  • Double covalent bond: Sharing two electron pairs (for example, \(\mathrm{O=C=O}\) in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) or \(\mathrm{C=C}\) in \(\mathrm{C_2H_4}\)).
  • Triple covalent bond: Sharing three electron pairs (for example, \(\mathrm{N\equiv N}\) in \(\mathrm{N_2}\) or \(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\) in \(\mathrm{C_2H_2}\)).

Systematic Procedure for Writing Lewis Structures

  1. Count total valence electrons (\(V\)): Sum the valence electrons of all atoms. Add \(1\) electron for each unit of negative charge (anions), or subtract \(1\) electron for each unit of positive charge (cations).
  2. Set the skeleton: Place the least electronegative atom in the central position (hydrogen and fluorine are always terminal).
  3. Distribute bonding and lone pairs: Draw single bonds to connect terminal atoms to the central atom. Allocate remaining electrons as lone pairs on terminal atoms first to complete their octets (duplet for \(\mathrm{H}\)), then on the central atom.
  4. Form multiple bonds if needed: If the central atom still lacks an octet, convert lone pairs from adjacent atoms into double or triple bonds.

Formal Charge

Formal charge tracks electron distribution within a Lewis structure assuming equal sharing of bonding pairs. It does not represent real ionic charges, but it helps identify the most stable (lowest-energy) canonical structure—namely, the structure with the smallest formal charges and any negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom.

Formal Charge Formula
\[\mathrm{F.C.} = V - L - \frac{1}{2}B\]

where \(V\) = valence electrons in the free neutral atom, \(L\) = number of non-bonding (lone-pair) electrons, and \(B\) = total number of bonding (shared) electrons.

Limitations of the Octet Rule

The octet rule works best for second-period elements (especially \(\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{N}\), \(\mathrm{O}\), \(\mathrm{F}\)) but fails in several well-defined cases:

Exception Category Characteristic Feature Representative Examples
Incomplete octet Central atom has fewer than 8 valence electrons (common for Group 1, 2, 13 elements). \(\mathrm{LiCl}\), \(\mathrm{BeH_2}\) (4 \(\mathrm{e^-}\)), \(\mathrm{BCl_3}\), \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) (6 \(\mathrm{e^-}\))
Odd-electron species Total valence electrons are odd; at least one atom has an unpaired electron. \(\mathrm{NO}\) (11 valence \(\mathrm{e^-}\)), \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) (17 valence \(\mathrm{e^-}\))

Additionally, the octet rule cannot predict molecular shapes or explain the relative bond energies and stabilities of molecules.

2. Ionic Bonding, Lattice Enthalpy, and Fajans' Rules

An ionic bond forms most readily between an electropositive metal with low ionization enthalpy (\(\Delta_i H\), always endothermic) and an electronegative non-metal with a large negative electron gain enthalpy (\(\Delta_{eg} H\)). Even when \(\Delta_i H + \Delta_{eg} H > 0\) in the gas phase (as for \(\mathrm{Na^+(g)} + \mathrm{Cl^-(g)}\), where \(495.8 - 348.7 = +147.1\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), the solid crystal is strongly stabilized by the energy released during 3D crystal-lattice formation.

Lattice Enthalpy is defined as the energy required to completely separate one mole of a solid ionic compound into its gaseous constituent ions at infinite separation (for \(\mathrm{NaCl(s)}\), lattice enthalpy is \(788\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)). A higher magnitude of lattice enthalpy indicates greater thermodynamic stability of the ionic crystal.

Partial Covalent Character: Fajans' Rules

No real bond is 100% ionic or 100% covalent. A small, highly charged cation distorts (polarizes) the electron cloud of a nearby anion, pulling electron density into the internuclear region and imparting covalent character. According to Fajans' rules, covalent character in an ionic bond increases with:

  • Smaller cation size and larger anion size: High charge density on the cation increases its polarising power, while a diffuse electron cloud on a large anion increases its polarisability.
  • Higher charge on the cation (or anion): Greater electrostatic pull causes stronger distortion (e.g., \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) is more covalent than \(\mathrm{MgCl_2}\), which is more covalent than \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)).
  • Pseudo-noble-gas configuration: For cations of similar size and charge, a cation with an \((n-1)d^{10}ns^0\) (or \(d\)-block) configuration polarises anions more strongly than one with an \(ns^2np^6\) noble-gas configuration because \(d\) electrons shield nuclear charge poorly.

3. Bond Parameters, Resonance, and Bond Polarity

Fundamental Bond Parameters

  • Bond Length: The equilibrium internuclear distance between two bonded atoms. For a covalent bond \(\mathrm{A-B}\), \(R = r_{\mathrm{A}} + r_{\mathrm{B}}\), where \(r_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(r_{\mathrm{B}}\) are covalent radii. The covalent radius is half the internuclear distance between two identical bonded atoms in a molecule, whereas the van der Waals radius is half the distance between two non-bonded identical atoms in adjacent molecules in the solid state (thus \(r_{\mathrm{vdw}} > r_{\mathrm{cov}}\); e.g., in \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\), \(r_{\mathrm{cov}} = 99\,\mathrm{pm}\) and \(r_{\mathrm{vdw}} = 180\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Bond Angle: The angle between orbitals containing bonding electron pairs around the central atom, determined spectroscopically (e.g., \(104.5°\) in \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)).
  • Bond Enthalpy: The energy required to break one mole of a specific bond in the gaseous state. In polyatomic molecules such as \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), successive \(\mathrm{O-H}\) bond dissociation enthalpies differ (\(502\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) for the first, \(427\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) for the second), so we report the mean (average) bond enthalpy (\(464.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  • Bond Order: The number of covalent bonds between two atoms in a Lewis structure. Isoelectronic species share the same bond order (e.g., \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{CO}\), and \(\mathrm{NO^+}\) all have bond order \(3\); \(\mathrm{F_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2^{2-}}\) have bond order \(1\)). As bond order increases, bond enthalpy increases and bond length decreases.

Resonance

When a single Lewis structure cannot match experimentally measured bond lengths or energies, the molecule is represented as a resonance hybrid of two or more contributing canonical forms linked by a double-headed arrow (\(\leftrightarrow\)). For example, ozone (\(\mathrm{O_3}\)) has two equivalent canonical forms each with one \(\mathrm{O-O}\) single bond (\(148\,\mathrm{pm}\)) and one \(\mathrm{O=O}\) double bond (\(121\,\mathrm{pm}\)), whereas experiment shows two identical bonds of length \(128\,\mathrm{pm}\). Similarly, \(\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\) has three equivalent \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bonds, and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) has \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bonds of \(115\,\mathrm{pm}\) (intermediate between \(\mathrm{C=O}\) at \(121\,\mathrm{pm}\) and \(\mathrm{C\equiv O}\) at \(110\,\mathrm{pm}\)).

Crucial Resonance Misconceptions to Avoid

  • Canonical forms are hypothetical constructs; they have no independent physical existence.
  • A molecule does not rapidly flip or spend fractions of time in different canonical forms, nor is resonance an equilibrium (unlike keto–enol tautomerism).
  • The resonance hybrid always has lower energy (higher stability) than any individual canonical form, and resonance averages bond characteristics across equivalent bonds.

Bond Polarity and Dipole Moment

Homonuclear diatomics (\(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{F_2}\)) form nonpolar covalent bonds because the shared electron pair is attracted equally. Heteronuclear bonds (\(\mathrm{H-F}\)) are polar covalent bonds because the more electronegative atom pulls electron density toward itself. The resulting charge separation is quantified by the dipole moment (\(\mu\)):

Dipole Moment and Unit Conversion
\[\mu = Q \times r \qquad \text{and} \qquad 1\,\mathrm{D} = 3.33564 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\]

In chemistry, the crossed arrow (\(\longmapsto\!\longrightarrow\)) points from the positive end (\(\delta^+\)) toward the negative end (\(\delta^-\)), showing the direction of electron-density shift.

For polyatomic molecules, the net dipole moment is the vector sum of all individual bond dipoles and lone-pair orbital dipoles:

  • Symmetric linear (\(\mathrm{BeF_2}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)), trigonal planar (\(\mathrm{BF_3}\)), and tetrahedral (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\)) molecules have \(\mu = 0\,\mathrm{D}\) because bond dipoles cancel identically.
  • Bent \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (\(104.5°\)) has a large net \(\mu = 1.85\,\mathrm{D}\) (\(6.17 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) vs \(\mathrm{NF_3}\): Both are trigonal pyramidal with one lone pair on nitrogen. In \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), the lone-pair orbital dipole points in the same direction as the resultant of the three \(\mathrm{N-H}\) bond dipoles (\(\mu = 1.47\,\mathrm{D}\) or \(4.90 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\)). In \(\mathrm{NF_3}\), fluorine is more electronegative than nitrogen, so the three \(\mathrm{N-F}\) bond dipoles point opposite to the lone-pair dipole, largely cancelling it (\(\mu = 0.23\,\mathrm{D}\) or \(0.80 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\)).

4. Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) Theory

Developed by Sidgwick and Powell (1940) and refined by Nyholm and Gillespie (1957), VSEPR theory predicts molecular geometry from the electrostatic repulsions among valence electron pairs around a central atom:

  • Electron pairs around the central atom arrange themselves on a spherical valence surface to maximize separation and minimize repulsion.
  • A multiple bond (double or triple) counts as a single electron domain ("super pair") for determining electron-pair geometry.
  • Because a lone pair (lp) is held by one nucleus while a bond pair (bp) is shared between two nuclei, lone pairs occupy more angular space and exert stronger repulsion:
VSEPR Repulsion Hierarchy
\[\mathrm{lp - lp} \;>\; \mathrm{lp - bp} \;>\; \mathrm{bp - bp}\]

This hierarchy explains why bond angles compress when lone pairs are present (for example, in tetrahedral electron-pair geometry: \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) is \(109.5°\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) with 1 lp is \(107°\), and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) with 2 lp is \(104.5°\)). In trigonal bipyramidal geometry, lone pairs always occupy equatorial positions rather than axial positions because an equatorial site experiences only two \(90°\) repulsions, whereas an axial site suffers three \(90°\) repulsions.

5. Valence Bond Theory and Orbital Hybridisation

While Lewis and VSEPR models describe connectivity and shapes qualitatively, Valence Bond (VB) Theory (Heitler–London, 1927; Pauling) explains bond energetics and directional overlap quantum mechanically. When two hydrogen atoms approach, new nucleus–electron attractions outweigh electron–electron and nucleus–nucleus repulsions until a potential energy minimum is reached at the equilibrium bond length (\(74\,\mathrm{pm}\) for \(\mathrm{H_2}\), releasing \(435.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Orbital Overlap: Sigma (\(\sigma\)) and Pi (\(\pi\)) Bonds

Covalent bond formation requires positive (in-phase) overlap of half-filled valence orbitals with matching wave-function signs (\(+/+\) or \(-/-\)) and proper spatial orientation. Negative (out-of-phase) or perpendicular (zero) overlap does not form a bond.

  • Sigma (\(\sigma\)) bond: Formed by axial (head-on) overlap along the internuclear axis (\(s\)–\(s\), \(s\)–\(p\), or \(p\)–\(p\) along the axis). Electron density is cylindrically symmetrical around the bond axis, allowing free rotation; large overlap makes \(\sigma\) bonds strong.
  • Pi (\(\pi\)) bond: Formed by lateral (sidewise) overlap of parallel \(p\) orbitals perpendicular to the internuclear axis. Electron density forms two lobes above and below the molecular plane. Smaller overlap makes a \(\pi\) bond weaker than a \(\sigma\) bond, and \(\pi\) bonds only form alongside a \(\sigma\) bond in multiple bonds (a double bond = \(1\sigma + 1\pi\); a triple bond = \(1\sigma + 2\pi\)).

Hybridisation of Atomic Orbitals

Pure \(p\) orbitals are mutually perpendicular (\(90°\)), which cannot explain the \(109.5°\) tetrahedral angle in \(\mathrm{CH_4}\). Pauling introduced hybridisation: the intermixing of valence orbitals of slightly different energies on the same atom to produce an equal number of equivalent hybrid orbitals oriented for minimum repulsion.

  • Number of hybrid orbitals formed = number of atomic orbitals mixed.
  • Promotion of an electron is not mandatory before hybridisation, and filled orbitals (holding lone pairs) can also hybridise (e.g., \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)).
  • For third-period elements, \(3s\), \(3p\), and \(3d\) (or \(3d\), \(4s\), \(4p\)) can hybridise, but \(3p\)–\(3d\)–\(4s\) mixing does not occur because the \(3p\)–\(4s\) energy gap is too large.

Because each axial bond pair experiences three \(90°\) repulsions from equatorial bond pairs (whereas equatorial pairs experience only two \(90°\) repulsions), axial \(\mathrm{P-Cl}\) bonds are slightly longer and weaker than equatorial \(\mathrm{P-Cl}\) bonds, making \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) reactive.

6. Molecular Orbital (MO) Theory

Formulated by Hund and Mulliken (1932), MO theory treats electrons as occupying polycentric molecular orbitals spread over the entire molecule. By the Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO) method, two atomic wave functions \(\psi_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(\psi_{\mathrm{B}}\) combine to give two MOs:

  • Bonding MO (\(\psi = \psi_{\mathrm{A}} + \psi_{\mathrm{B}}\)): Constructive interference concentrates electron density between the nuclei, lowering energy and stabilizing the molecule.
  • Antibonding MO (\(\psi^* = \psi_{\mathrm{A}} - \psi_{\mathrm{B}}\)): Destructive interference creates a nodal plane between the nuclei, raising energy above the parent atomic orbitals and destabilizing the molecule.

Conditions for LCAO: Combining atomic orbitals must have (1) comparable energies, (2) the same symmetry about the internuclear \(z\)-axis (so \(2p_z\) combines with \(2p_z\) to give \(\sigma 2p_z / \sigma^* 2p_z\), whereas \(2p_x\) and \(2p_y\) combine sidewise to give \(\pi 2p_x / \pi^* 2p_x\) and \(\pi 2p_y / \pi^* 2p_y\)), and (3) maximum overlap.

Energy Ordering and \(2s\)–\(2p\) Mixing

Spectroscopic data show two distinct MO energy sequences for second-period homonuclear diatomics (taking \(z\) as the internuclear axis):

MO Energy Sequences (Second Period)

1. Up to 14 electrons (\(\mathrm{Li_2, Be_2, B_2, C_2, N_2}\) — with \(2s\text{–}2p\) mixing):

\[\sigma 1s < \sigma^* 1s < \sigma 2s < \sigma^* 2s < (\pi 2p_x = \pi 2p_y) < \sigma 2p_z < (\pi^* 2p_x = \pi^* 2p_y) < \sigma^* 2p_z\]

2. More than 14 electrons (\(\mathrm{O_2, F_2, Ne_2}\) — without \(2s\text{–}2p\) mixing):

\[\sigma 1s < \sigma^* 1s < \sigma 2s < \sigma^* 2s < \sigma 2p_z < (\pi 2p_x = \pi 2p_y) < (\pi^* 2p_x = \pi^* 2p_y) < \sigma^* 2p_z\]
Bond Order, Stability, and Magnetism
\[\mathrm{Bond\;Order} = \frac{1}{2}(N_b - N_a)\]

where \(N_b\) = electrons in bonding MOs and \(N_a\) = electrons in antibonding MOs. A molecule is stable if \(\text{Bond Order} > 0\) (\(N_b > N_a\)) and does not exist if \(\text{Bond Order} \le 0\) (e.g., \(\mathrm{He_2}\), \(\mathrm{Be_2}\), \(\mathrm{Ne_2}\)). Higher bond order means higher bond enthalpy and shorter bond length. Species with unpaired electrons in MOs are paramagnetic (e.g., \(\mathrm{B_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\)); those with all electrons paired are diamagnetic (e.g., \(\mathrm{C_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{F_2}\)).

7. Hydrogen Bonding

When hydrogen is covalently bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom—specifically fluorine (\(\mathrm{F}\)), oxygen (\(\mathrm{O}\)), or nitrogen (\(\mathrm{N}\))—the shared electron pair shifts strongly toward the electronegative atom. This leaves the hydrogen atom with a pronounced partial positive charge (\(\delta^+\)), enabling it to form a weak electrostatic bridge (represented by a dashed line, \(---\)) with a lone pair on another \(\mathrm{F}\), \(\mathrm{O}\), or \(\mathrm{N}\) atom.

  • Strength and physical state: Hydrogen bonds are weaker than covalent bonds but stronger than ordinary van der Waals forces. Their magnitude is maximum in the solid state and minimum in the gaseous state.
  • Intermolecular hydrogen bonding: Occurs between two different molecules of the same or different compounds (e.g., \(\mathrm{HF}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), alcohols), elevating boiling points and viscosity.
  • Intramolecular hydrogen bonding: Occurs when hydrogen bridges two electronegative atoms within the same molecule (e.g., o-nitrophenol), forming a stable ring and reducing intermolecular association.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Computing Formal Charges and Selecting the Preferred Lewis Structure

Problem: Consider the thiocyanate ion, \(\mathrm{SCN^-}\), with carbon as the central atom. Two plausible octet-satisfying Lewis structures are:

Structure A: \([:\ddot{\mathrm{S}}=\mathrm{C}=\ddot{\mathrm{N}}:]^-\)   and   Structure B: \([:\ddot{\mathrm{S}}-\mathrm{C}\equiv\mathrm{N}:]^-\)

Calculate the formal charge on each atom in both structures and identify the lower-energy contributor.

Solution: Total valence electrons = \(6(\mathrm{S}) + 4(\mathrm{C}) + 5(\mathrm{N}) + 1(\text{charge}) = 16\,\mathrm{e^-}\). Using \(\mathrm{F.C.} = V - L - \frac{1}{2}B\):

  • Structure A: \(\mathrm{F.C.(S)} = 6 - 4 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = 0\);   \(\mathrm{F.C.(C)} = 4 - 0 - \frac{1}{2}(8) = 0\);   \(\mathrm{F.C.(N)} = 5 - 4 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = -1\).
  • Structure B: \(\mathrm{F.C.(S)} = 6 - 6 - \frac{1}{2}(2) = -1\);   \(\mathrm{F.C.(C)} = 4 - 0 - \frac{1}{2}(8) = 0\);   \(\mathrm{F.C.(N)} = 5 - 2 - \frac{1}{2}(6) = 0\).

Both structures have formal charges of magnitude \(\le 1\). Because nitrogen (\(\chi = 3.0\)) is more electronegative than sulfur (\(\chi = 2.5\)), placing the \(-1\) formal charge on nitrogen makes Structure A the lower-energy (major) resonance contributor.

Example 2: Predicting Hybridisation, Shape, and Bond-Angle Distortion via VSEPR

Problem: Determine the hybridisation of the central atom, electron-pair geometry, and actual molecular shape of \(\mathrm{BrF_3}\) and \(\mathrm{ICl_4^-}\).

Solution:

  • \(\mathrm{BrF_3}\): Bromine has \(7\) valence electrons and forms \(3\) single bonds with \(\mathrm{F}\), leaving \(4\) non-bonding electrons (\(2\) lone pairs). Total electron domains = \(3 + 2 = 5\) (\(\mathrm{AB_3E_2}\) type), corresponding to \(sp^3d\) hybridisation and a trigonal bipyramidal electron arrangement. Placing both lone pairs in equatorial positions minimizes \(90°\) \(\mathrm{lp-bp}\) repulsions, giving a T-shaped molecule (with \(\mathrm{F_{ax}-Br-F_{eq}}\) slightly compressed below \(90°\)).
  • \(\mathrm{ICl_4^-}\): Iodine contributes \(7\) valence electrons plus \(1\) electron for the negative charge (\(8\,\mathrm{e^-}\)), forming \(4\) single bonds with \(\mathrm{Cl}\) and retaining \(2\) lone pairs. Total electron domains = \(4 + 2 = 6\) (\(\mathrm{AB_4E_2}\) type), corresponding to \(sp^3d^2\) hybridisation and an octahedral electron arrangement. Placing the two lone pairs opposite each other (\(180°\) apart) minimizes \(\mathrm{lp-lp}\) repulsion, yielding a square planar ion.

Example 3: Comparing Bond Order, Bond Length, and Magnetism with MO Theory

Problem: Arrange \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2^+}\), and \(\mathrm{N_2^-}\) in order of increasing bond length and state the magnetic nature of each species.

Solution: Neutral \(\mathrm{N_2}\) has \(14\) electrons (\(\le 14\,\mathrm{e^-}\) ordering applies):

  • \(\mathrm{N_2}\) (\(14\,\mathrm{e^-}\)): \(\mathrm{KK}\,(\sigma 2s)^2(\sigma^* 2s)^2(\pi 2p_x)^2(\pi 2p_y)^2(\sigma 2p_z)^2\). Bond order \(= \frac{1}{2}(10 - 4) = 3.0\); all electrons paired → diamagnetic.
  • \(\mathrm{N_2^+}\) (\(13\,\mathrm{e^-}\)): Remove \(1\,\mathrm{e^-}\) from \(\sigma 2p_z\). Bond order \(= \frac{1}{2}(9 - 4) = 2.5\); \(1\) unpaired \(\mathrm{e^-}\) → paramagnetic.
  • \(\mathrm{N_2^-}\) (\(15\,\mathrm{e^-}\)): Add \(1\,\mathrm{e^-}\) into \(\pi^* 2p_x\). Bond order \(= \frac{1}{2}(10 - 5) = 2.5\); \(1\) unpaired \(\mathrm{e^-}\) → paramagnetic.

Both \(\mathrm{N_2^+}\) and \(\mathrm{N_2^-}\) have bond order \(2.5\), which is lower than \(\mathrm{N_2}\) (\(3.0\)). However, \(\mathrm{N_2^-}\) has an extra antibonding electron, increasing interelectronic repulsion and making \(\mathrm{N_2^-}\) slightly less stable and slightly longer-bonded than \(\mathrm{N_2^+}\). Thus, bond length increases as: \(\mathbf{N_2 < N_2^+ < N_2^-}\).

Example 4: Calculating Percentage Ionic Character from Hypothetical Dipole Data

Problem: (Assumption: Suppose a hypothetical diatomic molecule \(\mathrm{XY}\) has an experimental dipole moment of \(1.60\,\mathrm{D}\) and an internuclear bond length of \(125\,\mathrm{pm}\).) Calculate the percentage ionic character of the \(\mathrm{X-Y}\) bond. (Take elementary charge \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)).

Solution:

  1. Convert the observed dipole moment to SI units: \[\mu_{\mathrm{obs}} = 1.60 \times 3.33564 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m} = 5.337 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\]
  2. Compute the theoretical dipole moment for 100% ionic character (\(Q = e\) at \(r = 125 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m}\)): \[\mu_{\mathrm{ionic}} = (1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}) \times (125 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m}) = 2.0025 \times 10^{-29}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\]
  3. Calculate percentage ionic character: \[\%\,\text{Ionic Character} = \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{obs}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{ionic}}} \times 100 = \frac{5.337 \times 10^{-30}}{2.0025 \times 10^{-29}} \times 100 \approx 26.65\%\]

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which statement accurately describes the bonding in the \(\mathrm{C_2}\) molecule according to Molecular Orbital Theory?

  1. (A) \(\mathrm{C_2}\) is paramagnetic with bond order \(2\).
  2. (B) The double bond in \(\mathrm{C_2}\) consists of one \(\sigma\) bond and one \(\pi\) bond.
  3. (C) The double bond in \(\mathrm{C_2}\) consists of two \(\pi\) bonds, and the molecule is diamagnetic.
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{C_2}\) has a bond order of \(1\) because \(\sigma 2p_z\) is lower in energy than \(\pi 2p_x\).
Answer: (C)

Explanation: \(\mathrm{C_2}\) has \(12\) electrons. Because of \(2s\text{–}2p\) mixing, \(\pi 2p_x\) and \(\pi 2p_y\) lie below \(\sigma 2p_z\): \(\mathrm{KK}\,(\sigma 2s)^2(\sigma^* 2s)^2(\pi 2p_x)^2(\pi 2p_y)^2\). Thus, \(\text{Bond Order} = \frac{1}{2}(8 - 4) = 2\), all electrons are paired (diamagnetic), and all four bonding valence electrons reside in \(\pi\) MOs.

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps

  • Mixing up electron-pair geometry and molecular shape: \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) both have tetrahedral electron-pair arrangements (\(sp^3\)), but their molecular shapes are trigonal pyramidal and bent, respectively.
  • Using the wrong MO energy order: Remember the \(14\)-electron cutoff! \(\mathrm{B_2}\), \(\mathrm{C_2}\), and \(\mathrm{N_2}\) have \(\pi 2p_{x,y} < \sigma 2p_z\), whereas \(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(\mathrm{F_2}\), and \(\mathrm{Ne_2}\) have \(\sigma 2p_z < \pi 2p_{x,y}\).
  • Comparing \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and \(\mathrm{NF_3}\) dipole moments by electronegativity alone: Fluorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, yet \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) has a much larger dipole moment because the lone-pair orbital dipole reinforces the \(\mathrm{N-H}\) dipoles but opposes the \(\mathrm{N-F}\) dipoles.
  • Confusing van der Waals and covalent radii: For any element, \(r_{\mathrm{vdw}}\) is always larger than \(r_{\mathrm{cov}}\) because covalent bonding involves orbital interpenetration.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Formal charge: \(\mathrm{F.C.} = V - L - \frac{1}{2}B\); lowest-energy structure minimizes formal charges.
  • Fajans' rules: Small cation, high cation charge, large anion, and \((n-1)d^{10}\) configuration maximize covalent character.
  • VSEPR repulsion order: \(\mathrm{lp-lp} > \mathrm{lp-bp} > \mathrm{bp-bp}\); lone pairs in trigonal bipyramidal geometry prefer equatorial sites.
  • Hybridisation shortcut for steric number: \(\mathrm{SN} = \text{bonded atoms} + \text{lone pairs on central atom}\) (\(2 \to sp\), \(3 \to sp^2\), \(4 \to sp^3\), \(5 \to sp^3d\), \(6 \to sp^3d^2\)).
  • MO Bond Order: \(\frac{1}{2}(N_b - N_a)\); \(\text{Bond Order} \propto \text{Bond Enthalpy} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Bond Length}}\).
  • Hydrogen bonding requires \(\mathrm{H}\) attached directly to \(\mathrm{F}\), \(\mathrm{O}\), or \(\mathrm{N}\).

Concise Final Recap

Chemical bonding lowers system energy by achieving stable electronic arrangements through ionic lattices or shared covalent pairs. While Lewis structures, formal charges, and resonance describe electron distribution, VSEPR theory and orbital hybridisation explain three-dimensional molecular shapes and directional \(\sigma/\pi\) bonding. Molecular Orbital Theory extends this picture to delocalized orbitals, accurately predicting bond order, bond stability, and paramagnetism (such as in \(\mathrm{O_2}\)), while hydrogen bonding governs key intermolecular and intramolecular interactions in polar \(\mathrm{F/O/N}\) compounds.
