Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

Foundational Principles of Matter, Measurement, Mole Concept, Stoichiometry, and Solution Concentration

Learning Objectives and Overview

Chemistry investigates the composition, structure, properties, and transformations of matter at the atomic and molecular scales. Mastering this foundational unit equips you with the quantitative toolkit needed across all branches of physical, inorganic, and organic chemistry for NEET UG. After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Classify matter by physical state (solid, liquid, gas) and chemical composition (elements, compounds, homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures).
  • Apply SI base units, metric prefixes, temperature scale conversions, and dimensional analysis accurately.
  • Handle experimental uncertainty using scientific notation, significant figures, rounding rules, precision, and accuracy.
  • State and apply the five laws of chemical combination alongside Dalton's atomic theory and its limitations.
  • Compute average atomic mass, molecular mass, formula mass, and convert seamlessly between mass, moles, number of particles, and gas volume at STP.
  • Determine percentage composition, empirical formulas, and molecular formulas from analytical data.
  • Solve stoichiometric problems involving limiting reagents and express solution concentrations using mass percent, mole fraction, molarity, and molality.

Historical and Applied Context: Long before modern laboratory chemistry emerged in eighteenth-century Europe, early Indian traditions—known as Rasayan Shastra, Rastantra, or Rasvidya—developed metallurgy, dyes, glassmaking, fermentation, and medicinal preparations such as metallic bhasmas (which contain metal nanoparticles). Around 600 BCE, Acharya Kanda (Kashyap) proposed in the Vaiseshika Sutras that all matter is composed of eternal, indivisible building blocks called Paramānu. In modern healthcare, chemistry enables the synthesis of life-saving pharmaceuticals such as cisplatin and taxol for cancer therapy and AZT (azidothymidine) for HIV/AIDS management, as well as environmentally safer alternatives to ozone-depleting chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs).

Nature and Classification of Matter

Matter is anything that possesses mass and occupies space. It can be examined both through its physical state (determined by intermolecular forces and thermal energy) and its chemical composition.

Physical States of Matter

  • Solid: Constituent particles are tightly packed in an orderly arrangement with minimal freedom of movement (only vibrational motion about fixed positions). Solids have a definite shape and a definite volume.
  • Liquid: Particles remain close together but can slide past one another. Liquids have a definite volume but no definite shape, adopting the shape of their container.
  • Gas: Particles are widely separated and move rapidly in random directions. Gases have neither definite shape nor definite volume, expanding to fill the entire container.

These three states are interconvertible by altering temperature and pressure: heating converts a solid to a liquid and then to a gas, whereas cooling reverses the sequence.

Chemical Classification of Matter

At the macroscopic level, matter is divided into pure substances and mixtures:

  • Pure Substances: Have a fixed, invariant chemical composition and cannot be separated into simpler components by physical methods.
    • Elements: Contain only one kind of atom (e.g., \(\mathrm{Na}\), \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(\mathrm{P_4}\)). Atoms of different elements differ in mass and properties.
    • Compounds: Formed when atoms of two or more different elements combine chemically in a fixed, definite ratio by mass (e.g., \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)). A compound's properties differ completely from those of its constituent elements, and its components can only be separated by chemical methods.
  • Mixtures: Contain two or more pure substances (components) mixed in any variable proportion. Their components can be separated by physical methods such as filtration, crystallization, or distillation.
    • Homogeneous Mixtures: Uniform composition throughout with no visible boundaries (e.g., aqueous sugar solution, clean air, brass).
    • Heterogeneous Mixtures: Non-uniform composition where distinct phases or components may be visible (e.g., salt and sand, oil and water, muddy water).

Properties of Matter, SI Units, and Measurement Uncertainty

Physical vs. Chemical Properties

Physical properties (such as colour, odour, density, melting point, and boiling point) can be measured or observed without altering the chemical identity of the substance. Chemical properties (such as combustibility, acidity, basicity, and reactivity) require a chemical change to be observed.

The International System of Units (SI)

Every quantitative measurement consists of a numerical value followed by a unit. The International System of Units (SI) establishes seven fundamental base quantities from which all other units (such as volume in \(\mathrm{m^3}\) and density in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) are derived.

Common SI prefixes allow convenient scaling across orders of magnitude: femto (\(\mathrm{f}, 10^{-15}\)), pico (\(\mathrm{p}, 10^{-12}\)), nano (\(\mathrm{n}, 10^{-9}\)), micro (\(\mathrm{µ}, 10^{-6}\)), milli (\(\mathrm{m}, 10^{-3}\)), centi (\(\mathrm{c}, 10^{-2}\)), deci (\(\mathrm{d}, 10^{-1}\)), kilo (\(\mathrm{k}, 10^{3}\)), mega (\(\mathrm{M}, 10^{6}\)), and giga (\(\mathrm{G}, 10^{9}\)).

Key Laboratory Quantities: Mass, Volume, Density, and Temperature

  • Mass vs. Weight: Mass is the invariant amount of matter in a body, whereas weight is the gravitational force acting on it and varies with local gravity.
  • Volume: Space occupied by matter. While the SI unit is \(\mathrm{m^3}\), laboratory work commonly uses \(\mathrm{cm^3}\), \(\mathrm{dm^3}\), millilitre (\(\mathrm{mL}\)), and litre (\(\mathrm{L}\)): \[1\,\mathrm{L} = 1000\,\mathrm{mL} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\]
  • Density: Mass per unit volume, \(\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}\), commonly expressed in \(\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) for solids/liquids and \(\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}\) for gases.
  • Temperature Scales: Measured in kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\), SI unit), degrees Celsius (°C), or degrees Fahrenheit (°F). Negative values are possible on the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales, but not on the absolute Kelvin scale. \[^{\circ}\mathrm{F} = \frac{9}{5}(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) + 32 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathrm{K} = \,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} + 273.15\]

Scientific Notation, Precision, Accuracy, and Significant Figures

In scientific notation, any number is written as \(N \times 10^n\), where \(1.000\ldots \le N < 10\) and \(n\) is an integer exponent. For multiplication/division, exponents are added/subtracted; for addition/subtraction, numbers must first be rewritten with the same exponent.

Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with one another, whereas accuracy describes how close a measurement (or mean) is to the true value. Experimental data can be precise without being accurate if a systematic calibration error is present.

Significant figures include all digits known with certainty plus the first uncertain (estimated) digit:

  1. All non-zero digits are significant (e.g., \(285\) has 3 significant figures).
  2. Leading zeros before the first non-zero digit are not significant (e.g., \(0.0052\) has 2).
  3. Zeros trapped between non-zero digits are significant (e.g., \(2.005\) has 4).
  4. Trailing zeros to the right of a decimal point are significant (e.g., \(0.200\) has 3; \(500.0\) has 4). Trailing zeros in a whole number without a decimal point are ambiguous and best written in scientific notation (e.g., \(1.0 \times 10^2\) has 2).
  5. Exact counted numbers and defined conversion factors have infinite significant figures.

Arithmetic & Rounding Rules: In addition and subtraction, round the result to the fewest decimal places among the operands. In multiplication and division, round the result to the fewest significant figures among the operands. When rounding off a trailing digit equal to \(5\), leave the preceding digit unchanged if it is even, and increase it by \(1\) if it is odd (round-half-to-even).

Dimensional Analysis (Factor-Label Method): Multiply the given quantity by unit factors equal to \(1\) (such as \(\frac{2.54\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{in}}\)) arranged so unwanted units cancel and the desired unit remains.

Laws of Chemical Combination and Dalton's Atomic Theory

Five fundamental experimental laws govern how elements combine to form compounds:

  1. Law of Conservation of Mass (Antoine Lavoisier, 1789): In any physical or chemical change, total mass is conserved; matter is neither created nor destroyed.
  2. Law of Definite Proportions (Joseph Proust): A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed proportion by mass, regardless of its natural or synthetic origin.
  3. Law of Multiple Proportions (John Dalton, 1803): When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other bear a simple whole-number ratio (e.g., in \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\), oxygen masses combining with \(2\,\mathrm{g}\) of hydrogen are \(16\,\mathrm{g}\) and \(32\,\mathrm{g}\), a \(1:2\) ratio).
  4. Gay-Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes (1808): When gases react or are produced at constant temperature and pressure, their volumes bear a simple whole-number ratio.
  5. Avogadro's Law (1811): Equal volumes of all gases under identical conditions of temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

Dalton proposed that: (1) matter consists of indivisible atoms; (2) all atoms of a given element have identical properties and mass, while atoms of different elements differ in mass; (3) compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in fixed whole-number ratios; and (4) chemical reactions merely reorganize atoms without creating or destroying them.

Scope and Limitations (High-Yield for NEET): While Dalton's theory explained the laws of conservation of mass, definite proportions, and multiple proportions, it could not explain Gay-Lussac's law of gaseous volumes (because Dalton did not distinguish between atoms and diatomic/polyatomic molecules), nor could it explain why atoms combine or the existence of isotopes and isobars.

Atomic Masses, the Mole Concept, and Chemical Formulas

Atomic, Average Atomic, Molecular, and Formula Masses

By international agreement, one unified atomic mass unit (\(\mathrm{u}\), formerly \(\mathrm{amu}\)) is defined as exactly one-twelfth of the mass of one \(^{12}\mathrm{C}\) atom:

\[1\,\mathrm{u} = \frac{1}{12} \times (\text{mass of one } ^{12}\mathrm{C} \text{ atom}) = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g} = \frac{1}{N_A}\,\mathrm{g}\]
  • Average Atomic Mass: Most elements occur naturally as mixtures of isotopes. The atomic mass listed on the periodic table is the abundance-weighted average: \[\text{Average Atomic Mass} = \sum_{i} \left(\frac{\%\text{ Abundance}_i}{100} \times \text{Isotopic Mass}_i\right)\]
  • Molecular Mass: Sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a discrete molecule (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_4} = 12.011 + 4 \times 1.008 = 16.043\,\mathrm{u}\)).
  • Formula Mass: Used for ionic solids such as \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) that form three-dimensional crystal lattices rather than discrete molecules (e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl} = 23.0 + 35.5 = 58.5\,\mathrm{u}\)).

The Mole Concept and Molar Mass

One mole (\(\mathrm{mol}\)) is the SI unit for amount of substance and contains exactly \(6.02214076 \times 10^{23}\) elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, or electrons). For routine calculations, Avogadro's constant is taken as \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\). The molar mass (\(M\)) of a substance in \(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\) is numerically equal to its atomic, molecular, or formula mass in \(\mathrm{u}\).

At standard temperature and pressure (STP: \(273.15\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure), \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal gas occupies \(22.7\,\mathrm{L}\) (whereas under the older \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) convention, it occupies \(22.4\,\mathrm{L}\)). Always check which STP pressure convention a question specifies.

Percentage Composition, Empirical Formula, and Molecular Formula

The mass percentage of an element in a compound is given by:

\[\text{Mass \% of element} = \frac{\text{Mass of that element in } 1\,\mathrm{mol}\text{ of compound}}{\text{Molar mass of compound}} \times 100\]

An empirical formula expresses the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound, whereas the molecular formula gives the exact number of atoms of each element in one molecule:

  1. Assume \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of compound so mass percentages convert directly to grams.
  2. Divide each element's mass by its atomic mass to obtain moles of each element.
  3. Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value to get a relative ratio; multiply by a small integer if needed to obtain whole numbers. This gives the empirical formula.
  4. Compute the integer multiplier \(n = \frac{\text{Molar mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}}\), and multiply all subscripts in the empirical formula by \(n\) to obtain the molecular formula.

Stoichiometry, Limiting Reagent, and Solution Concentration

Stoichiometry deals with quantitative relationships between reactants and products in a balanced chemical equation. Never alter subscripts when balancing an equation; adjust only the stoichiometric coefficients so that atoms of each element are conserved on both sides.

Limiting Reagent

When reactants are mixed in non-stoichiometric proportions, the reactant that is completely consumed first stops the reaction and dictates the maximum amount of product formed. This reactant is the limiting reagent; any remaining reactant is the excess reagent. To identify the limiting reagent rapidly in exams, divide the available moles of each reactant by its stoichiometric coefficient in the balanced equation—the smallest quotient identifies the limiting reagent.

Concentration Terms for Solutions

Most chemical and biological reactions occur in solution. Four standard concentration expressions are routinely tested in NEET UG:

Concentration Term Mathematical Definition Temperature Dependence
Mass Percent (w/w %) \(\frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}} \times 100\) Independent (mass-based)
Mole Fraction (\(\chi_A\)) \(\frac{n_A}{n_A + n_B}\) (for binary solution; \(\chi_A + \chi_B = 1\)) Independent (mole-based)
Molarity (\(M\)) \(\frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution in litres}}\) (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) Dependent (volume changes with \(T\))
Molality (\(m\)) \(\frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}}\) (\(\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\)) Independent (mass-based)

Dilution & Mixing Relations: When a stock solution is diluted by adding pure solvent, the moles of solute remain conserved: \(M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2\). When two non-reacting solutions of the same solute are mixed, the resulting molarity is \(M_{\text{mix}} = \frac{M_1 V_1 + M_2 V_2}{V_1 + V_2}\).

Worked Examples

Calculating Average Atomic Mass of Boron

Problem: Naturally occurring boron consists of two stable isotopes: \(^{10}\mathrm{B}\) with an isotopic mass of \(10.013\,\mathrm{u}\) and natural abundance \(19.9\%\), and \(^{11}\mathrm{B}\) with an isotopic mass of \(11.009\,\mathrm{u}\) and natural abundance \(80.1\%\). Calculate the average atomic mass of boron.

Solution:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Average atomic mass} &= (0.199 \times 10.013\,\mathrm{u}) + (0.801 \times 11.009\,\mathrm{u}) \\ &= 1.9926\,\mathrm{u} + 8.8182\,\mathrm{u} \\ &= 10.8108\,\mathrm{u} \approx 10.81\,\mathrm{u} \end{aligned} \]

Determining Empirical and Molecular Formulas of Butanoic Acid

Problem: In a hypothetical laboratory analysis, a volatile carboxylic acid is found to contain \(54.53\%\) carbon, \(9.15\%\) hydrogen, and \(36.32\%\) oxygen by mass. Its molar mass is determined to be \(88.11\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). Find its empirical and molecular formulas.

Solution: Assume a \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) sample containing \(54.53\,\mathrm{g}\) \(\mathrm{C}\), \(9.15\,\mathrm{g}\) \(\mathrm{H}\), and \(36.32\,\mathrm{g}\) \(\mathrm{O}\).

  1. Convert masses to moles: \[n_{\mathrm{C}} = \frac{54.53}{12.011} = 4.540\,\mathrm{mol}, \quad n_{\mathrm{H}} = \frac{9.15}{1.008} = 9.077\,\mathrm{mol}, \quad n_{\mathrm{O}} = \frac{36.32}{15.999} = 2.270\,\mathrm{mol}\]
  2. Divide by the smallest mole value (\(2.270\)): \[\mathrm{C} : \mathrm{H} : \mathrm{O} = \frac{4.540}{2.270} : \frac{9.077}{2.270} : \frac{2.270}{2.270} = 2.00 : 4.00 : 1.00\] Thus, the empirical formula is \(\mathrm{C_2H_4O}\).
  3. Empirical formula mass \(= 2(12.011) + 4(1.008) + 15.999 = 44.053\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).
  4. Multiplier \(n = \frac{88.11}{44.053} = 2.00 \approx 2\). Therefore, the molecular formula is \((\mathrm{C_2H_4O})_2 = \mathrm{C_4H_8O_2}\).

Identifying the Limiting Reagent in Magnesium Nitride Synthesis

Problem: A reaction vessel is charged with \(12.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of magnesium ribbon and \(7.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of dinitrogen gas to form solid magnesium nitride (\(\mathrm{Mg_3N_2}\)). Identify the limiting reagent and calculate the mass of \(\mathrm{Mg_3N_2}\) produced. (Assume atomic masses: \(\mathrm{Mg} = 24\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{N} = 14\,\mathrm{u}\))

Solution:

  1. Write the balanced equation: \[3\mathrm{Mg}(\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{N_2}(\mathrm{g}) \rightarrow \mathrm{Mg_3N_2}(\mathrm{s})\]
  2. Calculate initial moles: \[n_{\mathrm{Mg}} = \frac{12.0\,\mathrm{g}}{24.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 0.50\,\mathrm{mol}, \qquad n_{\mathrm{N_2}} = \frac{7.0\,\mathrm{g}}{28.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 0.25\,\mathrm{mol}\]
  3. Divide by stoichiometric coefficients: \(\frac{n_{\mathrm{Mg}}}{3} = \frac{0.50}{3} \approx 0.167\), whereas \(\frac{n_{\mathrm{N_2}}}{1} = 0.25\). Since \(0.167 < 0.25\), magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg}\)) is the limiting reagent.
  4. Moles of \(\mathrm{Mg_3N_2}\) formed \(= \frac{1}{3} \times 0.50\,\mathrm{mol} = \frac{1}{6}\,\mathrm{mol}\).
  5. Molar mass of \(\mathrm{Mg_3N_2} = 3(24) + 2(14) = 100\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). Mass formed \(= \frac{1}{6} \times 100\,\mathrm{g} = 16.67\,\mathrm{g}\).

Converting Molarity to Molality for an Aqueous Urea Solution

Problem: A \(2.0\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous solution of urea (\(\mathrm{NH_2CONH_2}\), molar mass \(= 60.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) has a density of \(1.05\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\). Calculate the molality of this solution.

Solution: Consider \(1.0\,\mathrm{L}\) (\(1000\,\mathrm{mL}\)) of the solution.

  • Moles of urea present \(= 2.0\,\mathrm{mol}\).
  • Mass of urea \(= 2.0\,\mathrm{mol} \times 60.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 120.0\,\mathrm{g}\).
  • Total mass of \(1000\,\mathrm{mL}\) solution \(= 1000\,\mathrm{mL} \times 1.05\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}} = 1050\,\mathrm{g}\).
  • Mass of water (solvent) \(= 1050\,\mathrm{g} - 120.0\,\mathrm{g} = 930\,\mathrm{g} = 0.930\,\mathrm{kg}\).
\[\text{Molality } (m) = \frac{2.0\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.930\,\mathrm{kg}} = 2.15\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 1: Evaluate the expression \((14.20 - 12.1) \times 2.50\) and report the answer to the correct number of significant figures.

  • (A) \(5.25\)
  • (B) \(5.3\)
  • (C) \(5.2\)
  • (D) \(5.250\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: First perform the subtraction: \(14.20 - 12.1 = 2.10\), which must be rounded to \(2.1\) (one decimal place, matching \(12.1\)). Now multiply: \(2.1 \times 2.50 = 5.25\). Since \(2.1\) has \(2\) significant figures, the product must have \(2\) significant figures. Applying the round-half-to-even rule on \(5.25\) leaves the even digit \(2\) unchanged, giving \(5.2\).

Practice Question 2: Two oxides of a metal \(\mathrm{M}\) contain \(27.6\%\) and \(30.0\%\) oxygen by mass, respectively. If the formula of the first oxide is \(\mathrm{M_3O_4}\), what is the formula of the second oxide?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{MO}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{MO_2}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{M_2O_3}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{M_2O_5}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: In \(\mathrm{M_3O_4}\), \(72.4\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{M}\) combines with \(27.6\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{O}\) (corresponding to \(4\) oxygen atoms per \(3\) metal atoms). In the second oxide, \(70.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{M}\) combines with \(30.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{O}\). Therefore, the mass of oxygen combining with \(72.4\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{M}\) in the second oxide is \(\frac{30.0}{70.0} \times 72.4 = 31.03\,\mathrm{g}\). By the Law of Multiple Proportions, the number of oxygen atoms per \(3\) atoms of \(\mathrm{M}\) is \(4 \times \frac{31.03}{27.6} = 4.5\). Thus, the atomic ratio \(\mathrm{M}:\mathrm{O} = 3 : 4.5 = 2 : 3\), giving \(\mathrm{M_2O_3}\).

Practice Question 3: Which of the following samples contains the largest total number of atoms? (Atomic masses: \(\mathrm{He}=4\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{C}=12\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{H}=1\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{N}=14\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{O}=16\,\mathrm{u}\))

  • (A) \(4.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{He}\)
  • (B) \(4.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4}\)
  • (C) \(4.4\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)
  • (D) \(3.4\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Compute moles of atoms \(= (\text{moles of molecules}) \times (\text{atomicity})\):
(A) \(\frac{4.0}{4} \times 1 = 1.00\,\mathrm{mol}\) atoms;
(B) \(\frac{4.0}{16} \times 5 = 0.25 \times 5 = 1.25\,\mathrm{mol}\) atoms;
(C) \(\frac{4.4}{44} \times 3 = 0.10 \times 3 = 0.30\,\mathrm{mol}\) atoms;
(D) \(\frac{3.4}{17} \times 4 = 0.20 \times 4 = 0.80\,\mathrm{mol}\) atoms.
Hence, \(4.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) contains the maximum number of atoms (\(1.25\,N_A\)).

Practice Question 4: A hypothetical biomolecule is found by elemental analysis to contain \(0.32\%\) sulfur by mass. What is the minimum possible molar mass of this biomolecule? (Atomic mass of \(\mathrm{S} = 32\,\mathrm{u}\))

  • (A) \(1.0 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(3.2 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(1.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(3.2 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: For the minimum molar mass, each molecule must contain at least one atom of sulfur (\(32\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{S}\) per mole of biomolecule). Since \(0.32\%\) of the molar mass \(M\) equals \(32\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\): \[M = \frac{32 \times 100}{0.32} = 10000\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 1.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 5: Complete combustion of \(0.22\,\mathrm{g}\) of an organic compound containing only carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen yields \(0.44\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.18\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). What is the empirical formula of the compound?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{CH_2O}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{C_2H_4O}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{C_2H_6O}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{C_3H_6O_2}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation:
Moles of \(\mathrm{C} = \frac{0.44\,\mathrm{g}}{44\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 0.01\,\mathrm{mol} \Rightarrow \text{mass of C} = 0.01 \times 12 = 0.12\,\mathrm{g}\).
Moles of \(\mathrm{H} = 2 \times \frac{0.18\,\mathrm{g}}{18\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 0.02\,\mathrm{mol} \Rightarrow \text{mass of H} = 0.02 \times 1 = 0.02\,\mathrm{g}\).
Mass of \(\mathrm{O} = 0.22 - (0.12 + 0.02) = 0.08\,\mathrm{g} \Rightarrow \text{moles of O} = \frac{0.08}{16} = 0.005\,\mathrm{mol}\).
Mole ratio \(\mathrm{C}:\mathrm{H}:\mathrm{O} = 0.01 : 0.02 : 0.005 = 2 : 4 : 1\), so the empirical formula is \(\mathrm{C_2H_4O}\).

Practice Question 6: A mixture of \(20\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO}(\mathrm{g})\) and \(30\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}(\mathrm{g})\) is sparked to react completely according to \(2\mathrm{CO}(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{O_2}(\mathrm{g}) \rightarrow 2\mathrm{CO_2}(\mathrm{g})\) at constant temperature and pressure. What is the total volume of the gaseous mixture after the reaction?

  • (A) \(20\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (B) \(30\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (C) \(40\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (D) \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: By Gay-Lussac's law, \(2\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO}\) reacts with \(1\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to form \(2\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\). Thus, \(20\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO}\) consumes \(10\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and produces \(20\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\). Unreacted \(\mathrm{O_2}\) left \(= 30 - 10 = 20\,\mathrm{mL}\). Total final gas volume \(= 20\,\mathrm{mL}\,(\mathrm{CO_2}) + 20\,\mathrm{mL}\,(\mathrm{O_2}) = 40\,\mathrm{mL}\).

Practice Question 7: \(200\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.5\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\) is mixed with \(300\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.2\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\) and the resulting mixture is diluted with water to a final volume of \(1.0\,\mathrm{L}\). What is the molarity of the final solution?

  • (A) \(0.16\,\mathrm{M}\)
  • (B) \(0.32\,\mathrm{M}\)
  • (C) \(0.35\,\mathrm{M}\)
  • (D) \(0.70\,\mathrm{M}\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Total moles of \(\mathrm{HCl} = (0.200\,\mathrm{L} \times 0.5\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}) + (0.300\,\mathrm{L} \times 0.2\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}) = 0.10 + 0.06 = 0.16\,\mathrm{mol}\). Since the final volume is \(1.0\,\mathrm{L}\), the final molarity is \(\frac{0.16\,\mathrm{mol}}{1.0\,\mathrm{L}} = 0.16\,\mathrm{M}\).

Practice Question 8: What is the molality of an aqueous glucose solution in which the mole fraction of glucose is \(0.10\)?

  • (A) \(5.55\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(6.17\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(9.00\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(10.0\,\mathrm{m}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Given \(\chi_{\text{glucose}} = 0.10\), we have \(\chi_{\text{water}} = 1 - 0.10 = 0.90\). Taking \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of total solution gives \(0.10\,\mathrm{mol}\) of glucose and \(0.90\,\mathrm{mol}\) of water. Mass of water \(= 0.90 \times 18\,\mathrm{g} = 16.2\,\mathrm{g} = 0.0162\,\mathrm{kg}\). Therefore, \[m = \frac{0.10\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.0162\,\mathrm{kg}} = 6.17\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 9 (Statement-Based): Read the two statements carefully and select the correct option.
Statement I: Molality of a solution does not change with temperature, whereas molarity varies with temperature.
Statement II: The mass of a solvent is independent of temperature, whereas the volume of a solution changes with temperature.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Molality is defined per kilogram of solvent (a mass-to-mole ratio) and is unaffected by temperature changes. Molarity is defined per litre of solution, and because liquid volumes expand or contract with temperature, molarity is temperature-dependent. Both statements are correct.

Practice Question 10 (Assertion-Reason): Given below are two statements—one labelled Assertion (A) and the other labelled Reason (R).
Assertion (A): Both \(12\,\mathrm{g}\) of carbon and \(27\,\mathrm{g}\) of aluminium contain \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\) atoms. (Atomic masses: \(\mathrm{C}=12\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{Al}=27\,\mathrm{u}\))
Reason (R): One mole of any element has a mass in grams numerically equal to its atomic mass in unified mass units (\(\mathrm{u}\)) and contains Avogadro's number of atoms.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: \(12\,\mathrm{g}\) of carbon equals \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{C}\) atoms and \(27\,\mathrm{g}\) of aluminium equals \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Al}\) atoms. By definition of molar mass and the mole, \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of any element weighs its atomic mass in grams and contains \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\) atoms. Thus, both (A) and (R) are true and (R) correctly explains (A).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid:
  • Confusing Molarity and Molality Denominators: Molarity divides by volume of solution (L), whereas molality divides by mass of solvent (kg)—never mass of total solution.
  • Temperature Dependence: Molarity and volume percentages change with temperature; molality, mole fraction, and mass percent do not.
  • Limiting Reagent Oversight: Never assume the reactant with the smaller mass or fewer moles is automatically the limiting reagent—always divide moles by the balanced stoichiometric coefficient first.
  • Significant Figures in Mixed Operations: Apply decimal-place rules for addition/subtraction before applying significant-figure-count rules for multiplication/division.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g}\) • \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\)
  • \(\mathrm{K} = \,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} + 273.15\) • \(^{\circ}\mathrm{F} = \frac{9}{5}(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) + 32\)
  • \(n = \frac{\text{Mass (g)}}{\text{Molar mass (g mol}^{-1}\text{)}} = \frac{N}{N_A} = \frac{V_{\text{STP}}\text{ (L)}}{22.7\text{ (at 1 bar)}}\)
  • \(\text{Molecular formula} = n \times (\text{Empirical formula})\), where \(n = \frac{\text{Molar mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}}\)
  • \(M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2\) (dilution) • \(m = \frac{1000\,M}{1000\,d - M \cdot M_{\text{solute}}}\) (where \(d\) is density in \(\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\))
