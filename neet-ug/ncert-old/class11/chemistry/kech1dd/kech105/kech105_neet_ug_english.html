States of Matter: Gases, Intermolecular Forces, and Liquids

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide covering molecular interactions, ideal and real gas behaviour, kinetic theory, liquefaction, and liquid-state properties.

Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

While atomic structure and chemical bonding describe individual atoms and molecules, observable physical properties—such as boiling, wetting, compressibility, and pressure—emerge from the collective behaviour of vast numbers of particles. Although the chemical identity of a substance (such as \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) in ice, liquid water, and steam) does not change across physical states, its physical properties and reaction rates depend strongly on the state of matter.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Explain why solids, liquids, and gases exist as a competition between intermolecular attractions and thermal energy.
  • Classify van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and short-range repulsive forces, including their distance dependence.
  • Apply Boyle's, Charles', Gay-Lussac's, Avogadro's, Combined, Ideal Gas, and Dalton's laws to quantitative problems.
  • Interpret Maxwell–Boltzmann speed distributions and the postulates of the Kinetic Molecular Theory of gases.
  • Analyze real gas deviations using the compressibility factor (\(Z\)), van der Waals equation, and critical constants (\(T_c, p_c, V_c\)).
  • Relate vapour pressure, boiling point, surface tension, and viscosity to intermolecular forces and temperature.

1. Intermolecular Forces vs. Thermal Energy

Intermolecular forces are the attractive and repulsive interactions operating between neighbouring atoms or molecules. They are distinct from intramolecular covalent bonds (which hold atoms together within a molecule) and pure electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions in an ionic lattice.

Attractive intermolecular forces are collectively termed van der Waals forces, comprising three primary types, alongside a specialized, stronger interaction called hydrogen bonding. Note that ion–dipole forces (between an ion and a polar molecule) are not classified as van der Waals forces.

Types of Attractive and Repulsive Interactions

  • Dispersion (London) Forces: Present in all atoms and molecules, and the only attractive force in nonpolar species (such as \(\mathrm{He}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), or \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)). Momentary fluctuations in the electron cloud create an instantaneous dipole on one particle, which distorts the electron cloud of a neighbour to create an induced dipole. Always attractive, significant at short distances (\(\approx 500\,\mathrm{pm}\)), and stronger for larger, more polarizable particles.
  • Dipole–Dipole Forces: Act between molecules with permanent dipoles (such as \(\mathrm{HCl}\)), where ends carry partial charges (\(\delta+\) and \(\delta-\)) smaller than the unit electronic charge (\(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)). Stronger than London forces alone, though weaker than ion–ion attractions. Polar molecules also experience London forces simultaneously.
  • Dipole–Induced Dipole Forces: Occur when a polar molecule with a permanent dipole distorts the symmetric electron cloud of a nearby nonpolar molecule. Strength increases with both the permanent dipole moment and the polarizability of the nonpolar partner.
  • Hydrogen Bonding: A particularly strong dipole–dipole interaction (\(10\text{ to }100\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) occurring when hydrogen is covalently bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom—primarily \(\mathrm{N}\), \(\mathrm{O}\), or \(\mathrm{F}\) (and occasionally \(\mathrm{Cl}\))—and interacts with a lone pair on another electronegative atom. Crucial in determining the structure of water, proteins, and nucleic acids.
  • Repulsive Forces: When electron clouds and nuclei of two molecules come into very close contact, strong short-range repulsion rises steeply. This explains why liquids and solids are nearly incompressible.
Interaction Type Participating Species Interaction Energy vs. Distance (\(r\))

The Balance That Determines Physical State

Thermal energy arises from the random motion of particles and is directly proportional to absolute temperature. While intermolecular forces pull molecules together, thermal energy drives them apart. A gas forms when thermal energy dominates; a solid forms when intermolecular attractions dominate; a liquid represents an intermediate balance.

2. The Gaseous State and Gas Laws

Under normal temperature and pressure conditions, only eleven elements exist as gases: \(\mathrm{H}\), \(\mathrm{N}\), \(\mathrm{O}\), \(\mathrm{F}\), \(\mathrm{Cl}\), and the six noble gases (\(\mathrm{He}\), \(\mathrm{Ne}\), \(\mathrm{Ar}\), \(\mathrm{Kr}\), \(\mathrm{Xe}\), \(\mathrm{Rn}\)). Gases are highly compressible, exert equal pressure in all directions, have low density, assume the shape and volume of their container, and mix homogeneously without mechanical stirring.

Ideal Gas Equation, Combined Gas Law, and Molar Mass

An ideal gas is a hypothetical gas with zero intermolecular forces and negligible molecular volume that obeys all gas laws strictly. Combining Boyle's, Charles', and Avogadro's laws yields the ideal gas equation (equation of state):

\[pV = nRT \qquad \text{and for a fixed mass:} \qquad \frac{p_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2 V_2}{T_2}\]

The Universal Gas Constant \(R\) takes different numerical values depending on units:

  • \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} = 8.314\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^3\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • \(R = 8.314 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} = 0.08314\,\mathrm{bar\,dm^3\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • \(R = 8.206 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{L\,atm\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \approx 0.0821\,\mathrm{L\,atm\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)

Substituting \(n = \dfrac{m}{M}\) and density \(d = \dfrac{m}{V}\) gives the direct link between gas density and molar mass:

\[d = \frac{pM}{RT} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad M = \frac{dRT}{p}\]

Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures

For a mixture of non-reacting gases at constant temperature and volume, the total pressure equals the sum of the partial pressures each gas would exert if present alone:

\[p_{\text{total}} = p_1 + p_2 + p_3 + \dots \qquad \text{and} \qquad p_i = x_i \, p_{\text{total}}\]

where \(x_i = \dfrac{n_i}{n_{\text{total}}}\) is the mole fraction of the \(i^{\text{th}}\) gas. When a gas is collected over water, it becomes saturated with water vapour. The pressure exerted by saturated water vapour at that temperature is called aqueous tension, so:

\[p_{\text{dry gas}} = p_{\text{total}} - \text{Aqueous tension}\]

3. Kinetic Molecular Theory and Molecular Speeds

The Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) provides a microscopic model explaining macroscopic gas laws:

  1. Gases consist of tiny particles separated by large empty spaces; their actual molecular volume is negligible compared to the container volume.
  2. No attractive forces exist between gas molecules at ordinary temperatures and pressures.
  3. Particles move continuously and randomly in straight lines, colliding with each other and with container walls; wall collisions produce gas pressure.
  4. Collisions are perfectly elastic—total kinetic energy is conserved before and after collision.
  5. At any instant, molecules possess a range of speeds, though the overall speed distribution remains constant at a fixed temperature.
  6. The average translational kinetic energy of gas molecules is directly proportional to absolute temperature (\(KE_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{2}m\overline{u^2} \propto T\)).

Maxwell–Boltzmann Speed Distribution

Because collisions continually redistribute energy, gas speeds are characterized by three statistical values:

  • Most Probable Speed (\(u_{\text{mp}}\)): Speed possessed by the maximum fraction of molecules (peak of the distribution curve).
  • Average Speed (\(u_{\text{av}}\)): Arithmetic mean of individual speeds, \(\dfrac{u_1 + u_2 + \dots + u_n}{n}\).
  • Root Mean Square Speed (\(u_{\text{rms}}\)): Square root of the mean of squared speeds, \(\sqrt{\overline{u^2}}\), directly tied to average kinetic energy.
\[u_{\text{rms}} > u_{\text{av}} > u_{\text{mp}} \qquad \text{with ratio} \qquad u_{\text{mp}} : u_{\text{av}} : u_{\text{rms}} = 1 : 1.128 : 1.224\]

Temperature and Mass Effects: Raising temperature shifts the peak to higher speeds and broadens the curve (more molecules move faster, peak height drops). At the same temperature, lighter molecules (e.g., \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(28\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) have higher \(u_{\text{mp}}\) and a broader distribution than heavier molecules (e.g., \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\), \(71\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)).

4. Real Gases, Deviations, and Liquefaction

Real gases deviate from \(pV = nRT\) at high pressures and low temperatures because two KMT assumptions fail:

  1. Intermolecular attractions are not zero; molecules hitting the wall are pulled inward by neighbours, making observed pressure lower than ideal pressure (\(p_{\text{ideal}} = p_{\text{real}} + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\)).
  2. Molecular volume is not negligible at high pressure; short-range repulsions restrict free movement to an effective volume \((V - nb)\).

Applying both corrections gives the van der Waals equation:

\[\left(p + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT\]
  • Constant \(a\): Measures the magnitude of intermolecular attractive forces (independent of \(T\) and \(p\)). Units: \(\mathrm{bar\,L^2\,mol^{-2}}\) or \(\mathrm{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}}\).
  • Constant \(b\): Represents the effective excluded volume occupied by one mole of gas molecules. Units: \(\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}\).

Compressibility Factor (\(Z\)) and Boyle Temperature

Deviation from ideality is quantified by the compressibility factor:

\[Z = \frac{pV}{nRT} = \frac{V_{\text{real}}}{V_{\text{ideal}}}\]
  • \(Z = 1\): Ideal gas behaviour (approached by all real gases as \(p \to 0\)).
  • \(Z < 1\): Negative deviation; attractive forces dominate, making the gas more compressible than an ideal gas (seen at intermediate pressures for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\)).
  • \(Z > 1\): Positive deviation; repulsive forces (finite molecular volume) dominate, making the gas less compressible (seen at very high pressures for all gases, and at all pressures above zero for \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and \(\mathrm{He}\) at \(273\,\mathrm{K}\)).
  • Boyle Temperature (\(T_B\)): The characteristic temperature at which a real gas obeys Boyle's law (\(Z \approx 1\)) over an appreciable pressure range. Above \(T_B\), \(Z > 1\) continuously; below \(T_B\), \(Z\) first dips below \(1\) before rising above \(1\).
Tutorial Diagram 4: Compressibility factor \(Z\) vs. pressure. All gases converge to \(Z = 1\) as \(p \to 0\). Gases with stronger attractions (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\)) show deeper dips below \(Z = 1\) before rising due to repulsive volume effects.

Liquefaction of Gases and Critical Constants

Studies on \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) isotherms showed that a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone above a specific temperature:

  • Critical Temperature (\(T_c\)): The highest temperature at which a gas can exist as a liquid (for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(T_c = 30.98\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} = 304.10\,\mathrm{K}\)). Higher \(T_c\) indicates stronger intermolecular attractions and easier liquefaction.
  • Critical Pressure (\(p_c\)): Minimum pressure required to liquefy the gas at \(T_c\) (for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(73.9\,\mathrm{bar}\)).
  • Critical Volume (\(V_c\)): Volume of one mole of the substance at \(T_c\) and \(p_c\) (for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(0.0956\,\mathrm{dm^3\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  • Vapour vs. Gas & Continuity of State: A substance below \(T_c\) in the gaseous phase can be liquefied by compression and is called a vapour. By heating above \(T_c\), compressing, and cooling back down, one can convert gas to liquid continuously without ever crossing a two-phase boundary—demonstrating the continuity of state (both are termed fluids).

5. Properties of the Liquid State

In liquids, intermolecular forces are strong enough to keep molecules close together (giving definite volume and low compressibility), yet weak enough to permit molecules to slide past one another (enabling fluidity).

Vapour Pressure and Boiling Point

  • Equilibrium Vapour Pressure: Pressure exerted by vapour in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid in a closed vessel at a given temperature. Increases with temperature and is higher for liquids with weaker intermolecular forces.
  • Boiling: Rapid vapourisation throughout the bulk of a liquid when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure.
  • Normal vs. Standard Boiling Point: At \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\), the boiling point is the normal boiling point (water: \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} = 373\,\mathrm{K}\)). At \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\), it is the standard boiling point (water: \(99.6\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} = 372.6\,\mathrm{K}\)), slightly lower because \(1\,\mathrm{bar} < 1\,\mathrm{atm}\).
  • Pressure Effects: At high altitudes, lower atmospheric pressure lowers the boiling point (requiring pressure cookers to cook food faster). Conversely, hospital autoclaves increase pressure to raise water's boiling point for sterilization. In a closed vessel, boiling never occurs; heating eventually equalizes liquid and vapour densities at \(T_c\), where the meniscus disappears.

Surface Tension (\(\gamma\))

An interior liquid molecule is pulled equally in all directions, experiencing zero net force. A surface molecule has no liquid neighbours above it, so it experiences a net inward attractive force and possesses higher potential energy. Consequently, liquids minimize their surface area—forming spherical droplets (minimum surface-to-volume ratio) and rounding sharp glass edges during fire polishing.

  • Definition & Units: Force acting per unit length perpendicular to a line on the liquid surface (\(\gamma\); SI unit: \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\), dimensions: \(\mathrm{kg\,s^{-2}}\)). Numerically equal to surface energy (\(\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}\)), the work required to increase surface area by one unit.
  • Temperature Effect: Increasing temperature raises kinetic energy and weakens intermolecular attractions, so surface tension decreases with increasing temperature.
Tutorial Diagram 5: Origin of surface tension. Interior molecules experience symmetric attractions, whereas surface molecules experience a net downward pull into the bulk.

Viscosity (\(\eta\))

Viscosity is a liquid's internal resistance to flow caused by friction between adjacent layers sliding past each other in laminar flow (regular velocity gradation across layers). The tangential force required to maintain a velocity gradient \(\dfrac{du}{dz}\) across contact area \(A\) is:

\[F = \eta A \frac{du}{dz}\]
  • Units of \(\eta\) (Coefficient of Viscosity): SI unit is \(\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{Pa\,s} = \mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\). CGS unit is poise (\(1\,\mathrm{poise} = 1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-1}\,s^{-1}} = 10^{-1}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\)).
  • Trends: Strong hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces increase viscosity (glass is an extremely viscous liquid). Viscosity decreases as temperature rises because higher kinetic energy helps molecules overcome intermolecular drag.

Worked Examples

1. Applying Boyle's Law and Density–Pressure Proportionality

Problem: Assume a fixed sample of oxygen gas occupies \(4.5\,\mathrm{L}\) at \(1.2\,\mathrm{bar}\) and \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) with a density of \(1.54\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}\). If the gas is compressed isothermally to \(1.5\,\mathrm{L}\), calculate the final pressure and final density.

Solution:

At constant temperature and fixed moles, Boyle's law gives \(p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2\):

\[p_2 = \frac{p_1 V_1}{V_2} = \frac{1.2\,\mathrm{bar} \times 4.5\,\mathrm{L}}{1.5\,\mathrm{L}} = 3.6\,\mathrm{bar}\]

Since density is directly proportional to pressure at constant temperature (\(d \propto p\)):

\[d_2 = d_1 \times \frac{p_2}{p_1} = 1.54\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}} \times \frac{3.6\,\mathrm{bar}}{1.2\,\mathrm{bar}} = 4.62\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}\]

2. Determining Molar Mass from the Ideal Gas Equation

Problem: Suppose \(1.00\,\mathrm{g}\) of an unknown ideal gas occupies \(500\,\mathrm{mL}\) at \(27\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and \(1.66\,\mathrm{bar}\). Using \(R = 0.083\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\), find the molar mass of the gas.

Solution:

Convert temperature to Kelvin and volume to litres: \(T = 27 + 273 = 300\,\mathrm{K}\), \(V = 0.500\,\mathrm{L}\). Thus, density \(d = \dfrac{1.00\,\mathrm{g}}{0.500\,\mathrm{L}} = 2.00\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}\).

\[M = \frac{dRT}{p} = \frac{2.00\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}} \times 0.083\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \times 300\,\mathrm{K}}{1.66\,\mathrm{bar}} = \frac{49.8}{1.66} = 30.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\]

3. Calculating Partial Pressure of a Moist Gas Mixture

Problem: Assume a mixture of \(0.40\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) and \(0.60\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{He}\) is collected over water at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). The total measured pressure is \(2.53\,\mathrm{bar}\), and the assumed aqueous tension at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(0.03\,\mathrm{bar}\). Calculate the partial pressure of dry \(\mathrm{N_2}\).

Solution:

First subtract aqueous tension to obtain the dry gas pressure:

\[p_{\text{dry}} = p_{\text{total}} - \text{Aqueous tension} = 2.53\,\mathrm{bar} - 0.03\,\mathrm{bar} = 2.50\,\mathrm{bar}\]

Calculate the mole fraction of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) in the dry mixture:

\[x_{\mathrm{N_2}} = \frac{0.40}{0.40 + 0.60} = 0.40\]

By Dalton's law, \(p_{\mathrm{N_2}} = x_{\mathrm{N_2}} \times p_{\text{dry}} = 0.40 \times 2.50\,\mathrm{bar} = 1.00\,\mathrm{bar}\).

4. Evaluating Compressibility Factor (\(Z\))

Problem: Assume \(1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a real gas at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(50\,\mathrm{bar}\) occupies \(0.40\,\mathrm{L}\). Taking \(R = 0.083\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\), calculate \(Z\) and identify whether attractive or repulsive forces dominate.

Solution:

Calculate the ideal molar volume under the same \(T\) and \(p\):

\[V_{\text{ideal}} = \frac{nRT}{p} = \frac{1.0 \times 0.083 \times 300}{50} = 0.498\,\mathrm{L}\] \[Z = \frac{V_{\text{real}}}{V_{\text{ideal}}} = \frac{0.40\,\mathrm{L}}{0.498\,\mathrm{L}} \approx 0.803\]

Since \(Z < 1\), the gas occupies less volume than an ideal gas (greater compressibility), showing that attractive intermolecular forces dominate.

5. Comparing Liquefaction Tendencies via Critical Temperature

Problem: Hypothetical gas X has \(T_c = 154\,\mathrm{K}\) and gas Y has \(T_c = 405\,\mathrm{K}\). Which gas has stronger intermolecular forces, and which will liquefy first when both are cooled from \(500\,\mathrm{K}\) under sufficient pressure?

Solution:

Critical temperature is directly related to the strength of intermolecular attractions. Because \(T_c(\mathrm{Y}) > T_c(\mathrm{X})\), gas Y has stronger intermolecular forces. On cooling from \(500\,\mathrm{K}\), \(405\,\mathrm{K}\) is reached before \(154\,\mathrm{K}\), so gas Y liquefies first.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following intermolecular interactions has an interaction energy inversely proportional to \(r^3\) (where \(r\) is the distance between interacting particles)?

  1. (A) Dispersion (London) forces between two helium atoms
  2. (B) Dipole–dipole interaction between stationary polar molecules in a solid
  3. (C) Dipole–induced dipole interaction between \(\mathrm{HCl}\) and \(\mathrm{Ar}\)
  4. (D) Dipole–dipole interaction between rapidly rotating polar molecules in a gas

Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: London dispersion forces, dipole–induced dipole forces, and dipole–dipole interactions between rotating polar molecules all have interaction energies proportional to \(1/r^6\). Only dipole–dipole interactions between stationary polar molecules (as in solids) are proportional to \(1/r^3\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

For a fixed amount of an ideal gas, a plot of pressure (\(p\)) versus \(1/V\) at three different constant temperatures \(T_1, T_2, T_3\) gives three straight lines passing through the origin with slopes \(m_3 > m_2 > m_1\). Which relation among the temperatures is correct?

  1. (A) \(T_1 > T_2 > T_3\)
  2. (B) \(T_3 > T_2 > T_1\)
  3. (C) \(T_1 = T_2 = T_3\)
  4. (D) \(T_2 > T_3 > T_1\)

Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: From the ideal gas equation, \(p = (nRT)\left(\frac{1}{V}\right)\). Thus, the slope of a \(p\) vs. \(1/V\) line is \(m = nRT\). Since \(n\) and \(R\) are constant, higher slope directly corresponds to higher temperature: \(m_3 > m_2 > m_1 \implies T_3 > T_2 > T_1\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The density of a gaseous compound is \(1.80\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}\) at \(27\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and \(1.245\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure. What is its molar mass? (Given \(R = 0.083\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\))

  1. (A) \(18.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)
  2. (B) \(28.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)
  3. (C) \(36.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)
  4. (D) \(44.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)

Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Convert temperature: \(T = 27 + 273 = 300\,\mathrm{K}\). Using \(M = \dfrac{dRT}{p}\):

\[M = \frac{1.80 \times 0.083 \times 300}{1.245} = \frac{1.80 \times 24.9}{1.245} = 1.80 \times 20 = 36.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\]
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A non-reacting gaseous mixture at \(3.0\,\mathrm{bar}\) total pressure contains \(20\%\) by mass of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) (molar mass \(2\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) and \(80\%\) by mass of \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) (molar mass \(16\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)). What is the partial pressure of \(\mathrm{H_2}\)?

  1. (A) \(0.60\,\mathrm{bar}\)
  2. (B) \(1.50\,\mathrm{bar}\)
  3. (C) \(2.00\,\mathrm{bar}\)
  4. (D) \(2.40\,\mathrm{bar}\)

Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Assume \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of mixture: \(m_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 20\,\mathrm{g}\) and \(m_{\mathrm{CH_4}} = 80\,\mathrm{g}\).

\[n_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \frac{20}{2} = 10\,\mathrm{mol}, \qquad n_{\mathrm{CH_4}} = \frac{80}{16} = 5\,\mathrm{mol}\] \[x_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \frac{10}{10 + 5} = \frac{2}{3} \implies p_{\mathrm{H_2}} = x_{\mathrm{H_2}} \, p_{\text{total}} = \frac{2}{3} \times 3.0\,\mathrm{bar} = 2.00\,\mathrm{bar}\]
Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If the absolute temperature of an ideal gas is increased fourfold from \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(1200\,\mathrm{K}\), by what factor does the root mean square speed (\(u_{\text{rms}}\)) of its molecules change?

  1. (A) Increases by a factor of \(2\)
  2. (B) Increases by a factor of \(4\)
  3. (C) Increases by a factor of \(16\)
  4. (D) Remains unchanged

Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: According to Kinetic Molecular Theory, average translational kinetic energy \(\frac{1}{2}m\overline{u^2}\) is directly proportional to absolute temperature \(T\). Since \(u_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{u^2}}\), it follows that \(u_{\text{rms}} \propto \sqrt{T}\). Quadrupling \(T\) increases \(u_{\text{rms}}\) by \(\sqrt{4} = 2\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

At very high pressures, the compressibility factor \(Z\) for a real gas is greater than \(1\) (\(Z > 1\)). Which statement best explains this observation?

  1. (A) Intermolecular attractive forces dominate, making the gas easier to compress.
  2. (B) Short-range repulsive forces and finite molecular volume (\(nb\)) dominate, making the gas harder to compress.
  3. (C) The gas obeys Boyle's law strictly at very high pressures.
  4. (D) The van der Waals constant \(a\) becomes zero at high pressure.

Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: At very high pressures, molecules are forced close together so short-range repulsions and the excluded molecular volume (\(nb\)) dominate over attractions. Consequently, \(V_{\text{real}} > V_{\text{ideal}}\) and \(Z = \frac{V_{\text{real}}}{V_{\text{ideal}}} > 1\), meaning the gas is less compressible than an ideal gas.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The critical temperatures (\(T_c\)) of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\), and \(\mathrm{H_2}\) are \(405.5\,\mathrm{K}\), \(304.1\,\mathrm{K}\), \(154.3\,\mathrm{K}\), and \(33.2\,\mathrm{K}\), respectively. Which gas exhibits the weakest intermolecular attractive forces?

  1. (A) \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)
  2. (B) \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)
  3. (C) \(\mathrm{O_2}\)
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{H_2}\)

Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: A lower critical temperature indicates weaker intermolecular attractive forces, as the gas must be cooled to a much lower temperature before attractions can overcome thermal motion to cause liquefaction. \(\mathrm{H_2}\) has the lowest \(T_c\) (\(33.2\,\mathrm{K}\)) and thus the weakest intermolecular forces.

Practice Question 8 (Statement-I & Statement-II Type)

Statement I: The standard boiling point of water (\(99.6\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)) is slightly lower than its normal boiling point (\(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)).
Statement II: Standard boiling point is measured at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure, which is slightly less than \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) pressure.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Boiling occurs when vapour pressure equals external pressure. Since \(1\,\mathrm{bar} = 100\,\mathrm{kPa}\) is slightly lower than \(1\,\mathrm{atm} = 101.325\,\mathrm{kPa}\), water's vapour pressure reaches \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) at a slightly lower temperature (\(99.6\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)) than \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) (\(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)). Both statements are correct.

Practice Question 9 (Assertion–Reason Type)

Assertion (A): Both surface tension and viscosity of a liquid decrease with an increase in temperature.
Reason (R): Increasing temperature increases the average kinetic energy of liquid molecules, enabling them to overcome intermolecular attractions more easily.

  1. (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  2. (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  3. (C) A is true, but R is false.
  4. (D) A is false, but R is true.

Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Both surface tension and viscosity originate from intermolecular attractive forces. Raising temperature boosts molecular kinetic energy, reducing the effectiveness of these cohesive forces—so both properties decrease as temperature rises.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Always convert temperature to Kelvin: Never substitute °C into Charles' law, Gay-Lussac's law, or \(pV = nRT\). Remember \(T(\mathrm{K}) = t(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) + 273.15\).
  • Match the units of \(R\) to \(p\) and \(V\): Use \(R = 0.08314\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) when pressure is in \(\mathrm{bar}\) and volume in \(\mathrm{L}\) (or \(\mathrm{dm^3}\)); use \(0.0821\) for \(\mathrm{L\,atm}\); use \(8.314\) for SI units (\(\mathrm{Pa}\) and \(\mathrm{m^3}\)).
  • Check STP definition: Modern STP is \(273.15\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) (\(22.71\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\)), whereas older data at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) gives \(22.41\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\).
  • Do not confuse \(Z < 1\) and \(Z > 1\): \(Z < 1\) means attractive forces dominate (more compressible); \(Z > 1\) means repulsive/volume effects dominate (less compressible).
  • Vapour vs. Gas: Only a gas below its critical temperature \(T_c\) is a vapour that can be liquefied by pressure alone.
  • Temperature trends in liquids: Vapour pressure increases with temperature, while both surface tension and viscosity decrease with temperature.
