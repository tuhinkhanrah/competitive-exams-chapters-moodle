Chemical Thermodynamics

Energy Transformations, Enthalpy, Entropy, Spontaneity, and Chemical Equilibrium

Learning Objectives and Scope

Thermodynamics investigates macroscopic energy transformations during physical and chemical changes. Unlike chemical kinetics, which studies reaction rates and mechanisms, thermodynamics focuses strictly on the initial and final equilibrium states of a system—telling us how much energy is exchanged and whether a process can occur spontaneously.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Classify thermodynamic systems (open, closed, isolated) and distinguish state functions from path functions, as well as intensive from extensive properties.
  • Apply the First Law of Thermodynamics using IUPAC sign conventions for heat (\(q\)) and work (\(w\)), and calculate pressure–volume work for reversible and irreversible processes.
  • Relate internal energy change (\(\Delta U\)) and enthalpy change (\(\Delta H\)) via \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\), and interpret constant-volume and constant-pressure calorimetry.
  • Calculate reaction enthalpies using standard enthalpies of formation, combustion, atomization, bond enthalpies, Born–Haber cycles, and Hess's Law.
  • Predict reaction spontaneity using entropy (\(\Delta S_{\text{total}}\)), the Second and Third Laws of Thermodynamics, and Gibbs energy (\(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\)).
  • Connect standard Gibbs energy change (\(\Delta_r G^\ominus\)) to the equilibrium constant (\(K\)).

1. Thermodynamic Systems, Boundaries, and State Variables

System, Surroundings, and Boundary

To analyze energy flow cleanly, we divide the universe into two parts separated by a real or imaginary wall called the boundary:

  • System: The specific portion of the universe chosen for observation (for example, a reacting solution inside a flask).
  • Surroundings: Everything outside the boundary that can interact with the system. Together, \(\text{Universe} = \text{System} + \text{Surroundings}\).
System Type Matter Exchange Energy Exchange Typical Example
Open System Allowed Allowed Reactants in an open beaker
Closed System Not allowed Allowed Gas in a sealed conducting steel cylinder
Isolated System Not allowed Not allowed Hot tea in a perfectly insulated thermos flask

State Functions vs. Path Functions

The macroscopic condition of a system is defined by measurable properties such as pressure (\(p\)), volume (\(V\)), temperature (\(T\)), and amount of substance (\(n\)).

  • State Functions: Properties whose values depend solely on the current state of the system and are completely independent of the path taken to reach that state. Examples: \(p\), \(V\), \(T\), internal energy (\(U\)), enthalpy (\(H\)), entropy (\(S\)), and Gibbs energy (\(G\)). For any cyclic process, the net change in a state function is zero (\(\oint dX = 0\)).
  • Path Functions: Quantities whose magnitudes depend on the specific route or process followed between initial and final states. Examples: heat (\(q\)) and work (\(w\)).

Extensive vs. Intensive Properties

Property Category Dependence on Amount of Matter Examples
Extensive Properties Directly proportional to system size or mass (additive). Mass (\(m\)), Volume (\(V\)), Internal Energy (\(U\)), Enthalpy (\(H\)), Entropy (\(S\)), Heat Capacity (\(C\)), Gibbs Energy (\(G\))
Intensive Properties Independent of system size or mass (non-additive). Temperature (\(T\)), Pressure (\(p\)), Density (\(\rho\)), Concentration, Specific Heat (\(c\)), Molar Properties (\(\chi_m = \chi / n\))
NEET UG Key Insight: The ratio of two extensive properties is always an intensive property. For instance, density (\(\rho = m/V\)), molar volume (\(V_m = V/n\)), and molar heat capacity (\(C_m = C/n\)) do not change when a system is partitioned into smaller identical parts.

2. Internal Energy, Work, Heat, and the First Law

Internal Energy (\(U\)) and Modes of Energy Transfer

The internal energy (\(U\)) represents the sum of all microscopic forms of energy (translational, rotational, vibrational, electronic, and chemical bond energies) within a system. While the absolute value of \(U\) cannot be measured, its change \(\Delta U = U_2 - U_1\) can be determined precisely. For a closed system, \(U\) changes only through heat transfer (\(q\)) or work (\(w\)).

First Law of Thermodynamics (Conservation of Energy): \[\Delta U = q + w\] For an isolated system (\(q = 0\) and \(w = 0\)), \(\Delta U = 0\); hence, the total energy of an isolated system remains constant.
IUPAC Sign Convention (Chemistry Standard):
  • \(q > 0\): Heat is absorbed by the system (endothermic).
  • \(q < 0\): Heat is released by the system to the surroundings (exothermic).
  • \(w > 0\): Work is done on the system (e.g., compression).
  • \(w < 0\): Work is done by the system (e.g., expansion).
Note: Be careful not to mix this with the physics convention where work done by the system is taken as positive (\(\Delta U = q - w\)). In NEET Chemistry, always use \(\Delta U = q + w\).

Pressure–Volume (\(pV\)) Work

When a gas expands or is compressed against an external pressure \(p_{\text{ex}}\), the mechanical work is given by:

\[w = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} p_{\text{ex}}\,dV\]
  1. Irreversible Process (Constant \(p_{\text{ex}}\)): \[w_{\text{irrev}} = -p_{\text{ex}}(V_f - V_i) = -p_{\text{ex}}\Delta V\] During compression, \(V_f < V_i\) (\(\Delta V < 0\)), so \(w > 0\). During expansion, \(V_f > V_i\) (\(\Delta V > 0\)), so \(w < 0\).
  2. Free Expansion (Expansion into Vacuum): Here \(p_{\text{ex}} = 0\), so \(w = 0\) regardless of whether the process is reversible or irreversible. For an ideal gas expanding isothermally into vacuum, Joule's experiment shows \(q = 0\) and \(\Delta U = 0\) as well.
  3. Reversible Isothermal Process (Ideal Gas): A reversible process proceeds infinitely slowly through a continuous sequence of equilibrium states where \(p_{\text{ex}} = p_{\text{in}} \pm dp\). Substituting \(p = nRT/V\) at constant \(T\): \[w_{\text{rev}} = -nRT \ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right) = -2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right) = -2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\left(\frac{p_i}{p_f}\right)\] For an ideal gas in an isothermal process, \(\Delta U = 0\), which gives \(q = -w\).
  4. Adiabatic Process: No heat crosses the insulated boundary (\(q = 0\)), so \(\Delta U = w_{\text{ad}}\).

3. Enthalpy, Heat Capacity, and Calorimetry

Enthalpy (\(H\)) and its Relation to Internal Energy

Most laboratory reactions occur in open vessels under constant atmospheric pressure rather than at constant volume. To track heat changes conveniently at constant pressure, we define the state function enthalpy (\(H\)):

\[H = U + pV\] At constant pressure: \(\Delta H = \Delta U + p\Delta V = q_p\)
At constant volume (\(\Delta V = 0\)): \(\Delta U = q_v\)

For reactions involving ideal gases at constant temperature and pressure, \(p\Delta V = \Delta n_g RT\), yielding the essential NEET UG relation:

\[\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\] where \(\Delta n_g = (\text{moles of gaseous products}) - (\text{moles of gaseous reactants})\).

Crucial Rule: Solids and liquids undergo negligible volume change compared to gases, so only gaseous species are counted when computing \(\Delta n_g\). When \(\Delta n_g = 0\), \(\Delta H = \Delta U\).

Heat Capacity (\(C\)) and Mayer's Relation

The heat absorbed by a substance is proportional to its temperature rise: \(q = C\Delta T = m c \Delta T = n C_m \Delta T\), where \(c\) is specific heat capacity (per unit mass) and \(C_m\) is molar heat capacity (per mole).

  • At constant volume: \(q_v = C_v \Delta T = \Delta U\)
  • At constant pressure: \(q_p = C_p \Delta T = \Delta H\)

For \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal gas, \(\Delta H = \Delta U + R\Delta T\), which leads directly to:

\[C_p - C_v = R\]

Calorimetry: Measuring \(\Delta U\) and \(\Delta H\)

  • Bomb Calorimeter (Constant Volume): A sample is combusted in excess pure \(\mathrm{O_2}\) inside a sealed steel vessel immersed in a water bath. Because volume is fixed (\(\Delta V = 0\)), the heat evolved equals \(\Delta U = q_v = -C_{\text{cal}}\Delta T\).
  • Constant-Pressure Calorimeter: Reactions in solution are carried out in an insulated (e.g., foamed polystyrene) cup open to atmospheric pressure, directly measuring \(\Delta H = q_p\).

4. Thermochemistry, Reaction Enthalpies, and Hess's Law

Standard State and Thermochemical Equations

The standard state of a substance at a specified temperature (commonly \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)) is its pure form at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure. Standard enthalpy changes are denoted with the superscript \(\ominus\) (e.g., \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\)).

In a balanced thermochemical equation:

  • Physical states and allotropic forms (such as \(\mathrm{C(graphite)}\) vs. \(\mathrm{C(diamond)}\)) must be specified explicitly.
  • Stoichiometric coefficients represent moles. Multiplying an equation by a factor \(k\) multiplies \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) by \(k\) (because enthalpy is extensive).
  • Reversing a chemical equation reverses the sign of \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\).

Key Types of Standard Enthalpy Changes

Enthalpy Type Definition Sign & Key Formula
Enthalpy of Fusion (\(\Delta_{\text{fus}}H^\ominus\)) Melting of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of solid to liquid at its melting point and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\). Always positive (\(> 0\))
Enthalpy of Vaporization (\(\Delta_{\text{vap}}H^\ominus\)) Vaporizing \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of liquid at constant \(T\) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\). Always positive (\(> 0\))
Enthalpy of Sublimation (\(\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\ominus\)) Direct conversion of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of solid to gas at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\). \(\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\ominus = \Delta_{\text{fus}}H^\ominus + \Delta_{\text{vap}}H^\ominus\)
Enthalpy of Formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)) Formation of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of compound from elements in their most stable reference states. \(\Delta_f H^\ominus(\text{element in ref. state}) = 0\)
Enthalpy of Combustion (\(\Delta_c H^\ominus\)) Complete combustion of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of substance in excess \(\mathrm{O_2}\) under standard conditions. Always negative (\(< 0\))
Enthalpy of Atomization (\(\Delta_a H^\ominus\)) Breaking \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of substance completely into gaseous atoms. Always positive (\(> 0\))

Calculating \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) from Formation Enthalpies vs. Bond Enthalpies

Method 1 (Exact, using \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)): \[\Delta_r H^\ominus = \sum a_i\,\Delta_f H^\ominus(\text{products}) - \sum b_i\,\Delta_f H^\ominus(\text{reactants})\] Method 2 (Approximate, for gas-phase reactions using mean bond enthalpies): \[\Delta_r H^\ominus \approx \sum \Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\ominus(\text{reactants}) - \sum \Delta_{\text{bond}} H^\ominus(\text{products})\]

Notice the reversed order: formation enthalpies take products minus reactants, whereas bond enthalpies take reactants minus products (because energy is absorbed to break reactant bonds and released when product bonds form).

Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation

Because enthalpy is a state function, the total enthalpy change for a reaction is identical whether it proceeds in a single step or through a sequence of intermediate steps at the same temperature:

\[\Delta_r H^\ominus = \Delta_r H_1^\ominus + \Delta_r H_2^\ominus + \Delta_r H_3^\ominus + \cdots\]

Born–Haber Cycle, Lattice Enthalpy, and Enthalpy of Solution

Lattice enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus\)) is the enthalpy change when \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ionic solid dissociates completely into gaseous ions (an endothermic process, e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na^+(g)} + \mathrm{Cl^-(g)}\)). It is determined indirectly via Hess's Law using a Born–Haber cycle combining sublimation enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\ominus\)), ionization enthalpy (\(\Delta_i H^\ominus\)), bond dissociation enthalpy (\(\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^\ominus\)), electron gain enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\text{eg}}H^\ominus\)), and standard enthalpy of formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)):

\[\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus = \Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\ominus + \Delta_i H^\ominus + \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^\ominus + \Delta_{\text{eg}}H^\ominus - \Delta_f H^\ominus\]

When an ionic solid dissolves in water, the enthalpy of solution (\(\Delta_{\text{sol}}H^\ominus\)) balances lattice breaking against ion hydration (\(\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\ominus < 0\)):

\[\Delta_{\text{sol}}H^\ominus = \Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus + \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\ominus\]

The enthalpy of dilution is the additional heat change observed when more solvent is added to a solution of a given concentration.

5. Spontaneity, Entropy, and the Second and Third Laws

What Does Spontaneity Mean?

A spontaneous process has an inherent thermodynamic tendency to proceed in a given direction without continuous external assistance. Importantly, spontaneity reveals nothing about reaction speed—the reaction between \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2(g)}\) at room temperature is thermodynamically spontaneous yet immeasurably slow without a spark or catalyst. All spontaneous processes are thermodynamically irreversible.

Entropy (\(S\)) and the Second Law of Thermodynamics

Decrease in enthalpy (\(\Delta H < 0\)) alone cannot predict spontaneity because many endothermic processes (such as ice melting at room temperature or dissolution of certain salts) occur spontaneously. The missing factor is entropy (\(S\)), a state function measuring the degree of randomness or molecular disorder in a system:

  • For a given substance, entropy increases in the order: \(\text{Crystalline Solid} < \text{Liquid} < \text{Gas}\).
  • Entropy increases when temperature rises, volume increases, a solid dissolves, or a reaction produces more moles of gas (\(\Delta n_g > 0\)).
Thermodynamic Definition of Entropy Change: \[\Delta S = \frac{q_{\text{rev}}}{T}\] Second Law Criterion for Spontaneity: \[\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} > 0\] At equilibrium, \(\Delta S_{\text{total}} = 0\). At constant temperature and pressure, \(\Delta S_{\text{surr}} = -\dfrac{\Delta H_{\text{sys}}}{T}\).

Third Law of Thermodynamics

The entropy of any pure, perfectly ordered crystalline substance approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero (\(0\,\mathrm{K}\)). This allows calculation of absolute standard molar entropies (\(S^\ominus\)) from thermal data.

6. Gibbs Energy and Chemical Equilibrium

Gibbs Energy (\(G\)) as a System-Only Criterion

Rather than computing \(\Delta S_{\text{surr}}\) for every reaction, we define the state function Gibbs energy (\(G\)):

\[G = H - TS\] At constant temperature and pressure: \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\)

Because \(\Delta G = -T\Delta S_{\text{total}}\) at constant \(T\) and \(p\), the spontaneity criterion simplifies to:

  • \(\Delta G < 0\): Spontaneous process (net useful work can be extracted).
  • \(\Delta G = 0\): System is in dynamic equilibrium.
  • \(\Delta G > 0\): Non-spontaneous in the forward direction (spontaneous in reverse).
\(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) \(\Delta_r S^\ominus\) \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = \Delta_r H^\ominus - T\Delta_r S^\ominus\) Spontaneity Behavior
\(-\) (Exothermic) \(+\) (More disorder) Always \(-\) Spontaneous at all temperatures
\(+\) (Endothermic) \(-\) (Less disorder) Always \(+\) Non-spontaneous at all temperatures
\(-\) (Exothermic) \(-\) (Less disorder) \(-\) at low \(T\); \(+\) at high \(T\) Spontaneous at low temperatures (\(T < \Delta H/\Delta S\))
\(+\) (Endothermic) \(+\) (More disorder) \(+\) at low \(T\); \(-\) at high \(T\) Spontaneous at high temperatures (\(T > \Delta H/\Delta S\))

Standard Gibbs Energy and Equilibrium Constant (\(K\))

At chemical equilibrium, \(\Delta_r G = 0\), which connects the standard Gibbs energy change \(\Delta_r G^\ominus\) directly to the equilibrium constant \(K\):

\[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -RT \ln K = -2.303\,RT \log_{10} K\]
  • If \(\Delta_r G^\ominus < 0\), then \(K > 1\) (products are favored at equilibrium).
  • If \(\Delta_r G^\ominus > 0\), then \(K < 1\) (reactants are favored at equilibrium).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Applying the First Law and IUPAC Sign Conventions

Problem: A gas sample (assumed ideal) absorbs \(450\,\mathrm{J}\) of heat from its surroundings and expands against a constant external pressure of \(2.0\,\mathrm{atm}\) from an initial volume of \(2.0\,\mathrm{L}\) to a final volume of \(3.5\,\mathrm{L}\). Calculate the change in internal energy (\(\Delta U\)) in joules. (Take \(1\,\mathrm{L\,atm} = 101.3\,\mathrm{J}\)).

Solution:

Heat is absorbed by the system, so \(q = +450\,\mathrm{J}\).

Work done during irreversible expansion against constant \(p_{\text{ex}}\):

\[w = -p_{\text{ex}}(V_f - V_i) = -2.0\,\mathrm{atm} \times (3.5 - 2.0)\,\mathrm{L} = -3.0\,\mathrm{L\,atm}\] \[w = -3.0 \times 101.3\,\mathrm{J} = -303.9\,\mathrm{J}\]

Applying the First Law of Thermodynamics:

\[\Delta U = q + w = 450\,\mathrm{J} + (-303.9\,\mathrm{J}) = +146.1\,\mathrm{J}\]

Example 2: Calculating Reversible Isothermal Work

Problem: Suppose \(2.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal gas expands isothermally and reversibly at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) from \(5.0\,\mathrm{L}\) to \(50.0\,\mathrm{L}\). Calculate \(w_{\text{rev}}\) and \(q\) in \(\mathrm{kJ}\). (\(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

\[w_{\text{rev}} = -2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right)\] \[w_{\text{rev}} = -2.303 \times 2.0\,\mathrm{mol} \times 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \times 300\,\mathrm{K} \times \log_{10}\left(\frac{50.0}{5.0}\right)\]

Since \(\log_{10}(10) = 1\):

\[w_{\text{rev}} = -11488.3\,\mathrm{J} \approx -11.49\,\mathrm{kJ}\]

For an isothermal process in an ideal gas, \(\Delta U = 0\). Therefore, \(q = -w_{\text{rev}} = +11.49\,\mathrm{kJ}\).

Example 3: Relating \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta U\) for a Combustion Reaction

Problem: Assume the internal energy change for the complete combustion of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of liquid benzene in a bomb calorimeter at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(\Delta U = -3263.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Calculate \(\Delta H\) at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\). (\(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

Write the balanced combustion equation with physical states at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\):

\[\mathrm{C_6H_6(l)} + \frac{15}{2}\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 6\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 3\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\]

Count only gaseous moles to find \(\Delta n_g\):

\[\Delta n_g = 6 - 7.5 = -1.5\] \[\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT = -3263.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} + (-1.5)(8.314 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}})(300\,\mathrm{K})\] \[\Delta H = -3263.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} - 3.74\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} = -3267.64\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Example 4: Determining Enthalpy of Formation via Hess's Law

Problem: Using the following hypothetical rounded standard enthalpies of combustion at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), calculate \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\) of ethane, \(\mathrm{C_2H_6(g)}\):

  • \(\Delta_c H^\ominus[\mathrm{C(graphite)}] = -394\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • \(\Delta_c H^\ominus[\mathrm{H_2(g)}] = -286\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • \(\Delta_c H^\ominus[\mathrm{C_2H_6(g)}] = -1560\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)

Solution:

The target formation reaction is:

\[2\mathrm{C(graphite)} + 3\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{C_2H_6(g)}\]

Multiply the carbon combustion equation by 2, the hydrogen combustion equation by 3, and add the reverse of the ethane combustion equation:

\[\Delta_f H^\ominus[\mathrm{C_2H_6(g)}] = 2(-394) + 3(-286) - (-1560)\] \[\Delta_f H^\ominus[\mathrm{C_2H_6(g)}] = -788 - 858 + 1560 = -86\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Example 5: Evaluating Temperature Threshold for Spontaneity

Problem: A hypothetical chemical reaction has \(\Delta H^\ominus = +60.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(\Delta S^\ominus = +150\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\). Assuming \(\Delta H^\ominus\) and \(\Delta S^\ominus\) are independent of temperature, above what temperature will the reaction become spontaneous?

Solution:

For spontaneity, \(\Delta G^\ominus = \Delta H^\ominus - T\Delta S^\ominus < 0\). First convert units consistently to joules:

\[\Delta H^\ominus = 60000\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}, \quad \Delta S^\ominus = 150\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\]

At the crossover temperature (\(\Delta G^\ominus = 0\)):

\[T = \frac{\Delta H^\ominus}{\Delta S^\ominus} = \frac{60000}{150} = 400\,\mathrm{K}\]

Since both \(\Delta H^\ominus > 0\) and \(\Delta S^\ominus > 0\), the reaction becomes spontaneous above \(400\,\mathrm{K}\).

Example 6: Connecting Standard Gibbs Energy Change to Equilibrium Constant

Problem: At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), an assumed equilibrium reaction has an equilibrium constant \(K = 1.0 \times 10^4\). Calculate \(\Delta_r G^\ominus\) in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). (\(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

\[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K\] \[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303 \times 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \times 298\,\mathrm{K} \times \log_{10}(10^4)\] \[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -5705.8 \times 4\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} = -22823\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx -22.82\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following thermodynamic properties is an intensive property?

  • (A) Enthalpy
  • (B) Internal energy
  • (C) Molar heat capacity
  • (D) Volume
Answer: (C) Molar heat capacity

Explanation: Enthalpy, internal energy, and volume depend on the amount of matter present and are extensive. Molar heat capacity (\(C_m = C/n\)) is the ratio of two extensive properties and is independent of system size, making it intensive.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Two moles of an ideal gas expand isothermally into a vacuum from \(2.0\,\mathrm{L}\) to \(10.0\,\mathrm{L}\) at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\). What are the values of work (\(w\)), heat absorbed (\(q\)), and internal energy change (\(\Delta U\))?

  • (A) \(w = -19.1\,\mathrm{kJ},\ q = +19.1\,\mathrm{kJ},\ \Delta U = 0\)
  • (B) \(w = 0,\ q = 0,\ \Delta U = 0\)
  • (C) \(w = -8.0\,\mathrm{L\,atm},\ q = +8.0\,\mathrm{L\,atm},\ \Delta U = 0\)
  • (D) \(w = 0,\ q = +19.1\,\mathrm{kJ},\ \Delta U = +19.1\,\mathrm{kJ}\)
Answer: (B) \(w = 0,\ q = 0,\ \Delta U = 0\)

Explanation: Expansion into a vacuum is free expansion where \(p_{\text{ex}} = 0\), so \(w = -p_{\text{ex}}\Delta V = 0\). Because the gas is ideal and the process is isothermal (\(\Delta T = 0\)), \(\Delta U = 0\). By the First Law, \(q = \Delta U - w = 0\).

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

For the gas-phase reaction \(2\mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{SO_3(g)}\) at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\), what is the value of \(\Delta H - \Delta U\)?

  • (A) \(-RT\)
  • (B) \(+RT\)
  • (C) \(-2RT\)
  • (D) \(0\)
Answer: (A) \(-RT\)

Explanation: Using \(\Delta H - \Delta U = \Delta n_g RT\), we have \(\Delta n_g = n_{\text{g, products}} - n_{\text{g, reactants}} = 2 - (2 + 1) = -1\). Therefore, \(\Delta H - \Delta U = -RT\).

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Given the mean bond enthalpies: \(\mathrm{H-H} = 436\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm{Cl-Cl} = 242\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), and \(\mathrm{H-Cl} = 431\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), calculate \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) for the reaction \(\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{Cl_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{HCl(g)}\).

  • (A) \(+184\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(-184\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(-92\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(-247\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B) \(-184\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)

Explanation: For gas-phase reactions, \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = \sum \text{BE}(\text{reactants}) - \sum \text{BE}(\text{products}) = (436 + 242) - 2(431) = 678 - 862 = -184\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 5 Single-Correct MCQ

Given \(\mathrm{A} \rightarrow \mathrm{B},\ \Delta H = +40\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(\mathrm{C} \rightarrow \mathrm{B},\ \Delta H = -25\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), what is \(\Delta H\) for the process \(2\mathrm{A} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{C}\)?

  • (A) \(+15\,\mathrm{kJ}\)
  • (B) \(+30\,\mathrm{kJ}\)
  • (C) \(+65\,\mathrm{kJ}\)
  • (D) \(+130\,\mathrm{kJ}\)
Answer: (D) \(+130\,\mathrm{kJ}\)

Explanation: Reversing the second equation gives \(\mathrm{B} \rightarrow \mathrm{C},\ \Delta H = +25\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Adding it to \(\mathrm{A} \rightarrow \mathrm{B}\) yields \(\mathrm{A} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}\) with \(\Delta H = 40 + 25 = +65\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Multiplying by 2 gives \(\Delta H = 2 \times 65 = +130\,\mathrm{kJ}\).

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

In which of the following processes does the entropy of the system decrease (\(\Delta S < 0\))?

  • (A) Vaporization of liquid ethanol
  • (B) Sublimation of solid iodine
  • (C) Crystallization of sugar from an aqueous solution
  • (D) Thermal decomposition of solid \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\) into \(\mathrm{CaO(s)}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)}\)
Answer: (C) Crystallization of sugar from an aqueous solution

Explanation: Crystallization arranges dissolved solute molecules into a highly ordered crystal lattice, decreasing disorder (\(\Delta S < 0\)). Vaporization, sublimation, and gas-producing decomposition all increase disorder (\(\Delta S > 0\)).

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

A reaction has \(\Delta_r H^\ominus < 0\) and \(\Delta_r S^\ominus > 0\). Under constant temperature and pressure, this reaction will be spontaneous:

  • (A) Only at high temperatures
  • (B) Only at low temperatures
  • (C) At all temperatures
  • (D) At no temperature
Answer: (C) At all temperatures

Explanation: In \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = \Delta_r H^\ominus - T\Delta_r S^\ominus\), both \(\Delta_r H^\ominus < 0\) and \(-T\Delta_r S^\ominus < 0\) (since \(T > 0\) and \(\Delta_r S^\ominus > 0\)). Thus \(\Delta_r G^\ominus\) is always negative at every temperature.

At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the standard Gibbs energy change for a reaction is \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = -11.41\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). What is the equilibrium constant \(K\)? (Use \(2.303\,RT = 5.705\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)).

  • (A) \(2.0\)
  • (B) \(10\)
  • (C) \(100\)
  • (D) \(1000\)
Answer: (C) \(100\)

Explanation: From \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K\):

Practice Question 9 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): The standard enthalpy of formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)) of \(\mathrm{C(diamond)}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) is zero.
Reason (R): By convention, the standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its most stable reference state is taken as zero.

Practice Question 10 Statement I & II

Statement I: For a spontaneous process occurring in an isolated system, \(\Delta U = 0\) and \(\Delta S > 0\).
Statement II: At chemical equilibrium under constant temperature and pressure, \(\Delta_r G = 0\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.

Explanation: In an isolated system, no heat or work is exchanged, so \(\Delta U = 0\); by the Second Law, any spontaneous change increases entropy (\(\Delta S > 0\)). At equilibrium under constant \(T\) and \(p\), Gibbs energy reaches a minimum, so \(\Delta_r G = 0\).

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Work Sign Convention: Always use \(\Delta U = q + w\) with \(w = -p_{\text{ex}}\Delta V\) in Chemistry. Expansion gives \(w < 0\); compression gives \(w > 0\).
  • Counting Solids/Liquids in \(\Delta n_g\): Only gaseous moles enter \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\).
  • Unit Mismatch in \(R\): Use \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) when \(\Delta H, \Delta U, \Delta G\) are in \(\mathrm{J}\), or \(8.314 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) when working in \(\mathrm{kJ}\).
  • Formation vs. Reaction Enthalpy: \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\) strictly refers to forming 1 mole of compound from its elements in their most stable reference states.
  • Bond Enthalpy Formula Order: Use \(\sum \text{BE(reactants)} - \sum \text{BE(products)}\), which is opposite to the \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\) formula.

Concise Final Recap

  • First Law: \(\Delta U = q + w\); \(q_v = \Delta U\), \(q_p = \Delta H\), and \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\).
  • Reversible Isothermal Ideal Gas: \(\Delta U = 0\), \(w_{\text{rev}} = -2.303\,nRT\log_{10}(V_f/V_i)\), \(q = -w_{\text{rev}}\).
  • Second & Third Laws: \(\Delta S = q_{\text{rev}}/T\); spontaneous if \(\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} > 0\); \(S \to 0\) as \(T \to 0\,\mathrm{K}\) for pure crystals.
  • Gibbs Energy & Equilibrium: \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\) (\(< 0\) spontaneous, \(= 0\) equilibrium); \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K\).
