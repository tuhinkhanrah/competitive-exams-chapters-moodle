Chemical and Ionic Equilibrium

A complete, exam-focused tutorial covering dynamic equilibrium, equilibrium constants, Le Chatelier's principle, acid-base theories, pH calculations, buffers, and solubility product equilibria.

Learning Objectives & Exam Scope

Equilibrium is one of the highest-weightage units in NEET UG Chemistry because it bridges thermodynamics, solution chemistry, and qualitative analysis. After mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Explain the microscopic, dynamic nature of physical and chemical equilibria in closed systems.
  • Write equilibrium constant expressions (\(K_c\) and \(K_p\)) for homogeneous and heterogeneous systems, and convert between them using \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\).
  • Predict reaction direction using the reaction quotient (\(Q\)) and relate equilibrium constants to standard Gibbs energy (\(\Delta G^\circ\)).
  • Apply Le Chatelier's principle to changes in concentration, pressure, volume, inert gas addition, temperature, and catalysts.
  • Classify acids and bases under Arrhenius, Brönsted-Lowry, and Lewis frameworks, and identify conjugate acid-base pairs.
  • Calculate pH and pOH for strong and weak electrolytes, polyprotic acids, salt solutions, and buffer mixtures.
  • Determine molar solubility from \(K_{sp}\), predict precipitation using \(Q_{sp}\), and quantify common-ion and pH effects on sparingly soluble salts.

1. The Dynamic Nature of Physical and Chemical Equilibrium

Many physical changes and chemical reactions are reversible: reactants convert into products (forward process), and products simultaneously convert back into reactants (reverse process). When a reversible system is kept in a closed vessel at a constant temperature, it eventually reaches a state where macroscopic properties—such as pressure, color intensity, density, and molar concentrations—stop changing with time.

Core Intuition: Dynamic, Not Static

At equilibrium, molecular activity does not halt. Instead, the rate of the forward process equals the rate of the reverse process (\(r_f = r_r\)). Because species are formed and consumed at identical speeds, there is zero net change in composition.

Physical Equilibria

Phase transitions and dissolution processes exhibit characteristic equilibrium parameters at a fixed temperature:

  • Solid ⇌ Liquid: At \(1.013\,\mathrm{bar}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{atm}\)), ice and liquid water coexist in dynamic equilibrium exclusively at \(273\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)), the normal melting/freezing point.
  • Liquid ⇌ Vapour: In an evacuated, closed container, evaporation occurs at a constant rate while condensation rate rises as vapour accumulates. Equilibrium is reached when the vapour exerts a constant pressure—the equilibrium vapour pressure—which increases with temperature. Liquids with higher vapour pressure are more volatile and have lower normal boiling points. Equilibrium cannot be attained in an open vessel because vapour disperses continuously.
  • Solid ⇌ Vapour: Sublimable solids such as \(\mathrm{I}_2(\mathrm{s})\), camphor, and \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}(\mathrm{s})\) establish constant vapour pressure in sealed containers.
  • Dissolution of Solids in Liquids: In a saturated solution, the rate of dissolution equals the rate of crystallization, keeping solute concentration constant at a given temperature.
  • Dissolution of Gases in Liquids: Governed by Henry's Law, the mass (or concentration) of a dissolved gas at a fixed temperature is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid: \([\mathrm{Gas(aq)}] \propto p_{\mathrm{Gas}}\). Gas solubility decreases as temperature increases.

Chemical Equilibrium

For a general reversible reaction \(a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}\), starting with pure reactants causes \([\mathrm{A}]\) and \([\mathrm{B}]\) to fall while \([\mathrm{C}]\) and \([\mathrm{D}]\) rise. Consequently, \(r_f\) decreases and \(r_r\) increases until \(r_f = r_r\). Importantly, the exact same equilibrium state is reached from either direction (starting with reactants or starting with products) provided total stoichiometry and temperature are identical.

2. Law of Chemical Equilibrium and Equilibrium Constants

According to the Law of Mass Action (Guldberg and Waage), for a balanced reversible reaction at a constant temperature:

\[ a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D} \]

the equilibrium constant \(K_c\) expressed in molar concentrations (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) is:

\[ K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c [\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a [\mathrm{B}]^b} \]

where all bracketed terms represent equilibrium molarities. For gaseous reactions, partial pressures (in \(\mathrm{bar}\) or \(\mathrm{atm}\)) are often more convenient, defining \(K_p\):

\[ K_p = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{C}})^c (p_{\mathrm{D}})^d}{(p_{\mathrm{A}})^a (p_{\mathrm{B}})^b} \]

Relationship Between \(K_p\) and \(K_c\)

Assuming ideal gas behaviour, \(p_i = \frac{n_i}{V}RT = [\mathrm{Gas}_i]RT\). Substituting this into the \(K_p\) expression yields:

\[ K_p = K_c (RT)^{\Delta n_g} \]

Here, \(\Delta n_g = (\text{total moles of gaseous products}) - (\text{total moles of gaseous reactants})\) from the balanced equation. Remember to use \(R = 0.0831\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) when pressures are in \(\mathrm{bar}\), or \(R = 0.0821\,\mathrm{atm\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) when pressures are in \(\mathrm{atm}\).

  • If \(\Delta n_g = 0\) (e.g., \(\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{I}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{HI}(\mathrm{g})\)), then \(K_p = K_c\).
  • If \(\Delta n_g > 0\) (e.g., \(\mathrm{PCl}_5(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{PCl}_3(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{Cl}_2(\mathrm{g})\)), then \(K_p > K_c\) (for \(RT > 1\)).
  • If \(\Delta n_g < 0\) (e.g., \(\mathrm{N}_2(\mathrm{g}) + 3\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH}_3(\mathrm{g})\)), then \(K_p < K_c\).

Homogeneous vs. Heterogeneous Equilibria

In a homogeneous equilibrium, all reactants and products exist in the same phase (all gases or all aqueous ions). In a heterogeneous equilibrium, more than one phase is present. Because the molar concentration (density/molar mass) of a pure solid or a pure liquid remains constant regardless of the amount present, pure solids and pure liquids are omitted from \(K_c\) and \(K_p\) expressions (their activities are taken as unity).

For example, in the thermal decomposition \(\mathrm{CaCO}_3(\mathrm{s}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CaO}(\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{CO}_2(\mathrm{g})\):

\[ K_c = [\mathrm{CO}_2(\mathrm{g})] \quad \text{and} \quad K_p = p_{\mathrm{CO}_2} \]

Note that although pure solids do not appear in the \(K\) expression, small amounts of both \(\mathrm{CaCO}_3(\mathrm{s})\) and \(\mathrm{CaO}(\mathrm{s})\) must physically exist in the vessel for the equilibrium to be established.

Algebraic Rules for Manipulating \(K\)

Modification to Balanced Equation New Equilibrium Constant
Original reaction: \(a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}\) \(K\)
Reversing the reaction: \(c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D} \rightleftharpoons a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B}\) \(K' = \dfrac{1}{K}\)
Multiplying coefficients by \(n\): \(na\mathrm{A} + nb\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons nc\mathrm{C} + nd\mathrm{D}\) \(K'' = K^n\)
Dividing coefficients by \(n\) (multiplying by \(1/n\)) \(K''' = K^{1/n}\)
Adding two reactions with constants \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) \(K_{\mathrm{net}} = K_1 \times K_2\)

Key property: \(K\) depends only on temperature and the stoichiometry of the written equation; it is completely independent of initial concentrations, pressure, volume, or the presence of a catalyst.

3. Predicting Extent, Direction, and Thermodynamic Spontaneity

Extent of Reaction from the Magnitude of \(K\)

  • \(K > 10^3\): Products strongly predominate; the reaction proceeds nearly to completion.
  • \(K < 10^{-3}\): Reactants strongly predominate; the reaction proceeds to a negligible extent.
  • \(10^{-3} \le K \le 10^3\): Appreciable concentrations of both reactants and products coexist at equilibrium.

Predicting Reaction Direction via Reaction Quotient (\(Q\))

The reaction quotient \(Q_c\) (or \(Q_p\)) has the exact same algebraic form as \(K_c\) (or \(K_p\)), but uses concentrations (or partial pressures) measured at any arbitrary instant \(t\), not necessarily at equilibrium.

Thermodynamic Connection: Gibbs Energy (\(\Delta G\)) and \(K\)

The Gibbs energy change \(\Delta G\) at any composition is related to the standard Gibbs energy change \(\Delta G^\circ\) and the reaction quotient \(Q\) by:

\[ \Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln Q \]

At equilibrium, \(\Delta G = 0\) and \(Q = K\), which gives the fundamental thermodynamic relation:

\[ \Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K = -2.303\,RT \log_{10} K \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad K = e^{-\Delta G^\circ / RT} \]
  • If \(\Delta G^\circ < 0\), then \(K > 1\) (products are favoured at standard conditions).
  • If \(\Delta G^\circ > 0\), then \(K < 1\) (reactants are favoured at standard conditions).
  • If \(\Delta G^\circ = 0\), then \(K = 1\).

4. Le Chatelier's Principle and Shifting Equilibria

Le Chatelier's Principle states that if a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in concentration, pressure, volume, or temperature, the equilibrium shifts in the direction that tends to counteract or minimize the applied stress.

Perturbation Applied Direction of Equilibrium Shift Effect on \(K\)
Add reactant (or remove product) Forward (towards products), because \(Q < K\) No change
Add product (or remove reactant) Reverse (towards reactants), because \(Q > K\) No change
Increase pressure (by decreasing volume) Towards the side with fewer moles of gas No change
Decrease pressure (by increasing volume) Towards the side with more moles of gas No change
Add inert gas at constant volume No shift (partial pressures of reacting gases are unchanged) No change
Add inert gas at constant pressure Volume increases; shifts towards more moles of gas No change
Increase temperature (exothermic, \(\Delta H < 0\)) Reverse (endothermic direction) \(K\) decreases
Increase temperature (endothermic, \(\Delta H > 0\)) Forward (endothermic direction) \(K\) increases
Add a catalyst No shift; lowers \(E_a\) of forward and reverse paths equally, reaching equilibrium faster No change

Industrial application: In the Haber process (\(\mathrm{N}_2(\mathrm{g}) + 3\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH}_3(\mathrm{g}),\;\Delta H = -92.38\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), high pressure (~200 atm) favours fewer gas moles (\(\mathrm{NH}_3\)), while a moderate temperature (~500 °C) paired with an iron catalyst balances reaction rate against the exothermic equilibrium penalty.

5. Acids, Bases, Water Autoionization, and the pH Scale

Substances whose aqueous solutions conduct electricity are electrolytes. Strong electrolytes (\(\mathrm{HCl}\), \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)) ionize nearly 100% in water, whereas weak electrolytes (\(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH}\), \(\mathrm{NH}_3\)) ionize only partially, establishing an ionic equilibrium between unionized molecules and their ions.

Three Complementary Acid-Base Theories

  • Arrhenius Concept (aqueous only): Acids yield \(\mathrm{H}^+(\mathrm{aq})\) (hydrated as hydronium, \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+\)); bases yield \(\mathrm{OH}^-(\mathrm{aq})\). Limitation: cannot explain non-aqueous systems or bases without \(\mathrm{OH}^-\) (like \(\mathrm{NH}_3\)).
  • Brönsted-Lowry Concept (proton transfer): An acid is a proton (\(\mathrm{H}^+\)) donor, and a base is a proton acceptor. Losing a proton converts an acid into its conjugate base; gaining a proton converts a base into its conjugate acid. A strong acid always has a weak conjugate base, and vice versa. Amphiprotic species such as \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\), \(\mathrm{HCO}_3^-\), and \(\mathrm{HSO}_4^-\) can both donate and accept a proton.
  • Lewis Concept (electron-pair transfer): An acid is an electron-pair acceptor (e.g., electron-deficient \(\mathrm{BF}_3\), \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3\), \(\mathrm{H}^+\), metal cations like \(\mathrm{Co}^{3+}\)); a base is an electron-pair donor (e.g., \(\ddot{\mathrm{N}}\mathrm{H}_3\), \(\mathrm{H}_2\ddot{\mathrm{O}}:\), \(\mathrm{OH}^-\), \(\mathrm{F}^-\)).

Autoionization of Water and the pH Scale

Pure water undergoes slight self-ionization: \(2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{OH}^-(\mathrm{aq})\). Its equilibrium constant is the ionic product of water, \(K_w = [\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+][\mathrm{OH}^-]\). At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)), \(K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{M}^2\), giving \([\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+] = [\mathrm{OH}^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\).

\[ \mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+], \qquad \mathrm{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{OH}^-], \qquad \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = \mathrm{p}K_w = 14\;\text{(at }298\,\mathrm{K)} \]
NEET Trap: Neutrality vs. Temperature

Water autoionization is endothermic (\(\Delta H > 0\)). As temperature rises, \(K_w\) increases (for instance, at \(310\,\mathrm{K}\), \(K_w \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-14}\)). Consequently, the pH of pure neutral water drops below 7, yet the water remains strictly neutral because \([\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+] = [\mathrm{OH}^-]\) always defines neutrality.

6. Weak Acids, Weak Bases, Polyprotic Acids, and Common Ion Effect

For a weak monoprotic acid \(\mathrm{HA}\) of initial concentration \(c\) and degree of ionization \(\alpha\):

\[ \mathrm{HA}(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{A}^-(\mathrm{aq}) \]
\[ K_a = \frac{c\alpha^2}{1 - \alpha} \quad \xrightarrow{\text{if }\alpha \ll 1\;(\le 0.05)} \quad \alpha \approx \sqrt{\frac{K_a}{c}}, \qquad [\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+] \approx \sqrt{K_a \cdot c} \]

This is Ostwald's Dilution Law: the degree of ionization \(\alpha\) increases as the solution is diluted (\(c\) decreases). Analogously, for a weak base \(\mathrm{BOH}\), \(K_b = \frac{c\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}\) and \([\mathrm{OH}^-] \approx \sqrt{K_b \cdot c}\). Note that \(\mathrm{p}K_a = -\log_{10} K_a\) and \(\mathrm{p}K_b = -\log_{10} K_b\); a stronger acid has a larger \(K_a\) and a smaller \(\mathrm{p}K_a\).

Conjugate Pair Relation

For any conjugate acid-base pair (\(\mathrm{HA}/\mathrm{A}^-\) or \(\mathrm{NH}_4^+/\mathrm{NH}_3\)) in water at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\):

\[ K_a \times K_b = K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_a + \mathrm{p}K_b = \mathrm{p}K_w = 14 \]

Polyprotic Acids and Periodic Acid-Strength Trends

  • Polyprotic acids (e.g., \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\), \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\), \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{PO}_4\)) ionize stepwise: \(K_{a_1} \gg K_{a_2} \gg K_{a_3}\). Removing a positively charged \(\mathrm{H}^+\) becomes progressively harder as the negative charge on the anion increases. Thus, pH is governed almost entirely by the first dissociation step.
  • Same group (down the column): \(\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{A}\) bond strength dominates. As atom \(\mathrm{A}\) gets larger, the \(\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{A}\) bond weakens and acidity rises: \(\mathrm{HF} \ll \mathrm{HCl} < \mathrm{HBr} < \mathrm{HI}\), and \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} < \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{S}\).
  • Same period (across the row): \(\mathrm{H}-\mathrm{A}\) bond polarity (electronegativity of \(\mathrm{A}\)) dominates: \(\mathrm{CH}_4 < \mathrm{NH}_3 < \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} < \mathrm{HF}\).

Common Ion Effect

Adding a strong electrolyte that shares an ion with a weak electrolyte suppresses the dissociation of the weak electrolyte (an application of Le Chatelier's principle). For example, adding sodium acetate (\(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COONa}\)) to acetic acid (\(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH}\)) supplies excess \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COO}^-\), shifting \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H}^+ + \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COO}^-\) to the left, lowering \([\mathrm{H}^+]\) and raising pH.

7. Salt Hydrolysis and Buffer Solutions

When an acid and base neutralize to form a salt, the resulting ions may react with water (hydrolysis) depending on the strength of the parent acid and base:

Salt Type Example Hydrolysis & Nature pH Formula (at 298 K)
Strong Acid + Strong Base \(\mathrm{NaCl},\;\mathrm{KNO}_3\) No hydrolysis; neutral \(\mathrm{pH} = 7\)
Weak Acid + Strong Base \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COONa},\;\mathrm{KCN}\) Anionic hydrolysis; basic (\(\mathrm{pH} > 7\)) \(\mathrm{pH} = 7 + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{p}K_a + \frac{1}{2}\log_{10} c\)
Strong Acid + Weak Base \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl},\;\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{NO}_3\) Cationic hydrolysis; acidic (\(\mathrm{pH} < 7\)) \(\mathrm{pH} = 7 - \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{p}K_b - \frac{1}{2}\log_{10} c\)
Weak Acid + Weak Base \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COONH}_4\) Both ions hydrolyse; \(\mathrm{pH}\) independent of \(c\) \(\mathrm{pH} = 7 + \frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{p}K_a - \mathrm{p}K_b)\)

Buffer Solutions and the Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

A buffer solution resists significant changes in pH upon dilution or upon addition of small amounts of strong acid or strong base.

  • Acidic Buffer: Weak acid + its salt with a strong base (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH} + \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COONa}\)).
  • Basic Buffer: Weak base + its salt with a strong acid (e.g., \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{OH} + \mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\)).
\[ \text{Acidic Buffer:}\quad \mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{Salt}]}{[\mathrm{Acid}]} \qquad\qquad \text{Basic Buffer:}\quad \mathrm{pOH} = \mathrm{p}K_b + \log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{Salt}]}{[\mathrm{Base}]} \]

Key properties: When \([\mathrm{Salt}] = [\mathrm{Acid}]\), \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a\) (maximum buffer capacity). Diluting a buffer leaves the ratio \([\mathrm{Salt}]/[\mathrm{Acid}]\) unchanged, so its pH remains constant upon moderate dilution.

8. Solubility Equilibria of Sparingly Soluble Salts

For a sparingly soluble ionic salt (solubility \(< 0.01\,\mathrm{M}\)) of general formula \(\mathrm{M}_x\mathrm{X}_y(\mathrm{s})\) in contact with its saturated aqueous solution:

\[ \mathrm{M}_x\mathrm{X}_y(\mathrm{s}) \rightleftharpoons x\mathrm{M}^{p+}(\mathrm{aq}) + y\mathrm{X}^{q-}(\mathrm{aq}) \]

If \(S\) is the molar solubility (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) in pure water, then \([\mathrm{M}^{p+}] = xS\) and \([\mathrm{X}^{q-}] = yS\), giving the solubility product constant (\(K_{sp}\)):

\[ K_{sp} = [\mathrm{M}^{p+}]^x[\mathrm{X}^{q-}]^y = (xS)^x(yS)^y = x^x y^y S^{(x+y)} \]
  • For \(1:1\) salts (\(\mathrm{AgCl},\;\mathrm{BaSO}_4\)): \(K_{sp} = S^2\)
  • For \(1:2\) or \(2:1\) salts (\(\mathrm{PbI}_2,\;\mathrm{Ag}_2\mathrm{CrO}_4\)): \(K_{sp} = 4S^3\)
  • For \(1:3\) or \(3:1\) salts (\(\mathrm{Fe(OH)}_3,\;\mathrm{Al(OH)}_3\)): \(K_{sp} = 27S^4\)
  • For \(2:3\) salts (\(\mathrm{A}_2\mathrm{X}_3\)): \(K_{sp} = 108S^5\)

Predicting Precipitation (\(Q_{sp}\) vs. \(K_{sp}\))

Let \(Q_{sp}\) be the ionic product calculated with instantaneous ion concentrations:

  • \(Q_{sp} < K_{sp}\): Unsaturated solution; no precipitation occurs, and more salt can dissolve.
  • \(Q_{sp} = K_{sp}\): Saturated solution at equilibrium.
  • \(Q_{sp} > K_{sp}\): Supersaturated solution; precipitation occurs until \(Q_{sp} = K_{sp}\).

Common Ion & pH Effects: Adding a common ion reduces the molar solubility \(S\) of a sparingly soluble salt (while \(K_{sp}\) remains constant at constant temperature). Conversely, salts whose anions are conjugate bases of weak acids (e.g., \(\mathrm{CO}_3^{2-}\), \(\mathrm{S}^{2-}\), \(\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{O}_4^{2-}\), \(\mathrm{PO}_4^{3-}\)) become more soluble at lower pH because \(\mathrm{H}^+\) protonates the anion, shifting the dissolution equilibrium forward.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Converting Between \(K_c\) and \(K_p\) in Gas-Phase Synthesis

Problem: Consider the reversible oxidation of sulfur dioxide: \(2\mathrm{SO}_2(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{O}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{SO}_3(\mathrm{g})\). At \(1000\,\mathrm{K}\), assume the concentration equilibrium constant is \(K_c = 2.5 \times 10^2\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\). Calculate \(K_p\) at \(1000\,\mathrm{K}\) (use \(R = 0.0831\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

First, find the change in gaseous moles: \(\Delta n_g = n_{\mathrm{products}} - n_{\mathrm{reactants}} = 2 - (2 + 1) = -1\).

Apply \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\):

\[ K_p = 250 \times (0.0831 \times 1000)^{-1} = \frac{250}{83.1} \approx 3.01\,\mathrm{bar^{-1}} \]
Example 2: Predicting Direction with \(Q_c\) and Finding Equilibrium Concentrations

Problem: For the water-gas shift reaction \(\mathrm{CO}(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CO}_2(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g})\) at \(900\,\mathrm{K}\), assume \(K_c = 4.0\). A \(2.0\,\mathrm{L}\) sealed vessel is initially charged with \(0.40\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{CO}\), \(0.40\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\), \(0.20\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{CO}_2\), and \(0.20\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{H}_2\). Predict the direction of net reaction and determine the equilibrium molarities of all four gases.

Solution:

Initial molarities (dividing moles by \(2.0\,\mathrm{L}\)): \([\mathrm{CO}]_0 = [\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}]_0 = 0.20\,\mathrm{M}\) and \([\mathrm{CO}_2]_0 = [\mathrm{H}_2]_0 = 0.10\,\mathrm{M}\).

Calculate \(Q_c\): \(Q_c = \frac{0.10 \times 0.10}{0.20 \times 0.20} = 0.25\). Since \(Q_c < K_c\;(0.25 < 4.0)\), the reaction proceeds in the forward direction.

Let \(x\,\mathrm{M}\) be the concentration of \(\mathrm{CO}\) and \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) consumed to reach equilibrium:

\[ \frac{(0.10 + x)(0.10 + x)}{(0.20 - x)(0.20 - x)} = 4.0 \implies \frac{0.10 + x}{0.20 - x} = 2.0 \] \[ 0.10 + x = 0.40 - 2x \implies 3x = 0.30 \implies x = 0.10\,\mathrm{M} \]

Therefore, at equilibrium: \([\mathrm{CO}_2] = [\mathrm{H}_2] = 0.20\,\mathrm{M}\) and \([\mathrm{CO}] = [\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}] = 0.10\,\mathrm{M}\).

Example 3: Relating Standard Gibbs Energy Change to Equilibrium Constant

Problem: At \(300\,\mathrm{K}\), assume a metabolic isomerization reaction has an equilibrium constant \(K = 100\). Calculate \(\Delta G^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) (take \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

\[ \Delta G^\circ = -2.303\,RT \log_{10} K = -2.303 \times 8.314 \times 300 \times \log_{10}(100) \] \[ \Delta G^\circ = -5744.1 \times 2 = -11488\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx -11.49\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]
Example 4: Calculating pH of an Extremely Dilute Strong Acid

Problem: Calculate the pH of a \(1.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous \(\mathrm{HCl}\) solution at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\)).

Solution:

At \(10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\), the \(\mathrm{H}^+\) contribution from water autoionization cannot be neglected. Let \(x = [\mathrm{OH}^-]\) produced by water. By charge balance, \([\mathrm{H}^+] = [\mathrm{Cl}^-] + [\mathrm{OH}^-] = 10^{-7} + x\).

\[ (10^{-7} + x)x = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \implies x^2 + 10^{-7}x - 10^{-14} = 0 \] \[ x = \frac{-10^{-7} + \sqrt{10^{-14} + 4 \times 10^{-14}}}{2} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \times 10^{-7} \approx 0.618 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M} \]

Total \([\mathrm{H}^+] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7} + 0.618 \times 10^{-7} = 1.618 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\).

\[ \mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}(1.618 \times 10^{-7}) = 7 - \log_{10}(1.618) \approx 7 - 0.21 = 6.79 \]
Example 5: Evaluating Buffer pH After Addition of Strong Base

Problem: Assume \(500\,\mathrm{mL}\) of an acidic buffer contains \(0.20\,\mathrm{M}\) formic acid (\(\mathrm{HCOOH}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_a = 3.75\)) and \(0.20\,\mathrm{M}\) sodium formate (\(\mathrm{HCOONa}\)). Calculate the pH before and after adding \(0.020\,\mathrm{mol}\) of solid \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) (assume negligible volume change).

Solution:

Initial pH: \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}\frac{0.20}{0.20} = 3.75 + 0 = 3.75\).

After adding \(\mathrm{NaOH}\): Initial moles in \(0.500\,\mathrm{L}\) are \(n_{\mathrm{HCOOH}} = 0.20 \times 0.500 = 0.10\,\mathrm{mol}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{HCOO}^-} = 0.10\,\mathrm{mol}\). The added \(0.020\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{OH}^-\) converts \(0.020\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{HCOOH}\) into \(\mathrm{HCOO}^-\):

\[ n_{\mathrm{HCOOH}} = 0.10 - 0.020 = 0.080\,\mathrm{mol}, \qquad n_{\mathrm{HCOO}^-} = 0.10 + 0.020 = 0.120\,\mathrm{mol} \] \[ \mathrm{pH} = 3.75 + \log_{10}\left(\frac{0.120}{0.080}\right) = 3.75 + \log_{10}(1.5) \approx 3.75 + 0.18 = 3.93 \]
Example 6: Common Ion Effect on Solubility of \(\mathrm{PbI}_2\)

Problem: Assume the solubility product of \(\mathrm{PbI}_2\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(K_{sp} = 7.2 \times 10^{-9}\). Compare its molar solubility in pure water with its molar solubility in a \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{KI}\) solution.

Solution:

In pure water: \(\mathrm{PbI}_2(\mathrm{s}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Pb}^{2+}(\mathrm{aq}) + 2\mathrm{I}^-(\mathrm{aq})\), so \(K_{sp} = S(2S)^2 = 4S^3\).

\[ 4S^3 = 7.2 \times 10^{-9} \implies S^3 = 1.8 \times 10^{-9} \implies S \approx 1.22 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{M} \]

In \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{KI}\): Let the new solubility be \(S'\). Then \([\mathrm{Pb}^{2+}] = S'\) and \([\mathrm{I}^-] = 0.10 + 2S' \approx 0.10\,\mathrm{M}\).

\[ S'(0.10)^2 = 7.2 \times 10^{-9} \implies S' = \frac{7.2 \times 10^{-9}}{10^{-2}} = 7.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M} \]

The common ion \(\mathrm{I}^-\) suppresses the solubility of \(\mathrm{PbI}_2\) by more than three orders of magnitude.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

For which of the following equilibria is \(K_p\) strictly greater than \(K_c\) at \(500\,\mathrm{K}\) (assuming \(R = 0.0831\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\))?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{N}_2(\mathrm{g}) + 3\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH}_3(\mathrm{g})\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{Cl}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{HCl}(\mathrm{g})\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{C}(\mathrm{s}) + \mathrm{CO}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{CO}(\mathrm{g})\)
  • (D) \(2\mathrm{NO}(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{O}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NO}_2(\mathrm{g})\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Using \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\) and \(RT = 0.0831 \times 500 = 41.55 > 1\), we need \(\Delta n_g > 0\). For (A), \(\Delta n_g = 2 - 4 = -2\). For (B), \(\Delta n_g = 2 - 2 = 0\). For (C), solid carbon is excluded from gaseous moles, so \(\Delta n_g = 2 - 1 = +1 > 0\). For (D), \(\Delta n_g = 2 - 3 = -1\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

At a certain temperature, the equilibrium constant for \(\mathrm{N}_2(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{O}_2(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NO}(\mathrm{g})\) is \(K_1\). What is the equilibrium constant for the reaction \(\mathrm{NO}(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{N}_2(\mathrm{g}) + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{O}_2(\mathrm{g})\) at the same temperature?

  • (A) \(\dfrac{1}{K_1}\)
  • (B) \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K_1}}\)
  • (C) \(\sqrt{K_1}\)
  • (D) \(\dfrac{K_1}{2}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: The target equation is obtained by reversing the original equation (giving \(1/K_1\)) and multiplying all coefficients by \(1/2\) (raising to the power \(1/2\)). Thus, \(K_{\mathrm{new}} = (1/K_1)^{1/2} = 1/\sqrt{K_1}\).

Practice Question 3 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Given two statements regarding the equilibrium \(\mathrm{PCl}_5(\mathrm{g}) \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{PCl}_3(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{Cl}_2(\mathrm{g})\) in a rigid, constant-volume vessel:

Statement I: Addition of argon gas at constant volume and constant temperature shifts the equilibrium towards the forward direction.
Statement II: Addition of a catalyst increases the equilibrium constant \(K_p\) of the reaction.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (B)

Solution: Adding an inert gas (argon) at constant volume does not change the molar concentrations or partial pressures of \(\mathrm{PCl}_5\), \(\mathrm{PCl}_3\), or \(\mathrm{Cl}_2\), so Statement I is incorrect. A catalyst lowers activation energy equally for both forward and reverse reactions and does not alter \(K_p\), so Statement II is also incorrect.

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following pairs correctly represents the conjugate acid and conjugate base of \(\mathrm{HSO}_4^-\), respectively?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{SO}_4^{2-}\) and \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\) and \(\mathrm{SO}_4^{2-}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+\) and \(\mathrm{OH}^-\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\) and \(\mathrm{HSO}_3^-\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Adding one \(\mathrm{H}^+\) to \(\mathrm{HSO}_4^-\) gives its conjugate acid, \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\). Removing one \(\mathrm{H}^+\) from \(\mathrm{HSO}_4^-\) gives its conjugate base, \(\mathrm{SO}_4^{2-}\).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Identify the set in which all species can act as Lewis acids:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{BF}_3,\;\mathrm{NH}_3,\;\mathrm{H}^+\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3,\;\mathrm{F}^-,\;\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{BCl}_3,\;\mathrm{AlCl}_3,\;\mathrm{Co}^{3+}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{OH}^-,\;\mathrm{CN}^-,\;\mathrm{NH}_3\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Lewis acids are electron-pair acceptors. \(\mathrm{BCl}_3\) and \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3\) have incomplete octets (6 valence electrons on the central atom), and \(\mathrm{Co}^{3+}\) is a positively charged metal ion with vacant orbitals. All species in (D) and \(\mathrm{NH}_3\), \(\mathrm{F}^-\), \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) are electron-pair donors (Lewis bases).

Practice Question 6 (Numerical MCQ)

The ionization constant \(K_a\) of a weak monoprotic acid \(\mathrm{HA}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(2.5 \times 10^{-5}\). What is the pH of a \(0.04\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{HA}\)?

  • (A) \(2.0\)
  • (B) \(3.0\)
  • (C) \(4.0\)
  • (D) \(5.0\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Checking \(\alpha = \sqrt{K_a/c} = \sqrt{2.5 \times 10^{-5} / 0.04} = \sqrt{6.25 \times 10^{-4}} = 0.025 \ll 1\). Thus, \([\mathrm{H}^+] \approx \sqrt{K_a \cdot c} = \sqrt{2.5 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.04} = \sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{M}\). Hence, \(\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}(10^{-3}) = 3.0\).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous salt solutions will have a pH less than 7 at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{KBr}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COONa}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{NO}_3\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{NaNO}_2\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{NO}_3\) is formed from a weak base (\(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{OH}\)) and a strong acid (\(\mathrm{HNO}_3\)). Its \(\mathrm{NH}_4^+\) ions undergo cationic hydrolysis to produce \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+\), making the solution acidic (\(\mathrm{pH} < 7\)). \(\mathrm{KBr}\) is neutral (\(\mathrm{pH} = 7\)), while \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COONa}\) and \(\mathrm{NaNO}_2\) are basic (\(\mathrm{pH} > 7\)).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following mixtures forms an acidic buffer solution?

  • (A) \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{HCl} + 50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{NaCl}\)
  • (B) \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.2\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH} + 50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{NaOH}\)
  • (C) \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH} + 50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{NaOH}\)
  • (D) \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.2\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{OH} + 50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{HCl}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: In (B), \(10\,\mathrm{mmol}\) of \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH}\) reacts with \(5\,\mathrm{mmol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), leaving \(5\,\mathrm{mmol}\) of unreacted weak acid (\(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH}\)) and \(5\,\mathrm{mmol}\) of its conjugate base salt (\(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COONa}\)), forming an acidic buffer. In (C), neutralization is complete (salt only). In (D), excess weak base + salt forms a basic buffer.

Practice Question 9 (Assertion-Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): At \(310\,\mathrm{K}\), the pH of pure water is less than \(7\), yet pure water is neutral.
Reason (R): The autoionization of water is endothermic, so \(K_w\) increases with temperature, keeping \([\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+] = [\mathrm{OH}^-]\).

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Because water autoionization absorbs heat, raising temperature from \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(310\,\mathrm{K}\) increases \(K_w\) above \(10^{-14}\), so \([\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+] = [\mathrm{OH}^-] = \sqrt{K_w} > 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\) and \(\mathrm{pH} < 7\). Neutrality is defined by \([\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+] = [\mathrm{OH}^-]\), not by \(\mathrm{pH} = 7\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), assume the solubility product of \(\mathrm{Ag}_2\mathrm{CrO}_4\) is \(1.08 \times 10^{-12}\) and that of \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) is \(1.6 \times 10^{-10}\). Which statement is correct regarding their molar solubilities in pure water?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) is more soluble than \(\mathrm{Ag}_2\mathrm{CrO}_4\) because its \(K_{sp}\) is larger.
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Ag}_2\mathrm{CrO}_4\) is more soluble than \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) even though its \(K_{sp}\) is smaller.
  • (C) Both salts have identical molar solubilities.
  • (D) Molar solubility cannot be compared without knowing the volume of water.
Answer: (B)

Solution: Never compare \(K_{sp}\) values directly when salt stoichiometries differ. For \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) (\(1:1\)), \(S_{\mathrm{AgCl}} = \sqrt{1.6 \times 10^{-10}} \approx 1.26 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{M}\). For \(\mathrm{Ag}_2\mathrm{CrO}_4\) (\(2:1\)), \(4S^3 = 1.08 \times 10^{-12} \implies S^3 = 2.7 \times 10^{-13} = 270 \times 10^{-15} \implies S \approx 6.5 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{M}\). Thus, \(\mathrm{Ag}_2\mathrm{CrO}_4\) has higher molar solubility.

Common Pitfalls & NEET Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Mistakes to Avoid
  • Comparing \(K_{sp}\) directly across different salt types: Always convert \(K_{sp}\) to molar solubility \(S\) (\(S = \sqrt{K_{sp}}\) for \(1:1\), \((K_{sp}/4)^{1/3}\) for \(1:2\), \((K_{sp}/27)^{1/4}\) for \(1:3\)) before comparing solubilities.
  • Including solids/liquids in \(\Delta n_g\) or \(K\): Only gaseous species contribute to \(\Delta n_g\) in \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\), and pure solids/liquids never appear in \(K_c\) or \(K_p\).
  • Ignoring water's \(\mathrm{H}^+\) in very dilute acids/bases: For \(10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\) or \(10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\), the pH is slightly below 7 (never 7 or 8).
  • Confusing inert gas addition at constant \(V\) vs. constant \(P\): Constant volume gives zero shift; constant pressure increases volume and shifts towards more gas moles.
  • Assuming \(\mathrm{pH} = 7\) is always neutral: \(\mathrm{pH} = 7\) is neutral only at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). At higher temperatures, neutral pH is \(< 7\).

Concise Final Recap

  • Dynamic Equilibrium: \(r_f = r_r\) in a closed system; \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\); \(K\) depends only on temperature.
  • Direction & Spontaneity: \(Q < K\) shifts forward; \(Q > K\) shifts reverse; \(\Delta G^\circ = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K\).
  • Acids & Bases: Brönsted-Lowry conjugate pairs differ by one \(\mathrm{H}^+\) with \(K_a \times K_b = K_w\).
  • pH & Buffers: Weak acid \([\mathrm{H}^+] \approx \sqrt{K_a c}\); acidic buffer \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}([\mathrm{Salt}]/[\mathrm{Acid}])\).
  • Solubility: \(K_{sp} = x^x y^y S^{x+y}\); precipitation occurs when \(Q_{sp} > K_{sp}\); common ions suppress solubility.
