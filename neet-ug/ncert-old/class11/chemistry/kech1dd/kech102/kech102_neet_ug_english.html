Structure of Atom

From subatomic particles and classical models to Bohr's quantised orbits and quantum mechanical orbitals

Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

Chemical reactivity, bonding, and periodic trends all originate from how electrons are arranged around an atomic nucleus. This tutorial builds the complete conceptual and mathematical framework required for NEET UG, moving from experimental discoveries of subatomic particles to the modern quantum mechanical model of the atom.

What You Will Master

  • Thomson's model, Rutherford's nuclear model from alpha-particle scattering, and why classical mechanics failed to explain atomic stability.
  • Electromagnetic wave relations, Planck's quantum theory, the photoelectric effect, and hydrogen line spectra.
  • Bohr's quantised model for hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions (\(\mathrm{He}^+\), \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\)), including orbit radii, energies, and spectral transitions.
  • de Broglie matter waves, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, Schrödinger's wave equation, quantum numbers, orbital shapes, nodal counts, and electronic configuration rules.

Subatomic Particles and Nuclear Composition

Early nineteenth-century atomic theory treated the atom as an indivisible unit of matter. However, gas-discharge experiments at low pressure and high voltage revealed that atoms contain smaller, electrically charged constituents.

Discovery and Properties of Fundamental Particles

  • Electron (\(e^-\)): Discovered through cathode-ray discharge tubes. Cathode rays travel in straight lines from the negative cathode toward the positive anode, produce fluorescence on a zinc sulphide screen, and deflect in electric and magnetic fields like negatively charged particles. Their properties are independent of the gas or electrode material, proving electrons are universal constituents of matter.
  • Charge-to-mass ratio and charge of the electron: J.J. Thomson balanced perpendicular electric and magnetic fields to measure \(e/m_e = 1.758820 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\). R.A. Millikan's oil-drop experiment showed that electric charge is quantised in integer multiples of \(e = 1.602176 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), yielding an electron mass of \(m_e = 9.1094 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\).
  • Proton (\(p^+\)): Identified via positively charged canal rays in modified discharge tubes. Unlike cathode rays, canal-ray \(e/m\) depends on the gas inside the tube because they are gaseous cations. The lightest positive ion, obtained from hydrogen, is the proton.
  • Neutron (\(n^0\)): Discovered by James Chadwick (1932) by bombarding a thin beryllium sheet with alpha (\(\alpha\)) particles, emitting electrically neutral particles slightly heavier than protons.

Atomic Number, Mass Number, Isotopes, and Isobars

An atom of element \(\mathrm{X}\) is represented as \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\), where:

  • Atomic number (\(Z\)): Number of protons in the nucleus. In a neutral atom, number of electrons \(= Z\).
  • Mass number (\(A\)): Total number of nucleons (protons \(+\) neutrons), so \(A = Z + n\), and the number of neutrons is \(n = A - Z\) (valid for both neutral atoms and ions).
  • Isotopes: Atoms of the same element (identical \(Z\)) with different mass numbers (\(A\)) due to different neutron counts—for example, \(^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) (protium), \(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{D}\) (deuterium), and \(^{3}_{1}\mathrm{T}\) (tritium), or \(^{35}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\) and \(^{37}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\). Chemical behaviour depends on electron count, so isotopes show identical chemical properties.
  • Isobars: Atoms of different elements (different \(Z\)) having the same mass number (\(A\))—for example, \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N}\).

Early Atomic Models: Thomson and Rutherford

Once subatomic particles were established, scientists needed a structural model that explained both overall electrical neutrality and experimental scattering data.

Thomson's Model and Radioactivity Context

J.J. Thomson (1898) pictured the atom as a uniform sphere of positive charge (radius \(\approx 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)) with electrons embedded throughout it like seeds in a watermelon. While it accounted for electrical neutrality, it assumed atomic mass and positive charge were spread evenly across the entire atomic volume.

Meanwhile, studies of X-rays (short-wavelength electromagnetic waves, \(\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{nm}\), unaffected by electric or magnetic fields) and radioactivity (\(\alpha\)-particles as \(\mathrm{He}^{2+}\) nuclei, \(\beta\)-rays as fast electrons, and \(\gamma\)-rays as neutral high-energy radiation with relative penetrating powers \(\alpha : \beta : \gamma \approx 1 : 100 : 1000\)) provided high-energy \(\alpha\)-particle beams to probe atomic interiors.

Rutherford's Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

Ernest Rutherford, Hans Geiger, and Ernest Marsden directed high-energy \(\alpha\)-particles at a thin gold foil (\(\sim 100\,\mathrm{nm}\) thick) surrounded by a circular fluorescent zinc sulphide screen.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Rutherford's α-particle scattering showing empty space and a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.
  • Key observations & deductions: Most \(\alpha\)-particles passed straight through (most of the atom is empty space); a small fraction deflected at small angles; about \(1\) in \(20,000\) bounced back near \(180^\circ\) (positive charge and almost all mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus of radius \(\sim 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\), whereas atomic radius is \(\sim 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)).
  • Drawbacks of Rutherford's model: According to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle (an electron revolving in a circular orbit) must continuously emit electromagnetic radiation, lose energy, and spiral into the nucleus within \(\sim 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}\). Thus, Rutherford's model could not explain atomic stability or the discrete distribution and energies of electrons.

Electromagnetic Radiation, Planck's Quanta, and the Photoelectric Effect

Understanding how light interacts with matter laid the foundation for Bohr's model and quantum theory.

Wave Properties of Electromagnetic Radiation

Electromagnetic waves consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation. Unlike sound waves, they require no medium and travel in vacuum at speed \(c = 2.997925 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

\[ c = \nu \lambda \qquad \text{and} \qquad \bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{\nu}{c} \]

Here \(\nu\) is frequency (\(\mathrm{Hz}\) or \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)), \(\lambda\) is wavelength (\(\mathrm{m}\)), and \(\bar{\nu}\) is wavenumber (\(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), commonly expressed in \(\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\)). The visible spectrum spans roughly \(400\,\mathrm{nm}\) (violet, \(7.5 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\)) to \(750\,\mathrm{nm}\) (red, \(4.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\)).

Planck's Quantum Theory

Classical wave theory explained diffraction and interference, but failed to explain black-body radiation (the shift of peak intensity toward shorter wavelengths as temperature rises) and the photoelectric effect. In 1900, Max Planck proposed that atoms absorb or emit electromagnetic energy only in discrete packets called quanta:

\[ E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s} \]

Photoelectric Effect

When light of frequency \(\nu\) strikes a clean alkali metal surface (such as \(\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{Rb}\), or \(\mathrm{Cs}\)) in vacuum, electrons are ejected instantaneously if \(\nu\) exceeds a metal-specific threshold frequency \(\nu_0\). Increasing light intensity increases the number of ejected photoelectrons (photocurrent), whereas increasing frequency increases their maximum kinetic energy.

Tutorial Diagram 2: Energy conservation in the photoelectric effect (Einstein's photon model).
\[ h\nu = W_0 + \frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\max}^2 = h\nu_0 + KE_{\max} \]

Here \(W_0 = h\nu_0 = hc/\lambda_0\) is the work function (minimum energy needed to eject an electron). Electromagnetic radiation therefore exhibits dual behaviour: wave-like during propagation (interference, diffraction) and particle-like during interaction with matter (black-body radiation, photoelectric effect).

Atomic Spectra and Bohr's Model of the Hydrogen Atom

While white light produces a continuous rainbow spectrum, excited gaseous atoms emit light only at specific discrete wavelengths, producing a line emission spectrum unique to each element (a spectroscopic fingerprint). An absorption spectrum is its photographic negative, showing dark lines at the exact wavelengths absorbed.

Bohr's Postulates and Equations for One-Electron Species

In 1913, Niels Bohr explained hydrogen's stability and line spectrum using four key ideas:

  1. Electrons revolve around the nucleus only in certain allowed circular paths called stationary states (orbits) without radiating energy.
  2. Energy is absorbed or emitted only when an electron jumps between two stationary states: \(\Delta E = E_f - E_i = h\nu\) (Bohr's frequency rule).
  3. The angular momentum of an electron is quantised: \(m_e v r = n \frac{h}{2\pi}\), where \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) is the principal quantum number.
\[ r_n = \frac{52.9\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{pm} = \frac{0.529\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{Å} \] \[ E_n = -R_H \left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right) = -2.18 \times 10^{-18} \left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right)\,\mathrm{J\,atom^{-1}} = -13.6 \left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right)\,\mathrm{eV\,atom^{-1}} \] \[ \Delta E = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\,Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_i^2} - \frac{1}{n_f^2}\right)\,\mathrm{J}, \qquad \bar{\nu} = 1.09677 \times 10^7\,Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \]

Why is \(E_n\) negative? A free electron at rest infinitely far from the nucleus (\(n = \infty\)) is assigned \(E_\infty = 0\). As the electron is bound closer to the nucleus, energy is released and \(E_n\) becomes more negative, reaching its lowest (most stable) value at \(n = 1\) (ground state). For one-electron ions (\(\mathrm{He}^+\) with \(Z=2\), \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\) with \(Z=3\)), orbits contract and bind more tightly as \(Z\) increases.

Limitations of Bohr's Model

Bohr's model fails to explain: (i) multi-electron atoms (even \(\mathrm{He}\)), (ii) closely spaced fine-structure doublets in hydrogen spectra, (iii) splitting of spectral lines in magnetic fields (Zeeman effect) and electric fields (Stark effect), and (iv) chemical bond formation.

Wave–Particle Duality and Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

Two foundational principles in the 1920s overturned classical orbits and paved the way for quantum mechanics.

de Broglie Matter Waves

Louis de Broglie (1924) proposed that every moving particle has an associated wavelength \(\lambda\) inversely proportional to its momentum \(p = mv\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m \cdot KE}} \]

For macroscopic objects, \(m\) is large so \(\lambda\) is unimaginably small and undetectable. For electrons, \(\lambda\) is comparable to atomic dimensions and is confirmed by electron diffraction (used in electron microscopes).

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

Werner Heisenberg (1927) proved that it is impossible to simultaneously determine both the exact position and exact momentum (or velocity) of a microscopic particle along the same axis:

\[ \Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \Delta x \cdot \Delta v_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi m} \]

Consequence: A well-defined circular trajectory requires knowing position and velocity simultaneously. Because this is impossible for electrons, fixed Bohr orbits do not exist in reality and must be replaced by three-dimensional probability distributions.

The Quantum Mechanical Model, Quantum Numbers, and Orbitals

Erwin Schrödinger (1926) formulated the wave equation \(\hat{H}\psi = E\psi\), where \(\hat{H}\) is the Hamiltonian operator, \(E\) is the quantised total energy, and \(\psi\) is the wave function (an atomic orbital). While \(\psi\) itself has no direct physical meaning, \(|\psi|^2\) gives the probability density—the probability per unit volume of finding the electron at a given point.

The Four Quantum Numbers

Quantum Number Symbol Allowed Values Physical Significance
Principal \(n\) \(1, 2, 3, \dots\) Identifies shell (\(\mathrm{K, L, M, N}\dots\)); determines orbital size and primary energy. Total orbitals in shell \(= n^2\); max electrons \(= 2n^2\).
Azimuthal (Angular Momentum) \(l\) \(0, 1, 2, \dots, (n-1)\) Identifies subshell (\(l=0\to s\), \(1\to p\), \(2\to d\), \(3\to f\)); determines 3D orbital shape and orbital angular momentum \(\sqrt{l(l+1)}\frac{h}{2\pi}\).
Magnetic Orbital \(m_l\) \(-l, \dots, 0, \dots, +l\) Specifies spatial orientation; gives \((2l+1)\) orbitals per subshell (\(s:1\), \(p:3\), \(d:5\), \(f:7\)).
Electron Spin \(m_s\) \(+\frac{1}{2}\) or \(-\frac{1}{2}\) Specifies intrinsic spin orientation (\(\uparrow\) or \(\downarrow\)).

Orbital Shapes and Nodes

A boundary surface diagram encloses the region where the probability of finding the electron is high (typically \(\sim 90\%\); a \(100\%\) boundary cannot be drawn because \(|\psi|^2\) remains nonzero at any finite distance). Surfaces where \(\psi\) and \(|\psi|^2\) drop to zero are called nodes:

  • Radial (spherical) nodes: \(n - l - 1\).
  • Angular (planar/conical) nodes: \(l\).
  • Total nodes: \((n - l - 1) + l = n - 1\).
Tutorial Diagram 4: 2D cross-sections of s, p, and d orbitals with their nodal planes (dashed lines).

Note that all five \(3d\) orbitals (\(d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{xz}, d_{x^2-y^2}, d_{z^2}\)) are degenerate in an isolated atom; \(d_{z^2}\) has two lobes along the \(z\)-axis plus a central torus in the \(xy\)-plane.

Orbital Energies, Filling Rules, and Electronic Configurations

In a hydrogen atom, orbital energy depends only on \(n\), so \(2s = 2p\) and \(3s = 3p = 3d\) (degenerate). In multi-electron atoms, electron–electron repulsions and shielding by inner electrons lower the nuclear charge felt by outer electrons to an effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\text{eff}}\)). Because \(s\)-electrons penetrate closer to the nucleus than \(p\)-, \(d\)-, or \(f\)-electrons, energy within a shell increases as \(s < p < d < f\).

Three Core Rules for Ground-State Configurations

  1. Aufbau Principle & \((n+l)\) Rule: Orbitals fill in order of increasing energy. Lower \((n+l)\) means lower energy; if two orbitals have equal \((n+l)\), the one with smaller \(n\) has lower energy. Standard filling sequence: \(1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s\dots\)
  2. Pauli Exclusion Principle: No two electrons in the same atom can share all four quantum numbers. Consequently, an orbital holds at most \(2\) electrons, and they must have opposite spins (\(+\frac{1}{2}\) and \(-\frac{1}{2}\)).
  3. Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity: Degenerate orbitals (\(p, d, f\)) are first occupied singly with parallel spins before any pairing begins.
Extra Stability of Half-Filled and Completely Filled Subshells

Configurations such as \(p^3, p^6, d^5, d^{10}, f^7, f^{14}\) possess extra stability due to symmetrical electron distribution (reduced mutual shielding, higher \(Z_{\text{eff}}\)) and maximum exchange energy (energy released when parallel-spin electrons exchange positions among degenerate orbitals). Hence, Chromium (\(Z=24\)) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1\) (not \(3d^4 4s^2\)) and Copper (\(Z=29\)) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1\) (not \(3d^9 4s^2\)).

Worked Examples

Determining Subatomic Composition of an Ion

Problem: Consider a hypothetical arsenide ion, \(^{75}_{33}\mathrm{As}^{3-}\). Calculate the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons present in one ion.

Solution:

  • Atomic number \(Z = 33\), so the number of protons \(= 33\).
  • Mass number \(A = 75\), so the number of neutrons \(= A - Z = 75 - 33 = 42\).
  • The ion carries a \(-3\) charge, meaning it has gained \(3\) extra electrons relative to the neutral atom: electrons \(= 33 + 3 = 36\).

Applying Einstein's Photoelectric Equation

Problem: A clean metal plate (assumed work function \(W_0 = 2.40\,\mathrm{eV}\)) is irradiated with ultraviolet light of wavelength \(\lambda = 250\,\mathrm{nm}\). Calculate the maximum kinetic energy (in \(\mathrm{eV}\)) and maximum speed of the ejected photoelectrons. (Use \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\), \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\), \(m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)).

Solution:

\[ E_{\text{photon}} = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^8}{250 \times 10^{-9}} = 7.951 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \] \[ E_{\text{photon}}(\mathrm{eV}) = \frac{7.951 \times 10^{-19}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 4.96\,\mathrm{eV} \] \[ KE_{\max} = E_{\text{photon}} - W_0 = 4.96 - 2.40 = 2.56\,\mathrm{eV} = 4.10 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \] \[ v_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2\,KE_{\max}}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 4.10 \times 10^{-19}}{9.109 \times 10^{-31}}} \approx 9.49 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Calculating Bohr Radius and Spectral Wavelength in \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\)

Problem: For the hydrogen-like ion \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\) (\(Z = 3\)), determine (a) the radius of the \(n = 2\) Bohr orbit, and (b) the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron transitions from \(n = 3\) to \(n = 2\). (Use \(R = 1.097 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

(a) Radius of the \(n = 2\) orbit:

\[ r_2 = \frac{52.9 \times n^2}{Z} = \frac{52.9 \times 2^2}{3} = \frac{211.6}{3} \approx 70.5\,\mathrm{pm} \]

(b) Wavenumber and wavelength for \(n_i = 3 \to n_f = 2\):

\[ \bar{\nu} = R Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2}\right) = 1.097 \times 10^7 \times 9 \times \left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{9}\right) = 1.097 \times 10^7 \times 9 \times \frac{5}{36} = 1.371 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \] \[ \lambda = \frac{1}{\bar{\nu}} = \frac{1}{1.371 \times 10^7} \approx 7.29 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m} = 72.9\,\mathrm{nm} \]

Computing de Broglie Wavelength and Velocity Uncertainty

Problem: A proton (\(m = 1.673 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) travels at an assumed speed of \(2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Find its de Broglie wavelength. If its position is measured within \(\Delta x = 1.0\,\mathrm{pm}\), what is the minimum uncertainty in its velocity?

Solution:

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{1.673 \times 10^{-27} \times 2.0 \times 10^5} \approx 1.98 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.98\,\mathrm{pm} \] \[ \Delta v_{\min} = \frac{h}{4\pi m \Delta x} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{4 \times 3.1416 \times 1.673 \times 10^{-27} \times 1.0 \times 10^{-12}} \approx 3.15 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Evaluating Nodal Counts and Orbital Energy Ordering

Problem: Find the number of radial and angular nodes in a \(4d\) orbital and a \(5p\) orbital, and arrange the orbitals \(4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d\) in increasing order of energy in a multi-electron atom.

Solution:

  • For \(4d\) (\(n = 4, l = 2\)): radial nodes \(= n - l - 1 = 4 - 2 - 1 = 1\); angular nodes \(= l = 2\); total nodes \(= 3\).
  • For \(5p\) (\(n = 5, l = 1\)): radial nodes \(= 5 - 1 - 1 = 3\); angular nodes \(= 1\); total nodes \(= 4\).
  • Using \((n+l)\): \(4s\,(4+0=4)\), \(3d\,(3+2=5)\), \(4p\,(4+1=5)\), \(5s\,(5+0=5)\), \(4d\,(4+2=6)\). Breaking the \((n+l)=5\) tie by smaller \(n\) gives: \(4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d\).

Practice Question 1: Which of the following pairs represents a set of isobars?
  • (A) \(^{35}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\) and \(^{37}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\)
  • (B) \(^{40}_{18}\mathrm{Ar}\) and \(^{40}_{20}\mathrm{Ca}\)
  • (C) \(^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) \(^{16}_{8}\mathrm{O}\) and \(^{18}_{8}\mathrm{O}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Isobars are atoms of different elements (different \(Z\)) with the same mass number (\(A\)). \(^{40}_{18}\mathrm{Ar}\) and \(^{40}_{20}\mathrm{Ca}\) both have \(A = 40\) but different atomic numbers (\(18\) and \(20\)). The other pairs are isotopes (same \(Z\), different \(A\)).

Practice Question 2: In Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment, approximately 1 in 20,000 alpha particles deflected by nearly 180°. This observation directly established that:
  • (A) electrons revolve in quantised circular orbits
  • (B) positive charge and mass of the atom are uniformly distributed
  • (C) the positive charge and almost all atomic mass are concentrated in an extremely small nucleus
  • (D) neutrons are present inside the atomic nucleus
Answer: (C)

Solution: Only a tiny fraction of heavy, positively charged \(\alpha\)-particles suffered head-on repulsion, proving that the positive charge and mass are densely packed in a tiny central nucleus (\(\sim 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\)) rather than spread over the entire atom (\(\sim 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)).

Practice Question 3: A monochromatic source of power \(50\,\mathrm{W}\) emits light of wavelength \(662.6\,\mathrm{nm}\). How many photons are emitted per second? (\(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\), \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\))
  • (A) \(1.67 \times 10^{20}\)
  • (B) \(3.00 \times 10^{19}\)
  • (C) \(1.67 \times 10^{18}\)
  • (D) \(5.00 \times 10^{20}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Energy of one photon \(E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{662.6 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\). Photons emitted per second \(= \frac{50\,\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}}{3.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}} = 1.67 \times 10^{20}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 4: Light of frequency \(\nu = 1.5\nu_0\) (where \(\nu_0\) is the threshold frequency) strikes a metal surface. If the frequency is doubled to \(3.0\nu_0\) while keeping intensity constant, the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected photoelectrons:
  • (A) doubles
  • (B) becomes four times
  • (C) remains unchanged
  • (D) becomes four times the initial maximum kinetic energy, i.e. \(4 \times (0.5h\nu_0) = 2h\nu_0\)
Answer: (D)

Solution: Initially, \(KE_1 = h(1.5\nu_0) - h\nu_0 = 0.5h\nu_0\). When \(\nu = 3.0\nu_0\), \(KE_2 = h(3.0\nu_0) - h\nu_0 = 2.0h\nu_0 = 4\,KE_1\). Thus \(KE_{\max}\) increases by a factor of \(4\).

Practice Question 5: According to Bohr's model, what is the ratio of the radius of the third orbit of \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\) to the radius of the second orbit of \(\mathrm{He}^+\)?
  • (A) \(3 : 2\)
  • (B) \(2 : 3\)
  • (C) \(9 : 4\)
  • (D) \(4 : 9\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Since \(r_n \propto \frac{n^2}{Z}\), we have \(\frac{r_3(\mathrm{Li}^{2+})}{r_2(\mathrm{He}^+)} = \frac{3^2 / 3}{2^2 / 2} = \frac{3}{2}\).

Practice Question 6: An electron in a hydrogen atom drops from the \(n = 5\) state to the ground state (\(n = 1\)). What is the maximum number of distinct emission spectral lines that can be observed from a large sample of such atoms?
  • (A) \(4\)
  • (B) \(6\)
  • (C) \(10\)
  • (D) \(15\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Maximum number of emission lines from level \(n\) to \(1\) is \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 10\).

Practice Question 7: An electron and a proton have the same kinetic energy. What is the ratio of the de Broglie wavelength of the electron to that of the proton (\(\lambda_e / \lambda_p\))?
  • (A) \(\sqrt{m_p / m_e}\)
  • (B) \(\sqrt{m_e / m_p}\)
  • (C) \(m_p / m_e\)
  • (D) \(1 : 1\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Using \(\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m \cdot KE}}\), for equal \(KE\), \(\lambda \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\). Therefore, \(\frac{\lambda_e}{\lambda_p} = \sqrt{\frac{m_p}{m_e}}\).

Practice Question 8: If the uncertainty in the position of a moving particle is zero (\(\Delta x \to 0\)), Heisenberg's uncertainty principle implies that the uncertainty in its momentum (\(\Delta p_x\)) is:
  • (A) zero
  • (B) equal to \(h / 4\pi\)
  • (C) infinite
  • (D) equal to \(m c\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: From \(\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}\), as \(\Delta x \to 0\), \(\Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi \Delta x} \to \infty\).

Practice Question 9: Which of the following sets of quantum numbers \((n, l, m_l, m_s)\) is NOT permissible for an electron in an atom?
  • (A) \((3, 2, -2, +\frac{1}{2})\)
  • (B) \((4, 0, 0, -\frac{1}{2})\)
  • (C) \((3, 3, -1, +\frac{1}{2})\)
  • (D) \((2, 1, 0, -\frac{1}{2})\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: For a given \(n\), \(l\) can only take values from \(0\) to \(n - 1\). When \(n = 3\), the maximum allowed value of \(l\) is \(2\), so \(l = 3\) (\(3f\)) is impossible.

Practice Question 10: The number of radial nodes and angular nodes in a \(4p\) orbital are, respectively:
  • (A) \(2\) and \(1\)
  • (B) \(1\) and \(2\)
  • (C) \(3\) and \(1\)
  • (D) \(2\) and \(2\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: For \(4p\), \(n = 4\) and \(l = 1\). Radial nodes \(= n - l - 1 = 4 - 1 - 1 = 2\). Angular nodes \(= l = 1\).

Practice Question 11 (Assertion–Reason):
Assertion (A): The ground-state valence electronic configuration of Chromium (\(Z = 24\)) is \(3d^5 4s^1\) rather than \(3d^4 4s^2\).
Reason (R): Half-filled degenerate \(3d\) orbitals gain extra stability due to symmetrical electron distribution and maximum exchange energy.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A)
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true
Answer: (A)

Solution: Because \(4s\) and \(3d\) are close in energy, shifting one electron from \(4s\) to \(3d\) produces a half-filled \(3d^5\) subshell, which is stabilised by symmetry and maximum exchange energy.

Practice Question 12 (Statement-Based):
Statement I: In a hydrogen atom, the \(3s\), \(3p\), and \(3d\) orbitals have the exact same energy.
Statement II: In multi-electron atoms, the energy of an orbital depends on both \(n\) and \(l\), and orbitals with lower \((n + l)\) have lower energy.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct
Answer: (A)

Solution: In one-electron systems like \(\mathrm{H}\), energy depends solely on \(n\) (Statement I is correct). In multi-electron atoms, shielding splits subshell energies according to the \((n+l)\) rule (Statement II is correct).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Orbit vs. Orbital: An orbit is a 2D circular Bohr path (ruled out by uncertainty); an orbital is a 3D wave function \(\psi\) representing electron probability density \(|\psi|^2\).
  • \(s\)-Orbital Degeneracy in \(\mathrm{H}\) vs. Multi-Electron Atoms: Do not apply the \((n+l)\) energy splitting to \(\mathrm{H}\) or \(\mathrm{He}^+\). For one-electron species, \(2s = 2p\) and \(3s = 3p = 3d\).
  • Radial vs. Angular Nodes: Always check which node type is asked! Radial \(= n - l - 1\); Angular \(= l\); Total \(= n - 1\).
  • Photoelectric Intensity vs. Frequency: Intensity controls the number of photoelectrons; frequency controls their kinetic energy (provided \(\nu > \nu_0\)).
  • Unit Conversions in Spectral Problems: Convert \(\mathrm{nm}\) (\(10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}\)) and \(\mathrm{Å}\) (\(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)) carefully, and remember \(R_{\mathrm{H}} = 109{,}677\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}} \approx 1.097 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\).

Concise Final Recap

Atoms consist of a dense, positively charged nucleus (protons and neutrons) surrounded by electrons. While Bohr's quantised model accurately predicts one-electron spectra and radii, wave–particle duality (de Broglie) and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle lead to the Schrödinger quantum mechanical model. Each electron is uniquely described by four quantum numbers \((n, l, m_l, m_s)\), and ground-state electronic configurations follow the Aufbau \((n+l)\) order, Pauli exclusion principle, Hund's rule, and half-/full-subshell stability.
