Classification of Elements and Periodicity in Properties

A complete, exam-oriented study guide covering periodic classification, electronic configuration, IUPAC nomenclature, and periodic trends with exceptions.

Learning Objectives and Big Picture

The Periodic Table is the central organizing framework of chemistry. Instead of memorizing isolated facts about over a hundred elements, periodicity allows you to predict atomic sizes, ionization energies, oxidation states, bonding character, and oxide acidity directly from an element's position and valence electronic configuration.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial
  • Trace the historical shift from atomic-weight classification (Dobereiner, Newlands, Mendeleev, Lothar Meyer) to Moseley's atomic-number law.
  • Apply IUPAC systematic naming rules for superheavy elements with atomic number \(Z > 100\).
  • Identify the period, group, and block (\(s, p, d, f\)) of any element from its ground-state electronic configuration.
  • Explain and compare periodic trends—atomic radius, ionic radius, ionization enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, and electronegativity—along with high-yield NEET UG exceptions.
  • Predict valence, oxidation states, diagonal relationships, and acidic/basic/amphoteric nature of oxides.

Evolution of Periodic Classification and the Modern Periodic Law

Early chemists noticed that elements could be grouped into families with recurring chemical behaviour as the number of known elements grew from 31 in 1800 to 63 by 1865 and 118 today.

Scientist / Milestone Core Principle Key Contribution & Limitation
Johann Dobereiner (1829)
Law of Triads		 Elements grouped in threes with similar properties; middle element's atomic weight is roughly the average of the other two. Examples: (Li, Na, K), (Ca, Sr, Ba), (Cl, Br, I). Worked only for a few sets of elements.
A.E.B. de Chancourtois (1862) Cylindrical (helical) arrangement by increasing atomic weight. Demonstrated periodic recurrence visually, but gained little attention at the time.
John Newlands (1865)
Law of Octaves		 Every eighth element arranged by increasing atomic weight resembles the first (like musical octaves). Valid only up to calcium (Ca); failed for heavier elements.
Lothar Meyer (1868–1869) Plotted physical properties (atomic volume, melting point, boiling point) against atomic weight. Observed repeating wave-like patterns with changing period lengths; published after Mendeleev.
Dmitri Mendeleev (1869)
Periodic Law		 Properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic weights. Left gaps for undiscovered elements (Eka-aluminium → Gallium, Eka-silicon → Germanium) and reversed order where chemical properties demanded (e.g., placing I after Te).
Henry Moseley (1913)
Modern Periodic Law		 Plot of \(\sqrt{\nu}\) of characteristic X-rays vs. atomic number \(Z\) gives a straight line (unlike atomic mass). Established that atomic number \(Z\) (nuclear charge) is the fundamental basis of periodicity.
Modern Periodic Law: The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers (\(Z\)).

Because \(Z\) equals the number of protons—and the number of electrons in a neutral atom—periodicity is a direct consequence of the repeating pattern of outer (valence) electronic configurations.

Structure of the Long-Form Periodic Table and Block Classification

The modern long-form Periodic Table consists of 7 horizontal rows (periods) and 18 vertical columns (groups) numbered 1 to 18 under IUPAC recommendations.

  • Period Number: Equals the highest principal quantum number (\(n\)) of the valence shell. The 7 periods contain 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, and 32 elements respectively (each orbital accommodates 2 electrons, so elements per period = \(2 \times \text{number of orbitals filled}\)).
  • Group Number: Elements in the same vertical column share the same outer-shell electronic configuration and therefore exhibit similar chemical properties.
  • Special Placements:
    • Hydrogen (\(1s^1\)): Placed separately at the top because it resembles both Group 1 alkali metals (loses 1 \(e^-\) to form \(\mathrm{H}^+\)) and Group 17 halogens (gains 1 \(e^-\) to form \(\mathrm{H}^-\)).
    • Helium (\(1s^2\)): Strictly fills an \(s\)-orbital, yet is placed in Group 18 because its completely filled valence shell gives it noble-gas inertness.

Key Characteristics of the Four Blocks

Block Groups General Outer Configuration Salient Features
s-Block 1 (Alkali metals), 2 (Alkaline earth metals) \(ns^{1\text{–}2}\) Reactive metals, low ionization enthalpy, form \(+1\) or \(+2\) ions, predominantly ionic compounds (except Li and Be).
p-Block 13 to 18 (with s-block = Representative Elements) \(ns^2 np^{1\text{–}6}\) Includes metals, metalloids (Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te), and non-metals (chalcogens Gr 16, halogens Gr 17, noble gases Gr 18).
d-Block 3 to 12 (Transition Elements) \((n-1)d^{1\text{–}10} ns^{0\text{–}2}\) All metals; variable oxidation states, coloured ions, paramagnetism, catalytic activity. Note: Zn, Cd, Hg have \((n-1)d^{10}ns^2\) and lack typical transition properties.
f-Block Placed in Group 3 (Lanthanoids & Actinoids) \((n-2)f^{1\text{–}14}(n-1)d^{0\text{–}1}ns^2\) All metals; inner-transition series. Actinoids are radioactive; elements after uranium (\(Z > 92\)) are transuranium elements.

Periodic Trends in Physical Properties and Key Exceptions

1. Atomic Radius

An atom has no sharp boundary, so atomic size (~1.2 Å = \(1.2 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)) is estimated from internuclear distances: covalent radius (half the single-bond distance in a homonuclear molecule, e.g., \(198\,\mathrm{pm}/2 = 99\,\mathrm{pm}\) for Cl) and metallic radius (half the distance between adjacent cores in a metal crystal, e.g., \(256\,\mathrm{pm}/2 = 128\,\mathrm{pm}\) for Cu). Noble gases are monoatomic and are measured by much larger van der Waals radii, so they are excluded from covalent-radius comparisons across a period.

  • Across a period (left → right): Atomic radius decreases because electrons enter the same valence shell while nuclear charge increases, raising effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\text{eff}}\)).
  • Down a group (top → bottom): Atomic radius increases because a new principal shell (\(n\)) is added and inner-shell shielding outweighs the nuclear charge increase.

2. Ionic Radius and Isoelectronic Species

  • Cation vs. Parent Atom: A cation is always smaller than its parent atom (fewer electrons, same nuclear charge, higher \(Z_{\text{eff}}\); e.g., \(\mathrm{Na} = 186\,\mathrm{pm}\), \(\mathrm{Na}^+ = 95\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Anion vs. Parent Atom: An anion is always larger than its parent atom (added electron increases inter-electronic repulsion and lowers \(Z_{\text{eff}}\); e.g., \(\mathrm{F} = 64\,\mathrm{pm}\), \(\mathrm{F}^- = 136\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Isoelectronic Series: Species with the same number of electrons (e.g., \(\mathrm{N}^{3-}, \mathrm{O}^{2-}, \mathrm{F}^-, \mathrm{Na}^+, \mathrm{Mg}^{2+}, \mathrm{Al}^{3+}\) all have \(10\,e^-\)). Radius decreases strictly as nuclear charge \(Z\) increases: \[\mathrm{Al}^{3+} < \mathrm{Mg}^{2+} < \mathrm{Na}^+ < \mathrm{F}^- < \mathrm{O}^{2-} < \mathrm{N}^{3-}\]

3. Ionization Enthalpy (\(\Delta_i H\))

Ionization enthalpy is the minimum energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state:

\[\mathrm{X}(g) \longrightarrow \mathrm{X}^+(g) + e^- \quad (\Delta_i H_1 > 0)\]

Successive ionization enthalpies always increase (\(\Delta_i H_1 < \Delta_i H_2 < \Delta_i H_3 \dots\)) because removing an electron from a positively charged ion requires more energy. A massive jump in \(\Delta_i H\) signals the removal of an electron from a stable noble-gas core.

  • General Trend: Increases across a period (smaller size, higher \(Z_{\text{eff}}\)) and decreases down a group (larger size, stronger shielding). Maxima occur at noble gases; minima at alkali metals.

High-Yield NEET Exceptions in Ionization Enthalpy
  • Be > B (and Mg > Al): In Be (\(1s^2 2s^2\)), the electron is removed from a penetrating \(2s\)-orbital, whereas in B (\(1s^2 2s^2 2p^1\)), it is removed from a \(2p\)-orbital that is better shielded by \(2s\) electrons.
  • N > O (and P > S): Nitrogen has a stable half-filled \(2p^3\) configuration (three electrons in separate orbitals). In oxygen (\(2p^4\)), two electrons pair in one \(2p\)-orbital, creating extra inter-electronic repulsion that eases removal of the fourth electron.
  • Group 13 Anomaly (Ga > Al slightly): \(\Delta_i H_1\) values (\(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) are B (801), Al (577), Ga (579), In (558), Tl (589). Poor shielding by \(3d^{10}\) electrons in Ga and \(4f^{14}5d^{10}\) electrons in Tl increases \(Z_{\text{eff}}\).

4. Electron Gain Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{eg} H\))

The enthalpy change when an electron is added to an isolated neutral gaseous atom:

\[\mathrm{X}(g) + e^- \longrightarrow \mathrm{X}^-(g)\]
  • Sign Convention: Exothermic addition gives a negative \(\Delta_{eg} H\) (e.g., halogens, chalcogens). Endothermic addition gives a positive \(\Delta_{eg} H\) (noble gases, because the electron must enter the next higher shell; also the second electron gain enthalpy of O or S is always positive due to electrostatic repulsion against an already negative ion).
  • General Trend: Becomes more negative across a period (left → right) and less negative down a group.
  • Crucial NEET Exception (Period 2 vs. Period 3): Chlorine has a more negative \(\Delta_{eg} H\) (\(-349\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) than Fluorine (\(-328\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), and Sulphur (\(-200\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is more negative than Oxygen (\(-141\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)). In compact \(n=2\) orbitals (F, O), incoming electrons suffer strong inter-electronic repulsion, whereas \(n=3\) orbitals (Cl, S) offer more space.

5. Electronegativity

Electronegativity is a qualitative measure of the ability of an atom in a chemical compound to attract shared electrons to itself. Unlike \(\Delta_i H\) and \(\Delta_{eg} H\), it is not a measurable thermodynamic quantity of an isolated atom and varies slightly with the bonded partner. On the Pauling scale, Fluorine is assigned the highest value (\(4.0\)), followed by O (\(3.5\)), N & Cl (\(3.0\)), Br (\(2.8\)), C, S & I (\(2.5\)). It increases across a period and decreases down a group.

Periodic Trends in Chemical Properties

1. Valence and Oxidation States

For representative (\(s\)- and \(p\)-block) elements, valence is generally equal to the number of valence electrons or \((8 - \text{number of valence electrons})\). Oxidation state is the apparent charge on an atom in a compound based on electronegativity differences. For example, since electronegativity follows \(\mathrm{F} > \mathrm{O} > \mathrm{Na}\), oxygen exhibits \(+2\) in \(\mathrm{OF}_2\) (fluorine is \(-1\)) but \(-2\) in \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{O}\) (sodium is \(+1\)).

2. Anomalous Behaviour of Second-Period Elements & Diagonal Relationship

The first member of each main group (Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F) differs markedly from heavier congeners due to: (i) very small atomic/ionic size, (ii) high charge-to-radius ratio, (iii) high electronegativity, and (iv) absence of \(d\)-orbitals.

  • Maximum Covalency of 4: Second-period elements have only four valence orbitals (\(2s, 2p_x, 2p_y, 2p_z\)), limiting their covalency to 4 (e.g., \([\mathrm{BF}_4]^-\)), whereas Period 3 elements can use \(3d\) orbitals to expand covalency beyond 4 (e.g., \([\mathrm{AlF}_6]^{3-}\)).
  • \(p\pi\text{–}p\pi\) Multiple Bonding: Small size allows effective sideways overlap to form \(\mathrm{C=C}\), \(\mathrm{C\equiv N}\), \(\mathrm{N\equiv N}\), \(\mathrm{C=O}\) bonds.
  • Diagonal Relationship: Similarity between Period 2 elements and Period 3 elements diagonally below-right (Li ↔ Mg, Be ↔ Al, B ↔ Si) due to comparable charge/radius ratios and polarizing power.

3. Chemical Reactivity and Nature of Oxides

  • Reactivity: Highest at the two extremes of a period—alkali metals (strong reducing agents, low \(\Delta_i H\)) and halogens (strong oxidizing agents, high negative \(\Delta_{eg} H\))—and lowest in the middle. Down Group 1, reactivity increases (\(\mathrm{Li} < \mathrm{Na} < \mathrm{K} < \mathrm{Rb} < \mathrm{Cs}\)); down Group 17, reactivity decreases (\(\mathrm{F} > \mathrm{Cl} > \mathrm{Br} > \mathrm{I}\)).
  • Oxide Character Across a Period: Changes from strongly basic on the left (\(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{O} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NaOH}\)) to amphoteric in the middle (\(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3\), \(\mathrm{As}_2\mathrm{O}_3\)) to strongly acidic on the right (\(\mathrm{Cl}_2\mathrm{O}_7 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{HClO}_4\)). Oxides such as \(\mathrm{CO}\), \(\mathrm{NO}\), and \(\mathrm{N}_2\mathrm{O}\) are neutral.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Determining Block, Period, Group, and IUPAC Name from Atomic Number

Problem: Consider a hypothetical newly synthesized superheavy element with atomic number \(Z = 119\) (assumed for this exercise). Write its temporary IUPAC name and symbol, its outer electronic configuration, and predict its period, group, and block.

Solution:

  1. IUPAC Name & Symbol: The digits of \(119\) are \(1\) (un), \(1\) (un), and \(9\) (enn). Combining the roots with "-ium" gives Ununennium with symbol Uue.
  2. Electronic Configuration: Element \(Z = 118\) (Oganesson, Og) completes the \(7p^6\) noble-gas shell. The \(119^{\text{th}}\) electron enters the \(8s\)-orbital: \([\mathrm{Og}]\,8s^1\).
  3. Position: Highest principal quantum number \(n = 8 \implies\) Period 8; last electron enters \(s\)-orbital \(\implies\) s-block; configuration \(ns^1 \implies\) Group 1 (alkali metal family).
Example 2: Comparing Radii of Isoelectronic Species

Problem: Arrange the following ions in increasing order of ionic radius: \(\mathrm{P}^{3-}\), \(\mathrm{S}^{2-}\), \(\mathrm{Cl}^-\), \(\mathrm{K}^+\), \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\).

Solution:

Count the total electrons in each species: \(\mathrm{P}^{3-}\,(15+3=18)\), \(\mathrm{S}^{2-}\,(16+2=18)\), \(\mathrm{Cl}^-\,(17+1=18)\), \(\mathrm{K}^+\,(19-1=18)\), \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\,(20-2=18)\). All five are isoelectronic with \(18\) electrons.

For isoelectronic species, higher nuclear charge (\(Z\)) pulls the \(18\)-electron cloud closer to the nucleus, reducing the radius. Since \(Z\) increases in the order \(\mathrm{P}\,(15) < \mathrm{S}\,(16) < \mathrm{Cl}\,(17) < \mathrm{K}\,(19) < \mathrm{Ca}\,(20)\), the increasing order of ionic radii is:

\[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+} < \mathrm{K}^+ < \mathrm{Cl}^- < \mathrm{S}^{2-} < \mathrm{P}^{3-}\]
Example 3: Identifying an Element from Successive Ionization Enthalpies

Problem: A hypothetical main-group element \(M\) has the following successive ionization enthalpies (assumed values in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)): \(\Delta_i H_1 = 590\), \(\Delta_i H_2 = 1145\), \(\Delta_i H_3 = 4912\), \(\Delta_i H_4 = 6474\). Predict the group of \(M\) and the formula of its chloride.

Solution:

Notice the sharp jump between \(\Delta_i H_2\) (\(1145\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) and \(\Delta_i H_3\) (\(4912\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\))—more than a four-fold increase. This indicates that \(M\) has 2 valence electrons; removing the 3rd electron breaks into a stable noble-gas core.

Therefore, \(M\) belongs to Group 2 (alkaline earth metals), exhibits a stable \(+2\) oxidation state, and forms a chloride of formula \(\mathrm{MCl}_2\).

Example 4: Ranking Electron Gain Enthalpy and Oxide Acidity

Problem: (a) Which of \(\mathrm{O}\), \(\mathrm{S}\), \(\mathrm{F}\), \(\mathrm{Cl}\) has the most negative electron gain enthalpy? (b) Arrange \(\mathrm{MgO}\), \(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3\), \(\mathrm{P}_4\mathrm{O}_{10}\), and \(\mathrm{SO}_3\) in increasing order of acidic character.

Solution:

(a) Halogens (Group 17) have more negative \(\Delta_{eg} H\) than chalcogens (Group 16). Between F and Cl, Cl has a more negative \(\Delta_{eg} H\) (\(-349\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) than F (\(-328\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) because the larger \(3p\)-orbital in Cl minimizes electron-electron repulsion. Hence, \(\mathrm{Cl}\) has the most negative \(\Delta_{eg} H\).

(b) Across Period 3, non-metallic character increases from left to right, so oxide acidity increases: \(\mathrm{MgO}\) (basic) < \(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3\) (amphoteric) < \(\mathrm{P}_4\mathrm{O}_{10}\) (acidic) < \(\mathrm{SO}_3\) (strongly acidic).

Practice Question 1 — Single-Correct MCQ
Which of the following represents the correct order of increasing first ionization enthalpy for \(\mathrm{Be}, \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{N}, \mathrm{O}\)?
  • (1) \(\mathrm{B} < \mathrm{Be} < \mathrm{C} < \mathrm{O} < \mathrm{N}\)
  • (2) \(\mathrm{Be} < \mathrm{B} < \mathrm{C} < \mathrm{N} < \mathrm{O}\)
  • (3) \(\mathrm{B} < \mathrm{Be} < \mathrm{C} < \mathrm{N} < \mathrm{O}\)
  • (4) \(\mathrm{Be} < \mathrm{B} < \mathrm{C} < \mathrm{O} < \mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (1)

Solution: Across Period 2, \(\Delta_i H_1\) generally increases, but two exceptions occur: \(\mathrm{Be}\,(2s^2) > \mathrm{B}\,(2s^2 2p^1)\) due to greater penetration of \(2s\) electrons, and \(\mathrm{N}\,(2p^3) > \mathrm{O}\,(2p^4)\) due to half-filled \(2p^3\) stability. Thus, \(\mathrm{B} < \mathrm{Be} < \mathrm{C} < \mathrm{O} < \mathrm{N}\).

Practice Question 2 — Statement-Based MCQ
Given below are two statements:
Statement I: The electron gain enthalpy of fluorine is less negative than that of chlorine.
Statement II: Addition of an electron to the compact \(2p\)-orbital of fluorine causes greater inter-electronic repulsion than addition to the larger \(3p\)-orbital of chlorine.
In the light of the above statements, choose the correct option:
  • (1) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (2) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (3) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (4) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (1)

Solution: \(\Delta_{eg} H\) of Cl is \(-349\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), whereas that of F is \(-328\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). The smaller \(n=2\) shell of F experiences stronger electron-electron repulsion, making Statement I and Statement II both correct.

Practice Question 4 — Single-Correct MCQ
Which set of oxides contains one basic oxide, one amphoteric oxide, one neutral oxide, and one acidic oxide, respectively?
  • (1) \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{O},\ \mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3,\ \mathrm{NO},\ \mathrm{Cl}_2\mathrm{O}_7\)
  • (2) \(\mathrm{MgO},\ \mathrm{SiO}_2,\ \mathrm{CO}_2,\ \mathrm{SO}_3\)
  • (3) \(\mathrm{K}_2\mathrm{O},\ \mathrm{As}_2\mathrm{O}_3,\ \mathrm{SO}_2,\ \mathrm{N}_2\mathrm{O}_5\)
  • (4) \(\mathrm{CaO},\ \mathrm{CO},\ \mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3,\ \mathrm{P}_4\mathrm{O}_{10}\)
Answer: (1)

Solution: \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{O}\) is basic, \(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3\) is amphoteric, \(\mathrm{NO}\) is neutral, and \(\mathrm{Cl}_2\mathrm{O}_7\) is strongly acidic. In option (2), \(\mathrm{SiO}_2\) and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) are acidic; in (3), \(\mathrm{SO}_2\) is acidic; in (4), \(\mathrm{CO}\) is neutral and \(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3\) is amphoteric (wrong order).

Practice Question 5 — Assertion–Reason MCQ
Assertion (A): Boron cannot form the \([\mathrm{BF}_6]^{3-}\) ion, whereas aluminium readily forms \([\mathrm{AlF}_6]^{3-}\).
Reason (R): Boron has only four valence orbitals (\(2s\) and \(2p\)) and cannot expand its covalency beyond 4, whereas aluminium has vacant \(3d\)-orbitals.
  • (1) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (2) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (3) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (4) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (1)

Solution: Second-period elements lack \(d\)-orbitals in their valence shell (\(n=2\)), restricting maximum covalency to 4 (e.g., \([\mathrm{BF}_4]^-\)). Aluminium (\(n=3\)) can use \(3d\)-orbitals to achieve a covalency of 6 in \([\mathrm{AlF}_6]^{3-}\).

Practice Question 6 — Single-Correct MCQ
An element has outer electronic configuration \((n-2)f^7 (n-1)d^1 ns^2\) for \(n = 6\). Its atomic number and block are:
  • (1) \(Z = 64\), \(f\)-block
  • (2) \(Z = 63\), \(f\)-block
  • (3) \(Z = 71\), \(d\)-block
  • (4) \(Z = 96\), \(f\)-block
Answer: (1)

Solution: For \(n = 6\), the configuration is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^7 5d^1 6s^2\). Since Xe has \(Z = 54\), total electrons \(= 54 + 7 + 1 + 2 = 64\) (Gadolinium, Gd), which belongs to the \(4f\)-series (\(f\)-block).

Practice Question 7 — Single-Correct MCQ
Among \(\mathrm{O}^{2-}\), \(\mathrm{F}^-\), \(\mathrm{Na}^+\), and \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\), the species with the smallest and largest ionic radii respectively are:
  • (1) \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\) and \(\mathrm{O}^{2-}\)
  • (2) \(\mathrm{Na}^+\) and \(\mathrm{F}^-\)
  • (3) \(\mathrm{O}^{2-}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\)
  • (4) \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\) and \(\mathrm{F}^-\)
Answer: (1)

Solution: All four are 10-electron isoelectronic species. Increasing nuclear charge (\(Z\): \(\mathrm{O}=8, \mathrm{F}=9, \mathrm{Na}=11, \mathrm{Mg}=12\)) decreases radius: \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+} < \mathrm{Na}^+ < \mathrm{F}^- < \mathrm{O}^{2-}\).

Practice Question 8 — Single-Correct MCQ
Which pair of elements shows a diagonal relationship in the Periodic Table?
  • (1) Be and Al
  • (2) Na and Mg
  • (3) B and Al
  • (4) Li and Na

Solution: Diagonal relationships occur between Period 2 and Period 3 elements diagonally adjacent: Li & Mg, Be & Al, and B & Si, due to similar charge-to-radius ratios.

Common Pitfalls and NEET Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent Traps
  • Noble Gas Radii: Never compare van der Waals radii of noble gases directly with covalent radii of halogens; noble gases appear anomalously large only because they are non-bonded.
  • Second Electron Gain Enthalpy: Adding a second electron to \(\mathrm{O}^-(g)\) or \(\mathrm{S}^-(g)\) is always endothermic (\(\Delta_{eg} H_2 > 0\)) due to repulsion between two negative charges.
  • Be vs. B and Mg vs. Al (\(\Delta_i H_1\)): Always check \(ns^2\) vs. \(ns^2 np^1\) before applying the left-to-right increase rule.
  • N vs. O and P vs. S (\(\Delta_i H_1\)): Always check half-filled \(np^3\) vs. \(np^4\).
  • F vs. Cl and O vs. S (\(\Delta_{eg} H\)): Period 3 element has a more negative \(\Delta_{eg} H\) than the Period 2 element above it.
  • Zn, Cd, Hg: They belong to the \(d\)-block (Group 12) but are not regarded as typical transition elements because they have completely filled \((n-1)d^{10}\) configurations in ground and common oxidation states.

Concise Final Recap

The Modern Periodic Law roots all chemical and physical periodicity in atomic number \(Z\) and valence electronic configuration. Across a period, increasing \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) shrinks atomic size and raises ionization enthalpy, electronegativity, and non-metallic character; down a group, additional shells increase size and metallic character while lowering ionization enthalpy. Mastering the three core NEET anomaly clusters—Be > B / N > O in ionization enthalpy, Cl > F / S > O in negative electron gain enthalpy, and diagonal relationships / covalency limits in Period 2—gives you a decisive edge on exam day.
