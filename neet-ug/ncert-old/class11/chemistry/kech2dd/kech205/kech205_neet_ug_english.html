NEET UG Chemistry Tutorial

Organic Chemistry: Basic Principles, Mechanisms, and Analytical Techniques

A complete, self-contained conceptual and problem-solving guide aligned with the NEET UG syllabus.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Organic chemistry studies carbon-containing covalent compounds—from simple hydrocarbons to biomolecules such as DNA and proteins, as well as polymers, dyes, and pharmaceuticals. Carbon's ability to form strong covalent bonds with itself (catenation) and with heteroatoms such as hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, phosphorus, and halogens explains the vast diversity of organic molecules.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial
  • Structure & Hybridisation: Explaining carbon's tetravalence, \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) bonding, and molecular geometry using \(sp^3\), \(sp^2\), and \(sp\) hybrid orbitals.
  • Representation & Classification: Translating between complete, condensed, bond-line, and 3D wedge-dash structures, and classifying acyclic, alicyclic, and aromatic systems.
  • IUPAC Nomenclature & Isomerism: Naming polyfunctional alkanes and substituted benzenes systematically, and distinguishing structural and stereoisomers.
  • Reaction Mechanisms & Electronic Effects: Predicting intermediate stability (carbocations, carbanions, free radicals) and reactivity using inductive, resonance, electromeric, and hyperconjugation effects.
  • Purification & Elemental Analysis: Selecting separation techniques (distillation variants, extraction, chromatography) and solving quantitative elemental estimation problems (Liebig, Dumas, Kjeldahl, Carius).

Historically, Berzelius proposed that a mysterious "vital force" was necessary to produce organic substances. This hypothesis was overturned in 1828 when Friedrich Wöhler synthesized urea, an organic compound, by heating inorganic ammonium cyanate:

\[\mathrm{NH_4CNO} \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} \mathrm{NH_2CONH_2}\]

Subsequent laboratory syntheses—acetic acid by Kolbe (1845) and methane by Berthelot (1856)—established that organic molecules obey the same chemical laws as inorganic compounds.

1. Tetravalence, Hybridisation, and Bonding in Carbon

Carbon has four valence electrons and forms four covalent bonds to achieve a stable octet. Mixing its \(2s\) and \(2p\) atomic orbitals produces hybrid orbitals whose \(s\)-character directly governs bond length, bond strength (enthalpy), bond angle, and carbon's effective electronegativity.

Hybridisation Example Geometry & Angle % \(s\)-Character Relative Bond Length & Strength
\(sp^3\) \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) (Methane) Tetrahedral, \(109.5^\circ\) \(25\%\) Longest, weakest \(\mathrm{C-C}\) / \(\mathrm{C-H}\) \(\sigma\) bond
\(sp^2\) \(\mathrm{C_2H_4}\) (Ethene) Trigonal planar, \(120^\circ\) \(33.3\%\) Intermediate length and enthalpy
\(sp\) \(\mathrm{C_2H_2}\) (Ethyne) Linear, \(180^\circ\) \(50\%\) Shortest, strongest bond; highest carbon electronegativity

Key Features of \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) Bonds

  • Why \(s\)-character raises electronegativity: An \(s\) orbital lies closer to the positively charged nucleus than a \(p\) orbital. Greater \(s\)-character pulls shared electrons closer to carbon, making \(sp > sp^2 > sp^3\) in electronegativity.
  • Geometry of \(\pi\) overlap: A \(\pi\) bond forms by sideways (lateral) overlap of two parallel unhybridised \(p\) orbitals on adjacent atoms. In ethene (\(\mathrm{H_2C=CH_2}\)), all six atoms lie in one plane, while the \(\pi\) electron cloud sits above and below this plane.
  • Restricted rotation & reactivity: Twisting one \(\mathrm{CH_2}\) group relative to the other breaks the parallel \(p\)-orbital overlap, so rotation around a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond is restricted. Because \(\pi\) electrons are exposed above and below the molecular plane, they act as prime reactive centres for electrophilic reagents.

2. Structural Representations and Classification

Ways to Depict Organic Structures

  • Complete Structural Formula: Shows every atom and every covalent bond as a dash (single, double, or triple). Lone pairs on heteroatoms may be shown explicitly.
  • Condensed Structural Formula: Omits most bond dashes and groups identical repeating units with subscripts, e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_6CH_3}\) for octane.
  • Bond-Line Formula: Draws the carbon skeleton as a zig-zag line. Every vertex and terminal end represents a carbon atom with enough hydrogens to satisfy tetravalence; heteroatoms (\(\mathrm{O}\), \(\mathrm{N}\), \(\mathrm{Cl}\), \(\mathrm{Br}\)) and their attached hydrogens are written explicitly.
  • 3D Physical Models: Framework models highlight bond geometry without atomic radii; ball-and-stick (or ball-and-spring for multiple bonds) show atoms and bonds; space-filling models depict relative van der Waals volumes.

Classification of Organic Compounds

Organic compounds are divided structurally into open-chain (acyclic) and closed-chain (cyclic) families, or chemically by their functional group—the specific atom or group of atoms that dictates characteristic reactivity. Members sharing a functional group and differing sequentially by a \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) unit form a homologous series.

3. Systematic IUPAC Nomenclature

The IUPAC system builds unambiguous names from three parts: Prefix(es) + Word Root + Suffix(es).

Rules for Branched Alkanes and Cycloalkanes

  1. Longest Chain Rule: Identify the longest continuous carbon chain as the parent. If two chains have equal length, choose the one with more side chains.
  2. Lowest Locant Rule: Number the parent chain from the end nearest a substituent so the set of locant numbers is as low as possible at the first point of difference.
  3. Alphabetical Order: List substituents alphabetically. Multiplicative prefixes (di-, tri-, tetra-) and hyphenated prefixes (sec-, tert-) are ignored when alphabetising; however, iso- and neo- are part of the fundamental name and are alphabetised.
  4. Tie-breaker at Equivalent Positions: If two different substituents sit at identical distances from opposite ends, give the lower number to the substituent that comes first alphabetically (e.g., 3-ethyl-6-methyloctane, not 6-ethyl-3-methyloctane).
  5. Cyclic Alkanes: Prefix cyclo- to the parent alkane. When multiple substituents are present, assign C-1 to the more branched carbon or alphabetically earlier substituent to yield the lowest locant set.

Polyfunctional Compounds and Priority Order

When a molecule contains multiple functional groups, the highest-priority group becomes the principal functional group (expressed as the class suffix), and all others become prefix substituents.

Decreasing Priority Order of Principal Functional Groups

\(-\mathrm{COOH} > -\mathrm{SO_3H} > -\mathrm{COOR} > -\mathrm{COCl} > -\mathrm{CONH_2} > -\mathrm{CN} > -\mathrm{CHO} > \mathrm{>C=O} > -\mathrm{OH} > -\mathrm{NH_2} > \mathrm{>C=C<} > -\mathrm{C\equiv C}-\)

Groups such as alkyl (\(-\mathrm{R}\)), phenyl (\(-\mathrm{C_6H_5}\)), halogens (\(-\mathrm{F}, -\mathrm{Cl}, -\mathrm{Br}, -\mathrm{I}\)), nitro (\(-\mathrm{NO_2}\)), and alkoxy (\(-\mathrm{OR}\)) are always prefixes—never principal suffixes.

Substituted Benzene Compounds

  • Disubstituted benzenes: Relative positions 1,2-, 1,3-, and 1,4- are denoted by trivial prefixes ortho- (o-), meta- (m-), and para- (p-), respectively.
  • Tri- or higher substituted benzenes: Use the lowest locant rule and list substituents alphabetically (o/m/p prefixes are not allowed). When a common base name such as toluene (\(-\mathrm{CH_3}\)), anisole (\(-\mathrm{OCH_3}\)), aniline (\(-\mathrm{NH_2}\)), or phenol (\(-\mathrm{OH}\)) is used, its defining substituent occupies C-1.
  • Benzene as a substituent: When attached to an alkane chain bearing a functional group, \(\mathrm{C_6H_5}-\) is named as a phenyl group.

4. Isomerism: Structural and Stereochemical

Isomers share the same molecular formula but differ in atomic connectivity or spatial arrangement, giving distinct physical and chemical properties.

(Different connectivity)		 Chain Different carbon skeletons: pentane, isopentane (2-methylbutane), and neopentane (2,2-dimethylpropane) for \(\mathrm{C_5H_{12}}\).

Metamerism Different alkyl chains flanking a polyvalent functional group: methoxypropane (\(\mathrm{CH_3OC_3H_7}\)) and ethoxyethane (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5OC_2H_5}\)).
Stereoisomerism
(Same connectivity)		 Geometrical & Optical Differ in 3D spatial orientation of atoms or groups (e.g., restricted rotation across \(\mathrm{C=C}\) or chiral centres).

5. Reaction Mechanisms and Electronic Displacements

A reaction mechanism traces bond breaking, bond making, electron movement, energetics, and kinetics as a substrate (the carbon-donating reactant) reacts with an attacking reagent via reactive intermediates.

Covalent Bond Fission and Reactive Intermediates

  • Homolytic Cleavage: Each bonded atom takes one electron from the shared pair (shown by half-headed fish-hook arrows), forming neutral, paramagnetic free radicals. Favoured by heat, UV light, or peroxides (non-polar reactions). Stability order: \(\dot{\mathrm{C}}(\mathrm{CH_3})_3 > \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{CH_3})_2 > \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H_2CH_3} > \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H_3}\).
  • Heterolytic Cleavage: Both bonding electrons move to the more electronegative atom (shown by full curved arrows), yielding ionic intermediates (polar reactions):
    • Carbocation: Positively charged carbon with a sextet of electrons, \(sp^2\) hybridised, trigonal planar geometry, and an empty unhybridised \(p\) orbital. Stability order: \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{C}^+ > (\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{CH}^+ > \mathrm{CH_3CH_2}^+ > \mathrm{CH_3}^+\).
    • Carbanion: Negatively charged carbon with an octet (including a lone pair), generally \(sp^3\) hybridised with a distorted tetrahedral (pyramidal) shape.
Tutorial Diagram 3: Orbital geometry of a methyl carbocation (empty 2p orbital, 6 valence electrons) versus a methyl carbanion (filled sp³ orbital, 8 valence electrons).

Nucleophiles and Electrophiles

  • Nucleophile (\(\mathrm{Nu:}\)): Electron-rich ("nucleus-seeking") species that donates an electron pair to an electron-deficient centre. Examples: \(\mathrm{HO^-}\), \(\mathrm{CN^-}\), \(\mathrm{HS^-}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{R_3N:}\).
  • Electrophile (\(\mathrm{E^+}\)): Electron-deficient ("electron-seeking") species that accepts an electron pair. Examples: \(\mathrm{CH_3^+}\), \(\mathrm{NO_2^+}\), \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\), and polar carbonyl/alkyl halide carbons (\(\mathrm{>C=O}\), \(\mathrm{R-X}\)).

Electronic Displacement Effects

Effect Nature & Mechanism Key Groups & Exam Rules
Inductive (\(I\)) Permanent \(\sigma\)-bond polarisation due to electronegativity difference; decays rapidly and becomes negligible after three bonds. \(-I\) (electron-withdrawing): \(-\mathrm{NO_2}, -\mathrm{CN}, -\mathrm{COOH}, -\mathrm{F} > -\mathrm{Cl} > -\mathrm{Br} > -\mathrm{I}\).
\(+I\) (electron-donating): alkyl groups (\(-\mathrm{CH_3}, -\mathrm{C_2H_5}\)).
Resonance (\(R\) or \(M\)) Permanent delocalisation of \(\pi\) or lone-pair electrons across conjugated systems. Hybrid is more stable than any canonical form by the resonance energy. \(+R\): \(-\mathrm{OH}, -\mathrm{OR}, -\mathrm{NH_2}, -\mathrm{X}\) (increase o/p electron density).
\(-R\): \(-\mathrm{NO_2}, -\mathrm{CN}, -\mathrm{CHO}, -\mathrm{COOH}\) (decrease o/p density).
Hyperconjugation Permanent "no-bond resonance": delocalisation of \(\alpha\text{-C-H}\) \(\sigma\) electrons into an adjacent empty \(p\) orbital or \(\pi\) system. More \(\alpha\text{-H}\) atoms \(\Rightarrow\) greater stability of carbocations, free radicals, and alkenes (e.g., \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{C}^+\) has \(9\;\alpha\text{-H}\)).
Electromeric (\(E\)) Temporary complete transfer of \(\pi\)-electrons to one atom of a multiple bond only in the presence of an attacking reagent. \(+E\): \(\pi\)-electrons shift to the atom where reagent attaches (e.g., \(\mathrm{H^+}\) addition to alkene).
\(-E\): \(\pi\)-electrons shift away (e.g., \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) attack on \(\mathrm{>C=O}\)).
Rules for Ranking Resonance Contributors

Among canonical structures (which must have identical nuclear positions and unpaired electron counts), stability increases with: (1) more covalent bonds, (2) complete octets on all second-period atoms, (3) minimal charge separation, and (4) negative charge on the more electronegative atom.

6. Purification and Separation Techniques

Purity of an organic compound is verified by a sharp melting or boiling point, or by chromatographic and spectroscopic methods.

  • Sublimation: Separates sublimable solids (e.g., camphor) directly as vapour from non-sublimable impurities (e.g., \(\mathrm{CaSO_4}\)).
  • Crystallisation: Dissolves an impure solid in a hot solvent where it is sparingly soluble at room temperature; cooling yields pure crystals while impurities remain in the mother liquor. Activated charcoal adsorbs coloured impurities.
  • Simple Distillation: Separates volatile liquids from non-volatile impurities or liquids with a large boiling-point difference (e.g., chloroform at \(334\;\mathrm{K}\) and aniline at \(457\;\mathrm{K}\)).
  • Fractional Distillation: Used when boiling points are close. A fractionating column provides repeated condensation–vaporisation cycles (theoretical plates), enriching the ascending vapour in the lower-boiling component (used to refine crude petroleum).
  • Distillation Under Reduced Pressure: Lowers external pressure using a vacuum pump so high-boiling liquids boil below their decomposition temperature (e.g., separating glycerol from spent-lye in soap manufacture).
  • Steam Distillation: Purifies steam-volatile, water-immiscible liquids (e.g., aniline–water). Boiling occurs when \(p_{\text{organic}} + p_{\text{water}} = p_{\text{atm}}\), so the organic liquid distils below \(373\;\mathrm{K}\).
  • Differential Extraction: Uses a separatory funnel with an immiscible organic solvent in which the compound is far more soluble than in water.

Chromatography

Chromatography distributes mixture components between a stationary phase and a moving mobile phase:

  • Adsorption Chromatography: Components adsorb to varying extents on silica gel or alumina. Includes Column Chromatography (eluted down a glass column; least adsorbed component elutes first) and Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC) (coated glass plate). Colourless TLC spots are visualised under UV light, in an iodine chamber (brown spots), or with ninhydrin spray (for amino acids).
  • Partition Chromatography: Based on continuous differential partitioning between liquid stationary and mobile phases. In Paper Chromatography, water trapped in cellulose paper fibres acts as the stationary phase.

7. Qualitative and Quantitative Elemental Analysis

Qualitative Detection of Elements

  • Carbon & Hydrogen: Heat with dry \(\mathrm{CuO}\). Carbon oxidises to \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (turns lime-water milky via \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\)) and hydrogen oxidises to \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (turns white anhydrous \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) blue).
  • Lassaigne's Test (N, S, Halogens): Fusing the organic compound with metallic sodium converts covalent elements into water-soluble ionic salts: \(\mathrm{NaCN}\), \(\mathrm{Na_2S}\), and \(\mathrm{NaX}\) (\(X = \mathrm{Cl, Br, I}\)). Boiling the fused mass in distilled water gives the sodium fusion extract (SFE).
    • Nitrogen: Boil SFE with \(\mathrm{FeSO_4}\), then acidify with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\). Formation of Prussian blue precipitate, \(\mathrm{Fe_4[Fe(CN)_6]_3\cdot xH_2O}\), confirms nitrogen.
    • Sulphur: (a) SFE + acetic acid + lead acetate \(\rightarrow\) black \(\mathrm{PbS}\) precipitate (acetic acid is used because \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) would precipitate \(\mathrm{PbSO_4}\)). (b) SFE + sodium nitroprusside \(\rightarrow\) violet \([\mathrm{Fe(CN)_5NOS}]^{4-}\).
    • Both N and S together: Fusion yields sodium thiocyanate (\(\mathrm{NaSCN}\)), giving a blood-red colour of \([\mathrm{Fe(SCN)}]^{2+}\) with \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\). Excess sodium decomposes \(\mathrm{NaSCN}\) into \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) and \(\mathrm{Na_2S}\).
    • Halogens: Boil SFE with conc. \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) first (to decompose interfering \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) and \(\mathrm{Na_2S}\)), then add \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\): white \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) (soluble in \(\mathrm{NH_4OH}\)), pale yellow \(\mathrm{AgBr}\) (sparingly soluble), yellow \(\mathrm{AgI}\) (insoluble).
  • Phosphorus: Oxidise with \(\mathrm{Na_2O_2}\) to phosphate, boil with \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\), and add ammonium molybdate \(\rightarrow\) yellow precipitate of ammonium phosphomolybdate, \((\mathrm{NH_4)_3PO_4\cdot 12MoO_3}\).

Quantitative Estimation Formulas

Let \(m\) (in \(\mathrm{g}\)) be the mass of the organic sample analysed:

1. Carbon and Hydrogen (Liebig's Combustion): \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (\(m_1\,\mathrm{g}\)) is absorbed by anhydrous \(\mathrm{CaCl_2}\), and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (\(m_2\,\mathrm{g}\)) by conc. \(\mathrm{KOH}\).

\[\%\mathrm{C} = \frac{12 \times m_2 \times 100}{44 \times m} \qquad \%\mathrm{H} = \frac{2 \times m_1 \times 100}{18 \times m}\]

2. Nitrogen — Dumas Method: Sample heated with \(\mathrm{CuO}\) in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) atmosphere yields \(\mathrm{N_2}\) collected over \(\mathrm{KOH}\). Correct pressure for aqueous tension: \(p_1 = p_{\text{atm}} - \text{aqueous tension}\).

\[V_{\mathrm{STP}} = \frac{p_1 V_1 \times 273}{760 \times T_1} \qquad \%\mathrm{N} = \frac{28 \times V_{\mathrm{STP}} \times 100}{22400 \times m}\]

3. Nitrogen — Kjeldahl's Method: Sample digested with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) forms \((\mathrm{NH_4)_2SO_4}\); \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) liberated by \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) is absorbed in \(V\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(M\,\mathrm{molar}\) \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), with \(V_1\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(M\,\mathrm{molar}\) \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) needed for back-titration. (Not applicable to nitro, azo, or ring-nitrogen compounds such as pyridine.)

\[\%\mathrm{N} = \frac{1.4 \times M \times 2\left(V - \frac{V_1}{2}\right)}{m}\]

4. Halogens (Carius Method): Precipitated as \(\mathrm{AgX}\) (\(m_1\,\mathrm{g}\)); Sulphur: Precipitated as \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\) (\(m_1\,\mathrm{g}\), molar mass \(233\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)).

\[\%X = \frac{\text{At. mass of } X \times m_1 \times 100}{\text{Molar mass of } \mathrm{Ag}X \times m} \qquad \%\mathrm{S} = \frac{32 \times m_1 \times 100}{233 \times m}\]

5. Phosphorus & Direct Oxygen: \(\mathrm{P}\) estimated as \((\mathrm{NH_4)_3PO_4\cdot 12MoO_3}\) (\(1877\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) or \(\mathrm{Mg_2P_2O_7}\) (\(222\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)); \(\mathrm{O}\) estimated by conversion to \(\mathrm{CO}\) over coke and oxidation with \(\mathrm{I_2O_5}\) to \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (\(m_1\,\mathrm{g}\)).

\[\%\mathrm{P} = \frac{31 \times m_1 \times 100}{1877 \times m} = \frac{62 \times m_{\mathrm{Mg_2P_2O_7}} \times 100}{222 \times m} \qquad \%\mathrm{O} = \frac{32 \times m_1 \times 100}{88 \times m}\]

Worked Examples

Example 1: Determining Hybridisation and Counting Sigma and Pi Bonds

Identify the hybridisation state of each carbon atom and count the total number of \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) bonds in the hypothetical molecule \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-CH_2-C\equiv N}\).

Solution: Numbering from the principal functional group (\(-\mathrm{CN}\)) carbon as \(\mathrm{C_1}\):

  • \(\mathrm{C_1}\) (\(-\mathrm{C\equiv N}\)): Forms 2 \(\sigma\) bonds and 2 \(\pi\) bonds \(\Rightarrow\) \(sp\) hybridised.
  • \(\mathrm{C_2}\) (\(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\)): Forms 4 \(\sigma\) bonds \(\Rightarrow\) \(sp^3\) hybridised.
  • \(\mathrm{C_3}\) (\(-\mathrm{CH}=\)): Forms 3 \(\sigma\) bonds and 1 \(\pi\) bond \(\Rightarrow\) \(sp^2\) hybridised.
  • \(\mathrm{C_4}\) (\(\mathrm{CH_2}=\)): Forms 3 \(\sigma\) bonds and 1 \(\pi\) bond \(\Rightarrow\) \(sp^2\) hybridised.

Bond Count: \(\mathrm{C-H}\) \(\sigma\) bonds \(= 2 + 1 + 2 = 5\); \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\sigma\) bonds \(= 3\); \(\mathrm{C-N}\) \(\sigma\) bond \(= 1\). Total \(\sigma\) bonds \(= 9\). Total \(\pi\) bonds \(= 1\;(\mathrm{C=C}) + 2\;(\mathrm{C\equiv N}) = 3\).

Example 2: Applying IUPAC Priority Rules to a Polyfunctional Compound

Write the systematic IUPAC name for the compound \(\mathrm{CH_3-CO-CH_2-CH(OH)-COOH}\).

Solution:

  1. Three functional groups are present: carboxylic acid (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)), ketone (\(\mathrm{>C=O}\)), and alcohol (\(-\mathrm{OH}\)).
  2. By the priority order, \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) is the principal functional group (suffix: -oic acid) and its carbon is numbered \(\mathrm{C_1}\).
  3. The longest chain has 5 carbons (pentanoic acid), with \(-\mathrm{OH}\) at \(\mathrm{C_2}\) (prefix: hydroxy-) and \(\mathrm{>C=O}\) at \(\mathrm{C_4}\) (prefix: oxo-).
  4. Listing prefixes alphabetically (h before o): 2-Hydroxy-4-oxopentanoic acid.
Example 3: Ranking Carbocation Stability via Hyperconjugation and Inductive Effects

Arrange the following carbocations in decreasing order of stability: (I) \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{C}^+\), (II) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2}^+\), (III) \((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{CH}^+\), (IV) \(\mathrm{CH_3}^+\).

Solution: Count the \(\alpha\text{-C-H}\) bonds adjacent to the positively charged carbon:

  • (I) \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{C}^+\) has \(9\;\alpha\text{-H}\) atoms and three \(+I\) methyl groups.
  • (III) \((\mathrm{CH_3})_2\mathrm{CH}^+\) has \(6\;\alpha\text{-H}\) atoms and two \(+I\) methyl groups.
  • (II) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2}^+\) has \(3\;\alpha\text{-H}\) atoms and one \(+I\) methyl group.
  • (IV) \(\mathrm{CH_3}^+\) has \(0\;\alpha\text{-H}\) atoms and no hyperconjugative stabilisation.

Therefore, the decreasing stability order is: (I) > (III) > (II) > (IV).

Example 4: Calculating Nitrogen Percentage by Kjeldahl's Method

(Assumed experimental data) A \(0.40\,\mathrm{g}\) sample of an organic compound is digested by Kjeldahl's method. The liberated \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) is absorbed in \(40\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.5\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\). The residual acid requires \(60\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.5\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{NaOH}\) for complete neutralisation. Calculate the percentage of nitrogen in the compound.

Solution:

Volume of \(0.5\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) neutralised by \(60\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.5\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{NaOH}\) \(= \frac{V_1}{2} = \frac{60}{2} = 30\,\mathrm{mL}\).

Volume of \(0.5\,\mathrm{M}\;\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) consumed by \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) \(= V - \frac{V_1}{2} = 40 - 30 = 10\,\mathrm{mL}\).

\[\%\mathrm{N} = \frac{1.4 \times M \times 2\left(V - \frac{V_1}{2}\right)}{m} = \frac{1.4 \times 0.5 \times 2 \times 10}{0.40} = \frac{14}{0.40} = 35.0\%\]
Example 5: Estimating Chlorine Percentage by Carius Method

(Assumed experimental data) In a Carius estimation, \(0.25\,\mathrm{g}\) of an organic chloro-compound yields \(0.287\,\mathrm{g}\) of dry \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) precipitate. Calculate the percentage of chlorine in the sample. (Atomic masses: \(\mathrm{Ag} = 108\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{Cl} = 35.5\,\mathrm{u}\)).

Solution:

Molar mass of \(\mathrm{AgCl} = 108 + 35.5 = 143.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).

\[\%\mathrm{Cl} = \frac{35.5 \times m_{\mathrm{AgCl}} \times 100}{143.5 \times m} = \frac{35.5 \times 0.287 \times 100}{143.5 \times 0.25} = \frac{35.5 \times 0.002 \times 100}{0.25} = \frac{7.1}{0.25} = 28.4\%\]

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the molecule \(\overset{5}{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H}_3-\overset{4}{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H}=\overset{3}{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H}-\overset{2}{\mathrm{C}}\equiv\overset{1}{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H}\), which pair of hybridised orbitals forms the \(\mathrm{C_2}-\mathrm{C_3}\) \(\sigma\) bond?

  • (a) \(sp^3 - sp^2\)
  • (b) \(sp^2 - sp^2\)
  • (c) \(sp - sp^2\)
  • (d) \(sp - sp^3\)
Answer: (c)

Solution: \(\mathrm{C_2}\) participates in a triple bond (\(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\)), so it is \(sp\) hybridised. \(\mathrm{C_3}\) participates in a double bond (\(\mathrm{C=C}\)), so it is \(sp^2\) hybridised. Hence the \(\mathrm{C_2}-\mathrm{C_3}\) \(\sigma\) bond is formed by \(sp - sp^2\) orbital overlap.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

What is the correct IUPAC name of \(\mathrm{HO-CH_2-CH_2-CO-CH_3}\)?

  • (a) 1-Hydroxybutan-3-one
  • (b) 4-Hydroxybutan-2-one
  • (c) 2-Oxobutan-4-ol
  • (d) 3-Oxobutan-1-ol
Answer: (b)

Solution: Ketone (\(\mathrm{>C=O}\)) has higher priority than alcohol (\(-\mathrm{OH}\)), so the suffix is -one and \(-\mathrm{OH}\) is a hydroxy- prefix. Numbering from the right gives the ketone C-2 (lowest locant) and the \(-\mathrm{OH}\) group C-4, yielding 4-hydroxybutan-2-one.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Methoxypropane (\(\mathrm{CH_3OC_3H_7}\)) and ethoxyethane (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5OC_2H_5}\)) are best classified as:

  • (a) Chain isomers
  • (b) Position isomers
  • (c) Functional group isomers
  • (d) Metamers
Answer: (d)

Solution: Both compounds have the same molecular formula (\(\mathrm{C_4H_{10}O}\)) and the same ether functional group (\(-\mathrm{O}-\)), but differ in the alkyl groups attached to either side of the oxygen atom. Such isomers are called metamers.

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following substituted acetic acids is the strongest acid in aqueous solution?

  • (a) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2COOH}\)
  • (b) \(\mathrm{FCH_2COOH}\)
  • (c) \(\mathrm{ClCH_2COOH}\)
  • (d) \(\mathrm{BrCH_2COOH}\)
Answer: (b)

Solution: Electron-withdrawing (\(-I\)) groups stabilise the conjugate carboxylate anion (\(\mathrm{RCOO^-}\)) and increase acidity. Because fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine and bromine, \(-\mathrm{F}\) exerts the strongest \(-I\) effect, making \(\mathrm{FCH_2COOH}\) the strongest acid among the choices.

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): The \(tert\)-butyl carbocation, \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{C}^+\), is significantly more stable than the methyl carbocation, \(\mathrm{CH_3}^+\).

Reason (R): \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{C}^+\) is stabilised by nine \(\alpha\text{-C-H}\) hyperconjugative interactions and the \(+I\) effect of three methyl groups, whereas \(\mathrm{CH_3}^+\) lacks \(\alpha\)-hydrogens.

  • (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (b) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (c) A is true, but R is false.
  • (d) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (a)

Solution: In \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{C}^+\), nine \(\alpha\text{-C-H}\) bonds can align with the vacant \(2p\) orbital to disperse the positive charge via hyperconjugation, reinforced by the \(+I\) effect of three methyl groups. \(\mathrm{CH_3}^+\) has no \(\alpha\text{-C-H}\) bonds, so both A and R are true and R correctly explains A.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Glycerol has a normal boiling point of \(563\,\mathrm{K}\) and decomposes before reaching that temperature at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\). Which purification method is used to recover glycerol from spent-lye in the soap industry?

Practice Question 8 (Numerical MCQ)

(Assumed experimental data) In a Dumas nitrogen estimation, \(0.28\,\mathrm{g}\) of an organic compound produced \(44.8\,\mathrm{mL}\) of dry \(\mathrm{N_2}\) gas reduced to STP. What is the percentage of nitrogen in the compound?

  • (a) \(10.0\%\)
  • (b) \(20.0\%\)
  • (c) \(28.0\%\)
  • (d) \(40.0\%\)
Answer: (b)

Solution:

\[\%\mathrm{N} = \frac{28 \times V_{\mathrm{STP}} \times 100}{22400 \times m} = \frac{28 \times 44.8 \times 100}{22400 \times 0.28} = \frac{28 \times 0.002 \times 100}{0.28} = \frac{5.6}{0.28} = 20.0\%\]
Practice Question 9 (Numerical MCQ)

(Assumed experimental data) In Carius estimation of sulphur, \(0.32\,\mathrm{g}\) of an organic compound gave \(0.466\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\) (molar mass \(= 233\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)). The percentage of sulphur in the compound is:

  • (a) \(15.0\%\)
  • (b) \(20.0\%\)
  • (c) \(25.0\%\)
  • (d) \(32.0\%\)
Answer: (b)

Solution:

\[\%\mathrm{S} = \frac{32 \times m_{\mathrm{BaSO_4}} \times 100}{233 \times m} = \frac{32 \times 0.466 \times 100}{233 \times 0.32} = \frac{32 \times 0.002 \times 100}{0.32} = \frac{6.4}{0.32} = 20.0\%\]
Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

During Lassaigne's test for halogens, why is the sodium fusion extract boiled with concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) prior to adding \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\)?

  • (a) To oxidise \(\mathrm{Ag^+}\) to \(\mathrm{Ag^{2+}}\)
  • (b) To decompose \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) and \(\mathrm{Na_2S}\) so they do not form interfering silver precipitates
  • (c) To precipitate \(\mathrm{NaNO_3}\) as a white solid
  • (d) To convert \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) into \(\mathrm{AgI}\)
Answer: (b)

Solution: If nitrogen or sulphur is present in the organic compound, the extract contains \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) and \(\mathrm{S^{2-}}\) ions, which would react with \(\mathrm{Ag^+}\) to form \(\mathrm{AgCN}\) (white) or \(\mathrm{Ag_2S}\) (black) and interfere with halogen identification. Boiling with conc. \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) decomposes these ions into \(\mathrm{HCN}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) gases.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Alphabetising IUPAC prefixes: Remember that iso- and neo- count in alphabetical ordering, whereas di-, tri-, sec-, and tert- do not.
  • Carbon count in \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) and \(-\mathrm{COOH}\): The carbonyl carbon of \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), \(-\mathrm{CHO}\), and \(-\mathrm{CN}\) is always \(\mathrm{C_1}\) of the parent chain—never exclude it when counting chain length.
  • Acetic acid vs. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) in lead acetate test: Always acidify SFE with acetic acid (not \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)) before adding lead acetate, otherwise white \(\mathrm{PbSO_4}\) precipitates.
  • Kjeldahl's stoichiometry factor: One mole of \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) neutralises two moles of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), which is why \(V_1/2\) appears in the formula.
  • \(R_f\) bounds: Because the solute cannot travel faster than the solvent front, \(R_f = x/y\) is strictly \(\le 1\).

Concise Final Recap

Carbon's tetravalence and hybridisation (\(sp^3\), \(sp^2\), \(sp\)) determine molecular geometry, bond strength, and electronegativity. Systematic IUPAC naming relies on selecting the principal functional group and lowest locant numbering. Organic reactivity is governed by homolytic or heterolytic bond cleavage and electronic effects—permanent (\(\pm I\), \(\pm R\), hyperconjugation) and temporary (\(\pm E\)). Finally, pure compounds are isolated via sublimation, crystallisation, distillation variants, extraction, or chromatography, and their elemental composition is established qualitatively by Lassaigne's test and quantitatively by Liebig, Dumas, Kjeldahl, and Carius analyses.
