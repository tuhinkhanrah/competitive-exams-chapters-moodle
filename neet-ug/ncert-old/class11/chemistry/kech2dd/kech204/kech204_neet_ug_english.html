1. General Architecture of the p-Block

Elements in which the differentiating (last) electron enters an outermost p-orbital constitute the p-block. Because a p-subshell contains three degenerate orbitals capable of holding up to six electrons, the p-block spans six vertical groups: Groups 13 to 18. Their general valence-shell electronic configuration is \(ns^2np^{1\text{–}6}\) (with helium as the lone exception, \(1s^2\)).

Unlike s-block elements, the inner electronic core of p-block elements changes significantly down each group: Period 2 and 3 elements possess a noble-gas core, Period 4 and 5 elements have an additional filled \((n-1)d^{10}\) subshell, and Period 6 elements carry both \(4f^{14}\) and \(5d^{10}\) inner subshells. Because d- and f-electrons shield the valence shell poorly from nuclear charge, these inner-core variations drive striking discontinuities in atomic radii, ionisation enthalpies, and oxidation-state stabilities.

Oxidation States and the Inert Pair Effect

The highest possible oxidation state of a p-block element—known as the group oxidation state—equals the total number of valence s- and p-electrons (i.e., Group number minus 10). In addition, elements commonly exhibit oxidation states differing by units of two from the group state.

Core Principle: The Inert Pair Effect

Down Groups 13, 14, and 15, the oxidation state two units lower than the group oxidation state (\(+1\) in Group 13, \(+2\) in Group 14, \(+3\) in Group 15) becomes progressively more stable. In heavy elements such as \(\mathrm{Tl}\) and \(\mathrm{Pb}\), intervening \(4f\) and \(5d\) orbitals provide weak screening, so the effective nuclear charge holds the valence \(ns^2\) pair tightly. Consequently, the \(ns^2\) electrons resist participation in bonding, leaving mainly the \(np\) electrons available.

Metallic Character, Oxides, and Second-Period Anomalies

The p-block uniquely houses all non-metals, metalloids, and post-transition metals. Non-metallic character decreases down every group, making the heaviest element in each family the most metallic. Correspondingly, non-metal oxides are acidic or neutral, metalloid/borderline oxides are often amphoteric, and heavy metal oxides are basic.

The first member of each group (\(\mathrm{B, C, N, O, F}\)) differs sharply from its heavier congeners for two fundamental reasons:

  1. Small atomic size and high electronegativity / ionisation enthalpy: This favours strong covalent bonding and \(p\pi\text{–}p\pi\) multiple bonds with itself (\(\mathrm{C=C}, \mathrm{C\equiv C}, \mathrm{N\equiv N}\)) or other Period 2 atoms (\(\mathrm{C=O}, \mathrm{C\equiv N}, \mathrm{N=O}\)). Heavier elements have larger, diffuse p-orbitals that overlap poorly side-on, so they prefer single bonds or \(d\pi\text{–}p\pi\) / \(d\pi\text{–}d\pi\) bonding.
  2. Absence of valence d-orbitals: Period 2 elements have only one \(2s\) and three \(2p\) orbitals, restricting their maximum covalence to 4 (e.g., \([\mathrm{BF}_4]^-\)). Period 3+ elements possess energetically accessible vacant \(nd\) orbitals and can expand their octet beyond 4 (e.g., \([\mathrm{AlF}_6]^{3-}\), \([\mathrm{SiF}_6]^{2-}\)).

2. Group 13 Elements: The Boron Family

Group 13 comprises boron (\(\mathrm{B}\), a typical non-metal), aluminium (\(\mathrm{Al}\), a metal with strong chemical similarities to boron), gallium (\(\mathrm{Ga}\)), indium (\(\mathrm{In}\)), thallium (\(\mathrm{Tl}\)), and the synthetic radioactive element nihonium (\(\mathrm{Nh}\), \(Z=113\), \([\mathrm{Rn}]5f^{14}6d^{10}7s^27p^1\), half-life ~20 s).

  • Natural Occurrence: Boron is rare (<0.0001% of Earth's crust), occurring as orthoboric acid (\(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{BO}_3\)), borax (\(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{B}_4\mathrm{O}_7\cdot 10\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)), and kernite (\(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{B}_4\mathrm{O}_7\cdot 4\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)). Naturally occurring boron has two isotopes: \(^{10}\mathrm{B}\) (19%) and \(^{11}\mathrm{B}\) (81%). Aluminium is the most abundant metal and third most abundant crustal element (8.3% by mass, after \(\mathrm{O}\) and \(\mathrm{Si}\)), extracted from bauxite (\(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3\cdot 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)) and cryolite (\(\mathrm{Na}_3\mathrm{AlF}_6\)).

  • Atomic Radius Anomaly (\(\mathrm{Ga} < \mathrm{Al}\)): Although atomic radius normally increases down a group, \(\mathrm{Ga}\) (\(135\,\mathrm{pm}\)) is smaller than \(\mathrm{Al}\) (\(143\,\mathrm{pm}\)).   • Ionisation Enthalpy Trend: \(\Delta_i H_1\) drops sharply from \(\mathrm{B}\) to \(\mathrm{Al}\), then shows slight increases at \(\mathrm{Ga}\) and \(\mathrm{Tl}\) due to poor shielding by \(3d^{10}\) and \(4f^{14}5d^{10}\) cores respectively. Gallium has an unusually low melting point (\(303\,\mathrm{K}\))—it can melt in the hand on a warm day—yet a very high boiling point (\(2676\,\mathrm{K}\)), making it suitable for high-temperature thermometers.

Chemical Reactivity & Trends

  1. Oxidation States & Redox Stability: Because the sum of the first three ionisation enthalpies of boron is very high, \(\mathrm{B}\) forms only covalent compounds and never \(\mathrm{B}^{3+}\) ions. Aluminium readily forms \(\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\) (\(E^\circ = -1.66\,\mathrm{V}\)). Down the group, the \(+1\) state gains stability: \(\mathrm{Al} < \mathrm{Ga} < \mathrm{In} < \mathrm{Tl}\). In thallium, \(\mathrm{Tl}^+\) is predominant and \(\mathrm{Tl}^{3+}\) (\(E^\circ = +1.26\,\mathrm{V}\)) is a powerful oxidising agent. Compounds in the \(+1\) state are more ionic than those in the \(+3\) state.
  2. Lewis Acidity & Dimerisation: Neutral trivalent monomers such as \(\mathrm{BF}_3\) and \(\mathrm{BCl}_3\) have only six valence electrons around boron. Being electron-deficient, they act as Lewis acids (e.g., \(\mathrm{F}_3\mathrm{B} + \mathrm{:NH}_3 \rightarrow \mathrm{F}_3\mathrm{B}\leftarrow\mathrm{NH}_3\), shifting B from trigonal planar \(sp^2\) to tetrahedral \(sp^3\)). Lewis acidity decreases down the group as atomic size increases. Anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3\) achieves an octet by dimerising into \(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{Cl}_6\) via halogen bridging, and fumes in moist air due to partial hydrolysis releasing \(\mathrm{HCl}\) gas.
  3. Hydrolysis & Coordination: Covalent trihalides hydrolyse in water to form tetrahedral \([\mathrm{M(OH)}_4]^-\) (\(sp^3\)). In acidified aqueous solution, \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3\) forms octahedral \([\mathrm{Al(H_2O)}_6]^{3+}\) (\(sp^3d^2\)). Boron cannot form \([\mathrm{BF}_6]^{3-}\) because it lacks \(2d\) orbitals.
  4. Air, Acids, and Alkalis: On heating, amorphous \(\mathrm{B}\) and \(\mathrm{Al}\) form \(\mathrm{E}_2\mathrm{O}_3\) with \(\mathrm{O}_2\) and \(\mathrm{EN}\) with \(\mathrm{N}_2\). Oxide character shifts from acidic (\(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{O}_3\)) to amphoteric (\(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3, \mathrm{Ga}_2\mathrm{O}_3\)) to basic (\(\mathrm{In}_2\mathrm{O}_3, \mathrm{Tl}_2\mathrm{O}_3\)). Boron resists non-oxidising acids and alkalis, whereas \(\mathrm{Al}\) is amphoteric:
    \[2\mathrm{Al(s)} + 6\mathrm{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\mathrm{(aq)} + 6\mathrm{Cl}^-\mathrm{(aq)} + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{(g)}\] \[2\mathrm{Al(s)} + 2\mathrm{NaOH(aq)} + 6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O(l)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Na}^+[\mathrm{Al(OH)}_4]^-\mathrm{(aq)} + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{(g)}\]
    Concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO}_3\) renders \(\mathrm{Al}\) passive by forming a thin, impervious protective oxide film on its surface.

3. Important Compounds of Boron & Industrial Uses

Borax (\(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{B}_4\mathrm{O}_7\cdot 10\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\))

Borax is a white crystalline solid whose actual structural formula is \(\mathrm{Na}_2[\mathrm{B}_4\mathrm{O}_5(\mathrm{OH})_4]\cdot 8\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\), containing tetranuclear \([\mathrm{B}_4\mathrm{O}_5(\mathrm{OH})_4]^{2-}\) units (two \(sp^2\) trigonal and two \(sp^3\) tetrahedral boron centres). It dissolves in water to yield an alkaline solution because it hydrolyses to strong base \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) and weak acid \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{BO}_3\):

\[\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{B}_4\mathrm{O}_7 + 7\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NaOH} + 4\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{BO}_3\]

On strong heating, borax loses water, swells, and melts into a transparent glass-like borax bead composed of sodium metaborate and boric anhydride (\(\mathrm{NaBO}_2 + \mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{O}_3\)). Transition metal oxides react with \(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{O}_3\) in a Bunsen flame to form characteristically coloured metaborates (for example, \(\mathrm{CoO}\) gives a deep blue \(\mathrm{Co(BO_2)}_2\) bead).

Orthoboric Acid (\(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{BO}_3\) or \(\mathrm{B(OH)}_3\))

Prepared by acidifying aqueous borax or hydrolysing boron halides/hydrides, orthoboric acid is a white, soapy crystalline solid that is sparingly soluble in cold water but highly soluble in hot water. Its solid-state structure consists of planar trigonal \(\mathrm{BO}_3\) units linked by intermolecular hydrogen bonds into 2D hexagonal sheets, which accounts for its slippery feel.

Crucially, \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{BO}_3\) is not a protonic (Brønsted) acid; it is a weak monobasic Lewis acid that accepts an \(\mathrm{OH}^-\) ion from water to complete boron's octet, releasing \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+\):

\[\mathrm{B(OH)}_3 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightleftharpoons [\mathrm{B(OH)}_4]^- + \mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{O}^+\]

Stepwise heating above \(370\,\mathrm{K}\) yields metaboric acid (\(\mathrm{HBO}_2\)) and ultimately boric oxide (\(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{O}_3\)).

Diborane (\(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6\))

  • Preparation:
    • Ether reduction: \(4\mathrm{BF}_3 + 3\mathrm{LiAlH}_4 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6 + 3\mathrm{LiF} + 3\mathrm{AlF}_3\)
    • Laboratory oxidation: \(2\mathrm{NaBH}_4 + \mathrm{I}_2 \rightarrow \mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6 + 2\mathrm{NaI} + \mathrm{H}_2\)
    • Industrial route: \(2\mathrm{BF}_3 + 6\mathrm{NaH} \xrightarrow{450\,\mathrm{K}} \mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6 + 6\mathrm{NaF}\)
  • Properties & Reactions: Colourless, toxic gas (b.p. \(180\,\mathrm{K}\)) that catches fire spontaneously in air (\(\Delta_r H^\circ = -1976\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) and hydrolyses rapidly in water to \(\mathrm{B(OH)}_3\) and \(\mathrm{H}_2\).
    • Symmetrical cleavage with bulky/soft Lewis bases (\(\mathrm{NMe}_3, \mathrm{CO}\)): \(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6 + 2\mathrm{L} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{BH}_3\cdot\mathrm{L}\).
    • Unsymmetrical cleavage with \(\mathrm{NH}_3\): forms ionic \([\mathrm{BH}_2(\mathrm{NH}_3)_2]^+[\mathrm{BH}_4]^-\), which on heating gives borazine (\(\mathrm{B}_3\mathrm{N}_3\mathrm{H}_6\), "inorganic benzene", isoelectronic and isostructural with benzene).
    • Borohydride synthesis: \(2\mathrm{MH} + \mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{M}^+[\mathrm{BH}_4]^-\) (\(\mathrm{M = Li, Na}\)), widely used organic reducing agents.
  • Structure & Banana Bonds: Both \(\mathrm{B}\) atoms are \(sp^3\) hybridised. Four terminal \(\mathrm{B–H}\) bonds (\(119\,\mathrm{pm}\), \(\angle\mathrm{H–B–H} = 120^\circ\)) are normal 2-centre 2-electron (2c–2e) bonds lying in one plane with the two boron atoms. Two bridging \(\mathrm{B–H–B}\) bonds (\(134\,\mathrm{pm}\), \(\angle\mathrm{H–B–H} = 97^\circ\)) lie perpendicular to this plane and are 3-centre 2-electron (3c–2e) banana bonds.

Key Uses of Boron and Aluminium

  • Boron & Compounds: Boron fibres are used in bulletproof vests and lightweight aircraft composites. The \(^{10}\mathrm{B}\) isotope has a high neutron-absorption cross-section, so metal borides serve as control rods and protective shields in nuclear reactors. Borax and boric acid are used in heat-resistant borosilicate glass (Pyrex), fibreglass, and as a soldering flux; dilute aqueous boric acid acts as a mild antiseptic.
  • Aluminium: On a weight-to-weight basis, \(\mathrm{Al}\) has twice the electrical conductivity of copper, making it ideal for overhead transmission cables. Alloys with \(\mathrm{Cu, Mn, Mg, Si, Zn}\) are used in aviation and transport, though domestic utensil use has declined due to toxicity concerns.

4. Group 14 Elements: The Carbon Family

Group 14 includes carbon (\(\mathrm{C}\), non-metal), silicon (\(\mathrm{Si}\), non-metal), germanium (\(\mathrm{Ge}\), metalloid), tin (\(\mathrm{Sn}\), metal), lead (\(\mathrm{Pb}\), metal), and flerovium (\(\mathrm{Fl}\), \(Z=114\), synthetic radioactive). Carbon has two stable isotopes (\(^{12}\mathrm{C}, ^{13}\mathrm{C}\)) and radioactive \(^{14}\mathrm{C}\) (\(t_{1/2} = 5770\,\mathrm{years}\), used in radiocarbon dating). Silicon is the second most abundant crustal element (27.7% by mass) as silica and silicates. Tin occurs mainly as cassiterite (\(\mathrm{SnO}_2\)) and lead as galena (\(\mathrm{PbS}\)).

Chemical Reactivity

  • Oxidation States (\(+4\) vs. \(+2\)): Tetravalent compounds are predominantly covalent (electron-precise, 8 valence electrons). Down the group, the \(+2\) state gains stability (\(\mathrm{Ge} < \mathrm{Sn} < \mathrm{Pb}\)). Thus, \(\mathrm{Sn}^{2+}\) is a good reducing agent (readily oxidised to \(\mathrm{Sn}^{4+}\)), whereas \(\mathrm{Pb}^{4+}\) is a strong oxidising agent (readily reduced to stable \(\mathrm{Pb}^{2+}\)).
  • Oxides: Monoxides (\(\mathrm{MO}\)) and dioxides (\(\mathrm{MO}_2\)) form on heating in \(\mathrm{O}_2\) (\(\mathrm{SiO}\) exists only at high temperatures). Higher oxidation-state oxides are more acidic: \(\mathrm{CO}_2, \mathrm{SiO}_2, \mathrm{GeO}_2\) are acidic, while \(\mathrm{SnO}_2, \mathrm{PbO}_2\) are amphoteric. Among monoxides, \(\mathrm{CO}\) is neutral, \(\mathrm{GeO}\) is acidic, and \(\mathrm{SnO, PbO}\) are amphoteric.
  • Water: \(\mathrm{C, Si, Ge}\) are unaffected by water; \(\mathrm{Sn}\) decomposes steam (\(\mathrm{Sn} + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{SnO}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\)); \(\mathrm{Pb}\) resists water due to a protective oxide film.
  • Halides & Hydrolysis: Tetrahalides (\(\mathrm{MX}_4\)) are covalent and tetrahedral (\(sp^3\)), except \(\mathrm{SnF}_4\) and \(\mathrm{PbF}_4\) which are ionic. \(\mathrm{PbI}_4\) does not exist because the weak \(\mathrm{Pb–I}\) bond cannot supply enough energy to unpair \(6s^2\) electrons, and \(\mathrm{I}^-\) reduces \(\mathrm{Pb}^{4+}\) to \(\mathrm{Pb}^{2+}\). Crucially, \(\mathrm{CCl}_4\) does not hydrolyse in water (no vacant d-orbitals on \(\mathrm{C}\)), whereas \(\mathrm{SiCl}_4\) hydrolyses vigorously to silicic acid, \(\mathrm{Si(OH)}_4\), because \(\mathrm{Si}\) accepts a lone pair from \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) into its vacant \(3d\) orbitals.
  • Complexes (\([\mathrm{SiF}_6]^{2-}\) vs. \([\mathrm{SiCl}_6]^{2-}\)): \([\mathrm{SiF}_6]^{2-}\) (\(sp^3d^2\)) is stable, whereas \([\mathrm{SiCl}_6]^{2-}\) does not form because six bulky \(\mathrm{Cl}^-\) ions cannot pack around small \(\mathrm{Si}^{4+}\) and \(\mathrm{Si–Cl}\) lone-pair interaction is weak.

Oxides of Carbon: \(\mathrm{CO}\) and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\)

  • Carbon Monoxide (\(\mathrm{CO}\)): Prepared on a small scale by dehydrating formic acid with conc. \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\) at \(373\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(\mathrm{HCOOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)). Industrially, steam over hot coke yields water gas / synthesis gas (\(\mathrm{CO} + \mathrm{H}_2\)), whereas air over hot coke yields producer gas (\(\mathrm{CO} + \mathrm{N}_2\)). \(\mathrm{CO}\) has a triple bond (\(\mathrm{:C\equiv O:}\), 1 \(\sigma\) + 2 \(\pi\)) and uses its carbon lone pair to form metal carbonyls and reduce metal oxides (\(\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3, \mathrm{ZnO}\)). Its extreme toxicity stems from forming carboxyhaemoglobin, which is ~300 times more stable than oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Carbon Dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO}_2\)): Linear \(sp\)-hybridised molecule (\(\mathrm{C–O} = 115\,\mathrm{pm}\), zero dipole moment) with \(p\pi\text{–}p\pi\) bonding. Prepared in the lab from \(\mathrm{CaCO}_3 + \text{dil. }\mathrm{HCl}\). In water, it forms carbonic acid (\(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\)); the \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{CO}_3/\mathrm{HCO}_3^-\) buffer maintains blood pH between 7.26 and 7.42. Solid \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) (dry ice) acts as a refrigerant. Excess atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) enhances the greenhouse effect.

Silicon Dioxide, Silicones, Silicates, and Zeolites

  • Silica (\(\mathrm{SiO}_2\)): Exists in crystalline forms (quartz, cristobalite, tridymite). Every \(\mathrm{Si}\) atom is tetrahedrally bonded to four \(\mathrm{O}\) atoms, and each \(\mathrm{O}\) bridges two \(\mathrm{Si}\) atoms, creating a giant 3D network of 8-membered alternating \(\mathrm{Si–O}\) rings. Due to high \(\mathrm{Si–O}\) bond enthalpy, \(\mathrm{SiO}_2\) resists most acids, halogens, and metals, reacting only with \(\mathrm{HF}\) (\(\mathrm{SiO}_2 + 4\mathrm{HF} \rightarrow \mathrm{SiF}_4 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)) and \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) (\(\mathrm{SiO}_2 + 2\mathrm{NaOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{SiO}_3 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)). Quartz is piezoelectric.
  • Silicones: Organosilicon polymers with repeating \((R_2\mathrm{SiO})\) units. Hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{Me}_2\mathrm{SiCl}_2\) followed by condensation polymerisation gives linear chains; adding \(\mathrm{Me}_3\mathrm{SiCl}\) caps chain ends to control molecular weight, while \(\mathrm{RSiCl}_3\) produces cross-linked 3D silicones. Non-polar alkyl shells make silicones hydrophobic, thermally stable, and electrically insulating.
  • Silicates & Zeolites: Built from discrete or corner-sharing \(\mathrm{SiO}_4^{4-}\) tetrahedra (sharing 1, 2, 3, or 4 corners gives pyrosilicates, chains, rings, sheets, or 3D frameworks). When \(\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\) replaces some \(\mathrm{Si}^{4+}\) in a 3D silica network, the resulting negatively charged aluminosilicate framework is balanced by \(\mathrm{Na}^+, \mathrm{K}^+\), or \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) ions. Zeolites (e.g., ZSM-5, which converts alcohols directly to gasoline) act as shape-selective petrochemical catalysts and ion-exchange water softeners.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Predicting Oxidation State Stability and Redox Behaviour via the Inert Pair Effect

Problem: Consider two hypothetical redox reactions in aqueous or acidic medium: (i) \(\mathrm{Tl}^{3+} + 2\mathrm{Fe}^{2+} \rightarrow \mathrm{Tl}^+ + 2\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}\) and (ii) \(\mathrm{PbO}_2 + 4\mathrm{HCl} \rightarrow \mathrm{PbCl}_2 + \mathrm{Cl}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\). Using Group 13 and 14 oxidation-state trends, explain why both reactions proceed spontaneously in the forward direction, whereas \(\mathrm{Sn}^{2+}\) tends to convert to \(\mathrm{Sn}^{4+}\).

Solution:

  1. In Group 13, the \(+1\) oxidation state becomes progressively more stable down the group (\(\mathrm{Al} < \mathrm{Ga} < \mathrm{In} < \mathrm{Tl}\)) due to the inert pair effect—poor shielding by \(4f^{14}\) and \(5d^{10}\) electrons binds the \(6s^2\) pair tightly in \(\mathrm{Tl}\). Consequently, \(\mathrm{Tl}^{3+}\) (\(E^\circ(\mathrm{Tl}^{3+}/\mathrm{Tl}) = +1.26\,\mathrm{V}\)) strongly prefers reduction to \(\mathrm{Tl}^+\), making \(\mathrm{Tl}^{3+}\) a powerful oxidising agent capable of oxidising \(\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}\) to \(\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}\).
  2. In Group 14, \(\mathrm{Pb}^{2+}\) is much more stable than \(\mathrm{Pb}^{4+}\) for the same reason. Thus, \(\mathrm{PbO}_2\) (\(\mathrm{Pb}\) in \(+4\) state) acts as a strong oxidising agent and oxidises \(\mathrm{Cl}^-\) in \(\mathrm{HCl}\) to \(\mathrm{Cl}_2\) while being reduced to \(\mathrm{PbCl}_2\) (\(+2\) state).
  3. By contrast, in tin (\(\mathrm{Sn}\)), the inert pair effect is weaker than in \(\mathrm{Pb}\), so \(\mathrm{Sn}^{4+}\) remains more stable than \(\mathrm{Sn}^{2+}\), making \(\mathrm{Sn}^{2+}\) a reducing agent.
Example 2: Explaining Atomic Radius and Ionisation Enthalpy Anomalies in Group 13

Problem: Arrange the elements \(\mathrm{B, Al, Ga, In, Tl}\) in increasing order of (a) atomic radius and (b) first ionisation enthalpy (\(\Delta_i H_1\)), and justify the deviations from smooth group trends.

Solution:

  1. Atomic Radius: Normally radius increases down a group, but \(\mathrm{Ga}\) (\(135\,\mathrm{pm}\)) is smaller than \(\mathrm{Al}\) (\(143\,\mathrm{pm}\)) because the 10 \(3d\)-electrons in \(\mathrm{Ga}\) shield the outer \(4s^24p^1\) electrons poorly. Therefore, the increasing order is: \[\mathrm{B}\,(88\,\mathrm{pm}) < \mathrm{Ga}\,(135\,\mathrm{pm}) < \mathrm{Al}\,(143\,\mathrm{pm}) < \mathrm{In}\,(167\,\mathrm{pm}) < \mathrm{Tl}\,(170\,\mathrm{pm})\]
  2. First Ionisation Enthalpy: Poor shielding by \(3d^{10}\) in \(\mathrm{Ga}\) and \(4f^{14}5d^{10}\) in \(\mathrm{Tl}\) increases the effective nuclear charge experienced by the valence electron, raising \(\Delta_i H_1\) of \(\mathrm{Ga}\) above \(\mathrm{Al}\) and \(\mathrm{Tl}\) above \(\mathrm{In}\). Using standard values (\(\mathrm{B}: 801\), \(\mathrm{Al}: 577\), \(\mathrm{Ga}: 579\), \(\mathrm{In}: 558\), \(\mathrm{Tl}: 589\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), the increasing order is: \[\mathrm{In} < \mathrm{Al} < \mathrm{Ga} < \mathrm{Tl} < \mathrm{B}\]
Example 3: Analysing Lewis Acidity and Hydrolysis of Group 13 & 14 Halides

Problem: Suppose a student adds water separately to \(\mathrm{CCl}_4\), \(\mathrm{SiCl}_4\), and \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3\). Predict the observations and the hybridisation of the central atom in the resulting species (if any).

Solution:

  • \(\mathrm{CCl}_4 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\): No reaction occurs. Carbon is a Period 2 element with a maximum covalence of 4 and no vacant d-orbitals to accept a lone pair from water.
  • \(\mathrm{SiCl}_4 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\): Vigorous hydrolysis occurs to give \(\mathrm{Si(OH)}_4\) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\) fumes. Silicon uses its vacant \(3d\) orbitals to accept a lone pair from \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) in the initial step, passing through a transient expanded-octet state before losing \(\mathrm{HCl}\); in the final \(\mathrm{Si(OH)}_4\), \(\mathrm{Si}\) is \(sp^3\) hybridised.
  • \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\): In neutral/alkaline water, \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3\) hydrolyses to tetrahedral \([\mathrm{Al(OH)}_4]^-\) (\(sp^3\)). In acidified aqueous solution, it forms the octahedral complex ion \([\mathrm{Al(H_2O)}_6]^{3+}\), in which \(\mathrm{Al}\) uses \(3d\) orbitals and is \(sp^3d^2\) hybridised.
Example 4: Counting Bonds, Rings, and Polymer Products in Boron and Carbon Chemistry

Problem: (a) How many 2c–2e and 3c–2e bonds are present in one molecule of \(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6\)? (b) If a student hydrolyses \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{SiCl}_3\) followed by condensation, what type of silicone polymer forms compared to \(\mathrm{(CH_3)}_2\mathrm{SiCl}_2\)?

Solution:

  1. In \(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6\): There are 4 terminal \(\mathrm{B–H}\) bonds, each a normal 2-centre 2-electron (2c–2e) bond, and 2 bridging \(\mathrm{B–H–B}\) banana bonds, each a 3-centre 2-electron (3c–2e) bond. (Total valence electrons = \(2(3) + 6(1) = 12\), distributed as \(4\times 2 + 2\times 2 = 12\).)
  2. Silicone polymerisation: Hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{(CH_3)}_2\mathrm{SiCl}_2\) gives \(\mathrm{(CH_3)}_2\mathrm{Si(OH)}_2\), which has two \(\mathrm{–OH}\) groups and condenses into a linear (straight-chain) silicone polymer. In contrast, \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{SiCl}_3\) hydrolyses to \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{Si(OH)}_3\) with three \(\mathrm{–OH}\) groups per silicon, enabling condensation in three directions to form a cross-linked 3D network silicone.

Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET Trap Alerts
  • \(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{BO}_3\) basicity: It is monobasic (accepts one \(\mathrm{OH}^-\)), NOT tribasic, and is a Lewis acid, not a Brønsted proton donor.
  • \(\mathrm{Ga}\) vs. \(\mathrm{Al}\) radius: \(\mathrm{Ga}\) (\(135\,\mathrm{pm}\)) < \(\mathrm{Al}\) (\(143\,\mathrm{pm}\)) due to poor \(3d\)-electron shielding.
  • \(\mathrm{CCl}_4\) vs. \(\mathrm{SiCl}_4\): \(\mathrm{CCl}_4\) is inert to water (no vacant \(d\)-orbitals), whereas \(\mathrm{SiCl}_4\) hydrolyses rapidly using vacant \(3d\) orbitals.
  • Thermodynamic stability of carbon: Graphite (\(\Delta_f H^\circ = 0\)) is more stable than diamond (\(+1.90\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{Cl}_6\) vs. \(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6\) bridges: \(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{Cl}_6\) has 3c–4e coordinate \(\mathrm{Cl}\) bridges; \(\mathrm{B}_2\mathrm{H}_6\) has 3c–2e \(\mathrm{H}\) banana bonds.
Concise Final Recap

Across Groups 13 and 14, chemical behaviour is governed by two overarching principles: (1) poor shielding by inner \(d\) and \(f\) electrons, which causes radius/ionisation anomalies at \(\mathrm{Ga}\) and \(\mathrm{Tl}/\mathrm{Pb}\) and drives the inert pair effect (stabilising \(\mathrm{Tl}^+\) and \(\mathrm{Pb}^{2+}\)); and (2) the absence of valence \(d\)-orbitals in Period 2 (\(\mathrm{B}\) and \(\mathrm{C}\)), restricting maximum covalence to 4, favouring \(p\pi\text{–}p\pi\) multiple bonds, and preventing hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{CCl}_4\) or formation of \([\mathrm{BF}_6]^{3-}\).
