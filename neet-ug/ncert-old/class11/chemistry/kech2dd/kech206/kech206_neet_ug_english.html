Hydrocarbons: Structure, Isomerism, and Reaction Mechanisms

A complete, exam-oriented study guide covering Alkanes, Alkenes, Alkynes, and Aromatic Hydrocarbons with worked examples and NEET UG practice assessment.

Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds composed exclusively of carbon and hydrogen atoms. Beyond serving as primary energy sources—such as liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), compressed natural gas (CNG), liquefied natural gas (LNG), petrol, diesel, and kerosene—they act as fundamental feedstocks for polymers (polythene, polypropene, polystyrene), solvents, dyes, and pharmaceuticals.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial
  • Apply IUPAC nomenclature rules and identify structural, chain, position, conformational, and geometrical isomers.
  • Compare carbon–carbon single, double, and triple bonds in terms of hybridisation, bond length, bond enthalpy, and reactivity.
  • Predict products of alkane preparation and reactions, including Wurtz coupling, decarboxylation, Kolbe's electrolysis, and free-radical halogenation.
  • Explain Markovnikov addition, the peroxide (Kharash) effect, reductive ozonolysis, and hydration of alkenes and alkynes.
  • Evaluate aromaticity using Hückel's \((4n+2)\pi\) rule and master the mechanism and directive effects of electrophilic aromatic substitution.

Classification & Bonding Foundations

Based on the nature of carbon–carbon bonding and ring topology, hydrocarbons are divided into three major classes: saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes and cycloalkanes containing only \(\sigma\) bonds), unsaturated hydrocarbons (alkenes with \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds and alkynes with \(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\) triple bonds), and aromatic hydrocarbons (arenes containing planar, conjugated cyclic \(\pi\) systems).

Alkanes: Saturated Hydrocarbons

Nomenclature, Carbon Degree, and Isomerism

Alkanes form a homologous series with the general formula \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2}\). Historically called paraffins (Latin: parum = little, affinis = affinity) due to their low reactivity toward acids, bases, and common oxidants, they feature tetrahedral \(sp^3\) carbons with \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond length \(154\,\mathrm{pm}\) and \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond length \(112\,\mathrm{pm}\).

  • Classification of Carbon Atoms: A carbon attached to zero or one other carbon is primary (\(1^\circ\)); attached to two carbons is secondary (\(2^\circ\)); attached to three carbons is tertiary (\(3^\circ\)); and attached to four carbons is quaternary (\(4^\circ\)).
  • Chain Isomerism: Methane, ethane, and propane have only one structural form. Butane (\(\mathrm{C}_4\mathrm{H}_{10}\)) has 2 chain isomers (butane and 2-methylpropane), pentane (\(\mathrm{C}_5\mathrm{H}_{12}\)) has 3 (pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane), hexane (\(\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{14}\)) has 5, and heptane (\(\mathrm{C}_7\mathrm{H}_{16}\)) has 9.
  • IUPAC Rules Recap: Select the longest continuous carbon chain, number from the end giving the lowest locant set to substituents, and list substituents alphabetically (ignoring multiplying prefixes like di, tri, sec-, tert-, while treating iso and neo as part of the fundamental substituent name).

Preparation of Alkanes

  1. Hydrogenation of Alkenes and Alkynes: Addition of \(\mathrm{H}_2\) over finely divided \(\mathrm{Pt}\), \(\mathrm{Pd}\) (room temperature), or \(\mathrm{Ni}\) (elevated temperature and pressure): \[ \mathrm{R-CH=CH_2 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} R-CH_2-CH_3} \]
  2. Reduction of Alkyl Halides: Alkyl chlorides, bromides, or iodides (not fluorides) react with \(\mathrm{Zn}\) and dilute \(\mathrm{HCl}\) to yield alkanes: \[ \mathrm{R-X + H_2 \xrightarrow{Zn,\,H^+} R-H + HX} \]
  3. Wurtz Reaction: Treating an alkyl halide with metallic \(\mathrm{Na}\) in dry ether produces a symmetrical alkane with an even number of carbons: \[ \mathrm{2\,R-X + 2\,Na \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} R-R + 2\,NaX} \] Limitation: Using two different alkyl halides (\(\mathrm{R-X}\) and \(\mathrm{R'-X}\)) yields a mixture of three alkanes (\(\mathrm{R-R}\), \(\mathrm{R'-R'}\), and \(\mathrm{R-R'}\)) that are difficult to separate; hence, Wurtz synthesis is unsuitable for odd-carbon alkanes.
  4. Soda-Lime Decarboxylation: Heating the sodium salt of a carboxylic acid with soda lime (\(\mathrm{NaOH + CaO}\)) eliminates \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) as \(\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}\), giving an alkane with one carbon fewer than the parent acid: \[ \mathrm{R-COO^-Na^+ + NaOH \xrightarrow{CaO,\,\Delta} R-H + Na_2CO_3} \]
  5. Kolbe's Electrolytic Method: Electrolysis of an aqueous solution of sodium or potassium carboxylate forms an even-carbon alkane and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) at the anode (via free-radical coupling) and \(\mathrm{H}_2\) gas with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at the cathode: \[ \mathrm{2\,CH_3COO^-Na^+ + 2\,H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{electrolysis}} CH_3-CH_3 + 2\,CO_2\uparrow + H_2\uparrow + 2\,NaOH} \] Key NEET Fact: Methane cannot be prepared by Kolbe's electrolysis because coupling of two alkyl radicals requires at least two carbons in the product.

Physical Properties & Chemical Reactions of Alkanes

Being non-polar with weak van der Waals dispersion forces, \(\mathrm{C_1\text{–}C_4}\) alkanes are gases, \(\mathrm{C_5\text{–}C_{17}}\) are liquids, and \(\mathrm{C_{18}}\) and above are solids at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). Boiling point increases steadily with molar mass. Among isomers, branching decreases boiling point because the molecule becomes more spherical, reducing surface area of contact and weakening intermolecular van der Waals forces (e.g., pentane \(309.1\,\mathrm{K} > \text{2-methylbutane } 300.9\,\mathrm{K} > \text{2,2-dimethylpropane } 282.5\,\mathrm{K}\)).

  • Free-Radical Halogenation: Occurs at \(573\text{–}773\,\mathrm{K}\) or under UV light (\(h\nu\)) via initiation, propagation, and termination steps.
    • Halogen reactivity: \(\mathrm{F_2 > Cl_2 > Br_2 > I_2}\) (fluorination is violently exothermic; iodination is slow and reversible, requiring oxidants like \(\mathrm{HIO_3}\) or \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) to consume \(\mathrm{HI}\)).
    • Ease of \(\mathrm{H}\)-abstraction: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\).
    • During chlorination of methane, trace ethane forms via termination coupling of two \(\cdot\mathrm{CH_3}\) radicals.
  • Complete & Incomplete Combustion: \[ \mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2} + \left(\frac{3n+1}{2}\right)\mathrm{O_2 \rightarrow} \; n\,\mathrm{CO_2} + (n+1)\mathrm{H_2O} \] Incomplete combustion in limited \(\mathrm{O_2}\) yields carbon black (used in inks and pigments).
  • Controlled Oxidation: Methane gives \(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\) with \(\mathrm{Cu}\) at \(523\,\mathrm{K}/100\,\mathrm{atm}\), and \(\mathrm{HCHO}\) with \(\mathrm{Mo_2O_3}/\Delta\). Ethane gives \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\) with manganese acetate. Alkanes containing a \(3^\circ\) hydrogen are oxidised by \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) to tertiary alcohols (e.g., 2-methylpropane \(\rightarrow\) 2-methylpropan-2-ol).
  • Isomerisation & Aromatisation: Heating \(n\)-alkanes with anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3/HCl}\) yields branched isomers. Heating \(n\)-alkanes with \(\ge 6\) carbons at \(773\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(10\text{–}20\,\mathrm{atm}\) over \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}\), \(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\), or \(\mathrm{Mo_2O_3}\) supported on alumina causes dehydrogenation and cyclisation to benzene or its homologues (e.g., \(n\)-hexane \(\rightarrow\) benzene; \(n\)-heptane \(\rightarrow\) toluene).
  • Steam Reforming & Pyrolysis: Methane reacts with steam over \(\mathrm{Ni}\) at \(1273\,\mathrm{K}\) to produce \(\mathrm{CO + 3H_2}\). Thermal cracking (pyrolysis) at \(773\text{–}973\,\mathrm{K}\) breaks higher alkanes into smaller alkanes and alkenes via free radicals.

Conformations of Ethane

Rotation around the \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\sigma\) bond (cylindrically symmetrical around the internuclear axis) generates infinite spatial arrangements called conformations or rotamers. Rotation is not completely free due to torsional strain—repulsion between adjacent \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond electron clouds—creating an energy barrier of \(12.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) in ethane. Because thermal collisions at room temperature easily overcome \(12.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), conformers cannot be isolated separately.

Alkenes: Unsaturated Hydrocarbons with Double Bonds

Structure & Geometrical (cis–trans) Isomerism

A carbon–carbon double bond (\(\mathrm{C=C}\), bond length \(134\,\mathrm{pm}\), total bond enthalpy \(681\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) consists of one strong \(\sigma\) bond (\(sp^2\text{–}sp^2\) head-on overlap, \(\approx 397\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) and one weaker \(\pi\) bond (sideways \(2p\text{–}2p\) overlap, \(\approx 284\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)). The exposed, loosely held \(\pi\) electron cloud makes alkenes nucleophilic toward electrophilic reagents.

Restricted rotation around the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond gives rise to geometrical isomerism whenever each doubly bonded carbon carries two different groups (types \(\mathrm{XYC=CXY}\), \(\mathrm{XYC=CXZ}\), or \(\mathrm{XYC=CZW}\)).

  • cis-Isomer: Identical groups lie on the same side of the double bond. It is generally more polar (higher dipole moment) and has a higher boiling point than the trans isomer.
  • trans-Isomer: Identical groups lie on opposite sides. In symmetrical alkenes like trans-but-2-ene, bond dipoles cancel (\(\mu = 0\,\mathrm{D}\)). Because trans isomers pack more efficiently in a crystal lattice, they typically have a higher melting point than cis isomers.

Preparation of Alkenes

  • Stereoselective Partial Reduction of Alkynes:
    • Lindlar's Catalyst (palladised charcoal partially poisoned with sulphur compounds or quinoline) + \(\mathrm{H_2}\) gives a cis-alkene.
    • Birch-type Reduction (\(\mathrm{Na}\) in liquid \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)) gives a trans-alkene.
  • Dehydrohalogenation (\(\beta\)-Elimination) of Alkyl Halides: Heating \(\mathrm{R-X}\) with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) eliminates \(\mathrm{HX}\) from adjacent \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) carbons. Rate order: \(\mathrm{I > Br > Cl}\) for halogens, and \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\) for alkyl groups.
  • Dehalogenation of Vicinal Dihalides: Heating a vicinal dihalide with \(\mathrm{Zn}\) dust removes \(\mathrm{ZnX_2}\) to form an alkene.
  • Acidic Dehydration of Alcohols: Heating an alcohol with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) eliminates \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) via \(\beta\)-elimination.

Alkynes: Triple-Bonded Hydrocarbons

Structure & Acidity of Terminal Alkynes

Alkynes (\(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}\)) contain a \(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\) triple bond (\(120\,\mathrm{pm}\), bond enthalpy \(823\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) formed by one \(sp\text{–}sp\) \(\sigma\) bond and two mutually perpendicular \(2p\text{–}2p\) \(\pi\) bonds, giving a linear geometry (\(180^\circ\)).

Why Are Terminal Alkynes Acidic?

An \(sp\)-hybridised carbon has 50% s-character (compared to 33.3% in \(sp^2\) alkenes and 25% in \(sp^3\) alkanes). Greater \(s\)-character pulls bonding electrons closer to the nucleus, making the \(sp\) carbon significantly more electronegative. Consequently, a terminal \(\equiv\mathrm{C-H}\) bond easily donates \(\mathrm{H^+}\) to strong bases like \(\mathrm{Na}\) or \(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\) (releasing \(\mathrm{H_2}\) or \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)). Internal alkynes (e.g., but-2-yne) lack a terminal \(\equiv\mathrm{C-H}\) proton and do not react.

\[ \text{Acidity order: } \mathrm{HC\equiv CH > CH_3-C\equiv CH \gg H_2C=CH_2 > CH_3-CH_3} \]

Preparation & Reactions of Alkynes

  • From Calcium Carbide: Limestone (\(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\)) \(\xrightarrow{\Delta}\) \(\mathrm{CaO} \xrightarrow{+3\mathrm{C}} \mathrm{CaC_2} \xrightarrow{+2\mathrm{H_2O}} \mathrm{C_2H_2 + Ca(OH)_2}\).
  • From Vicinal Dihalides: Treatment with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) forms a vinyl halide, which on further dehydrohalogenation with sodamide (\(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\)) yields an alkyne.
  • Electrophilic Additions: Two equivalents of \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{X_2}\), or \(\mathrm{HX}\) add sequentially. Addition of \(\mathrm{HX}\) to unsymmetrical alkynes follows Markovnikov's rule via the more stable vinylic cation to give geminal dihalides (e.g., propyne + \(2\,\mathrm{HBr} \rightarrow\) 2,2-dibromopropane).
  • Hydration (Kucherov-type): Warming with \(\mathrm{HgSO_4}\) and dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) at \(333\,\mathrm{K}\) adds one molecule of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) to form an unstable enol, which tautomerises to a carbonyl compound:
    • Ethyne \(\longrightarrow\) Ethanal (\(\mathrm{CH_3CHO}\))
    • Propyne (or higher terminal alkynes) \(\longrightarrow\) Ketone (e.g., propanone, \(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3}\))
  • Polymerisation: Linear polymerisation of ethyne gives conducting polyacetylene; passing ethyne through a red-hot iron tube at \(873\,\mathrm{K}\) causes cyclic trimerisation to benzene (\(\mathrm{C_6H_6}\)).

Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Arenes)

Structure of Benzene & Hückel's Rule of Aromaticity

Benzene (\(\mathrm{C_6H_6}\)) is a planar hexagon of six \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbons. X-ray diffraction confirms all six \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond lengths are identical at \(139\,\mathrm{pm}\)—intermediate between a single bond (\(154\,\mathrm{pm}\)) and a double bond (\(133\,\mathrm{pm}\)). The six unhybridised \(2p\) orbitals overlap laterally to form a continuous, delocalised \(\pi\)-electron cloud above and below the ring plane, conferring exceptional resonance stability and a preference for substitution over addition.

Hückel's Criteria for Aromaticity:
(1) Cyclic & Planar  •  (2) Complete \(\pi\)-conjugation around the ring  •  (3) \((4n + 2)\,\pi\) electrons (\(n = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\))

Examples satisfying Hückel's rule include benzene (\(6\pi\), \(n=1\)), cyclopentadienyl anion (\(6\pi\), \(n=1\)), cycloheptatrienyl/tropylium cation (\(6\pi\), \(n=1\)), naphthalene (\(10\pi\), \(n=2\)), anthracene (\(14\pi\), \(n=3\)), and phenanthrene (\(14\pi\), \(n=3\)).

Preparation & Electrophilic Substitution (\(S_\mathrm{E}\)) Mechanism

In the laboratory, benzene is prepared by: (i) cyclic polymerisation of ethyne at \(873\,\mathrm{K}\) in a red-hot iron tube, (ii) soda-lime decarboxylation of sodium benzoate, or (iii) reduction of phenol vapours over heated \(\mathrm{Zn}\) dust.

Electrophilic aromatic substitution proceeds in three universal steps:

  1. Generation of the Electrophile (\(\mathrm{E^+}\)): e.g., \(\mathrm{HNO_3 + H_2SO_4 \rightleftharpoons NO_2^+ + HSO_4^- + H_2O}\) (nitronium ion); \(\mathrm{Cl_2 + AlCl_3 \rightarrow Cl^+ + [AlCl_4]^-}\); \(\mathrm{R-Cl + AlCl_3 \rightarrow R^+ + [AlCl_4]^-}\).
  2. Formation of the Arenium Ion (\(\sigma\)-complex): Attack of \(\mathrm{E^+}\) converts one ring carbon to \(sp^3\), temporarily breaking aromaticity while the positive charge is resonance-stabilised over the remaining five carbons.
  3. Deprotonation: A base (\(\mathrm{[HSO_4]^-}\) or \(\mathrm{[AlCl_4]^-}\)) abstracts \(\mathrm{H^+}\) from the \(sp^3\) carbon, restoring the aromatic \(6\pi\) ring.
Crucial Exam Note on Friedel–Crafts Alkylation

When benzene reacts with 1-chloropropane in the presence of anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\), the initially formed primary propyl carbocation rearranges via a 1,2-hydride shift to the more stable secondary isopropyl carbocation, yielding isopropylbenzene (cumene) as the major product rather than \(n\)-propylbenzene.

Under vigorous conditions, benzene also undergoes addition: catalytic hydrogenation over \(\mathrm{Ni}\) at high temperature/pressure gives cyclohexane, and UV-initiated chlorination at \(500\,\mathrm{K}\) gives benzene hexachloride (BHC / gammaxane, \(\mathrm{C_6H_6Cl_6}\)).

Carcinogenicity and Toxicity

Benzene and fused polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) containing more than two fused rings—formed during incomplete combustion of tobacco, coal, and petroleum—are toxic and carcinogenic. Examples include 1,2-benzanthracene, 1,2-benzpyrene, 1,2,5,6-dibenzanthracene, 3-methylcholanthrene, and 9,10-dimethyl-1,2-benzanthracene; they undergo metabolic biochemical reactions that damage DNA.

Worked Examples

1. Identifying Carbon Degrees and IUPAC Naming of a Branched Alkane

Problem: Consider the hypothetical alkane \(\mathrm{CH_3-C(CH_3)_2-CH_2-CH(CH_3)-CH_2-CH_3}\). Determine its IUPAC name and count the number of primary (\(1^\circ\)), secondary (\(2^\circ\)), tertiary (\(3^\circ\)), and quaternary (\(4^\circ\)) carbon atoms.

Solution:

  • Longest Chain & Numbering: The longest continuous chain has 6 carbons (hexane). Numbering from the left gives substituents at C-2, C-2, and C-4 (sum \(= 8\)), which is lower than numbering from the right (C-3, C-5, C-5; sum \(= 13\)).
  • IUPAC Name: 2,2,4-Trimethylhexane.
  • Carbon Classification:
    • \(1^\circ\) carbons (terminal \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\) groups): 5 carbons (one at C-1, two methyl branches at C-2, one methyl branch at C-4, and one at C-6).
    • \(2^\circ\) carbons (attached to 2 carbons): 2 carbons (C-3 and C-5).
    • \(3^\circ\) carbons (attached to 3 carbons): 1 carbon (C-4).
    • \(4^\circ\) carbons (attached to 4 carbons): 1 carbon (C-2).
2. Predicting the Alkene Structure from Reductive Ozonolysis

Problem: An unknown alkene (assumed single double bond) undergoes reductive ozonolysis (\(\mathrm{O_3}\), followed by \(\mathrm{Zn/H_2O}\)) to yield an equimolar mixture of butan-2-one and propanal. Deduce the structure and IUPAC name of the alkene.

Solution:

Write the two carbonyl products with their \(\mathrm{C=O}\) groups facing each other, remove the oxygen atoms, and join the carbonyl carbons with a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond:

\[ \mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-C(CH_3)=O} \;+\; \mathrm{O=CH-CH_2-CH_3} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-C(CH_3)=CH-CH_2-CH_3} \]

The longest chain containing the double bond has 6 carbons (hex-3-ene), with a methyl group at C-3. Thus, the alkene is 3-methylhex-3-ene.

3. Markovnikov Addition vs. Peroxide Effect

Problem: Predict the major organic product when but-1-ene reacts with: (a) \(\mathrm{HBr}\) in the absence of peroxide, (b) \(\mathrm{HBr}\) in the presence of benzoyl peroxide, and (c) \(\mathrm{HCl}\) in the presence of benzoyl peroxide.

Solution:

  • (a) \(\mathrm{HBr}\) without peroxide: Proceeds via ionic Markovnikov addition. Protonation forms the more stable \(2^\circ\) carbocation (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2\overset{+}{C}HCH_3}\)), yielding 2-bromobutane.
  • (b) \(\mathrm{HBr}\) with benzoyl peroxide: Proceeds via free-radical Anti-Markovnikov addition. \(\cdot\mathrm{Br}\) attacks C-1 to form the more stable \(2^\circ\) radical (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2\dot{C}HCH_2Br}\)), yielding 1-bromobutane.
  • (c) \(\mathrm{HCl}\) with benzoyl peroxide: The peroxide effect does not operate with \(\mathrm{HCl}\) (the \(\mathrm{H-Cl}\) bond is too strong to undergo endothermic radical abstraction by the carbon radical). Hence, normal ionic Markovnikov addition occurs, giving 2-chlorobutane.
4. Evaluating Aromaticity Using Hückel's Rule

Problem: Classify each of the following cyclic species as aromatic or non-aromatic: (i) cyclohexa-1,3-diene, (ii) cyclopentadienyl anion (\(\mathrm{C_5H_5^-}\)), and (iii) cyclooctatetraene (\(\mathrm{C_8H_8}\)).

Solution:

  • (i) Cyclohexa-1,3-diene: Contains two \(sp^3\)-hybridised \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) carbons, breaking continuous \(\pi\)-conjugation around the ring. It is non-aromatic.
  • (ii) Cyclopentadienyl anion: Planar, fully conjugated 5-membered ring with 4 \(\pi\) electrons from two double bonds plus 2 \(\pi\) electrons from the carbanion lone pair \(= 6\,\pi\) electrons (\(4n+2\) for \(n=1\)). It is aromatic.
  • (iii) Cyclooctatetraene: Possesses \(8\,\pi\) electrons (a \(4n\) system, not \(4n+2\)) and adopts a non-planar tub shape. It is non-aromatic.
5. Designing Multi-Step Aromatic Conversions

Problem: How can benzene be converted into (a) p-nitrobromobenzene and (b) m-nitrobromobenzene? Explain why the order of reagents differs.

Solution:

  • (a) To obtain p-nitrobromobenzene: Introduce \(-\mathrm{Br}\) first (which is \(o/p\)-directing), then nitrate: \[ \mathrm{C_6H_6 \xrightarrow{Br_2 / FeBr_3} C_6H_5Br \xrightarrow{Conc.\,HNO_3 + Conc.\,H_2SO_4} \text{\textit{p}-O_2N-C_6H_4-Br} \;(\text{major, separated from \textit{o}-isomer})} \]
  • (b) To obtain m-nitrobromobenzene: Introduce \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) first (which is \(m\)-directing), then brominate: \[ \mathrm{C_6H_6 \xrightarrow{Conc.\,HNO_3 + Conc.\,H_2SO_4} C_6H_5NO_2 \xrightarrow{Br_2 / FeBr_3} \text{\textit{m}-O_2N-C_6H_4-Br}} \]

NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: The following are original practice questions designed at the NEET UG standard.

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ
Which of the following isomeric pentanes has the lowest boiling point?
  • (A) Pentane
  • (B) 2-Methylbutane
  • (C) 2,2-Dimethylpropane
  • (D) All three have identical boiling points
Answer: (C)
Explanation: All three are chain isomers of \(\mathrm{C_5H_{12}}\) with the same molar mass. As branching increases, the molecule becomes more spherical, decreasing surface area and weakening intermolecular van der Waals forces. Thus, 2,2-dimethylpropane (neopentane) has the lowest boiling point (\(282.5\,\mathrm{K}\)).
Practice Question 2 • Single-Correct MCQ
Sodium butanoate on heating with soda lime (\(\mathrm{NaOH + CaO}\)) predominantly produces:
  • (A) Butane
  • (B) Propane
  • (C) Hexane
  • (D) Ethane
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Soda-lime decarboxylation removes one carbon as \(\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}\): \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2COONa + NaOH \xrightarrow{CaO,\,\Delta} CH_3CH_2CH_3 + Na_2CO_3}\). Thus, sodium butanoate (4 carbons) gives propane (3 carbons).

Common Pitfalls & Exam Revision Checklist

Frequent Mistakes to Avoid in NEET UG
  • Peroxide Effect Scope: Remember that Anti-Markovnikov radical addition in peroxides occurs only with \(\mathrm{HBr}\), never with \(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{HI}\).
  • Halogens on Benzene: Although \(-\mathrm{Cl}\) and \(-\mathrm{Br}\) are ortho/para-directing, they are deactivating groups (slower than benzene), not activating groups.
  • Kolbe's Electrolysis: Cannot be used to prepare methane; always yields an alkane with at least two carbons at the anode.
  • Wurtz Reaction: Only reliable for symmetrical alkanes with an even number of carbons; mixed halides give an inseparable mixture of three alkanes.
  • Distinguish \(1^\circ, 2^\circ, 3^\circ, 4^\circ\) carbons and apply alphabetical IUPAC numbering correctly.
  • Recall the \(12.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) torsional barrier in ethane and why staggered is more stable than eclipsed.
  • Identify cis–trans isomerism eligibility and compare dipole moments (cis > trans) and melting points (trans > cis).
  • Reconstruct alkenes from reductive ozonolysis products and predict carbonyl products from alkyne hydration.
  • Verify aromaticity using Hückel's \((4n+2)\pi\) electron rule and planarity/conjugation criteria.
  • Predict major products of electrophilic aromatic substitution using \(o/p\) vs. \(m\) directive effects.

Concise Final Recap

Alkanes (\(sp^3\), \(154\,\mathrm{pm}\)) are saturated and undergo free-radical substitution, combustion, and aromatisation. Alkenes (\(sp^2\), \(134\,\mathrm{pm}\)) and alkynes (\(sp\), \(120\,\mathrm{pm}\)) are unsaturated, undergoing electrophilic additions governed by carbocation stability (Markovnikov's rule), with terminal alkynes showing distinct acidity. Arenes (\(sp^2\), \(139\,\mathrm{pm}\)) owe their stability to \((4n+2)\pi\) electron delocalisation and characteristically undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution directed by existing ring substituents.
