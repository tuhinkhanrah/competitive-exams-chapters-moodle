Redox Reactions

Electron Transfer, Oxidation States, Reaction Balancing, Titrations, and Electrode Potentials

Learning Objectives and Overview

Chemical transformations that power biological respiration, industrial metal extraction, batteries, and environmental cycles rely on the simultaneous transfer of electrons between reactants. Such coupled processes are called redox reactions (short for reduction–oxidation reactions). For NEET UG, this topic bridges stoichiometry, chemical bonding, and electrochemistry.

What You Will Master
  • Distinguishing oxidation, reduction, oxidising agents (oxidants), and reducing agents (reductants) across classical, electronic, and oxidation-number frameworks.
  • Assigning oxidation numbers accurately, writing Stock notation, and resolving fractional oxidation states using molecular structure.
  • Classifying redox processes into combination, decomposition, displacement, and disproportionation reactions.
  • Balancing ionic redox equations in acidic and basic media using both the oxidation-number method and the half-reaction method.
  • Applying redox stoichiometry to titrations and interpreting standard electrode potentials (\(E^\circ\)) to predict reaction feasibility.

Three Perspectives on Oxidation and Reduction

Chemistry describes redox processes at three progressively deeper levels. Each level remains useful depending on whether a reaction involves ionic solids, covalent molecules, or aqueous ions.

1. Classical Concept

Originally, oxidation referred to the combination of a substance with oxygen or any other electronegative element (such as fluorine, chlorine, or sulphur), or the removal of hydrogen or an electropositive element from a compound. Conversely, reduction referred to the removal of oxygen or an electronegative element, or the addition of hydrogen or an electropositive element.

For example, when hydrogen sulphide reacts with chlorine gas:

\[ \mathrm{H_2S(g)} + \mathrm{Cl_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{HCl(g)} + \mathrm{S(s)} \]

Hydrogen sulphide is oxidised because electropositive hydrogen is removed from sulphur, while chlorine is reduced because hydrogen is added to it. Because one species cannot lose hydrogen or gain oxygen without another species accepting or donating it, oxidation and reduction always occur together.

2. Electronic (Electron-Transfer) Concept

For ionic reactions, redox is best understood as a complete transfer of electrons. The overall change can be split into two half-reactions: an oxidation half-reaction (electron loss) and a reduction half-reaction (electron gain).

  • Oxidation: Loss of one or more electrons by a chemical species.
  • Reduction: Gain of one or more electrons by a chemical species.
  • Oxidising agent (Oxidant): Electron acceptor; it oxidises another species and gets reduced itself.
  • Reducing agent (Reductant): Electron donor; it reduces another species and gets oxidised itself.

Consider the synthesis of sodium hydride from its elements:

\[ 2\,\mathrm{Na(s)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Na^+H^-(s)} \]

Here, each sodium atom loses one electron (\(2\,\mathrm{Na} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Na^+} + 2\,\mathrm{e^-}\)) and acts as the reductant, whereas dihydrogen gains those electrons (\(\mathrm{H_2} + 2\,\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{H^-}\)) and acts as the oxidant.

3. Oxidation Number Concept

In covalent reactions such as \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)} + 2\,\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\), electrons are shared unequally rather than completely transferred. To track electron shifts systematically, chemists assign an oxidation number (or oxidation state) to each atom by treating every covalent bond as if its bonding pair belonged entirely to the more electronegative atom.

Oxidation Gain of O / electronegative atom; loss of H / electropositive atom Loss of electrons Increase in oxidation number
Reduction Loss of O / electronegative atom; gain of H / electropositive atom Gain of electrons Decrease in oxidation number

Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers and Exceptions

Use the following hierarchy of rules to determine the oxidation state of any atom in a molecule or polyatomic ion:

  1. Free elements: Every atom in an uncombined element (\(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_3}\), \(\mathrm{P_4}\), \(\mathrm{S_8}\), \(\mathrm{Fe}\)) has an oxidation number of \(0\).
  2. Monoatomic ions: The oxidation number equals the ionic charge (e.g., \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\) is \(+2\); \(\mathrm{N^{3-}}\) is \(-3\)). In compounds, alkali metals (Group 1) are always \(+1\), alkaline earth metals (Group 2) are always \(+2\), and aluminium is \(+3\).
  3. Hydrogen: Usually \(+1\), except in binary metal hydrides (such as \(\mathrm{NaH}\), \(\mathrm{LiH}\), \(\mathrm{CaH_2}\)), where hydrogen is \(-1\).
  4. Oxygen: Usually \(-2\), with three key exceptions:
    • Peroxides (\(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\), \(\mathrm{Na_2O_2}\), \(\mathrm{BaO_2}\)): each O is \(-1\).
    • Superoxides (\(\mathrm{KO_2}\), \(\mathrm{RbO_2}\)): each O is \(-\frac{1}{2}\).
    • Oxygen fluorides: in \(\mathrm{OF_2}\), O is \(+2\); in \(\mathrm{O_2F_2}\), O is \(+1\) (since fluorine is more electronegative than oxygen).
  5. Halogens: Fluorine is always \(-1\) in all compounds. Chlorine, bromine, and iodine are \(-1\) in binary halides, but exhibit positive oxidation states (up to \(+7\)) when bonded to oxygen or a more electronegative halogen (as in oxoacids and interhalogens).
  6. Charge balance: The algebraic sum of oxidation numbers of all atoms equals \(0\) for a neutral compound and equals the net charge for a polyatomic ion.

Stock Notation

To indicate the oxidation state of a metal unambiguously in a compound name or formula, Alfred Stock introduced Roman numerals in parentheses immediately after the metal symbol. For example, \(\mathrm{FeCl_2}\) is written as \(\mathrm{Fe(II)Cl_2}\), \(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}\) as \(\mathrm{Fe_2(III)O_3}\), \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\) as \(\mathrm{Cu_2(I)O}\), and \(\mathrm{MnO_2}\) as \(\mathrm{Mn(IV)O_2}\).

Fractional Oxidation Numbers: Average vs. Structural Reality

Standard algebraic rules sometimes yield a fractional oxidation number—for example, \(+\frac{4}{3}\) for carbon in \(\mathrm{C_3O_2}\), \(+2.5\) for sulphur in \(\mathrm{S_4O_6^{2-}}\), \(+\frac{16}{3}\) for bromine in \(\mathrm{Br_3O_8}\), or \(+\frac{8}{3}\) for iron in \(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\). Because electrons cannot be transferred in fractions, a fractional value simply represents the average oxidation state of chemically non-equivalent atoms of the same element.

Mixed oxides such as \(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\) (\(\mathrm{FeO \cdot Fe_2O_3}\)) and \(\mathrm{Pb_3O_4}\) (\(2\,\mathrm{PbO \cdot PbO_2}\)) contain the metal in two distinct integer states (\(+2\) and \(+3\) for Fe; \(+2\) and \(+4\) for Pb). Similarly, covalent structures resolve the true integer states of carbon suboxide and the tetrathionate ion.

Classification of Redox Reactions

Redox reactions fall into four major structural categories:

  1. Combination Reactions (\(\mathrm{A + B \rightarrow C}\)): At least one reactant must be in the elemental state. All combustion processes, such as \(\mathrm{C(s)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\), and metal–nonmetal syntheses, such as \(3\,\mathrm{Mg(s)} + \mathrm{N_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mg_3N_2(s)}\), are combination redox reactions.
  2. Decomposition Reactions (\(\mathrm{C \rightarrow A + B}\)): A compound breaks down into two or more products, at least one of which is an element (e.g., \(2\,\mathrm{KClO_3(s)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\,\mathrm{KCl(s)} + 3\,\mathrm{O_2(g)}\)). Note that not every decomposition is redox: \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{CaO(s)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\) shows no change in oxidation numbers (\(\mathrm{Ca}: +2, \mathrm{C}: +4, \mathrm{O}: -2\)).
  3. Displacement Reactions (\(\mathrm{X + YZ \rightarrow XZ + Y}\)):
    • Metal displacement: A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt or oxide (e.g., \(\mathrm{Zn(s)} + \mathrm{CuSO_4(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnSO_4(aq)} + \mathrm{Cu(s)}\); \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3(s)} + 2\,\mathrm{Al(s)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{Al_2O_3(s)} + 2\,\mathrm{Cr(s)}\)).
    • Non-metal displacement: Alkali metals and heavier alkaline earth metals (\(\mathrm{Ca, Sr, Ba}\)) displace \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from cold water; \(\mathrm{Mg}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe}\) react with steam; metals above hydrogen in the activity series (\(\mathrm{Zn, Fe, Mg}\)) displace \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from dilute acids, whereas \(\mathrm{Cu, Ag, Au}\) do not. Among halogens, oxidising power decreases down Group 17 (\(\mathrm{F_2 > Cl_2 > Br_2 > I_2}\)), so \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) displaces \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) and \(\mathrm{I^-}\) from aqueous solutions (basis of the qualitative layer test). Fluorine is so strongly oxidising that it oxidises water itself to \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and therefore cannot be generated by chemical oxidation of \(\mathrm{F^-}\).
  4. Disproportionation Reactions: A single element in an intermediate oxidation state is simultaneously oxidised to a higher state and reduced to a lower state. Thus, the element must possess at least three accessible oxidation states.
    \[ \overset{0}{\mathrm{Cl_2}}(\mathrm{g}) + 2\,\mathrm{OH^-}(\mathrm{aq}) \rightarrow \overset{+1}{\mathrm{ClO^-}}(\mathrm{aq}) + \overset{-1}{\mathrm{Cl^-}}(\mathrm{aq}) + \mathrm{H_2O}(\mathrm{l}) \]

    Key NEET UG Exception: Fluorine cannot disproportionate because it is the most electronegative element and cannot adopt a positive oxidation state; with alkali it forms \(\mathrm{F^-}\) and \(\mathrm{OF_2}\) (where oxygen is oxidised to \(+2\)). Likewise, a species already in its maximum oxidation state (such as \(\mathrm{ClO_4^-}\) with \(\mathrm{Cl} = +7\) or \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) with \(\mathrm{Mn} = +7\)) cannot disproportionate.

Balancing Redox Equations and Redox Titrations

Method A: Oxidation Number Method

  1. Write the skeletal ionic equation and assign oxidation numbers to identify the atoms undergoing oxidation and reduction.
  2. Calculate the total increase and total decrease in oxidation number per formula unit. Multiply the oxidant and reductant by integers so that total increase equals total decrease.
  3. Balance all atoms other than O and H.
  4. Balance net ionic charge by adding \(\mathrm{H^+}\) (acidic medium) or \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) (basic medium) to the appropriate side.
  5. Balance H and O atoms by adding \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) molecules; verify that oxygen atoms match on both sides.

Method B: Half-Reaction (Ion-Electron) Method

  1. Split the skeletal equation into an oxidation half-reaction and a reduction half-reaction.
  2. In each half-reaction, balance atoms other than O and H.
  3. Balance O atoms by adding \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), then balance H atoms by adding \(\mathrm{H^+}\). (If the medium is basic, add one \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) to both sides for every \(\mathrm{H^+}\) present, and combine \(\mathrm{H^+ + OH^- \rightarrow H_2O}\).)
  4. Balance charge in each half-reaction by adding electrons (\(\mathrm{e^-}\)).
  5. Multiply the half-reactions so that electrons gained equal electrons lost, add them together, and cancel common terms.

Redox Titrations and Indicators

Just as acid–base titrations locate the equivalence point using pH indicators, redox titrations track the stoichiometric completion of electron transfer:

  • Self-indicator: Deep purple \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) oxidises \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) or \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\) to colourless products. A single drop of excess \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) (\(\sim 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) imparts a persistent pale pink tint.
  • External/internal redox indicator: Potassium dichromate (\(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\)) is not a self-indicator; it oxidises diphenylamine immediately after the equivalence point to produce an intense blue colour.
  • Iodometric titration: Oxidants such as \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) liberate \(\mathrm{I_2}\) from \(\mathrm{KI}\) (\(2\,\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + 4\,\mathrm{I^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu_2I_2(s)} + \mathrm{I_2}\)). The liberated \(\mathrm{I_2}\) forms a deep blue complex with starch and is titrated against standard thiosulphate (\(\mathrm{I_2} + 2\,\mathrm{S_2O_3^{2-}} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{I^-} + \mathrm{S_4O_6^{2-}}\)) until the blue colour disappears.

Competitive Electron Transfer and Electrode Processes

When a zinc strip is placed directly into aqueous \(\mathrm{Cu(NO_3)_2}\), \(\mathrm{Zn}\) reduces \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) to metallic copper while heat is released. If the two half-reactions are separated into different beakers—a \(\mathrm{Zn}\) rod in \(\mathrm{ZnSO_4(aq)}\) and a \(\mathrm{Cu}\) rod in \(\mathrm{CuSO_4(aq)}\)—and connected externally by a metallic wire and internally by a salt bridge (a U-tube filled with \(\mathrm{KCl}\) or \(\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}\) in agar-agar gel), electrons flow through the wire, converting chemical energy into electrical energy. This device is a Daniell cell (a type of galvanic cell).

Daniell cell separating the \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}/Zn}\) oxidation half-cell from the \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}\) reduction half-cell.

Standard Electrode Potential (\(E^\circ\))

A redox couple pairs the oxidised and reduced forms of a species at an electrode interface (written as \(\text{Oxidised}/\text{Reduced}\), e.g., \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}/Zn}\)). At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), \(1\,\mathrm{M}\) ionic concentration, and \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) gas pressure, the reduction potential measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (\(E^\circ_{\mathrm{H^+/H_2}} = 0.00\,\mathrm{V}\)) is the standard electrode potential (\(E^\circ\)).

  • More negative \(E^\circ\): Stronger tendency for the reduced form to lose electrons \(\Rightarrow\) stronger reducing agent (e.g., \(\mathrm{Li}\), \(\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{Zn}\)).
  • More positive \(E^\circ\): Stronger tendency for the oxidised form to gain electrons \(\Rightarrow\) stronger oxidising agent (e.g., \(\mathrm{F_2}\), \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\), \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)).
  • Reaction feasibility: For any galvanic combination, \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}\). A positive \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}}\) indicates a spontaneous forward redox reaction.
Reduction Half-Reaction (at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)) \(E^\circ\) (\(\mathrm{V}\)) Relative Behaviour
\(\mathrm{F_2(g)} + 2\,\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{F^-(aq)}\) \(+2.87\) Strongest oxidant
\(\mathrm{MnO_4^-(aq)} + 8\,\mathrm{H^+(aq)} + 5\,\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mn^{2+}(aq)} + 4\,\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\) \(+1.51\) Strong oxidant (acidic)
\(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)} + 2\,\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)}\) \(+1.36\) Oxidises \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) and \(\mathrm{I^-}\)
\(\mathrm{Ag^+(aq)} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Ag(s)}\) \(+0.80\) Weaker reductant than \(\mathrm{H_2}\)
\(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)} + 2\,\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu(s)}\) \(+0.34\) Does not displace \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from acids
\(2\,\mathrm{H^+(aq)} + 2\,\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2(g)}\) \(0.00\) Reference electrode (SHE)
\(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}(aq)} + 2\,\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe(s)}\) \(-0.44\) Displaces \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from acids
\(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)} + 2\,\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn(s)}\) \(-0.76\) Stronger reductant than \(\mathrm{Fe}\)
\(\mathrm{Li^+(aq)} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Li(s)}\) \(-3.05\) Strongest reductant in aqueous solution

Worked Examples

Determining True Oxidation States from Molecular Structure

Problem: Calculate the oxidation number of sulphur in peroxodisulphuric acid (\(\mathrm{H_2S_2O_8}\)) and explain why the simple algebraic formula gives an unrealistic value.

Solution: If all eight oxygen atoms are assumed to be \(-2\), the algebraic sum gives:

\[ 2(+1) + 2x + 8(-2) = 0 \implies 2x = +14 \implies x = +7 \]

Sulphur belongs to Group 16 and has only six valence electrons, so its oxidation state cannot exceed \(+6\). Structurally, \(\mathrm{H_2S_2O_8}\) contains one peroxide linkage (\(-\mathrm{O}-\mathrm{O}-\)) bridging the two \(\mathrm{SO_3H}\) groups. Therefore, two oxygen atoms have an oxidation number of \(-1\), while the remaining six oxygen atoms have \(-2\):

\[ 2(+1) + 2x + 2(-1) + 6(-2) = 0 \implies 2 + 2x - 2 - 12 = 0 \implies x = +6 \]

Thus, each sulphur atom is in the \(+6\) oxidation state.

Identifying Disproportionation and Dual Roles

Problem: In acidic solution, manganate(VI) ions convert spontaneously as follows:

\[ 3\,\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}(aq)} + 4\,\mathrm{H^+(aq)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{MnO_4^-(aq)} + \mathrm{MnO_2(s)} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \]

Classify this redox reaction and identify the oxidant and reductant.

Solution: Assigning oxidation numbers to manganese:

  • In \(\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}\): \(x + 4(-2) = -2 \implies x = +6\).
  • In \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\): \(x + 4(-2) = -1 \implies x = +7\) (oxidation).
  • In \(\mathrm{MnO_2}\): \(x + 2(-2) = 0 \implies x = +4\) (reduction).

Because manganese in the \(+6\) intermediate state is simultaneously oxidised to \(+7\) and reduced to \(+4\), this is a disproportionation reaction. Consequently, \(\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}\) serves as both the oxidising agent and the reducing agent.

Balancing a Redox Equation in Basic Medium

Problem: Balance the following ionic reaction occurring in basic medium using the half-reaction method:

\[ \mathrm{Cr(OH)_3(s)} + \mathrm{IO_3^-(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}(aq)} + \mathrm{I^-(aq)} \]

Solution:

  1. Oxidation half-reaction (\(\mathrm{Cr}: +3 \rightarrow +6\)):
    Balance O with \(\mathrm{H_2O}\): \(\mathrm{Cr(OH)_3 + H_2O \rightarrow CrO_4^{2-}}\)
    Balance H with \(\mathrm{H^+}\): \(\mathrm{Cr(OH)_3 + H_2O \rightarrow CrO_4^{2-} + 5\,H^+}\)
    Add \(5\,\mathrm{OH^-}\) to both sides and simplify \(\mathrm{H^+ + OH^- \rightarrow H_2O}\):
    \(\mathrm{Cr(OH)_3 + 5\,OH^- \rightarrow CrO_4^{2-} + 4\,H_2O + 3\,e^-}\)
  2. Reduction half-reaction (\(\mathrm{I}: +5 \rightarrow -1\)):
    Balance O and H: \(\mathrm{IO_3^- + 6\,H^+ \rightarrow I^- + 3\,H_2O}\)
    Add \(6\,\mathrm{OH^-}\) to both sides and simplify:
    \(\mathrm{IO_3^- + 3\,H_2O + 6\,e^- \rightarrow I^- + 6\,OH^-}\)
  3. Equalise electrons and add: Multiply the oxidation half by \(2\) to transfer \(6\,\mathrm{e^-}\), then add both half-reactions and cancel \(6\,\mathrm{OH^-}\) and \(3\,\mathrm{H_2O}\):
\[ 2\,\mathrm{Cr(OH)_3(s)} + \mathrm{IO_3^-(aq)} + 4\,\mathrm{OH^-(aq)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}(aq)} + \mathrm{I^-(aq)} + 5\,\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \]

Atom and charge check: \(\mathrm{Cr}\) (\(2=2\)), \(\mathrm{I}\) (\(1=1\)), \(\mathrm{O}\) (\(13=13\)), \(\mathrm{H}\) (\(10=10\)), and net charge (\(-5 = -5\)).

Predicting Reaction Feasibility from Electrode Potentials

Problem: Consider three hypothetical metals \(\mathrm{A}\), \(\mathrm{B}\), and \(\mathrm{C}\) with assumed standard reduction potentials at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \(E^\circ(\mathrm{A^{2+}/A}) = -1.18\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ(\mathrm{B^{2+}/B}) = +0.28\,\mathrm{V}\), and \(E^\circ(\mathrm{C^{+}/C}) = +0.85\,\mathrm{V}\). Arrange the metals in increasing order of reducing power, and determine whether \(\mathrm{B}\) can reduce \(\mathrm{C^+}\) to \(\mathrm{C}\).

Solution: A more negative \(E^\circ\) corresponds to a stronger reducing agent. Since \(-1.18\,\mathrm{V} < +0.28\,\mathrm{V} < +0.85\,\mathrm{V}\), the increasing order of reducing power is:

\[ \mathrm{C} < \mathrm{B} < \mathrm{A} \]

For the proposed reaction \(\mathrm{B(s)} + 2\,\mathrm{C^+(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{B^{2+}(aq)} + 2\,\mathrm{C(s)}\), \(\mathrm{B}\) is oxidised (anode) and \(\mathrm{C^+}\) is reduced (cathode):

\[ E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = +0.85\,\mathrm{V} - (+0.28\,\mathrm{V}) = +0.57\,\mathrm{V} > 0 \]

Because \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0\), the reaction is thermodynamically feasible.

Applying Stoichiometry to a Permanganate Titration

Problem: As an illustrative calculation, assume \(25.0\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.20\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{FeSO_4}\) solution is titrated in acidic medium against a \(0.050\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) solution. What volume of \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) is required to reach the equivalence point?

Solution: The balanced ionic equation in acidic medium is:

\[ \mathrm{MnO_4^-(aq)} + 5\,\mathrm{Fe^{2+}(aq)} + 8\,\mathrm{H^+(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mn^{2+}(aq)} + 5\,\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq)} + 4\,\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \]

Millimoles of \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) present \(= 25.0\,\mathrm{mL} \times 0.20\,\mathrm{mmol\,mL^{-1}} = 5.0\,\mathrm{mmol}\).
From the \(1:5\) mole ratio, millimoles of \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) required \(= \dfrac{5.0}{5} = 1.0\,\mathrm{mmol}\).
Volume of \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) required \(= \dfrac{1.0\,\mathrm{mmol}}{0.050\,\mathrm{mmol\,mL^{-1}}} = 20.0\,\mathrm{mL}\).

NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are designed to reflect NEET UG single-correct, numerical, statement-based, and assertion–reason formats. Attempt each question independently before checking the worked solution.

Practice Question 1 (Oxidation State & Structure)

What is the true oxidation state of chromium in chromium(VI) peroxide, \(\mathrm{CrO_5}\)?

  • (A) \(+10\)
  • (B) \(+6\)
  • (C) \(+5\)
  • (D) \(+4\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: \(\mathrm{CrO_5}\) has a butterfly-like structure containing one oxo ligand (\(\mathrm{Cr=O}\), where \(\mathrm{O}\) is \(-2\)) and two peroxo linkages (\(-\mathrm{O}-\mathrm{O}-\), giving four \(\mathrm{O}\) atoms at \(-1\) each). Letting \(x\) be the oxidation state of \(\mathrm{Cr}\): \(x + (-2) + 4(-1) = 0 \implies x = +6\).

Practice Question 2 (Identifying Non-Redox Reactions)

Which of the following chemical equations does not represent a redox reaction?

  • (A) \(2\,\mathrm{KClO_3(s)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{KCl(s)} + 3\,\mathrm{O_2(g)}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Zn(s)} + 2\,\mathrm{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnCl_2(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{BaCl_2(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{BaSO_4(s)} + 2\,\mathrm{HCl(aq)}\)
  • (D) \(2\,\mathrm{NaH(s)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Na(s)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: In \(\mathrm{BaCl_2 + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow BaSO_4 + 2\,HCl}\), every element retains its oxidation state (\(\mathrm{Ba}: +2\), \(\mathrm{Cl}: -1\), \(\mathrm{H}: +1\), \(\mathrm{S}: +6\), \(\mathrm{O}: -2\)). It is a double-displacement precipitation reaction, not a redox process.

Practice Question 3 (Disproportionation Eligibility)

Which of the following species cannot undergo disproportionation?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{ClO_2^-}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\)
Answer: (D)

Explanation: Disproportionation requires an element to be in an intermediate oxidation state so it can be both oxidised and reduced. In \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\), manganese is already in its highest possible oxidation state (\(+7\)) and cannot be oxidised further.

Practice Question 4 (Balancing Coefficients)

In the balanced acidic-medium ionic equation: \[ a\,\mathrm{MnO_4^-(aq)} + b\,\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}(aq)} + c\,\mathrm{H^+(aq)} \rightarrow d\,\mathrm{Mn^{2+}(aq)} + e\,\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + f\,\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \] what are the smallest whole-number values of \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\)?

  • (A) \(a = 2,\; b = 5,\; c = 16\)
  • (B) \(a = 2,\; b = 5,\; c = 8\)
  • (C) \(a = 1,\; b = 5,\; c = 8\)
  • (D) \(a = 5,\; b = 2,\; c = 16\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) (\(\mathrm{Mn}: +7 \rightarrow +2\)) gains \(5\,\mathrm{e^-}\), while \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\) (\(\mathrm{C}: +3 \rightarrow +4\) for two C atoms) loses \(2\,\mathrm{e^-}\). Multiplying by \(2\) and \(5\) respectively balances the electrons at \(10\,\mathrm{e^-}\): \(2\,\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 5\,\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}} + 16\,\mathrm{H^+} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + 10\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 8\,\mathrm{H_2O}\). Thus \(a=2\), \(b=5\), \(c=16\).

Practice Question 5 (Stock Notation)

Which of the following pairs of chemical formula and Stock notation is incorrectly matched?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}\) — \(\mathrm{Fe_2(III)O_3}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{SnCl_4}\) — \(\mathrm{Sn(IV)Cl_4}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\) — \(\mathrm{Cu_2(II)O}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{MnO_2}\) — \(\mathrm{Mn(IV)O_2}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: In \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\), two copper atoms balance one oxide ion (\(-2\)), so each copper atom has an oxidation state of \(+1\). The correct Stock notation is \(\mathrm{Cu_2(I)O}\), not \(\mathrm{Cu_2(II)O}\).

Practice Question 6 (Halogen Displacement Series)

Which of the following displacement reactions will not occur spontaneously in aqueous solution?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)} + 2\,\mathrm{Br^-(aq)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)} + \mathrm{Br_2(l)}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Br_2(l)} + 2\,\mathrm{I^-(aq)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Br^-(aq)} + \mathrm{I_2(s)}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)} + 2\,\mathrm{I^-(aq)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)} + \mathrm{I_2(s)}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{I_2(s)} + 2\,\mathrm{Br^-(aq)} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{I^-(aq)} + \mathrm{Br_2(l)}\)
Answer: (D)

Explanation: Oxidising power among halogens follows \(\mathrm{F_2 > Cl_2 > Br_2 > I_2}\). A halogen can displace only halides of elements below it in Group 17. Since \(\mathrm{I_2}\) is a weaker oxidant than \(\mathrm{Br_2}\), it cannot oxidise \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) to \(\mathrm{Br_2}\).

Practice Question 7 (Reducing Power from \(E^\circ\))

Given the assumed standard reduction potentials at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \(E^\circ(\mathrm{P^{2+}/P}) = -2.37\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Q^{2+}/Q}) = -0.44\,\mathrm{V}\), \(E^\circ(\mathrm{R^{2+}/R}) = +0.34\,\mathrm{V}\), and \(E^\circ(\mathrm{S^{+}/S}) = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\). Which metal is the strongest reducing agent?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{P}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Q}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{R}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{S}\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: The species with the most negative standard reduction potential has the greatest tendency to lose electrons and undergo oxidation. Therefore, \(\mathrm{P}\) (\(E^\circ = -2.37\,\mathrm{V}\)) is the strongest reducing agent.

Practice Question 8 (Redox Stoichiometry)

How many moles of \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) are required to completely oxidise \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of ferrous oxalate (\(\mathrm{FeC_2O_4}\)) in acidic medium?

  • (A) \(0.25\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (B) \(0.50\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (C) \(1.00\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (D) \(1.50\,\mathrm{mol}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: In \(\mathrm{FeC_2O_4}\), both \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\) are oxidised: \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+} \rightarrow Fe^{3+} + e^-}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{e^-}\)) and \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-} \rightarrow 2\,CO_2 + 2\,e^-}\) (\(2\,\mathrm{e^-}\)), giving a total of \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of electrons lost per mole of \(\mathrm{FeC_2O_4}\). Each mole of \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) gains \(6\,\mathrm{mol}\) of electrons (\(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-} + 14\,H^+ + 6\,e^- \rightarrow 2\,Cr^{3+} + 7\,H_2O}\)). Therefore, moles of \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) required \(= \dfrac{3}{6} = 0.50\,\mathrm{mol}\).

Practice Question 9 (Statement-Based Question)

Read the two statements carefully and choose the correct option.

Statement I: Molecular fluorine (\(\mathrm{F_2}\)) does not undergo disproportionation when treated with aqueous alkali.
Statement II: Fluorine is the most electronegative element and cannot exhibit any positive oxidation state.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Disproportionation requires an element to simultaneously increase and decrease its oxidation number. Because fluorine is the most electronegative element, it can only drop from \(0\) to \(-1\) (never to a positive state), so both statements are correct.

Practice Question 10 (Assertion–Reason Question)

Read the Assertion (A) and Reason (R) carefully and select the correct option.

Assertion (A): In a Daniell cell, the zinc electrode acts as the anode and is assigned a negative polarity.
Reason (R): Oxidation occurs at the zinc electrode, releasing electrons into the external circuit toward the copper cathode.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: In a galvanic (Daniell) cell, oxidation always occurs at the anode (\(\mathrm{Zn(s) \rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq) + 2\,e^-}\)). Accumulation of released electrons makes the zinc anode negatively charged relative to the copper cathode, and those electrons flow externally to the cathode. Hence, (R) correctly explains (A).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent Mistakes to Avoid in NEET UG
  • Blindly applying \(-2\) for oxygen: Always check for peroxide linkages (\(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2SO_5}\), \(\mathrm{H_2S_2O_8}\), \(\mathrm{CrO_5}\)) and fluorine compounds (\(\mathrm{OF_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_2F_2}\)) whenever an element's calculated oxidation state exceeds its group valence.
  • Confusing average and individual oxidation numbers: In \(\mathrm{S_4O_6^{2-}}\) or \(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\), remember that fractional values are averages of distinct integer states.
  • Omitting \(\mathrm{H^+}\) / \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) conversion in basic medium: When using the half-reaction method in basic solution, always neutralize \(\mathrm{H^+}\) with an equal number of \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) ions on both sides.
  • Reversing \(E^\circ\) trends: Standard electrode potentials are tabulated as reduction potentials; a more negative \(E^\circ\) means a stronger reducing agent, not a stronger oxidising agent.

Concise Final Recap

Oxidation (electron loss / oxidation number increase) and reduction (electron gain / oxidation number decrease) always occur simultaneously. By combining oxidation-number bookkeeping with structural awareness, you can classify reactions, balance complex ionic equations in acidic or basic media, solve titration stoichiometry rapidly, and use standard reduction potentials (\(E^\circ\)) to predict spontaneous electron flow in electrochemical cells.
