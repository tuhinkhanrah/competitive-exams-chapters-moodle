The s-Block Elements: Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals

Conceptual foundations, periodic trends, anomalous behaviour, industrial compounds, and biological roles aligned to the NEET UG examination.

Learning Objectives and Overview

The s-block of the periodic table comprises Group 1 (the alkali metals) and Group 2 (the alkaline earth metals), so named because the differentiating electron enters the outermost \(s\)-orbital. Because an \(s\)-orbital accommodates at most two electrons, the s-block contains exactly two groups with general valence configurations \([\text{noble gas}]\,ns^1\) and \([\text{noble gas}]\,ns^2\).

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Explain the periodic trends in atomic radii, ionization enthalpy, hydration enthalpy, density, and electrode potentials across Group 1 and Group 2.
  • Predict the products and reactivity patterns of s-block metals with air, water, dihydrogen, halogens, and liquid ammonia.
  • Account for the anomalous behaviour of lithium and beryllium and their diagonal relationships with magnesium and aluminium, respectively.
  • Analyse the solubility and thermal stability of oxides, hydroxides, halides, carbonates, sulphates, and nitrates using lattice and hydration enthalpies.
  • Describe the industrial manufacture, reactions, and applications of key sodium and calcium compounds, including Portland cement.
  • Identify the physiological roles of \(\mathrm{Na}^+\), \(\mathrm{K}^+\), \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\), and \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) ions in living systems.

Foundations of the s-Block

Group 1 (Alkali Metals): Lithium (\(\mathrm{Li}\)), sodium (\(\mathrm{Na}\)), potassium (\(\mathrm{K}\)), rubidium (\(\mathrm{Rb}\)), caesium (\(\mathrm{Cs}\)), and francium (\(\mathrm{Fr}\)). They are called alkali metals because their reaction with water yields strongly alkaline hydroxides. Among them, \(\mathrm{Na}\) and \(\mathrm{K}\) are abundant in the Earth's crust, whereas \(\mathrm{Li}\), \(\mathrm{Rb}\), and \(\mathrm{Cs}\) occur in much smaller amounts. Francium is strongly radioactive; its longest-lived isotope, \(^{223}\mathrm{Fr}\), has a half-life of only \(21\text{ min}\).

Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals): Beryllium (\(\mathrm{Be}\)), magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg}\)), calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca}\)), strontium (\(\mathrm{Sr}\)), barium (\(\mathrm{Ba}\)), and radium (\(\mathrm{Ra}\)). Except for beryllium, these are termed alkaline earth metals because their oxides and hydroxides are alkaline and their metal oxides occur abundantly in the Earth's crust. Calcium and magnesium rank fifth and sixth in crustal abundance, respectively, whereas beryllium is rare and radium is exceedingly scarce (about \(10^{-10}\%\) of igneous rocks).

Diagonal Relationship and Anomalous First-Member Behaviour

The first element of each s-block group (\(\mathrm{Li}\) in Group 1 and \(\mathrm{Be}\) in Group 2) differs noticeably from its heavier congeners and instead resembles the second element of the adjacent group (\(\mathrm{Mg}\) and \(\mathrm{Al}\), respectively). This cross-group similarity is called a diagonal relationship. It arises because descending a group increases ionic radius, whereas moving right across a period increases ionic charge and decreases radius; the two effects roughly offset each other, giving diagonal pairs nearly identical ionic sizes and charge-to-radius ratios (polarizing power).

Group 1 Elements: Alkali Metals

Atomic and Physical Properties

  • Electronic Configuration & Electropositivity: Each alkali metal has a single loosely held \(ns^1\) valence electron outside a noble gas core. They readily lose this electron to form \(\mathrm{M}^+\) ions, making them the most electropositive metals and preventing their occurrence in the free elemental state in nature.
  • Atomic and Ionic Radii: Alkali metal atoms are the largest in their respective periods. Both atomic and ionic radii increase steadily from \(\mathrm{Li}\) to \(\mathrm{Cs}\) as new electron shells are added. A univalent cation \(\mathrm{M}^+\) is always smaller than its parent atom.
  • Ionization Enthalpy: First ionization enthalpies are very low and decrease down the group (\(\mathrm{Li} > \mathrm{Na} > \mathrm{K} > \mathrm{Rb} > \mathrm{Cs}\)) because increasing atomic size and inner-shell screening outweigh the rise in nuclear charge.
  • Hydration Enthalpy: Smaller ions have higher charge density and attract water molecules more strongly. Thus, hydration enthalpy decreases down the group: \[\mathrm{Li}^+ > \mathrm{Na}^+ > \mathrm{K}^+ > \mathrm{Rb}^+ > \mathrm{Cs}^+\] Because \(\mathrm{Li}^+\) has the highest degree of hydration, lithium salts commonly crystallize as hydrates (e.g., \(\mathrm{LiCl}\cdot 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)), whereas heavier alkali metal salts are usually anhydrous. Moreover, in aqueous solution, the heavily hydrated \(\mathrm{Li}^+\) ion has the largest effective radius and therefore the lowest ionic mobility (\(\mathrm{Li}^+ < \mathrm{Na}^+ < \mathrm{K}^+ < \mathrm{Rb}^+ < \mathrm{Cs}^+\)).
  • Density, Melting Point, and Boiling Point: All are silvery-white, soft, light metals. Weak metallic bonding (only one valence electron per atom) leads to low melting and boiling points that decrease from \(\mathrm{Li}\) to \(\mathrm{Cs}\) (\(\mathrm{Cs}\) melts at \(302\text{ K}\)). Density generally increases down the group, with a famous exception: potassium (\(0.86\text{ g cm}^{-3}\)) is lighter than sodium (\(0.97\text{ g cm}^{-3}\)) because of the unusually large jump in atomic volume at \(\mathrm{K}\).
  • Flame Colours and Photoelectric Effect: Flame heat excites the valence \(ns^1\) electron to a higher energy level; when it returns to the ground state, visible light of characteristic wavelength is emitted. Low ionization enthalpies also allow \(\mathrm{K}\) and \(\mathrm{Cs}\) to eject electrons upon light irradiation, making them ideal electrodes in photoelectric cells.

Chemical Properties of Alkali Metals

  1. Reactivity towards Air and Oxygen: Alkali metals tarnish in dry air and burn vigorously in \(\mathrm{O}_2\). As cation size increases, larger anions are stabilized by lattice energy effects: \(\mathrm{Li}\) forms mainly the monoxide (\(\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{O}\)), \(\mathrm{Na}\) forms the peroxide (\(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{O}_2\)), while \(\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{Rb}\), and \(\mathrm{Cs}\) form superoxides (\(\mathrm{MO}_2\)). In all three, the metal oxidation state is \(+1\). Superoxides contain the \(\mathrm{O}_2^-\) ion, which has one unpaired electron in a \(\pi^*2p\) molecular orbital, making \(\mathrm{KO}_2\) yellow/orange and paramagnetic. Uniquely among Group 1, \(\mathrm{Li}\) also reacts directly with atmospheric \(\mathrm{N}_2\) to form lithium nitride (\(\mathrm{Li}_3\mathrm{N}\)). Because of high reactivity with air and moisture, alkali metals are stored under kerosene oil.
  2. Reactivity towards Water: All react to liberate \(\mathrm{H}_2\) and form \(\mathrm{MOH}\): \[2\mathrm{M} + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{M}^+ + 2\mathrm{OH}^- + \mathrm{H}_2\] NEET Key Point: Although \(\mathrm{Li}\) has the most negative \(E^\circ\) (\(-3.04\text{ V}\)), its reaction with water is gentler than that of \(\mathrm{Na}\) (\(-2.714\text{ V}\)), whereas \(\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{Rb}\), and \(\mathrm{Cs}\) react explosively. Lithium's slower kinetics arise from its high melting point and small size/high hydration energy. Alkali metals also react with proton donors such as alcohols, gaseous \(\mathrm{NH}_3\), and alkynes.
  3. Hydrides and Halides: With \(\mathrm{H}_2\) at \(673\text{ K}\) (\(\mathrm{Li}\) at \(1073\text{ K}\)), they form ionic hydrides \(\mathrm{M}^+\mathrm{H}^-\). With halogens, they form ionic halides \(\mathrm{M}^+\mathrm{X}^-\); however, \(\mathrm{Li}^+\) has high polarizing power, giving lithium halides significant covalent character (with \(\mathrm{LiI}\) being the most covalent because \(\mathrm{I}^-\) is the largest, most polarizable halide ion).
  4. Reducing Nature: The standard electrode potential \(E^\circ\) reflects three steps: sublimation (\(\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\circ\)), ionization (\(\Delta_i H^\circ\)), and hydration (\(\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ\)). Because \(\mathrm{Li}^+\) has an extraordinarily large negative hydration enthalpy, \(\mathrm{Li}\) has the most negative \(E^\circ\) and is the strongest reducing agent in aqueous solution, while \(\mathrm{Na}\) is the weakest among the alkali metals.
  5. Solutions in Liquid Ammonia: Alkali metals dissolve in liquid \(\mathrm{NH}_3\) to give conducting, paramagnetic, deep blue solutions due to ammoniated electrons: \[\mathrm{M} + (x + y)\mathrm{NH}_3 \rightarrow [\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_x]^+ + [\mathrm{e}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_y]^-\] On standing, the solution slowly liberates \(\mathrm{H}_2\) and forms metal amide (\(\mathrm{MNH}_2\)). At high concentrations, the colour turns bronze and the solution becomes diamagnetic.

General Characteristics of Group 1 Compounds

  • Oxides & Hydroxides: Hydrolysis yields strongly basic hydroxides: \[\mathrm{M}_2\mathrm{O} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{M}^+ + 2\mathrm{OH}^-\] \[\mathrm{M}_2\mathrm{O}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{M}^+ + 2\mathrm{OH}^- + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}_2\] \[2\mathrm{MO}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{M}^+ + 2\mathrm{OH}^- + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}_2 + \mathrm{O}_2\]
  • Halides: Melting and boiling points follow \(\text{fluoride} > \text{chloride} > \text{bromide} > \text{iodide}\). Both \(\mathrm{LiF}\) (very high lattice enthalpy) and \(\mathrm{CsI}\) (low hydration enthalpy of both large ions) have low water solubility. Other lithium halides dissolve in organic solvents (ethanol, acetone, ethyl acetate; \(\mathrm{LiCl}\) also in pyridine).
  • Carbonates & Hydrogencarbonates: Thermal stability of \(\mathrm{M}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\) and \(\mathrm{MHCO}_3\) increases down the group as electropositive character rises. Exceptionally, \(\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\) decomposes on heating to \(\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{O} + \mathrm{CO}_2\) because small \(\mathrm{Li}^+\) strongly polarizes the large \(\mathrm{CO}_3^{2-}\) ion; \(\mathrm{LiHCO}_3\) does not exist as a solid.
Anomalous Lithium vs. Diagonal Partner Magnesium

Unlike other Group 1 metals, \(\mathrm{Li}\) is harder, has higher m.p./b.p., forms \(\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{O}\) and \(\mathrm{Li}_3\mathrm{N}\) in air, does not form an ethynide with ethyne, forms deliquescent \(\mathrm{LiCl}\cdot 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\), and its nitrate decomposes like Group 2 nitrates: \(4\mathrm{LiNO}_3 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{O} + 4\mathrm{NO}_2 + \mathrm{O}_2\) (whereas \(\mathrm{NaNO}_3\) gives \(\mathrm{NaNO}_2 + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{O}_2\)).
Shared \(\mathrm{Li}\)–\(\mathrm{Mg}\) traits: Both are harder/lighter than group peers; react slowly with water; form nitrides directly (\(\mathrm{Li}_3\mathrm{N}, \mathrm{Mg}_3\mathrm{N}_2\)); form no superoxides; have thermally unstable carbonates and no solid hydrogencarbonates; and have ethanol-soluble, deliquescent chlorides (\(\mathrm{LiCl}\cdot 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\), \(\mathrm{MgCl}_2\cdot 8\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)).

Group 2 Elements: Alkaline Earth Metals

Atomic, Physical, and Chemical Trends

  • Size & Ionization Enthalpy: Higher nuclear charge makes Group 2 atoms and \(\mathrm{M}^{2+}\) ions smaller than the corresponding Group 1 species. Consequently, \(IE_1\) is higher than in Group 1; however, \(IE_2\) of Group 2 is lower than \(IE_2\) of Group 1 (since removing a second electron from \(\mathrm{M}^+\) in Group 1 breaks a stable noble gas core).
  • Hydration Enthalpy: Decreases down the group (\(\mathrm{Be}^{2+} > \mathrm{Mg}^{2+} > \mathrm{Ca}^{2+} > \mathrm{Sr}^{2+} > \mathrm{Ba}^{2+}\)) and is much larger than for Group 1 ions due to higher charge and smaller size. Hence, \(\mathrm{MgCl}_2\cdot 6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) and \(\mathrm{CaCl}_2\cdot 6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) are heavily hydrated.
  • Flame Colours: \(\mathrm{Ca}\) (brick red), \(\mathrm{Sr}\) (crimson), and \(\mathrm{Ba}\) (apple green) impart characteristic flame colours. \(\mathrm{Be}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}\) give no flame colour because their valence electrons are too strongly bound to be excited by a Bunsen flame.
  • Reactivity: Less reactive than Group 1; reactivity increases down the group. \(\mathrm{Be}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}\) are kinetically inert to \(\mathrm{O}_2\) and \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) due to a protective surface oxide film, though powdered \(\mathrm{Be}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}\) burn brilliantly to give \(\mathrm{MO}\) and \(\mathrm{M}_3\mathrm{N}_2\). \(\mathrm{Ca}\), \(\mathrm{Sr}\), and \(\mathrm{Ba}\) react readily with cold water.
  • Hydrides & Halides: All except \(\mathrm{Be}\) combine directly with \(\mathrm{H}_2\) on heating to give \(\mathrm{MH}_2\). \(\mathrm{BeH}_2\) is prepared indirectly: \[2\mathrm{BeCl}_2 + \mathrm{LiAlH}_4 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{BeH}_2 + \mathrm{LiCl} + \mathrm{AlCl}_3\] \(\mathrm{BeCl}_2\) is best prepared from \(\mathrm{BeO} + \mathrm{C} + \mathrm{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{600\text{–}800\text{ K}} \mathrm{BeCl}_2 + \mathrm{CO}\).
  • Liquid Ammonia & Reducing Power: All are strong reducing agents (less than Group 1); \(E^\circ\) for \(\mathrm{Ca}, \mathrm{Sr}, \mathrm{Ba}\) is nearly constant (\(-2.84\) to \(-2.92\text{ V}\)), while \(\mathrm{Be}\) (\(-1.97\text{ V}\)) owes its reducing ability to huge hydration energy of \(\mathrm{Be}^{2+}\). In liquid \(\mathrm{NH}_3\), they form deep blue-black solutions yielding ammoniates \([\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_6]^{2+}\).

Structure of Beryllium Chloride (\(\mathrm{BeCl}_2\)) Across Phases

Unlike the ionic halides of \(\mathrm{Ca}, \mathrm{Sr}, \mathrm{Ba}\), beryllium halides are covalent and soluble in organic solvents. In the solid state, \(\mathrm{BeCl}_2\) forms an infinite polymeric chain with tetrahedral \(\mathrm{Be}\) centres bridged by \(\mathrm{Cl}\) atoms. In the vapour phase, it forms a chloro-bridged dimer, which dissociates into a linear monomer (\(sp\)-hybridized \(\mathrm{Be}\)) around \(1200\text{ K}\).

Anomalous Beryllium & Diagonal Relationship with Aluminium

\(\mathrm{Be}\) cannot exceed a coordination number of \(4\) because its valence shell has only four orbitals (\(2s, 2p_x, 2p_y, 2p_z\)), whereas heavier Group 2 elements can reach coordination number \(6\) using \(d\)-orbitals. Because \(\mathrm{Be}^{2+}\) (\(31\text{ pm}\)) and \(\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\) have nearly equal charge/radius ratios, they share key traits: (i) passivity toward acids due to an oxide film; (ii) amphoteric hydroxides forming \([\mathrm{Be(OH)}_4]^{2-}\) and \([\mathrm{Al(OH)}_4]^-\); (iii) \(\mathrm{Cl}^-\)-bridged covalent chlorides soluble in organic solvents and used as Friedel–Crafts Lewis acid catalysts; and (iv) strong complexation (\([\mathrm{BeF}_4]^{2-}, [\mathrm{AlF}_6]^{3-}\)).

Industrially Important Compounds of Sodium and Calcium

Sodium Carbonate (Washing Soda, \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\cdot 10\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)) — Solvay Process

Sodium carbonate is manufactured by the Solvay process, which exploits the low solubility of \(\mathrm{NaHCO}_3\). Carbon dioxide is passed into concentrated brine (\(\mathrm{NaCl}\)) saturated with \(\mathrm{NH}_3\):

\[2\mathrm{NH}_3 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} + \mathrm{CO}_2 \rightarrow (\mathrm{NH}_4)_2\mathrm{CO}_3 \xrightarrow{+\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}+\mathrm{CO}_2} 2\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{HCO}_3\] \[\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{HCO}_3 + \mathrm{NaCl} \rightarrow \mathrm{NaHCO}_3\downarrow + \mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\] \[2\mathrm{NaHCO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3 + \mathrm{CO}_2\uparrow + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\uparrow\]

Ammonia is regenerated by treating \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\) with slaked lime, leaving \(\mathrm{CaCl}_2\) as the sole major by-product: \(2\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl} + \mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NH}_3 + \mathrm{CaCl}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\). Crucial NEET point: The Solvay process cannot be used for \(\mathrm{K}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\) because \(\mathrm{KHCO}_3\) is too soluble to precipitate from saturated \(\mathrm{KCl}\).

On heating, \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\cdot 10\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) loses \(9\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) at \(375\text{ K}\) to give the monohydrate, and above \(373\text{ K}\) forms anhydrous soda ash (\(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\)). Aqueous \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\) is alkaline due to carbonate hydrolysis (\(\mathrm{CO}_3^{2-} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{HCO}_3^- + \mathrm{OH}^-\)).

Other Key Sodium Compounds

  • Sodium Chloride (\(\mathrm{NaCl}\)): Obtained from sea water (\(2.7\text{–}2.9\%\) by mass). Crude salt contains deliquescent \(\mathrm{CaCl}_2\) and \(\mathrm{MgCl}_2\); passing \(\mathrm{HCl}\) gas through a saturated aqueous solution precipitates pure \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) via the common-ion effect, leaving \(\mathrm{CaCl}_2\) and \(\mathrm{MgCl}_2\) in solution.
  • Sodium Hydroxide (Caustic Soda, \(\mathrm{NaOH}\)): Prepared by electrolysis of brine in the Castner–Kellner cell (mercury cathode forms \(\mathrm{Na}\)-amalgam; carbon anode evolves \(\mathrm{Cl}_2\)). Treating the amalgam with water gives \(\mathrm{NaOH} + \mathrm{H}_2 + \mathrm{Hg}\). Used in soap, paper, petroleum refining, bauxite purification, and mercerizing cotton.
  • Sodium Hydrogencarbonate (Baking Soda, \(\mathrm{NaHCO}_3\)): Precipitated by saturating \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\) solution with \(\mathrm{CO}_2\). Decomposes on heating to release \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) (baking); also used as a mild skin antiseptic and in fire extinguishers.

Key Calcium Compounds and Portland Cement

  • Quick Lime (\(\mathrm{CaO}\)): Made by heating limestone (\(\mathrm{CaCO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CaO} + \mathrm{CO}_2\)) at \(1070\text{–}1270\text{ K}\) with continuous \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) removal. Adding limited water causes slaking of lime to \(\mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2\). Being basic, \(\mathrm{CaO}\) reacts with acidic oxides (\(\mathrm{SiO}_2 \rightarrow \mathrm{CaSiO}_3\); \(\mathrm{P}_4\mathrm{O}_{10} \rightarrow \mathrm{Ca}_3(\mathrm{PO}_4)_2\)).
  • Slaked Lime (\(\mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2\)): Aqueous solution is lime water; suspension is milk of lime. Passing \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) turns lime water milky (\(\mathrm{CaCO}_3\)), which clears with excess \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) due to soluble \(\mathrm{Ca(HCO_3)}_2\). With \(\mathrm{Cl}_2\), milk of lime forms bleaching powder constituent \(\mathrm{Ca(OCl)}_2\): \[2\mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2 + 2\mathrm{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \mathrm{CaCl}_2 + \mathrm{Ca(OCl)}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\]
  • Plaster of Paris (\(\mathrm{CaSO}_4\cdot \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)): Obtained by heating gypsum (\(\mathrm{CaSO}_4\cdot 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)) at \(393\text{ K}\). Heating above \(393\text{ K}\) removes all water to give anhydrous \(\mathrm{CaSO}_4\) (dead burnt plaster), which loses setting ability. With water, Plaster of Paris sets to a hard mass in \(5\text{–}15\text{ min}\).
  • Portland Cement: Manufactured by strongly heating limestone and clay to form cement clinker, then grinding with \(2\text{–}3\%\) gypsum (\(\mathrm{CaSO}_4\cdot 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)). Gypsum slows down the setting of cement so it hardens properly. Typical oxide composition: \(\mathrm{CaO}\) (\(50\text{–}60\%\)), \(\mathrm{SiO}_2\) (\(20\text{–}25\%\)), \(\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3\) (\(5\text{–}10\%\)), \(\mathrm{MgO}\) (\(2\text{–}3\%\)), \(\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3\) (\(1\text{–}2\%\)), \(\mathrm{SO}_3\) (\(1\text{–}2\%\)). Quality ratios: \(\mathrm{SiO}_2/\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3\) between \(2.5\) and \(4\); \(\mathrm{CaO}/(\mathrm{SiO}_2 + \mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_3 + \mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3) \approx 2\). Major ingredients: tricalcium silicate \(\mathrm{Ca}_3\mathrm{SiO}_5\) (\(51\%\)), dicalcium silicate \(\mathrm{Ca}_2\mathrm{SiO}_4\) (\(26\%\)), and tricalcium aluminate \(\mathrm{Ca}_3\mathrm{Al}_2\mathrm{O}_6\) (\(11\%\)).

Worked Examples

Predicting Oxide Products and Magnetic Behaviour

Problem: Suppose a chemist burns separate \(0.50\text{ mol}\) samples (assumed) of lithium, sodium, and rubidium in excess dry oxygen. Identify the major solid product in each case, state the oxidation number of oxygen, and predict which product is paramagnetic.

Solution:

  1. Small \(\mathrm{Li}^+\) stabilizes the small oxide ion \(\mathrm{O}^{2-}\), giving lithium monoxide, \(\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{O}\) (oxygen oxidation state \(= -2\)).
  2. Intermediate \(\mathrm{Na}^+\) stabilizes the peroxide ion \(\mathrm{O}_2^{2-}\), giving sodium peroxide, \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{O}_2\) (oxygen oxidation state \(= -1\)).
  3. Large \(\mathrm{Rb}^+\) stabilizes the large superoxide ion \(\mathrm{O}_2^-\), giving rubidium superoxide, \(\mathrm{RbO}_2\) (average oxygen oxidation state \(= -\frac{1}{2}\)).
  4. Only \(\mathrm{RbO}_2\) is paramagnetic because \(\mathrm{O}_2^-\) contains one unpaired electron in its \(\pi^*2p\) antibonding orbital; \(\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{O}\) and \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{O}_2\) have all electrons paired and are diamagnetic.

Explaining Solubility Trends Using Lattice and Hydration Enthalpies

Problem: Why does \(\mathrm{Ba(OH)}_2\) dissolve more readily in water than \(\mathrm{Mg(OH)}_2\), whereas \(\mathrm{BaSO}_4\) is far less soluble than \(\mathrm{MgSO}_4\)?

Solution:

  • A salt dissolves when hydration enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H\)) compensates for lattice enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H\)).
  • For hydroxides \(\mathrm{M(OH)}_2\), \(\mathrm{OH}^-\) is relatively small, so increasing cation radius from \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\) to \(\mathrm{Ba}^{2+}\) causes \(\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H\) to decrease much more steeply than \(\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H\). Thus, solubility increases down the group: \(\mathrm{Mg(OH)}_2 < \mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2 < \mathrm{Sr(OH)}_2 < \mathrm{Ba(OH)}_2\).
  • For sulphates \(\mathrm{MSO}_4\), \(\mathrm{SO}_4^{2-}\) is much larger than the cations, so \(\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H\) remains nearly constant down the group. Meanwhile, \(\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H\) drops markedly from \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\) to \(\mathrm{Ba}^{2+}\), causing solubility to decrease: \(\mathrm{MgSO}_4 \gg \mathrm{CaSO}_4 > \mathrm{SrSO}_4 > \mathrm{BaSO}_4\).

Stoichiometry of the Solvay Process and Ammonia Recovery

Problem: In a hypothetical Solvay trial (assumed \(100\%\) yield), \(2.0\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaHCO}_3\) precipitates and is calcined to \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\). How many moles of \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\) are formed alongside it, and how many moles of \(\mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2\) are needed to recover all \(\mathrm{NH}_3\)?

Solution:

  1. From \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{HCO}_3 + \mathrm{NaCl} \rightarrow \mathrm{NaHCO}_3 + \mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\), \(2.0\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaHCO}_3\) produces \(2.0\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\).
  2. Ammonia recovery follows \(2\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl} + \mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2 \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NH}_3 + \mathrm{CaCl}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\). Therefore, \(2.0\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\) requires exactly \(1.0\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2\) (and yields \(1.0\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\) upon calcining \(2.0\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaHCO}_3\)).

Thermal Decomposition of s-Block Nitrates

Problem: Two white crystalline nitrates, \(X\) (a Group 1 nitrate) and \(Y\) (a Group 1 nitrate showing diagonal similarity to \(\mathrm{Mg}\)), are heated strongly. \(X\) evolves only a colourless gas that relights a glowing splint, while \(Y\) evolves brown fumes along with oxygen. Identify \(X\) and \(Y\) (give plausible examples) and write the balanced equations.

Solution:

  • Heavier Group 1 nitrates (such as \(X = \mathrm{NaNO}_3\) or \(\mathrm{KNO}_3\)) decompose to nitrite and \(\mathrm{O}_2\) (colourless gas relighting a glowing splint): \[2\mathrm{NaNO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\mathrm{NaNO}_2 + \mathrm{O}_2\]
  • Lithium (\(Y = \mathrm{LiNO}_3\)) behaves diagonally like \(\mathrm{Mg(NO_3)}_2\) and decomposes to the oxide, brown \(\mathrm{NO}_2\) fumes, and \(\mathrm{O}_2\): \[4\mathrm{LiNO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\mathrm{Li}_2\mathrm{O} + 4\mathrm{NO}_2 + \mathrm{O}_2\]

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following alkali metal ions has the highest ionic mobility in aqueous solution?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Li}^+\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Na}^+\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{K}^+\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Cs}^+\)
Answer: (D) \(\mathrm{Cs}^+\)
Explanation: Smaller ions have higher charge density and are more heavily hydrated in water. Consequently, the hydrated radius follows \(\mathrm{Li}^+_{\text{(aq)}} > \mathrm{Na}^+_{\text{(aq)}} > \mathrm{K}^+_{\text{(aq)}} > \mathrm{Rb}^+_{\text{(aq)}} > \mathrm{Cs}^+_{\text{(aq)}}\). Being least hydrated and smallest in effective hydrated size, \(\mathrm{Cs}^+\) moves fastest and has the highest ionic mobility.
Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Which pair of elements gives no characteristic colour in a standard Bunsen flame test?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Li}\) and \(\mathrm{Ca}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Be}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Sr}\) and \(\mathrm{Ba}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{K}\) and \(\mathrm{Cs}\)
Answer: (B) \(\mathrm{Be}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}\)
Explanation: The valence electrons in \(\mathrm{Be}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}\) are held too tightly by their small atoms (high ionization enthalpy) to be excited by the energy of a Bunsen flame, so they emit no visible flame colour.

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following halides has the greatest covalent character?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{LiF}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{LiCl}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{LiBr}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{LiI}\)
Answer: (D) \(\mathrm{LiI}\)
Explanation: According to Fajans' rules, a small cation with high polarizing power (\(\mathrm{Li}^+\)) distorts a large anion most effectively. Since \(\mathrm{I}^-\) is the largest and most polarizable halide ion, \(\mathrm{LiI}\) has the highest covalent character.
Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

Heating gypsum (\(\mathrm{CaSO}_4\cdot 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)) well above \(393\text{ K}\) produces:

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

Which alkaline earth metal carbonate is thermally the most stable?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{MgCO}_3\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CaCO}_3\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{SrCO}_3\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{BaCO}_3\)
Answer: (D) \(\mathrm{BaCO}_3\)
Explanation: Thermal stability of Group 2 carbonates increases down the group as cationic size increases and polarizing power decreases. Large \(\mathrm{Ba}^{2+}\) distorts \(\mathrm{CO}_3^{2-}\) the least, making \(\mathrm{BaCO}_3\) the most thermally stable.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Density anomaly: \(\mathrm{K}\) (\(0.86\text{ g cm}^{-3}\)) is lighter than \(\mathrm{Na}\) (\(0.97\text{ g cm}^{-3}\)); do not assume density increases monotonically from \(\mathrm{Li}\) to \(\mathrm{Cs}\).
  • \(E^\circ\) vs Reactivity with Water: \(\mathrm{Li}\) has the most negative \(E^\circ\) (strongest thermodynamic reducing agent), yet \(\mathrm{Na}\) reacts more vigorously with water than \(\mathrm{Li}\).
  • Hydrated Halide Heating: \(\mathrm{CaCl}_2\cdot 6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) dehydrates cleanly on heating, whereas \(\mathrm{MgCl}_2\cdot 6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) and \(\mathrm{BeCl}_2\cdot 4\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) undergo hydrolysis.
  • Solubility Opposites in Group 2: Hydroxides become more soluble down Group 2, whereas carbonates and sulphates become less soluble.
  • Solvay Process Limit: Works for \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\), fails for \(\mathrm{K}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\) due to soluble \(\mathrm{KHCO}_3\).

Concise Final Recap

The s-block elements (\(\mathrm{ns}^1\) alkali metals and \(\mathrm{ns}^2\) alkaline earth metals) are strongly electropositive metals whose chemistry is governed by atomic size, low ionization enthalpy, and hydration energy. Descending either group increases metallic character, reactivity, and thermal stability of oxo-acid salts, while decreasing ionization and hydration enthalpies. The small, highly polarizing first members (\(\mathrm{Li}\) and \(\mathrm{Be}\)) display covalent tendencies and diagonal similarities to \(\mathrm{Mg}\) and \(\mathrm{Al}\). Industrially, \(\mathrm{Na}_2\mathrm{CO}_3\), \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), \(\mathrm{CaO}\), \(\mathrm{Ca(OH)}_2\), Plaster of Paris, and Portland cement underpin chemical manufacturing and construction, while \(\mathrm{Na}^+\), \(\mathrm{K}^+\), \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\), and \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) are indispensable for nerve conduction, enzyme catalysis, photosynthesis, and bone homeostasis.
