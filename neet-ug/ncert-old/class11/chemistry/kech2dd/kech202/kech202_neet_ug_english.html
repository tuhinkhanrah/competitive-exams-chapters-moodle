Hydrogen: Structure, Isotopes, Hydrides, Water, and Peroxide

Complete conceptual tutorial, quantitative derivations, and NEET UG practice assessment

Learning Objectives and Exam Scope

Hydrogen is the simplest element in nature, yet its chemistry spans ionic salts, covalent networks, fuel-cell technology, water-softening equilibria, and redox duality. For NEET UG, questions frequently test periodic anomalies, isotope effects, hydride classification, water hardness calculations, and the volume strength of hydrogen peroxide.

Core Competencies Covered
  • Justify the unique position of hydrogen in the periodic table using ionization enthalpy and ionic size arguments.
  • Compare protium, deuterium, and tritium in terms of nuclear composition, radioactivity, and kinetic isotope effects.
  • Master laboratory and industrial preparations of dihydrogen, including syngas and the water-gas shift reaction.
  • Classify hydrides as saline, molecular (electron-deficient, electron-precise, electron-rich), or metallic/non-stoichiometric.
  • Relate the bent structure of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) and the open cage of ice to hydrogen bonding, density, and amphoteric/redox chemistry.
  • Distinguish temporary and permanent hardness of water and write balanced equations for softening methods.
  • Calculate concentration and volume strength of \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\), interpret its non-planar structure, and balance its redox reactions.

1. Periodic Position, Occurrence, and Isotopes

1.1 Why Hydrogen Occupies a Separate Position

With a ground-state electronic configuration of \(1\mathrm{s}^1\), hydrogen shares features with both Group 1 (alkali metals) and Group 17 (halogens), yet belongs to neither group:

  • Resemblance to Alkali Metals (\(n\mathrm{s}^1\)): Hydrogen can lose one electron to form a unipositive ion \(\mathrm{H}^+\), and it forms oxides (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)), halides (\(\mathrm{HX}\)), and sulphides (\(\mathrm{H_2S}\)).
  • Resemblance to Halogens (\(n\mathrm{s}^2 n\mathrm{p}^5\)): Hydrogen is one electron short of the nearest noble gas configuration (helium, \(1\mathrm{s}^2\)). It forms a diatomic molecule (\(\mathrm{H_2}\)), gains an electron to form the hydride ion (\(\mathrm{H}^-\)), and forms numerous covalent compounds. Its ionization enthalpy (\(1312\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is closer to fluorine (\(1680\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) than to lithium (\(520\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  • Unique Behaviour: Unlike alkali metals, hydrogen is a non-metal under normal conditions and has much lower reactivity than halogens. Crucially, removing the single electron from a hydrogen atom leaves a bare proton (\(\mathrm{H}^+\)) of radius \(\sim 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{pm}\), which is minuscule compared with typical atomic or ionic radii (\(50\text{–}200\,\mathrm{pm}\)). Because of this extreme charge density, free \(\mathrm{H}^+\) never exists independently in condensed phases; it always associates with other atoms or molecules (for example, as \(\mathrm{H_3O}^+\)).

1.2 Occurrence and Isotopes

Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe (about \(70\%\) of total mass), dominating stars and gas giants like Jupiter and Saturn. On Earth, free \(\mathrm{H_2}\) is scarce in the atmosphere (\(0.15\%\) by mass) because light molecules escape Earth's gravity, whereas combined hydrogen accounts for \(15.4\%\) of the Earth's crust and oceans (in water, carbohydrates, proteins, and hydrocarbons).

Hydrogen exists as three isotopes differing in neutron count. Because they share the same electronic configuration (\(1\mathrm{s}^1\)), their chemical properties are nearly identical, though reaction rates differ due to differences in bond dissociation enthalpy (the kinetic isotope effect). Physical properties (melting point, boiling point, density) vary substantially because the relative mass ratios are \(1 : 2 : 3\).

2. Dihydrogen (\(\mathrm{H_2}\)): Preparation, Properties, and Fuel Economy

2.1 Laboratory and Commercial Preparation

In the laboratory, dihydrogen is generated by reacting granulated zinc with either dilute mineral acid or hot aqueous alkali:

\[\mathrm{Zn(s)} + 2\mathrm{H^+(aq)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}\] \[\mathrm{Zn(s)} + 2\mathrm{NaOH(aq)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Na_2ZnO_2(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}\quad (\text{sodium zincate})\]

On an industrial scale, roughly \(77\%\) of dihydrogen comes from petrochemicals, \(18\%\) from coal, \(4\%\) from electrolysis, and \(1\%\) from other sources:

  1. Electrolysis of Acidified Water: Using platinum electrodes and traces of acid or base: \[2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \xrightarrow{\text{Electrolysis}} 2\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}\]
  2. High-Purity Dihydrogen (>99.95%): Obtained by electrolysing warm aqueous barium hydroxide (\(\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2}\)) between nickel electrodes.
  3. Byproduct of Chlor-Alkali Process: Electrolysis of aqueous brine (\(\mathrm{NaCl}\)) yields \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) at the anode and \(\mathrm{H_2}\) + \(\mathrm{OH}^-\) at the cathode: \[2\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Cl_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\]
  4. Steam Reforming & Coal Gasification: Passing steam over hydrocarbons or coke at \(1270\,\mathrm{K}\) produces water gas (also called synthesis gas or syngas, \(\mathrm{CO} + \mathrm{H_2}\)): \[\mathrm{CH_4(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(g)} \xrightarrow[1270\,\mathrm{K}]{\mathrm{Ni}} \mathrm{CO(g)} + 3\mathrm{H_2(g)}\] \[\mathrm{C(s)} + \mathrm{H_2O(g)} \xrightarrow{1270\,\mathrm{K}} \mathrm{CO(g)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)} \quad (\text{coal gasification})\]
  5. Water-Gas Shift Reaction: To maximize \(\mathrm{H_2}\) output from syngas, \(\mathrm{CO}\) is reacted with steam over an iron chromate catalyst at \(673\,\mathrm{K}\); \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is then removed by scrubbing with sodium arsenite solution: \[\mathrm{CO(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(g)} \xrightarrow[673\,\mathrm{K}]{\text{iron chromate}} \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}\]

2.2 Chemical Reactivity and Applications

Dihydrogen is colourless, odourless, tasteless, lighter than air, and insoluble in water. Its \(\mathrm{H-H}\) single-bond dissociation enthalpy (\(435.88\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is the highest for any single bond between two atoms of the same element. Consequently, \(\mathrm{H_2}\) is relatively unreactive at room temperature; dissociation into atomic hydrogen is only \(\sim 0.081\%\) at \(2000\,\mathrm{K}\) and reaches \(95.5\%\) at \(5000\,\mathrm{K}\). Atomic hydrogen produced in an electric arc recombines on metal surfaces to generate \(\sim 4000\,\mathrm{K}\) in an atomic hydrogen torch for welding high-melting metals.

Hydrogen Economy and Fuel Characteristics

On a mass basis, \(\mathrm{H_2}\) releases \(143\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}\) (gas) or \(142\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}\) (liquid) on combustion—roughly three times the energy per gram of octane (\(47\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}\)) or LPG (\(50\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}\)). Its combustion produces water without carbon pollutants; trace nitrogen oxides from air impurities can be suppressed by injecting water into the cylinder to lower combustion temperature. The primary bottleneck is storage: a compressed \(\mathrm{H_2}\) cylinder weighs \(\sim 30\) times as much as an equivalent-energy petrol tank, and liquefying \(\mathrm{H_2}\) requires cooling to \(20\,\mathrm{K}\). Alloy hydrides such as \(\mathrm{LaNi_5}\) (or \(\mathrm{NaNi_5}\)), \(\mathrm{Ti-TiH_2}\), and \(\mathrm{Mg-MgH_2}\) offer compact solid-state storage.

3. Classification and Chemistry of Hydrides

Dihydrogen combines with nearly all elements (except noble gases) to form binary compounds called hydrides (\(\mathrm{EH}_x\) or \(\mathrm{E}_m\mathrm{H}_n\)), grouped into three major classes:

3.1 Ionic or Saline Hydrides

Formed by strongly electropositive \(s\)-block metals (e.g., \(\mathrm{NaH}\), \(\mathrm{KH}\), \(\mathrm{CaH_2}\)), these are crystalline, non-volatile solids that do not conduct electricity in the solid state. However, their molten state conducts electricity and liberates \(\mathrm{H_2}\) at the anode, confirming the presence of the \(\mathrm{H}^-\) ion:

\[2\mathrm{H^-(melt)} \xrightarrow{\text{anode}} \mathrm{H_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{e^-}\]

Lighter \(s\)-block hydrides (\(\mathrm{LiH}\), \(\mathrm{BeH_2}\), \(\mathrm{MgH_2}\)) exhibit significant covalent character; \(\mathrm{BeH_2}\) and \(\mathrm{MgH_2}\) are polymeric. Saline hydrides react violently with water, liberating \(\mathrm{H_2}\) gas and catching fire:

\[\mathrm{NaH(s)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{NaOH(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}\]

Because \(\mathrm{LiH}\) is unreactive toward \(\mathrm{O_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) at moderate temperatures, it is used to synthesise complex hydride reducing agents:

\[8\mathrm{LiH} + \mathrm{Al_2Cl_6} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{LiAlH_4} + 6\mathrm{LiCl} \qquad \text{and} \qquad 2\mathrm{LiH} + \mathrm{B_2H_6} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{LiBH_4}\]

3.2 Covalent or Molecular Hydrides

Formed primarily by \(p\)-block non-metals, these are volatile molecular compounds sub-classified by valence electron count relative to available bonds:

  • Electron-Deficient Hydrides (Group 13): Have too few electrons for conventional two-centre, two-electron Lewis structures (e.g., diborane, \(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\)). They act as Lewis acids (electron-pair acceptors).
  • Electron-Precise Hydrides (Group 14): Have the exact number of electrons needed for normal covalent bonds (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{SiH_4}\)), adopting tetrahedral geometry. They act as neither Lewis acids nor Lewis bases.
  • Electron-Rich Hydrides (Groups 15–17): Possess excess valence electrons as lone pairs (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\) has 1, \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) has 2, \(\mathrm{HF}\) has 3). They behave as Lewis bases. In \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), and \(\mathrm{HF}\), strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding leads to molecular association and anomalously high boiling points compared with heavier hydrides of their respective groups.

3.3 Metallic or Non-Stoichiometric (Interstitial) Hydrides

Formed by many \(d\)-block and \(f\)-block metals, these conduct heat and electricity (though less efficiently than the parent metals) and are typically non-stoichiometric (hydrogen-deficient), such as \(\mathrm{LaH_{2.87}}\), \(\mathrm{YbH_{2.55}}\), \(\mathrm{TiH_{1.5\text{–}1.8}}\), \(\mathrm{ZrH_{1.3\text{–}1.75}}\), \(\mathrm{VH_{0.56}}\), and \(\mathrm{PdH_{0.6\text{–}0.8}}\). Most have crystal lattices different from the parent metal (exceptions retaining the parent lattice include hydrides of \(\mathrm{Ni}\), \(\mathrm{Pd}\), \(\mathrm{Ce}\), and \(\mathrm{Ac}\)).

The Hydride Gap (Important NEET UG Fact)

Metals of Groups 7, 8, and 9 do not form hydrides at all. Even in Group 6, only chromium forms a hydride (\(\mathrm{CrH}\)). Transition metals such as \(\mathrm{Pd}\) and \(\mathrm{Pt}\) absorb large volumes of \(\mathrm{H_2}\), making them valuable for catalytic hydrogenation and ultrapurification/storage of hydrogen.

4. Water (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)), Ice, Heavy Water, and Water Softening

4.1 Molecular Geometry of Water and Structure of Ice

In the gas phase, \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) is a bent molecule (\(\mathrm{sp^3}\) hybridised oxygen with two lone pairs) having an \(\mathrm{H-O-H}\) bond angle of \(104.5^\circ\) and an \(\mathrm{O-H}\) bond length of \(95.7\,\mathrm{pm}\). Because oxygen is much more electronegative than hydrogen, \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) has a large permanent dipole moment and high dielectric constant (\(78.39\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)), making it an exceptional solvent for ionic and polar covalent compounds (alcohols, carbohydrates).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Bent geometry (\(104.5^\circ\), \(95.7\,\mathrm{pm}\)) and net dipole orientation of a water molecule.

At atmospheric pressure, ice crystallises in a hexagonal lattice (at very low temperatures, a cubic form forms). X-ray diffraction reveals that each oxygen atom in ice is surrounded tetrahedrally by four other oxygen atoms at a distance of \(276\,\mathrm{pm}\) via hydrogen bonds. This creates an open, cage-like 3D network with wide interstitial holes, making ice less dense than liquid water (whose maximum density occurs at \(276.98\,\mathrm{K}\), i.e., \(3.98^\circ\mathrm{C}\)). Consequently, ice floats on lakes and insulates aquatic life during winter.

4.2 Chemical Properties of Water

  • Amphoteric Nature & Auto-Protolysis: Water acts as a Brönsted acid toward \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and as a Brönsted base toward \(\mathrm{H_2S}\). It undergoes self-ionization (auto-protolysis): \[2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_3O^+(aq)} + \mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\]
  • Redox Reactions: Strongly electropositive metals reduce water to \(\mathrm{H_2}\) (\(2\mathrm{Na} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NaOH} + \mathrm{H_2}\)). Conversely, water is oxidised to \(\mathrm{O_2}\) during photosynthesis and by fluorine: \[2\mathrm{F_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \longrightarrow 4\mathrm{H^+(aq)} + 4\mathrm{F^-(aq)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}\]
  • Hydrolysis: Covalent oxides, halides, and ionic nitrides hydrolyse in water: \[\mathrm{P_4O_{10}(s)} + 6\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \longrightarrow 4\mathrm{H_3PO_4(aq)} \qquad \mathrm{SiCl_4(l)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{SiO_2(s)} + 4\mathrm{HCl(aq)}\] \[\mathrm{N^{3-}(s)} + 3\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{NH_3(g)} + 3\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\]
  • Hydrate Formation: Water binds in crystalline salts in three ways: (i) coordinated water, e.g., \([\mathrm{Cr(H_2O)_6}]^{3+}3\mathrm{Cl}^-\); (ii) interstitial water, e.g., \(\mathrm{BaCl_2\cdot 2H_2O}\); and (iii) hydrogen-bonded water, e.g., in \(\mathrm{CuSO_4\cdot 5H_2O}\), formulated as \([\mathrm{Cu(H_2O)_4}]^{2+}\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}\cdot H_2O}\), where four water molecules are coordinated to \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) and one is hydrogen-bonded outside the coordination sphere.

4.3 Hard and Soft Water

Dissolved \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\) salts prevent soap (sodium stearate, \(\mathrm{C_{17}H_{35}COONa}\)) from lathering by precipitating insoluble calcium/magnesium stearate scum, and deposit boiler scale that reduces thermal efficiency.

4.4 Heavy Water (\(\mathrm{D_2O}\))

Prepared by exhaustive electrolysis of ordinary water or as a fertiliser-industry byproduct, \(\mathrm{D_2O}\) has a higher melting point (\(276.8\,\mathrm{K}\)), boiling point (\(374.4\,\mathrm{K}\)), and temperature of maximum density (\(284.2\,\mathrm{K}\)) than \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). It serves as a moderator in nuclear reactors to slow fast neutrons and is used in isotopic exchange studies and synthesis of deuterated compounds:

\[\mathrm{CaC_2} + 2\mathrm{D_2O} \longrightarrow \mathrm{C_2D_2} + \mathrm{Ca(OD)_2} \qquad \mathrm{SO_3} + \mathrm{D_2O} \longrightarrow \mathrm{D_2SO_4} \qquad \mathrm{Al_4C_3} + 12\mathrm{D_2O} \longrightarrow 3\mathrm{CD_4} + 4\mathrm{Al(OD)_3}\]

5. Hydrogen Peroxide (\(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\)): Preparation, Structure, and Redox Dualism

5.1 Preparation and Concentration

  1. Acidification of Barium Peroxide: \[\mathrm{BaO_2\cdot 8H_2O(s)} + \mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{BaSO_4(s)} + \mathrm{H_2O_2(aq)} + 8\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\]
  2. Electrolytic Oxidation of Acidified Sulphate: At high current density, \(\mathrm{HSO_4^-}\) oxidises to peroxodisulphate (\(\mathrm{HO_3SOOSO_3H}\)), which hydrolyses to \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\). Using \(\mathrm{K_2S_2O_8}\) with \(\mathrm{D_2O}\) prepares \(\mathrm{D_2O_2}\) in the laboratory: \[\mathrm{K_2S_2O_8(s)} + 2\mathrm{D_2O(l)} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{KDSO_4(aq)} + \mathrm{D_2O_2(l)}\]
  3. Industrial Auto-Oxidation of 2-Alkylanthraquinols: Oxidation of 2-ethylanthraquinol by air (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)) gives \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\), while the quinone is reduced back with \(\mathrm{H_2}/\mathrm{Pd}\). Extraction and reduced-pressure distillation yield \(30\%\), then \(85\%\) \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\); freezing removes remaining water.

5.2 Non-Planar "Open-Book" Structure

Hydrogen peroxide has a non-planar structure in both gas and solid phases, with the two \(\mathrm{O-H}\) bonds lying in planes inclined at a dihedral angle that decreases in the solid state due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

5.3 Volume Strength, Redox Behaviour, and Storage

A "\(V\)-volume" \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) solution means \(1\,\mathrm{mL}\) (or \(1\,\mathrm{L}\)) of that solution decomposes to liberate \(V\,\mathrm{mL}\) (or \(V\,\mathrm{L}\)) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) gas at STP (\(22.7\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}, 273\,\mathrm{K}\)):

\[2\mathrm{H_2O_2(l)} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}\] \[\text{Molarity }(M) = \frac{\text{Volume Strength }(V)}{11.35} \quad (\text{using } 22.7\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\text{ at STP}) \qquad \text{Normality }(N) = \frac{V}{5.675}\]

Because oxygen in \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) is in the intermediate \(-1\) oxidation state, \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) acts as both an oxidising agent and a reducing agent in acidic and basic media:

  • Oxidising action (acidic): \(2\mathrm{Fe^{2+}} + 2\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Fe^{3+}} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\); \(\quad \mathrm{PbS(s)} + 4\mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{PbSO_4(s)} + 4\mathrm{H_2O}\)
  • Reducing action (acidic): \(2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 6\mathrm{H^+} + 5\mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + 8\mathrm{H_2O} + 5\mathrm{O_2}\); \(\quad \mathrm{HOCl} + \mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_3O^+} + \mathrm{Cl^-} + \mathrm{O_2}\)
  • Oxidising action (basic): \(2\mathrm{Fe^{2+}} + \mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Fe^{3+}} + 2\mathrm{OH^-}\); \(\quad \mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + \mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mn^{4+}} + 2\mathrm{OH^-}\)
  • Reducing action (basic): \(\mathrm{I_2} + \mathrm{H_2O_2} + 2\mathrm{OH^-} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{I^-} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{O_2}\); \(\quad 2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 3\mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{MnO_2} + 3\mathrm{O_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} + 2\mathrm{OH^-}\)

Storage: Light, metal surfaces, dust, and trace alkali from glass catalyse \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) decomposition. Therefore, it is stored in wax-lined glass or plastic bottles in the dark, with urea added as a stabiliser.

Worked Examples

Calculating Volume Strength and Molarity of Hydrogen Peroxide

Problem (Assumed Sample): An aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) is labelled "\(20\text{ volume}\)". Assuming STP molar volume of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) is \(22.7\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\), calculate (a) the molarity of the solution and (b) its mass-by-volume percentage strength (\(\%\,\mathrm{w/v}\)).

Solution:

  1. By definition, \(1\,\mathrm{L}\) of \(20\text{ volume}\) \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) liberates \(20\,\mathrm{L}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) at STP.
  2. Moles of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) produced per litre of solution: \[n(\mathrm{O_2}) = \frac{20\,\mathrm{L}}{22.7\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}} \approx 0.881\,\mathrm{mol}\]
  3. From \(2\mathrm{H_2O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{O_2}\), \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) yield \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\). Thus: \[\text{Molarity } (M) = 2 \times 0.881 = 1.762\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} \approx 1.76\,\mathrm{M}\]
  4. Mass of \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) per litre (molar mass \(= 34\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)): \[1.762\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} \times 34\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 59.91\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}} \approx 6.0\,\mathrm{g\text{ per }100\,\mathrm{mL}} = 6.0\%\,\mathrm{w/v}\]

Determining Water Hardness and Ion-Exchange Resin Requirement

Problem (Assumed Sample): A \(1000\,\mathrm{L}\) tank of well water contains \(0.015\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Ca(HCO_3)_2}\) and \(0.025\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{MgSO_4}\). (a) Identify the types of hardness present. (b) Calculate the total moles of \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) ions exchanged when this water passes completely through a sodium-form cation-exchange resin (\(\mathrm{RNa}\)).

Solution:

  1. \(\mathrm{Ca(HCO_3)_2}\) causes temporary hardness, while \(\mathrm{MgSO_4}\) causes permanent hardness.
  2. Both \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\) are divalent cations (\(\mathrm{M^{2+}}\)). Total moles of \(\mathrm{M^{2+}}\) ions: \[n(\mathrm{M^{2+}}) = 0.015 + 0.025 = 0.040\,\mathrm{mol}\]
  3. From the exchange stoichiometry \(2\mathrm{RNa(s)} + \mathrm{M^{2+}(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{R_2M(s)} + 2\mathrm{Na^+(aq)}\), each mole of \(\mathrm{M^{2+}}\) exchanges \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Na^+}\): \[n(\mathrm{Na^+}) = 2 \times 0.040\,\mathrm{mol} = 0.080\,\mathrm{mol}\]

Predicting Hydride Class, Lewis Character, and Boiling Point Trends

Problem: Classify \(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{SiH_4}\), \(\mathrm{NaH}\), and \(\mathrm{TiH_{1.6}}\) by hydride type. Identify which acts as a Lewis acid, which acts as a Lewis base, and explain why \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) boils at a higher temperature than \(\mathrm{PH_3}\).

Solution:

  • \(\mathrm{NaH}\): Ionic (saline) hydride.
  • \(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\): Covalent, electron-deficient hydride; acts as a Lewis acid.
  • \(\mathrm{SiH_4}\): Covalent, electron-precise hydride (tetrahedral).
  • \(\mathrm{NH_3}\): Covalent, electron-rich hydride (1 lone pair on \(\mathrm{N}\)); acts as a Lewis base.
  • \(\mathrm{TiH_{1.6}}\): Metallic (non-stoichiometric/interstitial) hydride.
  • Boiling point: Although \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) has a lower molar mass than \(\mathrm{PH_3}\), the high electronegativity of nitrogen produces strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding in \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), elevating its boiling point above that of \(\mathrm{PH_3}\).

Stoichiometry of Syngas and the Water-Gas Shift Reaction

Problem (Assumed Values): Suppose \(4.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)}\) undergoes complete steam reforming over a nickel catalyst at \(1270\,\mathrm{K}\), and all resulting \(\mathrm{CO(g)}\) is then converted via the water-gas shift reaction at \(673\,\mathrm{K}\). Calculate the total moles of \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) obtained.

Solution:

  1. Steam reforming: \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO(g)} + 3\mathrm{H_2(g)}\).
    From \(4.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), we obtain \(4.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{CO}\) and \(3 \times 4.0 = 12.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2}\).
  2. Water-gas shift: \(\mathrm{CO(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}\).
    Converting \(4.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{CO}\) yields an additional \(4.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2}\).
  3. Total \(\mathrm{H_2}\) produced \(= 12.0 + 4.0 = 16.0\,\mathrm{mol}\).

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which statement accurately describes the isotopes of hydrogen?

  • (A) Tritium emits \(\alpha\)-particles with a half-life of \(12.33\,\mathrm{years}\).
  • (B) Protium and deuterium have identical boiling points because their electronic configurations are identical.
  • (C) Deuterium has one neutron in its nucleus and reacts more slowly than protium due to a higher bond dissociation enthalpy.
  • (D) Terrestrial hydrogen contains roughly equal proportions of protium and deuterium.
Answer: (C)
Solution: Deuterium (\(^2_1\mathrm{H}\)) has 1 proton and 1 neutron. Because the \(\mathrm{D-D}\) bond dissociation enthalpy (\(443.35\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) exceeds that of \(\mathrm{H-H}\) (\(435.88\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), deuterium reacts more slowly. Tritium is a low-energy \(\beta^-\) emitter, boiling points differ significantly (\(20.39\,\mathrm{K}\) vs. \(23.67\,\mathrm{K}\)), and deuterium abundance is only \(0.0156\%\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Fire Extinguisher Trap: Never use \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to extinguish fires caused by saline hydrides (\(\mathrm{NaH}\), \(\mathrm{LiH}\)) reacting with water; saline hydrides reduce \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).
  • \(\mathrm{F_2}\) vs. \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) Redox: Fluorine does not liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from water; it oxidises water to \(\mathrm{O_2}\) (and some \(\mathrm{O_3}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{D_2O}\) for Drinking: Heavy water (\(\mathrm{D_2O}\)) is injurious to living organisms because slower \(\mathrm{O-D}\) bond-breaking rates disrupt enzyme-catalysed metabolic reactions.
  • Demineralised vs. Drinking Water: Completely demineralised water lacks essential dissolved minerals (\(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\), \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\), \(\mathrm{Na^+}\)) and is unsuitable for drinking until remineralised.
  • \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) Volume Strength Factor: Use \(22.7\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\) at STP (\(1\,\mathrm{bar}, 273\,\mathrm{K}\)) for NCERT-aligned calculations (\(M = V/11.35\)).

Concise Final Recap

Hydrogen (\(1\mathrm{s}^1\)) stands apart in the periodic table because of its high ionization enthalpy and the vanishingly small size of \(\mathrm{H}^+\). Its three isotopes—protium, deuterium, and radioactive tritium—share chemical behaviour but differ in reaction rates and physical constants. Dihydrogen, with the strongest homonuclear single bond (\(435.88\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), is produced industrially via syngas and the water-gas shift reaction and forms saline, molecular, and interstitial hydrides. Water's bent polar geometry (\(104.5^\circ\)) and extensive hydrogen bonding govern ice's open lattice, amphoteric/hydrolytic reactivity, and hardness equilibria, while hydrogen peroxide's non-planar structure and \(-1\) oxygen oxidation state make it a versatile dual redox reagent and clean industrial oxidant.
