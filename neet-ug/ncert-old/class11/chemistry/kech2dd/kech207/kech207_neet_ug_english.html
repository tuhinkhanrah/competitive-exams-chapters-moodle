Environmental Chemistry

Atmospheric Pollution, Water & Soil Quality, Waste Management, and Green Chemistry

Learning Objectives and Overview

Environmental chemistry examines the origin, transport, chemical reactions, biological effects, and ultimate fate of chemical species in air, water, and soil. Because an average human inhales roughly 12 to 15 times more air by mass than the food consumed daily, even trace atmospheric pollutants exert significant physiological impacts.

NEET UG Focus Areas
  • Distinguish between tropospheric and stratospheric pollution, including classical vs. photochemical smog and the Antarctic ozone hole mechanism.
  • Memorize key chemical reactions for acid rain, \(\mathrm{NO_x}\)/\(\mathrm{SO_2}\) oxidation, carboxyhaemoglobin formation, and CFC-driven ozone depletion.
  • Master international drinking water thresholds (fluoride, nitrate, lead, sulphate, and heavy metals) and Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) benchmarks.
  • Understand biomagnification of persistent pesticides and real-world applications of Green Chemistry.

Atmospheric Structure and Tropospheric Pollution

Earth's atmosphere consists of concentric layers of varying density. Two regions are central to environmental chemistry:

  • Troposphere (0 to ~10 km above sea level): The lowest, turbulent, dusty layer containing air, clouds, and water vapour where humans and other organisms live.
  • Stratosphere (~10 to 50 km above sea level): A drier layer above the troposphere containing dinitrogen (\(\mathrm{N_2}\)), dioxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)), and the protective ozone (\(\mathrm{O_3}\)) shield that absorbs roughly 99.5% of incoming solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation.

1. Gaseous Air Pollutants in the Troposphere

  • Oxides of Sulphur (\(\mathrm{SO_2}\), \(\mathrm{SO_3}\)): Released by burning sulphur-containing fossil fuels. Even low \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) levels trigger respiratory illnesses (asthma, bronchitis, emphysema) and eye irritation, while high concentrations stiffen flower buds and cause them to fall off. Uncatalysed oxidation of \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) in air is slow, but particulate matter, ozone, or hydrogen peroxide accelerates conversion to \(\mathrm{SO_3}\) and sulphuric acid:
    \[2\mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \xrightarrow{\text{particulates}} 2\mathrm{SO_3(g)}\] \[\mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_3(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{SO_3(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}\] \[\mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O_2(l)} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq)}\]
  • Oxides of Nitrogen (\(\mathrm{NO}\), \(\mathrm{NO_2}\)): At ambient temperatures, \(\mathrm{N_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) do not react. However, lightning at high altitudes and fossil-fuel combustion inside automobile engines (around \(1483\,\mathrm{K}\)) drive the formation of nitric oxide (\(\mathrm{NO}\)), which rapidly oxidises to nitrogen dioxide (\(\mathrm{NO_2}\)):
    \[\mathrm{N_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \xrightarrow{1483\,\mathrm{K}} 2\mathrm{NO(g)}\] \[2\mathrm{NO(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NO_2(g)}\]
    In the stratosphere, \(\mathrm{NO}\) reacts even faster with ozone: \(\mathrm{NO(g)} + \mathrm{O_3(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{NO_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}\). High \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) causes the reddish-brown haze in congested traffic, damages plant leaves, slows photosynthesis, and irritates human lungs (especially in children).
  • Hydrocarbons: Emitted from incomplete combustion of automobile fuels. Many are carcinogenic and injure plants by accelerating ageing, tissue breakdown, and shedding of leaves, flowers, and twigs.
  • Carbon Monoxide (\(\mathrm{CO}\)): A colourless, odourless, highly toxic gas from incomplete combustion of carbon-based fuels (vehicle exhaust, coal, firewood). \(\mathrm{CO}\) binds to haemoglobin to form carboxyhaemoglobin, a complex approximately 300 times more stable than oxyhaemoglobin. When carboxyhaemoglobin reaches 3–4% in blood, oxygen delivery drops sharply, causing headaches, weak eyesight, nervousness, and cardiovascular disorders. In pregnant smokers, elevated \(\mathrm{CO}\) increases the risk of premature birth and developmental defects.
  • Carbon Dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)): Confined to the troposphere (normally ~0.03% by volume), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is released by respiration, fossil-fuel combustion, limestone decomposition in cement manufacture, and volcanic eruptions. Green plants remove excess \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) via photosynthesis, but deforestation and fuel burning disrupt this equilibrium.

Greenhouse Effect, Global Warming, and Acid Rain

Greenhouse Effect and Global Warming

About 75% of solar energy reaching Earth is absorbed by the surface, warming it, while the remainder radiates back as infrared (thermal) radiation. Greenhouse gases—principally \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), along with methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)), water vapour, nitrous oxide (\(\mathrm{N_2O}\)), ozone (\(\mathrm{O_3}\)), and chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)—are transparent to incoming sunlight but absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation. This natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth habitable. However, excessive \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (above 0.03%), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) (from anaerobic decay in paddy fields, coal mines, and garbage dumps), and \(\mathrm{N_2O}\) (from chemical fertilizers and fossil fuels) amplify warming, threatening polar ice melt, coastal flooding, and wider spread of vector-borne diseases such as malaria and dengue.

Acid Rain

Clean rainwater is naturally slightly acidic with a \(\mathrm{pH}\) of 5.6 because atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) dissolves to form weak carbonic acid:

\[\mathrm{H_2O(l)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2CO_3(aq)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+(aq)} + \mathrm{HCO_3^-(aq)}\]

When rainwater \(\mathrm{pH}\) drops below 5.6, it is classified as acid rain. Atmospheric \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) undergo particulate-catalysed oxidation and react with water to form strong acids:

\[2\mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq)}\] \[4\mathrm{NO_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{HNO_3(aq)}\]

Acidic species settle as wet deposition (acid rain, fog, snow, or ammonium-salt aerosols) or dry deposition (direct absorption of \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) on ground surfaces). Acid rain leaches soil nutrients, harms aquatic ecosystems, corrodes metal water pipes (leaching \(\mathrm{Fe}\), \(\mathrm{Pb}\), and \(\mathrm{Cu}\) into drinking water), and erodes marble monuments such as the Taj Mahal:

\[\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)} + \mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CaSO_4(s)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\]

Control measures include switching to low-sulphur fuels or natural gas (as implemented in the Taj Trapezium Zone), adding powdered limestone to neutralise acidic soils, and fitting automobiles with catalytic converters (ceramic honeycombs coated with \(\mathrm{Pd}\), \(\mathrm{Pt}\), and \(\mathrm{Rh}\) operating at \(573\,\mathrm{K}\)) that convert \(\mathrm{CO}\), unburnt hydrocarbons, and \(\mathrm{NO_x}\) into \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{N_2}\).

Particulate Pollutants and Smog

Particulate pollutants are tiny solid particles or liquid droplets suspended in air. They are grouped into:

  • Viable particulates: Living microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, moulds, and algae that can trigger allergies or plant diseases.
  • Non-viable particulates: Classified by origin and size:
    • Smoke: Solid or solid-liquid particles from combustion of organic matter (fossil fuels, garbage, cigarettes).
    • Dust: Fine solid particles (> \(1\,\mathrm{µm}\) diameter) from crushing, grinding, sandblasting, coal pulverisation, and fly ash.
    • Mists: Liquid spray droplets or condensed vapours (e.g., sulphuric acid mist, drifted herbicide sprays).
    • Fumes: Condensed vapours from sublimation, distillation, boiling, and metallurgical reactions.

Particulate health risk depends on size: particles larger than 5 microns lodge in the nasal passage, whereas particles around 10 microns enter the lungs easily. Air-borne lead (historically from leaded petrol) interferes with red blood cell development and maturation.

Mechanism and Control of Photochemical Smog

In sunlit urban air containing vehicle exhaust (\(\mathrm{NO}\) and unburnt hydrocarbons), \(\mathrm{NO}\) oxidises to \(\mathrm{NO_2}\), initiating a photochemical chain reaction:

\[\mathrm{NO_2(g)} \xrightarrow{h\nu} \mathrm{NO(g)} + \mathrm{O(g)}\] \[\mathrm{O(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{O_3(g)}\] \[\mathrm{NO(g)} + \mathrm{O_3(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{NO_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}\]

Both \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_3}\) are strong oxidants that react with unburnt hydrocarbons to yield eye- and throat-irritating compounds such as formaldehyde (\(\mathrm{CH_2=O}\)), acrolein (\(\mathrm{CH_2=CHCH=O}\)), and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN) (\(\mathrm{CH_3C(=O)OONO_2}\)). Photochemical smog causes rubber cracking, metal and stone corrosion, and severe respiratory distress. Control relies on catalytic converters to curb primary precursors (\(\mathrm{NO_x}\) and hydrocarbons) and planting \(\mathrm{NO_x}\)-metabolising species such as Pinus, Juniperus, Quercus, Pyrus, and Vitis.

Stratospheric Pollution and Ozone Depletion

Stratospheric ozone absorbs harmful UV radiation (\(\lambda \approx 255\,\mathrm{nm}\)) that causes skin cancer (melanoma), cataracts, phytoplankton death, and plant protein damage. Ozone is maintained in dynamic equilibrium:

\[\mathrm{O_2(g)} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{UV}} \mathrm{O(g)} + \mathrm{O(g)} \qquad \mathrm{O(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \xrightleftharpoons{\mathrm{UV}} \mathrm{O_3(g)}\]

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs or freons)—non-flammable, non-toxic industrial chemicals used in refrigeration, air conditioning, and foam production—drift into the stratosphere, where intense UV radiation cleaves \(\mathrm{C–Cl}\) bonds to release chlorine free radicals (\(\dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\)):

\[\mathrm{CF_2Cl_2(g)} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{UV}} \dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\mathrm{(g)} + \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{F_2Cl(g)}\] \[\dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\mathrm{(g)} + \mathrm{O_3(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl\dot{O}(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}\] \[\mathrm{Cl\dot{O}(g)} + \mathrm{O(g)} \rightarrow \dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\mathrm{(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)}\]

Because \(\dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\) is continuously regenerated, a single radical destroys thousands of ozone molecules.

The Antarctic Ozone Hole

Over Antarctica, seasonal chemistry drives severe spring ozone depletion:

  • Summer (Chlorine Sinks): \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) trap active radicals as stable reservoirs: \(\mathrm{Cl\dot{O}} + \mathrm{NO_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{ClONO_2}\) and \(\dot{\mathrm{Cl}} + \mathrm{CH_4} \rightarrow \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H_3} + \mathrm{HCl}\).
  • Winter (Polar Stratospheric Clouds, PSCs): Ice-cloud surfaces catalyse heterogeneous conversion of chlorine nitrate into photolabile species:
    \[\mathrm{ClONO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{HOCl(g)} + \mathrm{HNO_3(g)}\] \[\mathrm{ClONO_2(g)} + \mathrm{HCl(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl_2(g)} + \mathrm{HNO_3(g)}\]
  • Spring (Sunlight Return): Solar radiation breaks up PSCs and photolyses \(\mathrm{HOCl}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) into \(\dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\) radicals (\(\mathrm{HOCl} \xrightarrow{h\nu} \dot{\mathrm{O}}\mathrm{H} + \dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\); \(\mathrm{Cl_2} \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2\dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\)), triggering rapid ozone destruction.

Water Pollution, BOD, and Drinking Water Standards

Water pollution arises from point sources (identifiable discharge pipes from factories or municipalities) and non-point sources (diffuse agricultural runoff, storm-water drainage, acid rain). Major water pollutants include:

  • Pathogens: Disease-causing bacteria from sewage and excreta, such as Escherichia coli and Streptococcus faecalis, causing gastrointestinal illness.
  • Organic Wastes and Dissolved Oxygen (DO): Cold water holds up to \(10\,\mathrm{ppm}\) of DO (compared to \(200,000\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in air). Aerobic bacteria decomposing biodegradable organic matter consume DO. If DO falls below \(6\,\mathrm{ppm}\), fish growth is inhibited. Complete DO exhaustion shifts decomposition to anaerobic bacteria, producing foul-smelling toxic compounds.
  • Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD): The mass of oxygen required by bacteria to biologically break down organic matter in a given volume of water. Clean water has BOD < \(5\,\mathrm{ppm}\), whereas highly polluted water has BOD \(\ge 17\,\mathrm{ppm}\).
  • Chemical Pollutants and Eutrophication: Heavy metals (\(\mathrm{Cd}\), \(\mathrm{Hg}\), \(\mathrm{Ni}\)) bioaccumulate and damage the kidneys, liver, and central nervous system. Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) are suspected carcinogens. Phosphate-rich fertilizers and detergents stimulate excessive algal blooms that cover water surfaces, block oxygen replenishment, and suffocate aquatic animal life—a biodiversity-destroying process called eutrophication.

Soil Pollution, Biomagnification, and Waste Management

Pesticides, Herbicides, and Biomagnification

Before World War II, natural pest deterrents such as nicotine (from tobacco plants) were common. Later, synthetic organochlorines like DDT, Aldrin, and Dieldrin were widely adopted. Because these compounds are water-insoluble and non-biodegradable, they persist in soil and undergo biomagnification—increasing in concentration by roughly 10-fold at each successive trophic level of a food chain, eventually causing severe metabolic disorders in top consumers. DDT is now banned in India. Less persistent alternatives such as organophosphates and carbamates are biodegradable but act as potent nerve toxins to humans. Herbicides such as sodium chlorate (\(\mathrm{NaClO_3}\)) and sodium arsenite (\(\mathrm{Na_2AsO_3}\)) decompose within months but remain toxic to mammals.

Industrial Waste and Waste Management

Industrial solid wastes are classified as biodegradable (cotton mills, paper mills, food-processing units) and non-biodegradable (fly ash from thermal power plants, blast furnace and steel melting slag from steel plants, mud and tailings from \(\mathrm{Al}/\mathrm{Zn}/\mathrm{Cu}\) plants, gypsum from fertilizer industries). Fly ash and steel slag are increasingly repurposed by the cement industry, while toxic wastes undergo controlled incineration. Innovative recycling converts plastic waste into lead-free, high-octane "green fuel" and synthetic textiles, and anaerobic bacterial digestion of sorted organic garbage produces methane biogas for electricity plus nutrient-rich manure.

Structured sanitation initiatives such as the Swachh Bharat Abhiyan—comprising Swachh Bharat Mission–Urban (SBM-U, targeting 100% scientific solid waste management and open-defecation-free cities) and Swachh Bharat Mission–Gramin (SBM-G, improving rural hygiene and sanitation)—emphasise segregation of biodegradable waste (composting/landfills) from non-biodegradable recyclables (plastics, glass, metals).

Green Chemistry in Daily Life

Green chemistry applies chemical principles to design products and processes that minimise or eliminate hazardous substances, waste generation, and energy consumption. Choosing non-toxic solvents (such as water, which has high specific heat, low volatility, and zero carcinogenicity) and achieving near-100% yield prevents pollution at the source. The 2005 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (awarded to Yves Chauvin, Robert H. Grubbs, and Richard R. Schrock for olefin metathesis) highlighted how smart catalytic synthesis dramatically cuts hazardous waste.

Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 • Assessing Water Quality Using BOD and DO

Scenario (Assumed Values): Water samples are collected from three lakes—Lake P, Lake Q, and Lake R—to evaluate organic pollution. Assume the measured Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) values are \(3.2\,\mathrm{ppm}\) for Lake P, \(9.5\,\mathrm{ppm}\) for Lake Q, and \(21.0\,\mathrm{ppm}\) for Lake R, while the Dissolved Oxygen (DO) in Lake R is \(2.4\,\mathrm{ppm}\). Classify the water quality of each lake and predict the ecological state of Lake R.

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  1. Compare BOD against standard benchmarks: Clean water has \(\mathrm{BOD} < 5\,\mathrm{ppm}\), whereas highly polluted water has \(\mathrm{BOD} \ge 17\,\mathrm{ppm}\). Thus, Lake P (\(3.2\,\mathrm{ppm}\)) is clean, Lake Q (\(9.5\,\mathrm{ppm}\)) is moderately polluted, and Lake R (\(21.0\,\mathrm{ppm}\)) is severely polluted with biodegradable organic matter.
  2. Evaluate Dissolved Oxygen: Fish growth is inhibited whenever DO drops below \(6\,\mathrm{ppm}\). At \(2.4\,\mathrm{ppm}\), Lake R cannot support normal fish populations; aerobic aquatic life will die off and anaerobic bacteria will take over, generating foul-smelling metabolites.
Worked Example 2 • Interpreting Drinking Water Ion Standards

Scenario (Assumed Values): A rural well-water sample is assumed to contain \(\mathrm{F^-} = 2.8\,\mathrm{ppm}\), \(\mathrm{NO_3^-} = 68\,\mathrm{ppm}\), \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}} = 220\,\mathrm{ppm}\), and \(\mathrm{Pb} = 20\,\mathrm{ppb}\). Identify which ions exceed international drinking water limits and state the specific health hazards associated with those excess ions.

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  1. Fluoride (\(\mathrm{F^-}\)): \(2.8\,\mathrm{ppm}\) exceeds the \(2\,\mathrm{ppm}\) threshold (optimum is \(1\,\mathrm{ppm}\)). This causes brown mottling of teeth (though it is below the \(10\,\mathrm{ppm}\) level associated with severe bone damage).
  2. Nitrate (\(\mathrm{NO_3^-}\)): \(68\,\mathrm{ppm}\) exceeds the maximum permissible limit of \(50\,\mathrm{ppm}\), posing a risk of methemoglobinemia ('blue baby' syndrome).
  3. Sulphate and Lead: \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) (\(220\,\mathrm{ppm} < 500\,\mathrm{ppm}\)) and \(\mathrm{Pb}\) (\(20\,\mathrm{ppb} < 50\,\mathrm{ppb}\)) are within safe limits.
Worked Example 3 • Distinguishing Smog Types from Environmental Conditions

Scenario (Assumed Values): City X experiences dense morning haze during a cold, damp winter week (\(4\,\mathrm{^\circ C}\), high humidity) with elevated coal-burning \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) emissions. City Y experiences stinging eye irritation and brownish haze on a bright, dry summer afternoon (\(36\,\mathrm{^\circ C}\)) with heavy petrol-vehicle traffic. Identify the smog type and chemical nature in each city.

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  1. City X: Cool, humid climate + smoke + fog + \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) defines classical smog, which is chemically a reducing mixture (reducing smog).
  2. City Y: Warm, dry, sunny climate + sunlight acting on \(\mathrm{NO_x}\) and unburnt hydrocarbons defines photochemical smog, which is rich in \(\mathrm{O_3}\), \(\mathrm{NO_2}\), and PAN and is chemically an oxidising smog.
Worked Example 4 • Calculating Biomagnification Across Trophic Levels

Scenario (Assumed Values): Assume an aquatic ecosystem is contaminated with a non-biodegradable organochlorine pesticide at an initial concentration of \(0.002\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in water. If the pollutant concentrates 10-fold at each successive trophic level along the chain: Water → Aquatic Plants → Small Fish → Large Fish → Fish-Eating Birds, calculate the concentration in the birds.

Step-by-Step Calculation:

  • Water: \(0.002\,\mathrm{ppm}\)
  • Aquatic Plants (×10): \(0.02\,\mathrm{ppm}\)
  • Small Fish (×10): \(0.2\,\mathrm{ppm}\)
  • Large Fish (×10): \(2.0\,\mathrm{ppm}\)
  • Fish-Eating Birds (×10): \(20.0\,\mathrm{ppm}\)

Result: The pesticide concentration reaches \(20.0\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in the top consumer—a \(10^4\)-fold amplification over water.

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding carbon monoxide (\(\mathrm{CO}\)) poisoning is correct?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{CO}\) binds to haemoglobin to form a complex that is about 300 times less stable than oxyhaemoglobin.
  • (B) Carboxyhaemoglobin concentration of 3–4% in blood significantly impairs oxygen delivery to tissues.
  • (C) \(\mathrm{CO}\) is primarily produced by complete combustion of fossil fuels in modern engines.
  • (D) \(\mathrm{CO}\) is a pungent, reddish-brown gas that reacts with rainwater to form acid rain.
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: \(\mathrm{CO}\) is a colourless, odourless gas produced by incomplete combustion. It forms carboxyhaemoglobin, which is about 300 times more stable than oxyhaemoglobin. Reaching 3–4% carboxyhaemoglobin in blood drastically reduces oxygen-carrying capacity.

Practice Question 2 • Single-Correct MCQ

In the Antarctic stratosphere, polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs) formed during winter accelerate spring ozone depletion primarily because they:

  • (A) Directly absorb UV radiation of wavelength \(255\,\mathrm{nm}\) to release \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  • (B) Provide a surface for the hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{ClONO_2}\) to \(\mathrm{HOCl}\) and reaction with \(\mathrm{HCl}\) to form \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\).
  • (C) Convert \(\mathrm{CF_2Cl_2}\) into inert \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) sinks.
  • (D) Prevent sunlight from reaching the stratosphere during spring.
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: During Antarctic winter, PSCs provide surfaces on which \(\mathrm{ClONO_2}\) reacts with \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) to yield \(\mathrm{HOCl}\) and with \(\mathrm{HCl}\) to yield \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\). When spring sunlight returns, \(\mathrm{HOCl}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) photolyse to release \(\dot{\mathrm{Cl}}\) radicals that destroy \(\mathrm{O_3}\).

Practice Question 3 • Single-Correct MCQ

Which set of species consists exclusively of secondary pollutants formed during photochemical smog?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{SO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CO}\), and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{O_3}\), acrolein, and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{NO}\), \(\mathrm{SO_3}\), and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{CF_2Cl_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), and \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Primary pollutants (\(\mathrm{NO_x}\) and unburnt hydrocarbons) react under sunlight to generate secondary pollutants characteristic of photochemical smog: ozone (\(\mathrm{O_3}\)), formaldehyde (\(\mathrm{CH_2=O}\)), acrolein (\(\mathrm{CH_2=CHCH=O}\)), and PAN.

Practice Question 4 • Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Clean rainwater has a \(\mathrm{pH}\) of 5.6, and rainwater with \(\mathrm{pH}\) below 5.6 is termed acid rain.
Reason (R): Atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) dissolves in rainwater to form carbonic acid, whereas \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) oxidise and dissolve to form \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) and \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\).

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: Dissolution of atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) produces weak \(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\), giving unpolluted rain a \(\mathrm{pH}\) of 5.6. Additional strong acids (\(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) and \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\)) formed from \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) lower the \(\mathrm{pH}\) below 5.6, causing acid rain.

Practice Question 5 • Statement-Based

Statement I: Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) of clean water is less than \(5\,\mathrm{ppm}\), whereas highly polluted water has a BOD of \(17\,\mathrm{ppm}\) or more.
Statement II: If the dissolved oxygen (DO) concentration in a lake drops below \(6\,\mathrm{ppm}\), the growth of fish is inhibited.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: Both statements are exact standard benchmarks: clean water has \(\mathrm{BOD} < 5\,\mathrm{ppm}\), heavily polluted water has \(\mathrm{BOD} \ge 17\,\mathrm{ppm}\), and fish growth is inhibited when \(\mathrm{DO} < 6\,\mathrm{ppm}\).

Practice Question 6 • Single-Correct MCQ

Adding soluble fluoride to drinking water up to \(1\,\mathrm{ppm}\) strengthens tooth enamel because \(\mathrm{F^-}\) ions:

  • (A) Precipitate calcium carbonate on the tooth surface.
  • (B) Convert hydroxyapatite into harder fluorapatite.
  • (C) Neutralise sulphuric acid formed in saliva.
  • (D) Replace phosphate ions in bone marrow.
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Fluoride ions at ~\(1\,\mathrm{ppm}\) convert tooth enamel hydroxyapatite \([\mathrm{3Ca_3(PO_4)_2 \cdot Ca(OH)_2}]\) into much harder fluorapatite \([\mathrm{3Ca_3(PO_4)_2 \cdot CaF_2}]\), preventing dental decay.

Practice Question 10 • Single-Correct MCQ

Which statement about eutrophication is incorrect?

  • (A) It is triggered by nutrient enrichment of water bodies, especially by phosphate-containing fertilizers.
  • (B) Dense algal blooms cover the water surface and deplete dissolved oxygen.
  • (C) It promotes aerobic fish diversity by increasing photosynthetic oxygen supply throughout the water column.
  • (D) It leads to anaerobic conditions, foul-smelling decay, and loss of biodiversity.
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Eutrophication causes profuse surface algal growth that blocks light and depletes dissolved oxygen as algae die and decompose, killing fish and reducing biodiversity rather than promoting fish diversity.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Confusing Classical and Photochemical Smog: Classical smog is reducing (cool, humid, \(\mathrm{SO_2}\)), whereas photochemical smog is oxidising (warm, dry, sunny, \(\mathrm{O_3}\) + \(\mathrm{NO_x}\) + PAN).
  • Mixing Up ppm and ppb Limits: Nitrate limit is \(50\,\mathrm{ppm}\), whereas Lead limit is \(50\,\mathrm{ppb}\) (1,000 times smaller). Cadmium limit is \(0.005\,\mathrm{ppm}\) (\(5\,\mathrm{ppb}\)).
  • Normal Rain vs. Acid Rain pH: Normal rain is already acidic (\(\mathrm{pH} = 5.6\)) due to \(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\); only \(\mathrm{pH} < 5.6\) is acid rain.
  • Greenhouse Effect vs. Ozone Depletion: Greenhouse warming occurs in the troposphere (trapping outgoing IR radiation), whereas ozone depletion occurs in the stratosphere (allowing excess incoming UV radiation).
Concise Final Recap

Environmental chemistry connects atmospheric reactions (\(\mathrm{SO_2}/\mathrm{NO_x}\) acid rain, \(\mathrm{CO_2}/\mathrm{CH_4}\) greenhouse warming, \(\mathrm{O_3}\)/PAN photochemical smog, and CFC-driven Antarctic ozone depletion), aquatic health (\(\mathrm{DO} < 6\,\mathrm{ppm}\) stress, \(\mathrm{BOD} < 5\,\mathrm{ppm}\) clean vs. \(\ge 17\,\mathrm{ppm}\) polluted, eutrophication, and strict \(\mathrm{F^-}/\mathrm{NO_3^-}/\mathrm{Pb}\) limits), soil persistence (10× trophic biomagnification of organochlorines), and sustainable solutions (waste segregation/recycling and Green Chemistry replacements such as liquid \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\), \(\mathrm{Pd(II)}/\mathrm{Cu(II)}\) aqueous catalysis, and tamarind kernel powder).
