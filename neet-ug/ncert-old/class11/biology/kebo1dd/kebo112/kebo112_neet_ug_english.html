Mineral Nutrition in Plants

Essential Elements, Absorption Mechanisms, Deficiency & Toxicity, and Nitrogen Metabolism

Learning Objectives and Overview

Green plants synthesize organic carbohydrates from carbon dioxide and water, yet their growth, structural integrity, enzyme catalysis, and reproduction depend equally on inorganic mineral ions absorbed from the soil. Understanding how plants acquire, utilize, and recycle these inorganic nutrients is a high-yield topic for NEET UG.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Explain the principles and experimental design of hydroponics for identifying essential nutrients.
  • Apply the three criteria of essentiality to distinguish essential elements from beneficial or non-essential elements.
  • Classify macronutrients and micronutrients by tissue concentration, absorbed ionic forms, and physiological roles.
  • Predict the location of deficiency symptoms using nutrient mobility and interpret micronutrient toxicity.
  • Compare passive apoplastic uptake and active symplastic absorption of mineral ions.
  • Trace the nitrogen cycle, biological nitrogen fixation by nitrogenase, nodule formation, and ammonia assimilation into amino acids and amides.

1. Experimental Methods and Criteria of Essentiality

Hydroponics: Soil-Free Nutrient Culture

In 1860, German botanist Julius von Sachs demonstrated that plants can grow to full maturity in a defined aqueous mineral solution without soil. This technique, called hydroponics, isolates the exact mineral requirements of a plant by systematically adding, omitting, or varying the concentration of individual salts.

Two experimental safeguards are mandatory in hydroponic research:

  • Purified water and analytical-grade mineral salts: Trace impurities in tap water or unrefined salts can mask deficiency symptoms of micronutrients required in minute quantities.
  • Continuous aeration: Root cells require dissolved oxygen for aerobic respiration, which supplies the ATP needed for active mineral uptake. Poor aeration leads to root hypoxia and stunted growth.

Beyond laboratory research, commercial hydroponics is widely used to cultivate high-value crops such as tomato, seedless cucumber, and lettuce.

Criteria for Essentiality

Although plants can absorb more than 60 elements from soil (including non-essential elements like gold, selenium, or radioactive strontium), only 17 elements are strictly essential. An element is classified as essential only if it satisfies all three criteria:

  1. Absolute necessity: The element is indispensable for normal growth and reproduction; without it, the plant cannot complete its life cycle or produce viable seeds.
  2. Specificity: Its requirement is specific and cannot be replaced by any other element.
  3. Direct metabolic role: The element must participate directly in plant metabolism (as a structural component, enzyme cofactor, or osmotic regulator).

In addition to the 17 essential elements, four beneficial elements—sodium (\(\mathrm{Na}\)), silicon (\(\mathrm{Si}\)), cobalt (\(\mathrm{Co}\)), and selenium (\(\mathrm{Se}\))—are required by certain higher plants for optimal performance.

2. Classification and Functions of Essential Elements

Quantitative Classification

  • Macronutrients (9 elements): Present in plant tissues in concentrations exceeding \(10\ \mathrm{mmol\ kg^{-1}}\) of dry matter. These are Carbon (\(\mathrm{C}\)), Hydrogen (\(\mathrm{H}\)), Oxygen (\(\mathrm{O}\)), Nitrogen (\(\mathrm{N}\)), Phosphorus (\(\mathrm{P}\)), Potassium (\(\mathrm{K}\)), Calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca}\)), Magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg}\)), and Sulphur (\(\mathrm{S}\)). Of these, \(\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{H}\), and \(\mathrm{O}\) are derived mainly from \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), whereas the remaining six are absorbed as mineral ions from soil.
  • Micronutrients / Trace Elements (8 elements): Needed in concentrations below \(10\ \mathrm{mmol\ kg^{-1}}\) of dry matter. These are Iron (\(\mathrm{Fe}\)), Manganese (\(\mathrm{Mn}\)), Copper (\(\mathrm{Cu}\)), Molybdenum (\(\mathrm{Mo}\)), Zinc (\(\mathrm{Zn}\)), Boron (\(\mathrm{B}\)), Chlorine (\(\mathrm{Cl}\)), and Nickel (\(\mathrm{Ni}\)).

Four Functional Categories

  1. Structural components of biomolecules: \(\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{H}\), \(\mathrm{O}\), and \(\mathrm{N}\) form the backbone of proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and carbohydrates.
  2. Components of energy-related compounds: \(\mathrm{Mg}\) in the porphyrin ring of chlorophyll; \(\mathrm{P}\) in ATP, nucleotides, and nucleic acids.
  3. Enzyme activators or inhibitors: \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\) activates both RuBisCO and PEP carboxylase; \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\) activates alcohol dehydrogenase; \(\mathrm{Mo}\) is an essential component of nitrogenase and nitrate reductase.
  4. Osmotic regulators: \(\mathrm{K^+}\) alters cell water potential and drives stomatal opening and closure.

3. Deficiency Symptoms and Micronutrient Toxicity

Critical Concentration and Nutrient Mobility

The critical concentration is the threshold concentration of an essential element below which plant growth is retarded. When an element falls below this level, the plant exhibits characteristic morphological deficiency symptoms that resolve once the nutrient is supplied.

The site where deficiency symptoms appear first depends on the mobility of the element within the plant:

  • Actively mobile elements (\(\mathrm{N}\), \(\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{Mg}\)): Broken down in older senescent tissues and exported to young, developing organs. Consequently, deficiency symptoms appear first in older leaves.
  • Relatively immobile elements (\(\mathrm{Ca}\), \(\mathrm{S}\)): Bound tightly in structural components of mature cells and cannot be easily remobilized. Deficiency symptoms appear first in young tissues.

Key Deficiency Symptoms

  • Chlorosis (yellowing due to chlorophyll loss): Caused by deficiency of \(\mathrm{N}\), \(\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{Mg}\), \(\mathrm{S}\), \(\mathrm{Fe}\), \(\mathrm{Mn}\), \(\mathrm{Zn}\), and \(\mathrm{Mo}\).
  • Necrosis (death of leaf tissue): Caused by deficiency of \(\mathrm{Ca}\), \(\mathrm{Mg}\), \(\mathrm{Cu}\), and \(\mathrm{K}\).
  • Inhibition of cell division: Caused by low levels of \(\mathrm{N}\), \(\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{S}\), and \(\mathrm{Mo}\).
  • Delayed flowering: Caused by low levels of \(\mathrm{N}\), \(\mathrm{S}\), and \(\mathrm{Mo}\).

Toxicity of Micronutrients

Micronutrients have a very narrow optimum concentration window: a slight decrease causes deficiency, whereas a moderate excess causes toxicity. Any mineral ion concentration that reduces tissue dry weight by approximately 10% is defined as toxic.

Manganese Toxicity — A Classic Competitive Inhibition Case:
Excess \(\mathrm{Mn}\) produces brown spots surrounded by chlorotic veins. Crucially, \(\mathrm{Mn}\) competes with \(\mathrm{Fe}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}\) for root uptake, competes with \(\mathrm{Mg}\) for enzyme binding sites, and inhibits \(\mathrm{Ca}\) translocation to the shoot apex. Thus, apparent \(\mathrm{Mn}\) toxicity symptoms are often secondary deficiencies of \(\mathrm{Fe}\), \(\mathrm{Mg}\), and \(\mathrm{Ca}\).

4. Mechanism of Mineral Absorption and Translocation

Biphasic Mineral Uptake

Ion absorption in roots occurs in two distinct phases:

  1. Initial Rapid Phase (Apoplastic Uptake): Passive movement of ions into the "free space" or "outer space" (cell walls and intercellular spaces) via selective transmembrane protein pores called ion-channels.
  2. Slow Second Phase (Symplastic Uptake): Movement of ions into the "inner space" (cytoplasm across the plasma membrane). This requires metabolic energy (ATP) and is an active process.

The movement of ions is termed flux: inward entry into cells is influx, while outward exit is efflux.

Long-Distance Translocation

Once absorbed into the root xylem, mineral salts are transported upward to shoots and leaves along with the ascending water stream driven by transpirational pull. This pathway is confirmed by chemical analysis of xylem sap and radioisotope tracing studies.

5. Nitrogen Cycle and Biological Nitrogen Fixation

The Nitrogen Cycle

Atmospheric nitrogen (\(\mathrm{N_2}\)) features a strong triple covalent bond (\(\mathrm{N \equiv N}\)) that plants cannot cleave directly. Nitrogen enters the soil pool through industrial fixation, electrical fixation (lightning and UV radiation forming \(\mathrm{NO}\), \(\mathrm{NO_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2O}\)), and biological fixation.

  • Ammonification: Decomposition of organic nitrogen from dead biomass into ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)).
  • Nitrification: Two-step oxidation of ammonia to nitrate by chemoautotrophic soil bacteria:
    \[2\mathrm{NH_3} + 3\mathrm{O_2} \xrightarrow{\textit{Nitrosomonas} / \textit{Nitrococcus}} 2\mathrm{NO_2^-} + 2\mathrm{H^+} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\] \[2\mathrm{NO_2^-} + \mathrm{O_2} \xrightarrow{\textit{Nitrobacter}} 2\mathrm{NO_3^-}\]
  • Denitrification: Reduction of soil nitrate back to gaseous \(\mathrm{N_2}\) by anaerobic bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Thiobacillus.

Biological Nitrogen Fixation and Root Nodule Formation

The enzyme nitrogenase—a molybdenum-iron (\(\mathrm{Mo\text{-}Fe}\)) protein complex—is found exclusively in prokaryotes. Nitrogen-fixing microbes exist in two ecological modes:

  • Free-living N₂-fixers: Aerobic bacteria (Azotobacter, Beijerinckia), anaerobic bacteria (Rhodospirillum), and cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc).
  • Symbiotic N₂-fixers: Rod-shaped Rhizobium forms root nodules on leguminous plants (pea, soybean, alfalfa, lentil, sweet clover), whereas the actinomycete Frankia forms nitrogen-fixing nodules on non-leguminous plants such as Alnus.

Sequence of Nodule Formation in Legumes:

  1. Rhizobium bacteria multiply near the rhizosphere and attach to epidermal and root hair cells.
  2. The root hair curls into a hook, and bacteria invade via a tubular infection thread extending into the root cortex.
  3. Bacteria are released into cortical cells, differentiate into specialized rod-shaped bacteroids, and stimulate division of inner cortical and pericycle cells to form the nodule.
  4. The mature nodule establishes a continuous vascular connection with the host root to exchange fixed nitrogen for host carbohydrates.

Biochemistry of Nitrogenase and Oxygen Protection

Nitrogenase catalyzes the reduction of atmospheric \(\mathrm{N_2}\) to ammonia as the first stable product:

\[\mathrm{N_2} + 8\mathrm{e^-} + 8\mathrm{H^+} + 16\mathrm{ATP} \xrightarrow{\text{Nitrogenase}} 2\mathrm{NH_3} + \mathrm{H_2} + 16\mathrm{ADP} + 16\mathrm{P_i}\]

Notice two critical NEET UG facts from this equation:

  • High ATP demand: Fixing one molecule of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) consumes \(16\ \mathrm{ATP}\), which corresponds to \(8\ \mathrm{ATP}\) per \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) produced. This energy is supplied by host cell respiration.
  • Oxygen sensitivity: Nitrogenase is irreversibly inactivated by molecular \(\mathrm{O_2}\). Although Rhizobium lives as an aerobe when free-living, inside the nodule it functions anaerobically, protected by the pink pigment leghaemoglobin, which acts as an oxygen scavenger.

Fate of Ammonia: Amino Acid Synthesis and Transport

At physiological pH, \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) is protonated to \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\) (ammonium ion), which is toxic if allowed to accumulate. Plants rapidly incorporate \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\) into amino acids through two main pathways:

  1. Reductive Amination: Ammonia reacts with \(\alpha\)-ketoglutaric acid to yield glutamic acid:
    \[\alpha\text{-ketoglutaric acid} + \mathrm{NH_4^+} + \mathrm{NADPH} \xrightarrow{\text{Glutamate dehydrogenase}} \text{Glutamate} + \mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{NADP^+}\]
  2. Transamination: Transfer of an amino group (\(-\mathrm{NH_2}\)) from a donor amino acid (chiefly glutamic acid) to the keto group of a keto acid, catalyzed by transaminase.

For long-distance transport via xylem vessels, plants convert aspartic acid and glutamic acid into the amides asparagine and glutamine by replacing the hydroxyl group with another amino group. Some legumes (such as soybean) also export fixed nitrogen as ureides, which possess a very high nitrogen-to-carbon (\(\mathrm{N:C}\)) ratio.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Diagnosing Nutrient Mobility from Symptom Location

Scenario (Assumed): A botanist grows two sets of potted tomato plants in controlled mineral solutions. Set A develops severe interveinal chlorosis first in its oldest basal leaves, whereas Set B develops necrosis and distortion first in its youngest apical shoot tips and emerging leaves.

Task: Identify which set lacks an actively mobile nutrient versus an immobile nutrient, and name plausible deficient elements for each set.

Solution:

  1. When a nutrient is actively mobile, the plant breaks down biomolecules in senescent older leaves and exports the element to young growing tissues. Thus, Set A lacks a mobile nutrient, such as magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg}\)), nitrogen (\(\mathrm{N}\)), or potassium (\(\mathrm{K}\)).
  2. When a nutrient is relatively immobile, it remains locked in structural components of mature cells and cannot reach newly dividing apices. Thus, Set B lacks an immobile nutrient, such as calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca}\)) or sulphur (\(\mathrm{S}\)).
Example 2: Calculating the Bioenergetic Cost of Biological Nitrogen Fixation

Scenario (Assumed): A root nodule of a garden pea plant fixes \(0.25\ \mathrm{mmol}\) of atmospheric \(\mathrm{N_2}\) over a given period.

Task: Calculate (a) the moles of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) produced and (b) the total \(\mathrm{mmol}\) of ATP hydrolyzed by nitrogenase.

Solution:

From the balanced nitrogenase reaction: \[\mathrm{N_2} + 8\mathrm{e^-} + 8\mathrm{H^+} + 16\mathrm{ATP} \longrightarrow 2\mathrm{NH_3} + \mathrm{H_2} + 16\mathrm{ADP} + 16\mathrm{P_i}\]

  • (a) Each mole of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) yields \(2\ \mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). Therefore, \(0.25\ \mathrm{mmol}\) of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) produces \(2 \times 0.25 = 0.50\ \mathrm{mmol}\) of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\).
  • (b) Each mole of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) requires \(16\ \mathrm{mol}\) of ATP (equivalently, \(8\ \mathrm{mol}\) ATP per \(\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)). Therefore, total ATP consumed \(= 16 \times 0.25 = 4.0\ \mathrm{mmol\ ATP}\).
Example 3: Distinguishing Direct Toxicity from Induced Deficiency

Scenario (Assumed): A hydroponic crop exposed to elevated manganese (\(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\)) exhibits brown spots with chlorotic veins, stunted shoot apex growth, and reduced tissue dry weight by \(12\%\).

Task: Explain why supplementing only \(\mathrm{Fe}\) might partially alleviate some chlorotic symptoms while shoot apex symptoms persist.

Solution:

A \(12\%\) reduction in dry weight exceeds the \(10\%\) threshold defining micronutrient toxicity. Excess \(\mathrm{Mn}\) competitively inhibits \(\mathrm{Fe}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg}\) uptake, blocks \(\mathrm{Mg}\) binding to enzymes, and suppresses \(\mathrm{Ca}\) translocation to the shoot apex. Adding \(\mathrm{Fe}\) can partially counter \(\mathrm{Fe}\)-deficiency chlorosis, but unless \(\mathrm{Mn}\) levels are lowered, \(\mathrm{Mg}\) enzyme inhibition and \(\mathrm{Ca}\) deficiency at the shoot apex will continue.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following elements is classified as a macronutrient and is also an essential constituent of the porphyrin ring structure of chlorophyll?

  • (A) Iron (\(\mathrm{Fe}\))
  • (B) Magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg}\))
  • (C) Manganese (\(\mathrm{Mn}\))
  • (D) Calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca}\))
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg}\)) is a macronutrient (\(>10\ \mathrm{mmol\ kg^{-1}}\) dry matter) that occupies the central position in the chlorophyll ring structure. Iron is a micronutrient essential for chlorophyll formation (catalyzing synthesis steps) but is not part of the chlorophyll ring itself.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

During the photolysis of water in photosynthesis to liberate oxygen, which pair of micronutrients plays an essential role?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Zn}\) and \(\mathrm{Cu}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Mo}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Mn}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Fe}\) and \(\mathrm{Ni}\)
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Both manganese (\(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\)) and chloride (\(\mathrm{Cl^-}\)) are directly required for the water-splitting reaction associated with Photosystem II that evolves molecular \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A farmer notices that the youngest emerging leaves of a crop plant show severe yellowing and malformation, while the older mature leaves remain green. This pattern indicates a deficiency of:

  • (A) Nitrogen
  • (B) Potassium
  • (C) Magnesium
  • (D) Calcium
Answer: (D)
Explanation: Calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca}\)) is relatively immobile within the plant because it is locked into cell walls as calcium pectate. Consequently, its deficiency appears first in young developing tissues. Nitrogen, potassium, and magnesium are mobile and show symptoms first in older leaves.
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following bacteria are chemoautotrophs responsible for oxidizing nitrite (\(\mathrm{NO_2^-}\)) to nitrate (\(\mathrm{NO_3^-}\)) during nitrification?

  • (A) Nitrosomonas
  • (B) Nitrobacter
  • (C) Pseudomonas
  • (D) Thiobacillus
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Nitrosomonas and Nitrococcus oxidize ammonia to nitrite, whereas Nitrobacter oxidizes nitrite to nitrate. Pseudomonas and Thiobacillus carry out denitrification (reducing nitrate to \(\mathrm{N_2}\)).
Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

How many molecules of ATP are required by the nitrogenase complex to synthesize four molecules of ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)) from atmospheric \(\mathrm{N_2}\)?

  • (A) \(16\ \mathrm{ATP}\)
  • (B) \(24\ \mathrm{ATP}\)
  • (C) \(32\ \mathrm{ATP}\)
  • (D) \(64\ \mathrm{ATP}\)
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Fixing \(1\ \mathrm{N_2}\) produces \(2\ \mathrm{NH_3}\) and consumes \(16\ \mathrm{ATP}\) (i.e., \(8\ \mathrm{ATP}\) per \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)). Therefore, synthesizing \(4\ \mathrm{NH_3}\) requires \(4 \times 8 = 32\ \mathrm{ATP}\).
Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following microbes is a free-living, anaerobic nitrogen-fixing bacterium?

  • (A) Azotobacter
  • (B) Beijerinckia
  • (C) Rhodospirillum
  • (D) Frankia
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Rhodospirillum is a free-living anaerobic \(\mathrm{N_2}\)-fixer. Azotobacter and Beijerinckia are free-living aerobes, while Frankia forms symbiotic root nodules on non-legumes such as Alnus.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Avoid These Frequent Exam Mistakes:
  • Confusing Fe and Mg in chlorophyll: \(\mathrm{Mg}\) is a structural constituent of the chlorophyll ring; \(\mathrm{Fe}\) is needed for chlorophyll biosynthesis but is not part of the ring.
  • Misidentifying Frankia as a legume symbiont: Rhizobium nodulates legumes; Frankia nodulates non-leguminous plants like Alnus.
  • Forgetting the ATP stoichiometry of nitrogenase: \(16\ \mathrm{ATP}\) are used per \(\mathrm{N_2}\) molecule, which equals \(8\ \mathrm{ATP}\) per \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) molecule.
  • Overlooking competitive inhibition in \(\mathrm{Mn}\) toxicity: Excess \(\mathrm{Mn}\) induces deficiencies of \(\mathrm{Fe}\), \(\mathrm{Mg}\), and \(\mathrm{Ca}\).
  • Verify that you can list all 9 macronutrients and 8 micronutrients without hesitation.
  • Memorize the symptom groups: Chlorosis (\(\mathrm{N, K, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Mo}\)), Necrosis (\(\mathrm{Ca, Mg, Cu, K}\)), Cell division inhibition (\(\mathrm{N, K, S, Mo}\)), and Delayed flowering (\(\mathrm{N, S, Mo}\)).
  • Distinguish apoplastic (passive, rapid) from symplastic (active, slow) mineral uptake.

Concise Final Recap

Plants require 17 essential elements—9 macronutrients and 8 micronutrients—identified via aerated hydroponic cultures using three strict criteria of essentiality. Mobile nutrients (\(\mathrm{N, K, Mg}\)) show deficiency first in older leaves, whereas immobile nutrients (\(\mathrm{Ca, S}\)) affect young tissues first. Roots absorb ions passively into the apoplast and actively into the symplast before translocating them upward via xylem. Atmospheric \(\mathrm{N_2}\) is fixed exclusively by prokaryotes possessing oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase (\(\mathrm{Mo\text{-}Fe}\) protein) protected in legume nodules by leghaemoglobin, yielding \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) that is assimilated into glutamic acid, transaminated to other amino acids, and transported as high-\(\mathrm{N:C}\) amides or ureides.
