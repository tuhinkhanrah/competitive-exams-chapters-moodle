Cell Cycle and Cell Division

A structured, exam-focused guide to interphase dynamics, equational mitosis, reductional meiosis, and chromosomal ploidy tracking.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Every multicellular organism begins life as a single cell. Growth, tissue repair, and sexual reproduction all depend on an orderly sequence of cellular growth, genome duplication, and division. For NEET UG, mastery of this topic requires precision in three areas: the sequence of events in each phase, the exact behaviour of chromosomes and organelles, and quantitative tracking of chromosome number (\(n\)) and DNA content (\(C\)).

Key Learning Goals
  • Distinguish between interphase (\(G_1\), \(S\), \(G_2\)), the quiescent state (\(G_0\)), and the division phase (\(M\)).
  • Track changes in DNA content (\(2C \to 4C\)) versus chromosome number (\(2n \to 2n\)) during DNA replication and nuclear division.
  • Identify the diagnostic events of mitotic stages and compare animal versus plant cytokinesis.
  • Master the five substages of Prophase I of meiosis and explain why Meiosis I is reductional while Meiosis II is equational.
  • Solve NEET UG-style ploidy, stage-identification, assertion–reason, and statement-based questions with zero ambiguity.

The Eukaryotic Cell Cycle: Interphase and Quiescence

The cell cycle is the genetically regulated sequence of events by which a cell duplicates its genome, synthesizes cellular constituents, and divides into two daughter cells. While cytoplasmic growth is largely continuous throughout the cycle, DNA synthesis occurs exclusively during a single dedicated window.

Cell cycle duration varies widely across species and cell types. A typical human cell in culture completes one cycle in approximately \(24\,\mathrm{hours}\), whereas a unicellular yeast cell can complete its cycle in about \(90\,\mathrm{minutes}\). In a \(24\,\mathrm{hour}\) human cell cycle, actual cell division (\(M\) phase) occupies only about \(1\,\mathrm{hour}\), while interphase accounts for more than \(95\%\) of the total duration.

Subphases of Interphase

  • \(G_1\) phase (Gap 1): The interval between the end of mitosis and the start of DNA replication. The cell is metabolically active, grows continuously, and duplicates most cell organelles, but does not replicate its DNA.
  • \(S\) phase (Synthesis): DNA replication occurs in the nucleus. If the initial DNA content is \(2C\), it doubles to \(4C\). Crucially, the chromosome number does not change: a diploid cell with \(2n\) chromosomes in \(G_1\) still has \(2n\) chromosomes after \(S\) phase, each now consisting of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. In animal cells, the centriole duplicates in the cytoplasm during \(S\) phase.
  • \(G_2\) phase (Gap 2): Proteins (such as tubulin for spindle assembly) are synthesized in preparation for mitosis while cytoplasmic growth continues.

The Quiescent Stage (\(G_0\))

Not all cells divide continuously. Adult cells such as heart muscle cells do not divide further, and many other cells divide only occasionally to replace cells lost to injury or cell death. Such cells exit the \(G_1\) phase and enter an inactive, non-proliferative state called the quiescent stage (\(G_0\)). Cells in \(G_0\) remain metabolically active and perform their specialized functions, but they do not proliferate unless signaled to re-enter the cell cycle.

Mitosis (Equational Division) and Cytokinesis

The \(M\) phase begins with nuclear division (karyokinesis) and typically concludes with cytoplasmic division (cytokinesis). Because the chromosome number in the parent cell and each daughter cell remains identical, mitosis is termed equational division. In animals, mitosis is generally restricted to diploid somatic cells, though notable exceptions exist (such as haploid cells in male honeybees). In contrast, plants exhibit mitotic division in both haploid and diploid cells (for example, in haploid gametophytes and diploid sporophytes).

Four Stages of Karyokinesis

Although karyokinesis is a continuous, progressive process, it is divided into four stages for clarity:

  1. Prophase: Follows \(S\) and \(G_2\). Intertwined chromatin fibers untangle and condense into compact mitotic chromosomes, each visibly composed of two sister chromatids attached at a centromere. Duplicated centrosomes migrate toward opposite poles, radiating microtubules called asters; two asters together with spindle fibers form the mitotic apparatus. By the end of prophase, the Golgi apparatus, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), nucleolus, and nuclear envelope disappear from microscopic view.
  2. Metaphase: Begins with the complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope, dispersing chromosomes into the cytoplasm. Condensation is complete, making metaphase the best stage to study chromosome morphology. Small disc-shaped protein structures on the surface of centromeres, called kinetochores, serve as attachment sites for spindle fibers. Chromosomes align at the cell equator on the metaphase plate, with sister chromatids connected via their kinetochores to opposite poles.
  3. Anaphase: Each centromere splits simultaneously, separating the two sister chromatids into independent daughter chromosomes. As spindle fibers shorten, daughter chromosomes migrate toward opposite poles with the centromere leading and the chromosome arms trailing behind.
  4. Telophase: Daughter chromosomes reach opposite poles, decondense, and lose their discrete individuality as chromatin. A nuclear envelope reforms around each chromosome cluster, and the nucleolus, Golgi complex, and ER reappear.

Cytokinesis: Animal vs. Plant Cells

  • Animal cells: A furrow appears in the plasma membrane and deepens centripetally (from the periphery toward the center), pinching the cytoplasm into two daughter cells.
  • Plant cells: Enclosed by a rigid, inextensible cell wall, plant cells cannot form a cleavage furrow. Instead, wall formation begins at the center with a precursor called the cell-plate (representing the future middle lamella) and grows centrifugally outward to fuse with the existing lateral walls. Organelles such as mitochondria and plastids are distributed between the daughter cells.
  • Syncytium (Exception): When karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis, a multinucleate condition arises, known as a syncytium (e.g., liquid endosperm in coconut).

Significance of Mitosis

Mitosis produces genetically identical diploid daughter cells, enabling multicellular growth and restoring the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio when cell growth disturbs it. It also drives continuous cell repair and replacement (such as in the upper epidermis, gut lining, and blood cells) and sustains lifelong plant growth via apical and lateral meristems.

Meiosis (Reductional Division) and Genetic Variation

Sexual reproduction relies on the fusion of two haploid gametes to restore the diploid chromosome number in the zygote. Meiosis is a specialized division of diploid germ cells that reduces the chromosome number by half, producing four haploid daughter cells. It involves one round of DNA replication (in \(S\) phase) followed by two sequential nuclear and cell divisions: Meiosis I and Meiosis II.

Meiosis I: The Reductional Division

Prophase I is markedly longer and more complex than mitotic prophase and is subdivided into five sequential stages based on chromosomal behaviour:

  1. Leptotene: Chromosomes gradually become visible under a light microscope as compaction begins.
  2. Zygotene: Homologous chromosomes pair together in a process called synapsis, accompanied by the assembly of a proteinaceous synaptonemal complex. Each paired homologous unit is called a bivalent or tetrad.
  3. Pachytene: The four chromatids of each bivalent become clearly distinct as tetrads. Recombination nodules appear, marking sites where crossing over (exchange of genetic segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes) takes place. Crossing over is catalyzed by the enzyme recombinase and is completed by the end of pachytene.
  4. Diplotene: Recognized by the dissolution of the synaptonemal complex. Recombined homologous chromosomes tend to separate from each other except at the sites of crossovers, forming characteristic X-shaped structures called chiasmata. In oocytes of some vertebrates, diplotene can last for months or years.
  5. Diakinesis: Marked by the terminalisation of chiasmata. Chromosomes are fully condensed, the meiotic spindle assembles, and by the end of this stage, the nucleolus and nuclear envelope break down.

Following Prophase I, Meiosis I proceeds through:

  • Metaphase I: Bivalent chromosomes align on the equatorial plate; spindle microtubules from opposite poles attach to the kinetochores of homologous chromosomes.
  • Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate and move to opposite poles, while sister chromatids remain attached at their centromeres (no centromere splitting occurs).
  • Telophase I and Interkinesis: Nuclear membrane and nucleolus reappear, and cytokinesis produces a dyad of cells. The brief interval between Meiosis I and Meiosis II is interkinesis, during which no DNA replication occurs.

Meiosis II: The Equational Division

Meiosis II resembles normal mitosis and separates sister chromatids:

  • Prophase II: Initiated immediately after cytokinesis; chromosomes re-compact and the nuclear membrane disappears.
  • Metaphase II: Chromosomes align at the equator; microtubules from opposite poles attach to kinetochores of sister chromatids.
  • Anaphase II: Simultaneous splitting of the centromere of each chromosome allows sister chromatids to move toward opposite poles.
  • Telophase II: Nuclear envelopes reform around the four chromosome groups, and cytokinesis yields a tetrad of four haploid daughter cells.

Significance of Meiosis

Meiosis conserves the specific chromosome number of each sexually reproducing species across generations by halving the chromosome complement in gametes prior to fertilization. Simultaneously, crossing over and independent assortment during Meiosis I generate genetic variability in populations, providing essential raw material for evolution.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Tracking DNA Content and Chromosome Number Across Interphase and Mitosis

Scenario (Assumed Values): Suppose a diploid meristematic root cell of a hypothetical flowering plant has \(2n = 20\) chromosomes and a DNA content of \(2C = 12\,\mathrm{pg}\) in the \(G_1\) phase. Determine the chromosome number and DNA content at (a) the end of \(S\) phase, (b) \(G_2\) phase, (c) Metaphase, and (d) each daughter cell after Telophase and cytokinesis.

Worked Reasoning
  • (a) End of \(S\) phase: DNA replicates, so DNA mass doubles from \(12\,\mathrm{pg}\) to \(24\,\mathrm{pg}\) (\(4C\)). However, sister chromatids remain joined at a single centromere, so the chromosome count stays \(2n = 20\).
  • (b) \(G_2\) phase: No further DNA replication occurs. DNA content remains \(24\,\mathrm{pg}\) (\(4C\)) and chromosome number remains \(20\).
  • (c) Metaphase: Chromosomes condense and align at the equator, still intact as \(20\) chromosomes (\(40\) chromatids) with \(24\,\mathrm{pg}\) (\(4C\)) of DNA.
  • (d) After cytokinesis: Sister chromatids separate in anaphase and are partitioned equally into two daughter cells. Each daughter cell receives \(20\) chromosomes (\(2n\)) and \(12\,\mathrm{pg}\) (\(2C\)) of DNA.
Example 2: Quantitative Ploidy Analysis Across Meiosis I and Meiosis II

Scenario (Assumed Values): A hypothetical diploid animal meiocyte enters \(G_1\) with \(2n = 14\) chromosomes and \(2C = 8\,\mathrm{pg}\) of DNA. Calculate the number of bivalents in Pachytene, the chromosome number and DNA content per cell in the dyad after Telophase I, and the chromosome number and DNA content per gamete after Telophase II.

Worked Reasoning
  • Pachytene: Homologous chromosomes pair to form bivalents. With \(2n = 14\) chromosomes, there are \(14 / 2 = 7\) bivalents (each bivalent is a tetrad of \(4\) chromatids, giving \(28\) chromatids total). DNA content after \(S\) phase is \(4C = 16\,\mathrm{pg}\).
  • Dyad after Telophase I: Homologous chromosomes separate in Anaphase I, halving the chromosome number per cell to \(n = 7\). Because each chromosome still carries two sister chromatids, DNA content per cell is \(2C = 8\,\mathrm{pg}\).
  • Gamete after Telophase II: Sister chromatids separate in Anaphase II (with no DNA replication during interkinesis). Each of the four haploid gametes contains \(n = 7\) chromosomes and \(1C = 4\,\mathrm{pg}\) of DNA.
Example 3: Identifying Stages from Microscopic Markers

Scenario: Under a light microscope, three dividing cells show the following distinct features: Cell X displays \(X\)-shaped links between homologous chromosomes after synaptonemal complex dissolution; Cell Y shows maximally condensed chromosomes aligned at the equator with kinetochores attached to opposite poles; Cell Z shows a growing central plate extending outward toward lateral cell walls. Identify the stage/process in each cell.

Worked Reasoning
  • Cell X: Dissolution of the synaptonemal complex and persistence of \(X\)-shaped chiasmata are diagnostic of Diplotene (Prophase I of meiosis).
  • Cell Y: Complete condensation, equatorial alignment, and bipolar kinetochore attachment characterize Metaphase (of mitosis, or Metaphase II if haploid).
  • Cell Z: Centrifugal growth of a cell-plate toward lateral walls represents plant cell cytokinesis.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Attempt all eight practice questions before reviewing the worked solutions. Each question is designed to test NEET UG conceptual precision.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a dividing animal cell, during which phase of the cell cycle does DNA replication occur in the nucleus simultaneously with centriole duplication in the cytoplasm?

  1. (A) \(G_1\) phase
  2. (B) \(S\) phase
  3. (C) \(G_2\) phase
  4. (D) Prophase
Correct Answer: (B) \(S\) phase

Explanation: During the \(S\) (Synthesis) phase of interphase, nuclear DNA doubles (\(2C \to 4C\)), and in animal cells, centriole duplication occurs concurrently in the cytoplasm. \(G_1\) involves growth without DNA synthesis, \(G_2\) involves protein synthesis, and prophase is the first stage of karyokinesis.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following correctly pairs a substage of Prophase I with its characteristic event?

  1. (A) Leptotene — Terminalisation of chiasmata
  2. (B) Zygotene — Action of recombinase enzyme
  3. (C) Pachytene — Crossing over at recombination nodules
  4. (D) Diplotene — Assembly of the synaptonemal complex
Correct Answer: (C) Pachytene — Crossing over at recombination nodules

Explanation: Crossing over between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes occurs at recombination nodules during Pachytene, mediated by recombinase. Terminalisation of chiasmata occurs in Diakinesis, synaptonemal complex assembly occurs in Zygotene, and its dissolution occurs in Diplotene.

Practice Question 3 (Numerical / Ploidy MCQ)

A hypothetical diploid organism has \(2n = 24\) chromosomes and \(2C = 16\,\mathrm{pg}\) of DNA in its \(G_1\) somatic cells. What will be the chromosome number and DNA content per cell at Metaphase I of meiosis?

  1. (A) \(12\) chromosomes, \(16\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
  2. (B) \(24\) chromosomes, \(16\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
  3. (C) \(24\) chromosomes, \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
  4. (D) \(48\) chromosomes, \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
Correct Answer: (C) \(24\) chromosomes, \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA

Explanation: Prior to Meiosis I, the cell undergoes \(S\) phase, doubling its DNA from \(16\,\mathrm{pg}\) to \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\) (\(4C\)) while keeping the chromosome count at \(2n = 24\). At Metaphase I, homologous chromosomes are paired into \(12\) bivalents, so the cell still contains \(24\) chromosomes and \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\) of DNA.

Practice Question 4 (Statement I & II)

Read the following two statements carefully and select the correct option:

Statement I: Cells in the quiescent stage (\(G_0\)) are metabolically inactive and permanently incapable of dividing.
Statement II: In plant cells, cytokinesis occurs via cell-plate formation that grows centrifugally from the center toward the lateral walls.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Explanation: Cells in \(G_0\) remain metabolically active and can re-enter the cell cycle if stimulated by physiological demand (Statement I is incorrect). Plant cells possess a rigid cell wall and divide cytoplasmically via a centrally initiated cell-plate growing outward (Statement II is correct).

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Anaphase I of meiosis reduces the chromosome number to half in each daughter nucleus, whereas mitotic anaphase maintains the same chromosome number.
Reason (R): During Anaphase I, homologous chromosomes separate while centromeres remain intact, whereas during mitotic anaphase, centromeres split and sister chromatids separate.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

Explanation: Because homologous chromosomes (each still composed of two chromatids) move to opposite poles in Anaphase I without centromere splitting, each pole receives \(n\) chromosomes instead of \(2n\). In mitotic anaphase, centromere splitting doubles the count of independent chromosomes temporarily and delivers \(2n\) chromosomes to each pole.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which stage of mitosis is most suitable for studying the morphology of chromosomes, and what key event marks its onset?

  1. (A) Prophase; initiation of chromatin condensation
  2. (B) Metaphase; complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope
  3. (C) Anaphase; splitting of centromeres
  4. (D) Telophase; reappearance of the nucleolus
Correct Answer: (B) Metaphase; complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope

Explanation: Complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope marks the start of metaphase. By this stage, chromosome condensation is complete, allowing chromosome morphology to be observed most clearly under a microscope.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements regarding the occurrence and significance of mitosis is incorrect?

  1. (A) Mitosis restores the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio disturbed by cell growth.
  2. (B) In plants, mitosis can occur in both haploid and diploid cells.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET Traps to Avoid
  • DNA Content vs. Chromosome Count in \(S\) Phase: DNA doubles (\(2C \to 4C\)), but chromosome number remains \(2n\). Never double \(n\) after \(S\) phase.
  • Interkinesis DNA Replication: There is no DNA replication during interkinesis between Meiosis I and Meiosis II.
  • Crossing Over Participants: Exchange occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes, never between sister chromatids.
  • Anaphase I vs. Anaphase II: Centromeres do not split in Anaphase I; they split in Anaphase II (and mitotic anaphase).
  • \(G_0\) Metabolic Status: \(G_0\) cells are metabolically active, not dead or metabolically inert—they simply halt proliferation.

Revision Checklist

  • Interphase occupies >\(95\%\) of the cell cycle; \(M\) phase occupies ~\(5\%\).
  • \(G_1\) (growth, no DNA replication) \(\to\) \(S\) (DNA \(2C \to 4C\), centriole duplicates in animal cytoplasm) \(\to\) \(G_2\) (protein synthesis).
  • End of Prophase: Golgi, ER, nucleolus, and nuclear envelope disappear; Telophase reverses this.
  • Metaphase: best stage for chromosome morphology; kinetochores attach to spindle fibers at the metaphase plate.
  • Prophase I order: Leptotene \(\to\) Zygotene (synapsis, synaptonemal complex) \(\to\) Pachytene (crossing over, recombinase) \(\to\) Diplotene (chiasmata) \(\to\) Diakinesis (terminalisation).
  • Cytokinesis: centripetal furrow in animals; centrifugal cell-plate in plants; syncytium when cytokinesis is skipped.

Concise Final Recap

The eukaryotic cell cycle alternates between a preparatory interphase (\(G_1\), \(S\), \(G_2\)) and a brief \(M\) phase. During \(S\) phase, DNA doubles (\(2C \to 4C\)) while chromosome number (\(2n\)) stays constant. Mitosis is an equational division (\(2n \to 2n\)) essential for growth, repair, and restoring the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio. Meiosis pairs one \(S\) phase with two divisions: Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes (after crossing over in pachytene) to halve the chromosome number (\(2n \to n\)), and Meiosis II splits centromeres equationally to produce four genetically varied haploid gametes.
