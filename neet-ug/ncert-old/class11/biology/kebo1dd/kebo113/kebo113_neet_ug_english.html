NEET UG Plant Physiology

Photosynthesis in Higher Plants

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide covering light harvesting, chemiosmosis, C₃ and C₄ carbon fixation, photorespiration, and limiting factors.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Green plants are photoautotrophs: they convert solar energy into chemical bond energy, synthesizing organic carbohydrates from inorganic carbon dioxide and water while releasing molecular oxygen. Nearly all heterotrophic life on Earth depends on this process both for reduced carbon compounds (food) and for atmospheric oxygen.

What You Will Master for NEET UG
  • Historical experiments that established the roles of light, chlorophyll, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) as the source of evolved \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  • Structural division of labour inside the chloroplast and the roles of chlorophyll a and accessory pigments.
  • Mechanisms of the photochemical phase: PS II, PS I, the Z-scheme, photolysis of water, cyclic vs. non-cyclic photophosphorylation, and chemiosmotic ATP synthesis.
  • Biosynthetic carbon assimilation: the Calvin (C₃) cycle, Hatch–Slack (C₄) pathway, Kranz anatomy, and the energetics of hexose formation.
  • Photorespiration (C₂ cycle) and why C₄ plants out-yield C₃ plants in hot, bright environments.
  • Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors and plant responses to light, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), temperature, and water stress.

Foundational Principles and Landmark Experiments

Basic physiological tests demonstrate three fundamental requirements for photosynthesis:

  • Chlorophyll and Light: When a variegated leaf or a leaf partially masked with opaque black paper is exposed to sunlight and tested with iodine, starch forms exclusively in the green, illuminated regions.
  • Carbon Dioxide: In a half-leaf experiment where one half of an illuminated leaf is sealed inside a vessel containing potassium hydroxide (\(\mathrm{KOH}\))-soaked cotton (which absorbs \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) and the other half remains exposed to air, only the air-exposed half tests positive for starch.

Historical Milestones in Photosynthesis Research

Our modern understanding of photosynthesis emerged from a sequence of classic investigations:

Investigator & Era Experimental Setup Core Conclusion
Joseph Priestley (1770) Closed bell jar with a burning candle, a mouse, and a mint plant. Animals and burning candles foul air; green plants restore the component of air (later identified as \(\mathrm{O_2}\) in 1774) needed for respiration and combustion.
Jan Ingenhousz (1730–1799) Bell jar setup in dark vs. sunlight; aquatic plant in bright sunlight. Sunlight is essential for air purification; only green plant parts release \(\mathrm{O_2}\) bubbles in light.
Julius von Sachs (1854) Growth and microscopy studies on green plant tissues. Glucose is synthesized during plant growth and typically stored as starch; the green pigment (chlorophyll) resides in specialized bodies (chloroplasts).
T. W. Engelmann (1843–1909) Prism-split spectrum projected onto filamentous green alga (Cladophora) suspended with aerobic bacteria. Bacteria clustered densely in the blue and red regions of the spectrum, producing the first action spectrum of photosynthesis.
Cornelius van Niel (1897–1985) Comparative studies of purple and green sulphur bacteria vs. green plants. Photosynthesis is a light-driven redox process where a hydrogen donor (\(\mathrm{H_2A}\)) reduces \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to carbohydrate. Since sulphur bacteria use \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) and release sulphur/sulphate instead of \(\mathrm{O_2}\), the \(\mathrm{O_2}\) evolved by green plants must originate from \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), not \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

Van Niel's Generalized Equation:

\[ 2\mathrm{H_2A} + \mathrm{CO_2} \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} 2\mathrm{A} + [\mathrm{CH_2O}] + \mathrm{H_2O} \]

Overall Balanced Equation for Oxygenic Photosynthesis (confirmed by \(^{18}\mathrm{O}\) radioisotopic tracing):

\[ 6\mathrm{CO_2} + 12\mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\mathrm{H_2O} + 6\mathrm{O_2} \]

Why are 12 molecules of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) shown as substrate? Every molecule of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) released requires the splitting of two \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) molecules (yielding \(4\mathrm{H^+} + 4\mathrm{e^-} + \mathrm{O_2}\)). Releasing \(6\,\mathrm{O_2}\) therefore consumes \(12\,\mathrm{H_2O}\), while \(6\,\mathrm{H_2O}\) are regenerated during carbon reduction.

Chloroplast Architecture and Photosynthetic Pigments

Photosynthesis occurs in green tissues—predominantly in the mesophyll cells of leaves. Chloroplasts align along mesophyll cell walls to optimize light capture: parallel to the cell wall (flat surface facing light) under low light, and perpendicular (edge-on) under intense light to avoid photodamage.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Structural organization of a chloroplast showing the membrane system (light reactions) and stroma (carbon reactions).

Division of Labour Within the Chloroplast

  • Membrane System (Grana & Stroma Lamellae): Traps light energy and synthesizes \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) via the light reactions (photochemical phase).
  • Stroma: Contains soluble enzymes that use \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) to reduce \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) into sugars (stored as starch) via the carbon reactions (biosynthetic phase). Calling these "dark reactions" is a misnomer: they do not require light directly, nor do they occur exclusively in darkness; rather, they depend on the continuous supply of light-reaction products (\(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\)) and cease shortly after light is removed.

Leaf Pigments and Spectra

Paper chromatography separates leaf extract into four distinct pigment bands:

Pigment Chromatogram Colour Primary Role
Chlorophyll a Bright or blue-green Chief photosynthetic pigment; forms the reaction centres of PS I and PS II. Most abundant plant pigment.
Chlorophyll b Yellow-green Accessory pigment; absorbs light and transfers excitation energy to Chlorophyll a.
Xanthophylls Yellow Accessory pigment; broadens usable spectrum and protects Chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation.
Carotenoids Yellow to yellow-orange Accessory pigment; harvests additional wavelengths and shields Chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation.

Absorption vs. Action Spectrum: Chlorophyll a shows major absorption peaks in the blue and red regions of the visible spectrum, which closely match the action spectrum maxima (highest \(\mathrm{O_2}\) evolution). However, the action spectrum and Chlorophyll a absorption spectrum do not show a 1:1 overlap at intermediate wavelengths because accessory pigments (Chl b, xanthophylls, carotenoids) absorb light in those regions and funnel energy to Chlorophyll a.

The Photochemical Phase (Light Reactions)

Pigments are organized into two discrete photochemical Light Harvesting Complexes (LHC) within Photosystem I (PS I) and Photosystem II (PS II). Note that PS I and PS II are named in the order of their discovery, not the sequence in which they operate during non-cyclic electron flow.

  • Antennae (LHC): Hundreds of protein-bound pigment molecules absorb photons across varied wavelengths and transfer energy by resonance to a single Chlorophyll a molecule.
  • Reaction Centre: A specialized Chlorophyll a molecule that ejects an excited electron to a primary electron acceptor. In PS II, the reaction centre absorbs maximally at \(680\,\mathrm{nm}\) (P680); in PS I, it absorbs maximally at \(700\,\mathrm{nm}\) (P700).

Non-Cyclic Electron Transport (The Z-Scheme) and Photolysis of Water

When P680 absorbs \(680\,\mathrm{nm}\) red light, electrons are excited to a higher redox state and captured by a primary acceptor. They then flow downhill along an electron transport system (ETS) of cytochromes to PS I (P700). Simultaneously, P700 absorbs \(700\,\mathrm{nm}\) light, excites electrons to another primary acceptor, and passes them downhill via ferredoxin to \(\mathrm{NADP^+}\) reductase, reducing \(\mathrm{NADP^+}\) to \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\). Plotted on a redox potential scale, this electron pathway forms a characteristic Z-shape.

Electrons lost by PS II are continuously replenished by the splitting of water (photolysis), catalyzed by the water-splitting complex located on the inner (lumen) side of the thylakoid membrane in association with PS II:

\[ 2\mathrm{H_2O} \longrightarrow 4\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{O_2} + 4\mathrm{e^-} \]

Tutorial Diagram 2: Non-cyclic electron flow (Z-scheme) linking PS II and PS I to produce both ATP and NADPH + H⁺.

Chemiosmotic Hypothesis for ATP Synthesis

ATP synthesis in chloroplasts is driven by a proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) electrochemical gradient across the thylakoid membrane, with \(\mathrm{H^+}\) accumulating inside the thylakoid lumen (lowering lumen pH) and depleting in the stroma. Note the contrast with mitochondria, where \(\mathrm{H^+}\) accumulates in the intermembrane space.

Three mechanisms build this proton gradient:

  1. Photolysis of Water: Occurs on the inner (lumen) side of the membrane, releasing \(\mathrm{H^+}\) directly into the lumen.
  2. Proton Translocation by H-Carrier: The primary electron acceptor of PS II (outer side) transfers electrons to plastoquinone (PQ, an H-carrier), which picks up \(\mathrm{H^+}\) from the stroma and releases it into the lumen upon passing electrons to Cytochrome \(b_6f\).
  3. NADP Reductase (FNR) Activity: Located on the stromal side, it consumes stromal \(\mathrm{H^+}\) to reduce \(\mathrm{NADP^+}\) to \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\).

Protons flow back down their electrochemical gradient into the stroma through the transmembrane channel of ATP synthase (\(\mathrm{CF_0}\)), inducing a conformational change in the protruding catalytic head (\(\mathrm{CF_1}\)) that synthesizes \(\mathrm{ATP}\) from \(\mathrm{ADP + P_i}\).

Note: In the chloroplast, \(\mathrm{CF_0}\) spans the thylakoid membrane while \(\mathrm{CF_1}\) protrudes into the stroma, where \(\mathrm{ATP}\) is released for the Calvin cycle.

Biosynthetic Phase: C₃, C₄, and Photorespiration

Melvin Calvin used radioactive \(^{14}\mathrm{C}\) in algal photosynthesis studies to elucidate the cyclic pathway of carbon fixation. Depending on the first stable product formed, plants are categorized into C₃ plants (first product: 3-phosphoglyceric acid, PGA) and C₄ plants (first product: oxaloacetic acid, OAA). Crucially, the Calvin cycle operates in all photosynthetic plants—both C₃ and C₄.

The Calvin Cycle (C₃ Pathway)

The primary \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) acceptor is a 5-carbon ketose sugar, ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), and the reaction is catalyzed by RuBisCO (RuBP carboxylase-oxygenase), the most abundant enzyme on Earth. The cycle proceeds in three stages:

  1. Carboxylation: \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) combines with RuBP (5C) to form two molecules of 3-PGA (3C).
  2. Reduction: Two molecules of \(\mathrm{ATP}\) (phosphorylation) and two molecules of \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) (reduction) are consumed per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed to yield triose phosphates, which form hexose sugars (glucose).
  3. Regeneration: One \(\mathrm{ATP}\) is consumed per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to regenerate RuBP so the cycle continues uninterrupted.

Per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed: \(3\,\mathrm{ATP} + 2\,\mathrm{NADPH}\)  |  Per Glucose (6 turns): \(18\,\mathrm{ATP} + 12\,\mathrm{NADPH}\)

The C₄ Pathway (Hatch and Slack Pathway) and Kranz Anatomy

Plants adapted to dry tropical climates (e.g., maize, sorghum) exhibit Kranz anatomy ("Kranz" = wreath): large bundle sheath cells form concentric layers around vascular bundles. Bundle sheath cells possess abundant chloroplasts, thick walls impervious to gaseous exchange, and no intercellular spaces.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Spatial division in the C₄ (Hatch and Slack) pathway concentrating CO₂ around RuBisCO in bundle sheath cells.

Photorespiration (C₂ Cycle)

The active site of RuBisCO can bind both \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) competitively. Although RuBisCO has a much higher affinity for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) when \(\mathrm{CO_2:O_2}\) is nearly equal, high \(\mathrm{O_2}\) or elevated temperature increases oxygenase activity in C₃ plants:

\[ \mathrm{RuBP} + \mathrm{O_2} \xrightarrow{\text{RuBisCO}} 1\,\text{3-PGA (3C)} + 1\,\text{Phosphoglycolate (2C)} \]

Photorespiration is a wasteful process: it synthesizes neither sugars nor \(\mathrm{ATP}\) or \(\mathrm{NADPH}\), and instead consumes \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and releases \(\mathrm{CO_2}\). C₄ plants avoid photorespiration because decarboxylation of C₄ acids inside thick-walled bundle sheath cells elevates intracellular \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) concentration at the RuBisCO site, ensuring RuBisCO acts almost exclusively as a carboxylase.

Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is governed by internal (plant) factors (number, size, age, and orientation of leaves; mesophyll cells; chloroplasts; internal \(\mathrm{CO_2}\); chlorophyll content—all under genetic and developmental control) and external factors (sunlight, temperature, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) concentration, and water).

Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors (1905)

If a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, then its rate is determined by the factor nearest to its minimal value—the factor that directly alters the rate when its quantity is changed.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Response of photosynthetic rate to increasing light intensity, illustrating Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors.

  • Light: Rate increases linearly at low intensities until saturation occurs at about 10% of full sunlight (hence light is rarely limiting except in dense shade). Excessive light causes chlorophyll breakdown (photo-oxidation).
  • \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) Concentration: The major limiting factor in nature (atmospheric level is \(0.03\text{–}0.04\%\)). Increasing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) up to \(0.05\%\) boosts fixation, though prolonged higher levels become damaging. Current atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) limits C₃ plants (saturation \(> 450\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\)), which is exploited in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-enriched greenhouses for crops like tomato and bell pepper; C₄ plants saturate earlier (\(\sim 360\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\)).
  • Temperature: Enzymatic dark reactions are strongly temperature-sensitive; light reactions are much less affected. Tropical (and C₄) plants have higher temperature optima than temperate (and C₃) plants.
  • Water: Acts mainly indirectly—water stress triggers stomatal closure (starving the leaf of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) and leaf wilting (reducing surface area and metabolic activity).

Worked Examples

Calculating ATP and NADPH Demand for Sucrose Synthesis

Problem: Assuming a C₃ plant synthesizes 1 molecule of sucrose (a disaccharide composed of glucose and fructose, formed from 2 hexose units) exclusively via the Calvin cycle, calculate the total number of \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) molecules required.

Solution:

  1. Fixing \(1\,\mathrm{CO_2}\) in the Calvin cycle requires \(3\,\mathrm{ATP}\) (2 in reduction, 1 in regeneration) and \(2\,\mathrm{NADPH}\) (in reduction).
  2. One hexose molecule (\(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\)) requires 6 turns of the cycle: \[ \text{ATP per hexose} = 6 \times 3 = 18\,\mathrm{ATP}, \quad \text{NADPH per hexose} = 6 \times 2 = 12\,\mathrm{NADPH} \]
  3. Sucrose (\(\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}}\)) requires 12 carbons (2 hexoses, i.e., 12 turns of the Calvin cycle): \[ \text{Total ATP} = 12 \times 3 = 36\,\mathrm{ATP}, \quad \text{Total NADPH} = 12 \times 2 = 24\,\mathrm{NADPH} \]

Exam Insight: Because the Calvin cycle consumes \(\mathrm{ATP}:\mathrm{NADPH}\) in a \(3:2\) ratio, cyclic photophosphorylation supplements the extra \(\mathrm{ATP}\) beyond what non-cyclic flow alone provides.

Comparing Energy Costs in C₃ and C₄ Pathways

Problem: Suppose a hypothetical C₄ plant fixes \(6\,\mathrm{mol}\) of atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to produce \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of glucose. If regenerating PEP from pyruvate in mesophyll cells consumes \(2\,\mathrm{ATP}\) equivalents per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed, determine the total \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) consumed compared with a C₃ plant.

Solution:

  • C₃ Plant: Only the Calvin cycle runs \(\Rightarrow 18\,\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(12\,\mathrm{NADPH}\).
  • C₄ Plant:
    • Hatch–Slack shuttle (mesophyll PEP regeneration): \(6 \times 2 = 12\,\mathrm{ATP}\).
    • Calvin cycle in bundle sheath: \(6 \times 3 = 18\,\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(6 \times 2 = 12\,\mathrm{NADPH}\).
    • Total for C₄ plant: \(12 + 18 = 30\,\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(12\,\mathrm{NADPH}\).

Applying Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors

Problem: A hypothetical C₃ greenhouse crop (bell pepper) is grown at \(24\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) under \(30\%\) of full sunlight and ambient \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (\(380\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\)). Increasing light intensity to \(60\%\) of full sunlight produces no change in photosynthetic rate, whereas raising \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to \(500\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\) increases the rate sharply. Identify the limiting factor before enrichment.

Solution:

Light saturation in most plants occurs at \(10\%\) of full sunlight, so at \(30\%\) light is already saturating; \(24\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is within the C₃ optimum (\(20\text{–}25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)). Because C₃ plants saturate only beyond \(450\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and increasing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) immediately elevated the rate, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) concentration was the limiting factor.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

In Cornelius van Niel's experiments on purple and green sulphur bacteria, \(\mathrm{O_2}\) was not evolved during photosynthesis because:

  • (A) These bacteria lack chlorophyll and use thermal energy instead of light.
  • (B) \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) acts as the hydrogen donor instead of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), yielding sulphur or sulphate as the oxidation product.
  • (C) Carbon dioxide is directly split to release \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) rather than \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  • (D) They perform photosynthesis only in the absence of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Van Niel demonstrated that photosynthesis is a light-dependent redox reaction (\(2\mathrm{H_2A} + \mathrm{CO_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{A} + [\mathrm{CH_2O}] + \mathrm{H_2O}\)). In sulphur bacteria, \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) is the hydrogen donor, so its oxidation yields sulphur or sulphate rather than \(\mathrm{O_2}\).

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following correctly describes the role of accessory pigments (chlorophyll b, xanthophylls, and carotenoids) in higher plants?

  • (A) They directly split water molecules in PS II to release \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  • (B) They form the P700 reaction centre of PS I.
  • (C) They absorb a wider range of wavelengths, transfer energy to chlorophyll a, and protect it from photo-oxidation.
  • (D) They catalyze the carboxylation of RuBP in the chloroplast stroma.

Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Chlorophyll a is the chief pigment forming reaction centres. Accessory pigments broaden the absorption spectrum by capturing intermediate wavelengths and funneling energy to Chlorophyll a, while shielding it from photo-oxidation.

Practice Question 3 Statement I & II

Statement I: Stroma lamellae membranes of the chloroplast lack both PS II and NADP reductase enzyme.
Statement II: Excitation of stroma lamellae results in cyclic photophosphorylation, producing both ATP and \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Stroma lamellae lack PS II and NADP reductase (Statement I is correct). Consequently, electrons excited from PS I cycle back to PS I via the ETS, generating only ATP and no \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\) (Statement II is incorrect).

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

During chemiosmotic ATP synthesis in the chloroplast, protons (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) accumulate in the:

  • (A) Intermembrane space between outer and inner chloroplast membranes
  • (B) Stroma, causing an increase in stromal acidity
  • (C) Thylakoid lumen, lowering the pH of the lumen
  • (D) Cytoplasm surrounding the chloroplast

Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Water splitting on the inner thylakoid surface, proton translocation via plastoquinone, and stromal \(\mathrm{H^+}\) consumption by NADP reductase all build a high \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration (low pH) inside the thylakoid lumen.

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

  • (B) The C₃ plant shows an increased photosynthetic rate, whereas the C₄ plant shows little or no further increase.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • PS I vs. PS II Order: PS II (P680) acts first in non-cyclic electron flow, even though PS I (P700) was discovered first.
  • "Dark Reaction" Misconception: Carbon reactions do not occur only in the dark; they stop soon after light ceases because \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) run out.
  • C₄ Mesophyll vs. Bundle Sheath Enzymes: Mesophyll cells in C₄ plants have PEPcase and lack RuBisCO; bundle sheath cells have RuBisCO and lack PEPcase.
  • Proton Accumulation Site: In chloroplasts, \(\mathrm{H^+}\) accumulates in the thylakoid lumen (not the intermembrane space).
  • Primary Acceptor Carbon Count: The primary \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) acceptor in the C₃ cycle is 5C RuBP (not a 2C compound), and in the C₄ cycle it is 3C PEP.

Concise Final Recap

Photosynthesis couples light-driven electron transport in thylakoid membranes (splitting \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) to release \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and generating \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) via chemiosmosis) with enzymatic \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixation in the stroma. All plants use the Calvin cycle (\(18\,\mathrm{ATP} + 12\,\mathrm{NADPH}\) per glucose), while C₄ plants add a mesophyll-to-bundle-sheath \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) pump via Kranz anatomy to eliminate wasteful photorespiration and thrive under high light and temperature. In nature, the rate of photosynthesis is governed by whichever factor—most commonly \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) for C₃ plants—is nearest to its minimum.
