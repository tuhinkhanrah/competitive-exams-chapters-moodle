NEET UG • Human Physiology

Excretory Products and Their Elimination

Comprehensive conceptual tutorial, physiological mechanisms, worked examples, and NEET UG practice assessment

Learning Objectives and Overview

Metabolic reactions inside living cells continuously generate nitrogenous wastes, carbon dioxide, excess water, and mineral ions such as \(\mathrm{Na^+}\), \(\mathrm{K^+}\), \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), phosphate, and sulphate. If these substances accumulate, they disrupt cellular pH, osmotic balance, and enzymatic function. Excretion is the regulated elimination of metabolic wastes, coupled closely with osmoregulation—the maintenance of water and ionic homeostasis in body fluids.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Classify animals as ammonotelic, ureotelic, or uricotelic based on nitrogenous waste toxicity and water availability.
  • Identify the major excretory structures across invertebrate and vertebrate taxa.
  • Describe the gross anatomy of the human urinary system and the microscopic organization of cortical and juxtamedullary nephrons.
  • Explain the three steps of urine formation: glomerular ultrafiltration, selective tubular reabsorption, and tubular secretion.
  • Trace solute and water movements along each nephron segment and explain how the counter-current mechanism concentrates urine.
  • Analyze hormonal regulation of kidney function via ADH, the Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System (RAAS), and Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF).
  • Understand the micturition reflex, accessory excretory roles of lungs, liver, and skin, and major renal disorders.

Nitrogenous Wastes and Modes of Excretion

The chemical form in which an animal eliminates excess nitrogen reflects an evolutionary trade-off between toxicity and water conservation. The three primary nitrogenous wastes are ammonia, urea, and uric acid.

  • Ammonotelism: Excretion of nitrogenous waste primarily as ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)). Ammonia is highly toxic and readily soluble in water, requiring a large volume of water for safe dilution and removal. In aquatic animals, it diffuses directly across body surfaces or gill epithelia as ammonium ions (\(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\)), with kidneys playing minimal roles.
  • Ureotelism: Excretion of nitrogenous waste mainly as urea. Terrestrial adaptation favored converting toxic ammonia into much less toxic urea in the liver. Urea enters the bloodstream, is filtered by the kidneys, and requires moderate water for elimination. Some animals also retain controlled amounts of urea in their renal medullary matrix to maintain tissue osmolarity.
  • Uricotelism: Excretion of nitrogenous waste as uric acid, the least toxic and least water-soluble of the three forms. Uric acid precipitates as a semi-solid paste or pellet, allowing maximal water conservation in arid terrestrial environments or inside shelled eggs.

Functional Anatomy of the Human Excretory System

The human excretory system comprises a pair of kidneys, a pair of ureters, a muscular urinary bladder, and a urethra.

Gross Structure of the Kidney

  • Location and Dimensions: Kidneys are reddish-brown, bean-shaped organs positioned retroperitoneally against the dorsal inner abdominal wall between the last thoracic (T12) and third lumbar (L3) vertebrae. In adults, each kidney measures approximately \(10\text{–}12\,\mathrm{cm}\) in length, \(5\text{–}7\,\mathrm{cm}\) in width, \(2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{cm}\) in thickness, and weighs \(120\text{–}170\,\mathrm{g}\).
  • Hilum and Pelvis: The medial concave border features a notch called the hilum, through which the ureter, renal blood vessels, and nerves pass. Just internal to the hilum lies a broad, funnel-shaped cavity—the renal pelvis—which branches into projections called calyces (singular: calyx).
  • Internal Zones: Enclosed by a tough outer renal capsule, the kidney parenchyma is organized into an outer cortex and an inner medulla. The medulla is partitioned into conical medullary pyramids whose tips project into the calyces. Cortical tissue extends inward between adjacent pyramids as renal columns known as the Columns of Bertini.

The Nephron: Structural and Functional Unit

Each human kidney contains nearly one million nephrons. Every nephron consists of two main components:

  1. Glomerulus: A dense tuft of capillaries fed by an afferent arteriole (a fine branch of the renal artery) and drained by an efferent arteriole.
  2. Renal Tubule: Begins as a double-walled, cup-shaped Bowman's capsule that surrounds the glomerulus. Together, the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule form the Malpighian body (or renal corpuscle). The tubule continues sequentially into:
    • Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): A highly coiled segment in the cortex.
    • Henle's Loop: A hairpin-shaped loop dipping into the medulla, with a descending limb and an ascending limb.
    • Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Another coiled cortical segment.
    • Collecting Duct: A straight duct receiving DCTs from multiple nephrons; many collecting ducts converge through medullary pyramids into the calyces and renal pelvis.

The efferent arteriole branches into a fine capillary network around the renal tubule called the peritubular capillaries. A specialized U-shaped vessel of this network runs parallel to Henle's loop and is termed the vasa recta.

Physiology of Urine Formation

Urine formation involves three coordinated processes: glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and tubular secretion.

1. Glomerular Filtration (Ultrafiltration)

On average, the kidneys filter \(1100\text{–}1200\,\mathrm{mL}\) of blood per minute—roughly one-fifth of the blood pumped by each ventricle per minute. Driven by glomerular capillary blood pressure, plasma filters across a three-layered filtration membrane:

  1. Endothelium of glomerular blood vessels
  2. Basement membrane situated between the two cellular layers
  3. Epithelium of Bowman's capsule, composed of specialized cells called podocytes arranged to leave minute filtration slits (slit pores)

Because this barrier blocks blood cells and large plasma proteins, virtually all other plasma constituents enter Bowman's capsule as a protein-free ultrafiltrate. The volume of filtrate formed per minute is the Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR):

\[ \mathrm{GFR} \approx 125\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}} = 180\,\mathrm{L\,day^{-1}} \]

Autoregulation of GFR: The Juxtaglomerular Apparatus (JGA) is a sensitive region formed by cellular modifications where the DCT contacts the afferent arteriole. A drop in GFR activates JG cells to release renin, which increases glomerular blood flow and restores GFR to normal.

2. Tubular Reabsorption and Secretion

Although \(180\,\mathrm{L}\) of filtrate is produced daily, only about \(1.5\,\mathrm{L}\) of urine is excreted—meaning nearly 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed by tubular epithelial cells. Reabsorption occurs via:

  • Active transport: Glucose, amino acids, and \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) ions.
  • Passive transport: Nitrogenous wastes (e.g., urea) and water (in initial nephron segments).

Simultaneously, tubular cells perform tubular secretion, actively transporting \(\mathrm{H^+}\), \(\mathrm{K^+}\), and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) from peritubular blood into the filtrate to regulate acid–base (pH) and ionic balance.

Mechanism of Concentration of the Filtrate

Mammals conserve water by producing urine up to four times more concentrated than the initial glomerular filtrate. This depends on the counter-current mechanism operating between the two limbs of Henle's loop and the parallel vasa recta.

  • Opposite Flow Directions: Filtrate flows in opposite directions in the descending and ascending limbs of Henle's loop. Blood likewise flows in opposite directions in the descending and ascending limbs of the vasa recta.
  • Cortico-Medullary Osmotic Gradient: Osmolarity increases steadily from \(300\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) in the cortex to about \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) in the inner medulla. This gradient is generated primarily by \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) and urea.
  • Solute Cycling: \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) transported out of the ascending limb of Henle's loop enters the descending limb of the vasa recta and is returned to the medullary interstitium by the ascending limb of the vasa recta. Simultaneously, small amounts of urea enter the thin ascending limb of Henle's loop and are recycled back into the deep medullary interstitium from the collecting duct.
  • Final Concentration: As filtrate travels down the collecting duct through this steep interstitial gradient, water moves out osmotically into the hypertonic medulla, concentrating the urine.

Regulation of Kidney Function

Kidney activity is tightly regulated by hormonal feedback loops involving the hypothalamus, the juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA), and the heart.

1. Hypothalamus and Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH / Vasopressin)

Changes in blood volume, body fluid volume, and ionic concentration activate osmoreceptors. Excessive fluid loss stimulates the hypothalamus to release ADH (vasopressin) from the neurohypophysis. ADH increases water reabsorption in the distal parts of the renal tubule, preventing diuresis (excessive urine loss). ADH also constricts blood vessels, raising blood pressure, glomerular blood flow, and GFR. Conversely, an increase in body fluid volume switches off osmoreceptors and suppresses ADH release.

2. JGA and the Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System (RAAS)

A fall in glomerular blood flow, glomerular blood pressure, or GFR triggers JG cells to release renin:

\[ \text{Angiotensinogen} \xrightarrow{\text{Renin}} \text{Angiotensin I} \longrightarrow \text{Angiotensin II} \]
  • Angiotensin II is a potent vasoconstrictor that elevates glomerular blood pressure and restores GFR.
  • Angiotensin II also stimulates the adrenal cortex to secrete aldosterone, which promotes \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) and water reabsorption from the distal tubules, further increasing blood pressure and GFR.

3. Heart and Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF)

When blood flow to the cardiac atria increases (high blood volume/pressure), atrial cells release Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF). ANF causes vasodilation and lowers blood pressure, acting as a physiological check on the renin–angiotensin mechanism.

Micturition, Accessory Excretory Organs, and Disorders

Micturition and Urine Characteristics

Urine is stored in the urinary bladder until stretching of its wall activates stretch receptors, which send signals to the central nervous system (CNS). The CNS responds with motor signals causing contraction of bladder smooth muscles and simultaneous relaxation of the urethral sphincter, releasing urine—a neural process called the micturition reflex.

  • Normal Output & Composition: An adult excretes \(1\text{–}1.5\,\mathrm{L}\) of light yellow, slightly acidic (\(\mathrm{pH} \approx 6.0\)) urine per day, containing \(25\text{–}30\,\mathrm{g}\) of urea daily.
  • Clinical Diagnostics: Presence of glucose (glycosuria) and ketone bodies (ketonuria) in urine indicates diabetes mellitus.

Role of Other Organs in Excretion

  • Lungs: Eliminate approximately \(200\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) along with significant quantities of water vapor.
  • Liver: Secretes bile containing bilirubin, biliverdin, cholesterol, degraded steroid hormones, vitamins, and drugs, which are eliminated with digestive wastes.
  • Skin: Sweat glands secrete watery fluid containing \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), small amounts of urea, and lactic acid (primarily for evaporative cooling). Sebaceous glands eliminate sterols, hydrocarbons, and waxes via sebum. Trace nitrogenous wastes are also eliminated in saliva.

Disorders of the Excretory System

  • Uremia: Accumulation of urea in the blood due to kidney malfunction, which can lead to renal failure.
  • Hemodialysis: Blood is drawn from a convenient artery, mixed with an anticoagulant (heparin), and pumped through a coiled cellophane tube inside an artificial kidney. The surrounding dialysing fluid matches plasma composition except that it lacks nitrogenous wastes, allowing wastes to diffuse out down a concentration gradient. Cleared blood is mixed with anti-heparin and returned via a vein.
  • Kidney Transplantation: Ultimate treatment for acute renal failure, ideally using a functioning kidney from a close relative to minimize immune rejection.
  • Renal Calculi: Stones or insoluble masses of crystallized salts (such as oxalates) formed within the kidney.
  • Glomerulonephritis: Inflammation of the kidney glomeruli.

Worked Examples

Calculating Daily Tubular Reabsorption Percentage

Scenario (Hypothetical Values): Suppose a healthy adult has an assumed GFR of \(120\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}\) and produces \(1.44\,\mathrm{L}\) of urine over \(24\,\mathrm{hours}\). Calculate the total volume of filtrate formed per day and the percentage of filtrate reabsorbed by the renal tubules.

Solution:

First, convert the assumed GFR from \(\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}\) to \(\mathrm{L\,day^{-1}}\) using \(1440\,\mathrm{min\,day^{-1}}\):

\[ \text{Daily Filtrate Volume} = 120\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}} \times 1440\,\mathrm{min\,day^{-1}} = 172{,}800\,\mathrm{mL\,day^{-1}} = 172.8\,\mathrm{L\,day^{-1}} \]

Next, subtract the daily urine output to find the volume reabsorbed:

\[ \text{Reabsorbed Volume} = 172.8\,\mathrm{L} - 1.44\,\mathrm{L} = 171.36\,\mathrm{L} \]

Calculate the percentage reabsorbed:

\[ \text{Percentage Reabsorbed} = \left(\frac{171.36}{172.8}\right) \times 100\% = 99.17\% \]

Takeaway: Consistent with normal renal physiology, slightly over \(99\%\) of the glomerular filtrate is reclaimed by tubular reabsorption.

Tracing Osmolarity Changes Along the Nephron

Scenario (Hypothetical Values): Assume glomerular filtrate enters Bowman's capsule at \(300\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) and passes through a juxtamedullary nephron where the inner medullary interstitium is maintained at \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\). Describe how the osmolarity of the tubular fluid changes as it moves through the descending limb of Henle's loop, the ascending limb of Henle's loop, and the collecting duct under high ADH levels.

Solution:

  1. Descending Limb of Henle's Loop: Permeable to water but nearly impermeable to electrolytes. Water diffuses out into the hypertonic medulla, so filtrate osmolarity rises from \(300\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) toward \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) at the hairpin bend.
  2. Ascending Limb of Henle's Loop: Impermeable to water, while \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) moves out into the interstitium (passively in the thin segment, actively in the thick segment). Consequently, the fluid becomes progressively diluted, dropping below \(300\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) as it enters the DCT.
  3. Collecting Duct (High ADH): High ADH increases water permeability. As the fluid descends through the medullary gradient (\(300 \to 1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\)), water leaves osmotically, concentrating the final urine up to \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) (fourfold concentration).

Predicting Hormonal Responses to Dehydration and Volume Overload

Scenario (Hypothetical Case): Compare the hormonal profile (ADH, Renin/Aldosterone, and ANF) of an individual experiencing severe dehydration (low blood volume and pressure) versus an individual receiving rapid intravenous saline infusion (high atrial blood flow).

Solution:

  • Severe Dehydration: Osmoreceptors stimulate the hypothalamus to increase ADH release (conserving water and constricting vessels). Simultaneously, low glomerular blood pressure activates JG cells to secrete renin, generating Angiotensin II and stimulating aldosterone release (\(\mathrm{Na^+}\) and water retention). ANF secretion remains suppressed.
  • High Atrial Blood Flow: Increased venous return stretches the cardiac atria, triggering ANF release. ANF promotes vasodilation, lowers blood pressure, and counteracts the RAAS pathway, while increased body fluid volume switches off osmoreceptors and suppresses ADH.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following combinations correctly pairs an animal with its excretory structure and primary nitrogenous waste?

  • (A) Amphioxus — Nephridia — Urea
  • (B) Cockroach — Malpighian tubules — Uric acid
  • (C) Prawn — Flame cells — Ammonia
  • (D) Planaria — Antennal glands — Uric acid
Answer: (B)

Solution: Insects such as cockroaches possess Malpighian tubules and are uricotelic (excreting uric acid). Amphioxus and Planaria have protonephridia (flame cells), whereas prawns use antennal (green) glands.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the human kidney, the cortical projections extending between adjacent medullary pyramids are known as:

  • (A) Renal calyces
  • (B) Columns of Bertini
  • (C) Vasa recta
  • (D) Renal pelvis
Answer: (B)

Solution: Cortical tissue extends inward between the medullary pyramids as renal columns called Columns of Bertini. Calyces and the renal pelvis are urine-collecting cavities, and vasa recta are medullary capillaries.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which statement accurately describes the permeability of the limbs of Henle's loop?

  • (A) Both descending and ascending limbs are freely permeable to water.
  • (B) The descending limb is permeable to electrolytes, whereas the ascending limb is permeable to water.
  • (C) The descending limb is permeable to water and almost impermeable to electrolytes, whereas the ascending limb is impermeable to water.
  • (D) Both limbs are impermeable to water and actively transport urea outward.
Answer: (C)

Solution: The descending limb of Henle's loop allows water reabsorption (concentrating the filtrate) and is almost impermeable to electrolytes. The ascending limb is impermeable to water but allows active or passive transport of electrolytes, diluting the filtrate.

Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Terrestrial reptiles and birds excrete nitrogenous wastes as uric acid rather than ammonia.
Reason (R): Uric acid is the least toxic nitrogenous waste and can be eliminated as a pellet or paste with minimum water loss.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Terrestrial adaptation requires conserving water. Because uric acid has very low toxicity and low solubility, birds and reptiles excrete it as a paste or pellet with minimal water loss, directly explaining why they are uricotelic.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

During glomerular ultrafiltration, blood plasma passes through three layers before entering the lumen of Bowman's capsule. Which layer contains specialized cells called podocytes?

  • (A) Endothelium of glomerular capillaries
  • (B) Basement membrane
  • (C) Epithelium of Bowman's capsule
  • (D) Tunica media of the afferent arteriole
Answer: (C)

Solution: Podocytes are specialized epithelial cells forming the inner wall of Bowman's capsule. They leave minute filtration slits (slit pores) that permit protein-free ultrafiltration.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A sudden drop in glomerular blood pressure activates the juxtaglomerular (JG) cells to release:

  • (A) Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF)
  • (B) Renin
  • (C) Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)
  • (D) Aldosterone
Answer: (B)

Solution: Low glomerular blood flow or GFR stimulates JG cells to release renin, initiating the conversion of angiotensinogen to angiotensin I and subsequently angiotensin II, which restores GFR and stimulates aldosterone secretion.

Practice Question 7 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Consider the following statements regarding the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT):
Statement I: PCT is lined by simple cuboidal brush-border epithelium that increases the surface area for reabsorption.
Statement II: Roughly 70–80% of electrolytes and water, along with nearly all essential nutrients, are reabsorbed in the PCT.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Both statements are accurate. The microvilli of the simple cuboidal brush-border epithelium greatly expand absorptive surface area, making the PCT the primary site for bulk reabsorption of nutrients, water, and electrolytes.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which pair of substances is primarily responsible for establishing the \(300 \to 1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) osmolarity gradient in the renal medullary interstitium?

  • (A) Glucose and \(\mathrm{KCl}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) and urea
  • (C) \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\) and creatinine
  • (D) Amino acids and uric acid
Answer: (B)

Solution: The cortico-medullary osmotic gradient is maintained primarily by \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (transported by the ascending limb of Henle's loop and exchanged with the vasa recta) and urea (recycled between the collecting duct and thin ascending limb).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

During hemodialysis, the dialysing fluid surrounding the cellophane tube has the same composition as blood plasma except that it lacks:

  • (A) Glucose and amino acids
  • (B) Sodium and chloride ions
  • (C) Nitrogenous wastes
  • (D) Bicarbonate ions
Answer: (C)

Solution: Because the dialysing fluid lacks nitrogenous wastes (such as urea), these wastes diffuse freely out of the patient's blood across the porous cellophane membrane down their concentration gradient, while essential plasma solutes remain balanced.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Amphioxus Excretion: Even though Amphioxus is a cephalochordate, it uses protonephridia (flame cells), not kidneys.
  • Ascending vs. Descending Limb: Do not confuse their permeabilities—Descending is water-permeable (Dehydrates/concentrates filtrate); Ascending is water-impermeable (dilutes filtrate).
  • Heparin vs. Anti-heparin: In hemodialysis, heparin is added before blood enters the dialyser (to prevent clotting), and anti-heparin is added before blood returns to the vein.
  • ANF vs. RAAS: RAAS raises blood pressure (vasoconstriction + \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) retention), whereas ANF lowers blood pressure (vasodilation) and checks RAAS.
  • Cortical Nephrons and Vasa Recta: Vasa recta are absent or highly reduced in cortical nephrons and well-developed in juxtamedullary nephrons.
Quick Revision Numbers
  • Renal blood flow: \(1100\text{–}1200\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}\) (~1/5th of cardiac output)
  • GFR: \(125\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}\) (\(180\,\mathrm{L\,day^{-1}}\))
  • Normal urine output: \(1\text{–}1.5\,\mathrm{L\,day^{-1}}\) (\(\mathrm{pH} \approx 6.0\); \(25\text{–}30\,\mathrm{g}\) urea/day)
  • PCT reabsorption: 70–80% of electrolytes and water; ~100% of essential nutrients
  • Medullary osmolarity gradient: \(300\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) (cortex) to \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) (inner medulla)
  • Lung \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) elimination: ~\(200\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}\)

Concise Final Recap

Animals eliminate nitrogenous wastes as ammonia, urea, or uric acid depending on water availability. In humans, one million nephrons per kidney filter blood at \(125\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}\) across a three-layered glomerular barrier, reabsorb ~99% of the filtrate, and secrete \(\mathrm{H^+}\), \(\mathrm{K^+}\), and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) to maintain acid–base and ionic balance. The counter-current arrangement of Henle's loop and the vasa recta builds a \(300\text{–}1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) medullary gradient using \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) and urea, enabling fourfold urine concentration under ADH control, while RAAS and ANF provide antagonistic blood-pressure and GFR regulation.
