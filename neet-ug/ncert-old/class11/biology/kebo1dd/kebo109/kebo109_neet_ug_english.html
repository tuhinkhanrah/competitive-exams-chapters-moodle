Biomolecules: Chemical Composition, Structure, Metabolism, and Enzymes

A complete, exam-oriented study guide covering cellular chemistry, macromolecular architecture, metabolic flux, and enzyme kinetics.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Living organisms exhibit extraordinary morphological diversity, yet their underlying chemical makeup and biochemical reactions follow remarkably uniform principles. This tutorial builds a complete conceptual bridge from simple elemental analysis to the complex three-dimensional catalysis of enzymes.

  • Distinguish between living tissue and inanimate matter based on relative elemental abundance and molecular organization.
  • Separate cellular chemicals into the acid-soluble micromolecular pool and the acid-insoluble macromolecular fraction, and explain why lipids appear in the insoluble pellet.
  • Identify the structures, classifications, and biological roles of amino acids, lipids, nucleotides, polysaccharides, proteins, and nucleic acids.
  • Analyze the four levels of protein structure and the Watson–Crick geometry of B-DNA.
  • Connect anabolic and catabolic pathways to ATP energy currency and the non-equilibrium steady state of living systems.
  • Interpret enzyme catalysis, activation energy profiles, Michaelis–Menten-style saturation behavior, competitive inhibition, IUBMB enzyme classes, and co-factors.

Chemical Analysis of Living Tissues

Qualitatively, every element found in a living cell is also present in the Earth's crust. However, quantitative elemental analysis reveals a striking difference: living organisms have a much higher relative abundance of carbon (C) and hydrogen (H) compared to non-living matter, whereas silicon (Si) is abundant in the crust yet negligible in living tissues.

Fractionation of Organic and Inorganic Constituents

Two complementary laboratory procedures are used to determine the organic and inorganic makeup of a tissue sample (such as a leaf or liver):

  1. Trichloroacetic Acid (\(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH}\)) Grinding: Living tissue is ground in \(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH}\) using a mortar and pestle to form a thick slurry, which is then strained through cheesecloth or cotton.
    • Filtrate (Acid-Soluble Pool): Contains thousands of small organic compounds (molecular weight roughly \(18\text{ to }800\text{ Da}\)) along with dissolved inorganic ions like \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) and \(\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}\). This fraction roughly represents the cytoplasmic composition.
    • Retentate (Acid-Insoluble Fraction): Contains polymeric macromolecules (proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides) with molecular weights of \(10,000\text{ Da}\) and above, plus lipids.
  2. Destructive Ash Analysis: A weighed fresh tissue sample (wet weight) is dried until all water evaporates, yielding its dry weight. Complete combustion oxidizes all carbon compounds into gaseous \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and water vapour, leaving behind ash, which contains inorganic elements (calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, etc.).

Key Concept: Why Lipids Appear in the Acid-Insoluble Fraction Lipids have low molecular weights (less than \(800\text{ Da}\)) and are not strictly macromolecules. However, when tissue is ground, cell membranes and organelle membranes break into fragments that spontaneously seal into water-insoluble vesicles. These vesicles are retained in the acid-insoluble pellet alongside true polymeric macromolecules.

Micromolecules and Metabolites

Biomolecules with molecular weights below \(1,000\text{ Da}\) are termed micromolecules. Because they participate in cellular chemical reactions, they are also called metabolites.

Amino Acids: Substituted Methanes

Amino acids are organic compounds in which an amino group (\(\mathrm{-NH_2}\)), a carboxyl group (\(\mathrm{-COOH}\)), a hydrogen atom (\(\mathrm{-H}\)), and a variable side chain (\(\mathrm{-R}\)) are all attached to the same \(\alpha\)-carbon. Only 20 types of amino acids occur in proteins.

  • Simple R-Group Examples: In glycine, \(\mathrm{R = -H}\); in alanine, \(\mathrm{R = -CH_3}\); in serine, \(\mathrm{R = -CH_2OH}\).
  • Classification by Functional Groups: Acidic (e.g., glutamic acid), basic (e.g., lysine), neutral (e.g., valine), and aromatic (tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan).
  • Nutritional Classification: Non-essential amino acids can be synthesized by the human body, whereas essential amino acids must be supplied through dietary proteins.
  • Zwitterionic Nature: Because both \(\mathrm{-NH_2}\) and \(\mathrm{-COOH}\) are ionizable, the net charge of an amino acid changes with solution pH. At a specific intermediate pH, it exists as a dipolar ion called a zwitterion, carrying both a positive and a negative charge.

Lipids

Lipids are generally water-insoluble organic compounds. They include:

  • Fatty Acids: A carboxyl group attached to an R group containing 1 to 19 carbons (e.g., palmitic acid has 16 carbons including the carboxyl carbon; arachidonic acid has 20 carbons including the carboxyl carbon). Fatty acids may be saturated (no double bonds) or unsaturated (one or more \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds).
  • Glycerol and Glycerides: Glycerol is trihydroxy propane. When fatty acids esterify with glycerol, they form monoglycerides, diglycerides, and triglycerides. Based on melting point, triglycerides are classified as fats (higher melting point, solid at room temperature) or oils (lower melting point, e.g., gingelly oil, which stays liquid even in winter).
  • Phospholipids: Contain phosphorus and a phosphorylated organic compound (e.g., lecithin) and form essential components of cell membranes.
  • Complex Lipids: Neural tissues, in particular, contain lipids with more complex structures (as well as sterols such as cholesterol).

Nitrogenous Bases, Nucleosides, and Nucleotides

Living cells contain heterocyclic nitrogenous bases classified into purines (adenine, guanine) and pyrimidines (cytosine, uracil, thymine). Combining these with a pentose sugar (ribose or 2'-deoxyribose) and phosphate yields the building blocks of nucleic acids:

  • Nucleoside = Nitrogenous base + Pentose sugar: Adenosine, guanosine, thymidine, uridine, cytidine.
  • Nucleotide = Nitrogenous base + Pentose sugar + Phosphate group: Adenylic acid, guanylic acid, thymidylic acid, uridylic acid, cytidylic acid.

Primary vs. Secondary Metabolites

Primary metabolites (amino acids, sugars, fatty acids, nucleotides) are found in all animal, plant, and microbial cells and perform identifiable roles in normal physiological processes. In contrast, plants, fungi, and microbes produce thousands of additional compounds called secondary metabolites. While their exact functions in the host organism are not always fully understood, many have ecological roles or high value for human welfare.

Biomacromolecules and Their Structural Hierarchy

True biomacromolecules are high-molecular-weight polymers (\(\ge 10,000\text{ Da}\)) formed by linking monomeric subunits via dehydration synthesis (elimination of a water molecule).

Proteins

Proteins are linear polypeptides—heteropolymers composed of 20 different amino acids joined by peptide bonds (formed between the \(\mathrm{-COOH}\) of one amino acid and the \(\mathrm{-NH_2}\) of the next). Because proteins are heteropolymers rather than homopolymers (which repeat a single monomer), they exhibit immense functional diversity:

Four Levels of Protein Structure

  1. Primary Structure: The linear sequence of amino acids from the first amino acid at the left end (N-terminal) to the last amino acid at the right end (C-terminal).
  2. Secondary Structure: Local folding of the polypeptide backbone into regular motifs such as a right-handed \(\alpha\)-helix (resembling a revolving staircase) or a \(\beta\)-pleated sheet. Note that only right-handed helices are observed in proteins.
  3. Tertiary Structure: The long protein chain folds upon itself like a hollow woolen ball, creating a compact 3-dimensional conformation stabilized by hydrogen bonds, disulphide bonds, and other interactions. Tertiary structure is absolutely necessary for the biological activity of proteins (including enzyme active sites).
  4. Quaternary Structure: Assembly of more than one polypeptide subunit into a functional architecture (e.g., adult human haemoglobin, \(\mathrm{Hb}\), consists of 4 subunits: two \(\alpha\)-type and two \(\beta\)-type chains).

Polysaccharides

Polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharides linked by glycosidic bonds (formed by dehydration between two carbon atoms of adjacent sugars).

  • Cellulose: A homopolymer of glucose forming plant cell walls, cotton fibre, and paper. It lacks complex helices and therefore cannot hold \(\mathrm{I_2}\) molecules.
  • Starch: A glucose homopolymer serving as the energy store in plant tissues. It forms helical secondary structures that trap \(\mathrm{I_2}\) molecules, giving a characteristic blue colour.
  • Glycogen: A branched glucose homopolymer serving as the energy store in animals. In a glycogen chain, the right end is the reducing end and the left end is the non-reducing end.
  • Inulin: A homopolymer of fructose.
  • Chitin: A complex homopolysaccharide made of chemically modified amino-sugars (such as N-acetyl glucosamine), forming the exoskeleton of arthropods.

Nucleic Acids and the B-DNA Double Helix

Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polynucleotides. Adjacent nucleotides are joined by a 3'–5' phosphodiester bond, where a phosphate moiety links the 3'-carbon of one sugar to the 5'-carbon of the next sugar via ester bonds on both sides.

In the Watson–Crick model of B-DNA:

  • Two polynucleotide strands run antiparallel (\(5'\to 3'\) and \(3'\to 5'\)).
  • The sugar-phosphate-sugar chain forms the outer backbone, while nitrogenous bases project inward, roughly perpendicular to the backbone.
  • Complementary base pairing is obligatory: Adenine (A) pairs with Thymine (T) via 2 hydrogen bonds, and Guanine (G) pairs with Cytosine (C) via 3 hydrogen bonds.
  • Each base pair step turns by 36°. One complete helical turn contains 10 base pairs (bp) with a pitch of 34 Å, giving a rise per base pair of 3.4 Å.

Metabolism and the Living State

All biomolecules inside a cell undergo constant turnover—they are continuously broken down and synthesized through linked chemical reactions collectively called metabolism. This continuous, directed flow of metabolites through linear or circular crisscrossing pathways is known as the dynamic state of body constituents.

Crucially, every metabolic reaction in living systems is catalysed by enzymes; even the physical dissolution of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) in water is enzyme-catalysed in cells.

Nature of pathwayBiosynthetic (simpler → complex structure)Degradative (complex → simpler structure)

ExamplesAcetic acid → Cholesterol; Amino acids → ProteinGlucose → Lactic acid (10-step glycolysis in muscle)
The Living State: A Non-Equilibrium Steady State Systems at thermodynamic equilibrium cannot perform work. Because living organisms must continuously perform osmotic, mechanical, and biosynthetic work, they maintain biomolecules at characteristic steady-state concentrations (for example, normal human blood glucose is \(4.2\text{–}6.1\text{ mmol/L}\)). Thus, the living state is a non-equilibrium steady state sustained by constant energy input from metabolism. Without metabolism, there cannot be a living state.

Enzymes: Mechanism, Kinetics, and Classification

Almost all enzymes are proteins with a specific three-dimensional tertiary structure containing crevices or pockets. One such pocket is the active site, where the substrate binds. A notable exception is ribozymes—nucleic acids that exhibit catalytic activity.

While inorganic catalysts operate efficiently at high temperatures and pressures, most enzymes denature and lose activity above \(40^\circ\mathrm{C}\). However, enzymes isolated from thermophilic organisms inhabiting hot vents and sulphur springs retain thermal stability and catalytic power up to \(80^\circ\mathrm{C}\text{–}90^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Reaction Rates and Activation Energy

The rate of a physical or chemical process is the amount of product formed per unit time:

\[\text{Rate} = \frac{\delta P}{\delta t}\]

As a general rule of thumb, reaction rate doubles or halves for every \(10^\circ\mathrm{C}\) change in temperature. Enzymes accelerate reactions enormously. For instance, the hydration of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to carbonic acid (\(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\)) produces only about \(200\text{ molecules/hour}\) without a catalyst, but with carbonic anhydrase it produces about \(600,000\text{ molecules/second}\)—an acceleration of roughly 10 million times.

During catalysis, the substrate (S) binds to the enzyme (E) to form a transient, unstable enzyme–substrate complex (ES), passes through an unstable high-energy transition state, forms an enzyme–product complex (EP), and releases the product (P):

\[\mathrm{E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \longrightarrow EP \longrightarrow E + P}\]

Enzymes speed up reactions by lowering the activation energy—the energy barrier between the average potential energy of the substrate and the transition state.

Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

  • pH and Temperature: Enzymes show maximal activity at an optimum pH and optimum temperature. Low temperatures preserve enzymes in a temporarily inactive state, whereas high temperatures denature the protein.
  • Substrate Concentration (\([\mathrm{S}]\)): Reaction velocity initially rises with \([\mathrm{S}]\) until it reaches a plateau at maximum velocity (\(V_{\max}\)), where all enzyme active sites are saturated. The substrate concentration at which velocity equals \(V_{\max}/2\) is the Michaelis constant (\(K_m\)).
  • Competitive Inhibition: An inhibitor structurally resembling the substrate competes for the active site, reducing enzyme activity (e.g., malonate competitively inhibits succinic dehydrogenase by mimicking succinate).

Six Classes of Enzymes and Co-factors

Enzymes are divided into 6 classes (each with 4–13 subclasses) and assigned a four-digit classification number:

Enzyme Class Reaction Type Catalysed
1. Oxidoreductases / DehydrogenasesCatalyse oxidoreduction between two substrates (\(\mathrm{S_{reduced} + S'_{oxidised} \to S_{oxidised} + S'_{reduced}}\))
2. TransferasesTransfer a group \(\mathrm{G}\) (other than hydrogen) between substrates (\(\mathrm{S-G + S' \to S + S'-G}\))
3. HydrolasesHydrolysis of ester, ether, peptide, glycosidic, \(\mathrm{C-C}\), \(\mathrm{C-halide}\), or \(\mathrm{P-N}\) bonds
4. LyasesRemoval of groups by non-hydrolytic mechanisms, leaving double bonds
5. IsomerasesInterconversion of optical, geometric, or positional isomers
6. LigasesLinking together of two compounds (forming \(\mathrm{C-O}\), \(\mathrm{C-S}\), \(\mathrm{C-N}\), \(\mathrm{P-O}\) bonds)

Many enzymes require non-protein constituents called co-factors to become catalytically active; the protein portion alone is the apoenzyme. Removing the co-factor abolishes catalytic activity.

  • Prosthetic Groups: Organic compounds tightly bound to the apoenzyme (e.g., haem at the active site of peroxidase and catalase, which break down \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) to \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\)).
  • Co-enzymes: Organic compounds whose association with the apoenzyme is transient during catalysis; many are derived from vitamins (e.g., NAD and NADP contain the vitamin niacin).
  • Metal Ions: Form coordination bonds with active-site side chains and the substrate simultaneously (e.g., zinc, \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\) is a co-factor for carboxypeptidase).

Worked Examples

Calculating Base Composition and Length of a B-DNA Segment

Problem (Assumed Hypothetical Values): A double-stranded B-DNA segment contains \(1,200\) base pairs. If \(300\) of these base pairs are Adenine–Thymine (\(\mathrm{A=T}\)) pairs, calculate: (a) the total number of Guanine–Cytosine (\(\mathrm{G\equiv C}\)) pairs, (b) the total number of hydrogen bonds holding the two strands together, and (c) the total physical length of this DNA segment in Ångströms (\(\mathrm{Å}\)).

Solution:

  1. Total base pairs \(= 1,200\). Since base pairs in B-DNA are either \(\mathrm{A=T}\) or \(\mathrm{G\equiv C}\), the number of \(\mathrm{G\equiv C}\) pairs is: \[1,200 - 300 = 900\text{ G}\equiv\text{C pairs}\]
  2. Each \(\mathrm{A=T}\) pair has \(2\) hydrogen bonds and each \(\mathrm{G\equiv C}\) pair has \(3\) hydrogen bonds: \[\text{Total H-bonds} = (300 \times 2) + (900 \times 3) = 600 + 2,700 = 3,300\text{ hydrogen bonds}\]
  3. In B-DNA, the rise per base pair is \(3.4\,\mathrm{Å}\) (since \(10\text{ bp}\) span a pitch of \(34\,\mathrm{Å}\)): \[\text{Length} = 1,200 \times 3.4\,\mathrm{Å} = 4,080\,\mathrm{Å}\]

Distinguishing Acid-Soluble and Acid-Insoluble Fractions

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A student grinds a fresh plant leaf in trichloroacetic acid and filters the slurry through cheesecloth. Four compounds are present in the original leaf: glycine, lecithin, glycogen, and \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) ions. Classify each into the filtrate (acid-soluble pool) or retentate (acid-insoluble fraction) and justify your answer.

Solution:

  • Glycine: Filtrate (acid-soluble pool) — It is a small amino acid (molecular weight \(75\text{ Da}\), well below \(800\text{ Da}\)) that dissolves in the aqueous acid medium.
  • \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) ions: Filtrate (acid-soluble pool) — Inorganic ions like sulphate and phosphate appear in the acid-soluble fraction.
  • Glycogen (or Starch in plants): Retentate (acid-insoluble fraction) — Polysaccharides are true polymeric macromolecules with molecular weights above \(10,000\text{ Da}\).
  • Lecithin: Retentate (acid-insoluble fraction) — Although lecithin is a phospholipid with molecular weight under \(800\text{ Da}\), it forms part of cell membranes that break into water-insoluble vesicles upon grinding, separating with the macromolecular pellet.

Determining Peptide Bonds and Water Molecules in Polypeptide Synthesis

Problem (Assumed Hypothetical Values): A hypothetical linear polypeptide chain is composed of \(85\) amino acid residues. How many peptide bonds link these residues, how many water molecules are eliminated during its dehydration synthesis, and how many free N-terminal and C-terminal ends does this single chain possess?

Solution:

  • A linear chain of \(n\) monomers contains \(n - 1\) covalent linkages: \[\text{Peptide bonds} = 85 - 1 = 84\]
  • Each peptide bond forms by eliminating one water molecule (dehydration), so \(84\) water molecules are released.
  • A single linear polypeptide has exactly 1 free N-terminal end (first amino acid) and 1 free C-terminal end (last amino acid).

Analyzing Enzyme Kinetics and Competitive Inhibition

Problem (Assumed Hypothetical Values): An enzyme-catalysed reaction has a maximum velocity \(V_{\max} = 120\,\mathrm{µmol/min}\) and reaches a velocity of \(60\,\mathrm{µmol/min}\) at a substrate concentration of \(4\,\mathrm{mmol/L}\). (a) What is the \(K_m\) of this enzyme? (b) If a competitive inhibitor is added, what happens to the rate of reaction at low substrate concentration, and why?

Solution:

  • By definition, \(K_m\) is the substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity is half of \(V_{\max}\): \[\frac{V_{\max}}{2} = \frac{120}{2} = 60\,\mathrm{µmol/min}\] Since this velocity occurs at \([\mathrm{S}] = 4\,\mathrm{mmol/L}\), we have \(K_m = 4\,\mathrm{mmol/L}\).
  • Adding a competitive inhibitor (which structurally mimics the substrate) causes it to compete for the enzyme's active site. At low substrate concentrations, fewer active sites are available to the substrate, so the reaction velocity decreases.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid:
  • Glycogen Ends: Do not confuse the ends—in glycogen, the right end is reducing and the left end is non-reducing.
  • Arachidonic vs. Palmitic Acid Carbon Count: Both counts include the carboxyl carbon (Palmitic = 16 carbons total; Arachidonic = 20 carbons total).
  • Ribozymes: Do not assume 100% of enzymes are proteins; ribozymes are catalytic nucleic acids.
  • Lipids as Macromolecules: Lipids are found in the acid-insoluble fraction due to membrane vesicle formation, not because their molecular weight exceeds \(10,000\text{ Da}\).
  • Prosthetic Group vs. Co-enzyme: Both are organic, but prosthetic groups are tightly bound (e.g., haem), whereas co-enzymes associate transiently during catalysis (e.g., NAD, NADP containing niacin).

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Can you state the relative order of cellular mass: Water (70–90%) > Proteins (10–15%) > Nucleic acids (5–7%) > Carbohydrates (3%) > Lipids (2%) > Ions (1%)?
  • Can you distinguish nucleosides (adenosine, uridine) from nucleotides (adenylic acid, uridylic acid)?
  • Do you remember the B-DNA metrics: \(36^\circ\) per bp, \(10\text{ bp/turn}\), \(34\,\mathrm{Å}\) pitch, \(3.4\,\mathrm{Å}\) rise per bp?
  • Can you match all 6 enzyme classes and give an example of competitive inhibition (malonate on succinic dehydrogenase)?

Concise Final Recap

Living systems are built from the same elements as the Earth's crust, but enriched in carbon and hydrogen, with water as the most abundant cellular chemical. Small biomolecules (< 800 Da) form the acid-soluble pool, whereas polymeric biomacromolecules—proteins (peptide bonds), polysaccharides (glycosidic bonds), and nucleic acids (phosphodiester bonds)—along with membrane-derived lipid vesicles form the acid-insoluble fraction. Continuous enzyme-catalysed anabolic and catabolic reactions sustain a non-equilibrium steady state powered by ATP. Enzymes accelerate biochemical conversions by lowering activation energy, depend on optimum pH, temperature, substrate concentration, and co-factors, and are systematically grouped into six functional classes.
