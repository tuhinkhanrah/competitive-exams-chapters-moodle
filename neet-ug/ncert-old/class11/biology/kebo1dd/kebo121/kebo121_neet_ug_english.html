Neural Control and Coordination

Comprehensive Concept Guide, Mechanism Breakdown, Worked Examples, and Practice Assessment

Learning Objectives and Overview

To maintain homeostasis, the organ systems of a multicellular organism must interact and complement one another continuously. For instance, during vigorous physical activity, elevated muscular work demands more oxygen and energy, prompting a simultaneous rise in breathing rate, heart rate, and blood flow. Once activity ceases, neural and vascular activities gradually return to baseline. In humans, two systems jointly integrate all organ functions in a synchronised manner:

  • The Neural System: Provides an organised network of point-to-point connections for rapid, short-lived coordination.
  • The Endocrine System: Provides slower, widespread chemical integration through circulating hormones.
Learning Objectives
  • Classify the divisions of the human neural system (CNS, PNS, somatic, autonomic, and visceral components).
  • Describe the microscopic anatomy of a neuron and distinguish multipolar, bipolar, and unipolar neurons.
  • Explain the ionic basis of resting potential, action potential, axonal impulse conduction, and synaptic transmission.
  • Identify the major anatomical regions of the human brain and map their key physiological functions.
  • Trace the pathway of an involuntary reflex arc.
  • Explain the structure and sensory transduction mechanisms of the human eye (vision) and ear (hearing and equilibrium).

Organisation of the Human Neural System

Across the animal kingdom, neural complexity scales with body organisation. In simple invertebrates such as Hydra, the neural system consists of a basic network of neurons. Insects possess a more organised arrangement featuring a brain alongside multiple ganglia and neural tissues, whereas vertebrates exhibit the most highly developed neural architecture.

The human neural system is divided into two principal parts:

  • Central Neural System (CNS): Comprises the brain and the spinal cord; serves as the primary site of information processing and control.
  • Peripheral Neural System (PNS): Comprises all nerves associated with the brain and spinal cord. Its nerve fibres are functionally grouped into:
    • Afferent fibres: Transmit sensory impulses from peripheral tissues and organs toward the CNS.
    • Efferent fibres: Transmit regulatory motor impulses from the CNS to target peripheral tissues and organs.

Somatic Neural System Skeletal muscles (voluntary control) Relays motor commands directly from the CNS to skeletal muscle fibres.
Autonomic Neural System (ANS) Involuntary organs and smooth muscles Further divided into the sympathetic and parasympathetic neural systems.
Visceral Nervous System Internal viscera (bidirectional pathway) Part of the PNS comprising nerves, fibres, ganglia, and plexuses carrying impulses between the CNS and viscera.

The Neuron: Structural and Functional Unit

A neuron is a specialised excitable cell capable of detecting, receiving, and transmitting stimuli. Every neuron consists of three major structural regions:

  1. Cell Body (Soma): Contains cytoplasm, typical cell organelles, and characteristic granular bodies known as Nissl's granules (involved in protein synthesis).
  2. Dendrites: Short, repeatedly branching fibres projecting from the cell body. They also contain Nissl's granules and conduct incoming impulses towards the cell body.
  3. Axon: A single long fibre whose distal end branches into terminals. Each branch ends in a bulb-like synaptic knob housing synaptic vesicles filled with chemical neurotransmitters. The axon conducts impulses away from the cell body toward a synapse or neuromuscular junction.

Classification of Neurons and Axons

Neuron Type Process Arrangement Primary Location
Multipolar One axon and two or more dendrites Cerebral cortex
Bipolar One axon and one dendrite Retina of the eye
Unipolar Cell body with one axon only Usually found in the embryonic stage

Axons are further categorised as myelinated or non-myelinated:

  • Myelinated nerve fibres: Enveloped by Schwann cells that form an insulating myelin sheath around the axon. Unmyelinated gaps between adjacent myelin segments are called nodes of Ranvier. Commonly found in spinal and cranial nerves.
  • Non-myelinated nerve fibres: Enclosed by a Schwann cell that does not form a myelin sheath around the axon. Commonly found in the autonomic and somatic neural systems.

Generation, Conduction, and Synaptic Transmission of Nerve Impulses

Neurons are excitable because their plasma membranes maintain an electrical potential difference—a polarised state—governed by selectively permeable ion channels and active ion transport.

1. Resting Membrane Potential (Polarised State)

When a neuron is not conducting an impulse (at rest), its axonal membrane is comparatively much more permeable to potassium ions (\( \mathrm{K^+} \)) and nearly impermeable to sodium ions (\( \mathrm{Na^+} \)) as well as negatively charged intracellular proteins. Consequently, the axoplasm has a high concentration of \( \mathrm{K^+} \) and negatively charged proteins, and a low concentration of \( \mathrm{Na^+} \), whereas the extracellular fluid has high \( \mathrm{Na^+} \) and low \( \mathrm{K^+} \).

These concentration gradients are actively maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, which transports:

\[ 3\,\mathrm{Na^+}\text{ (outward)} \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad 2\,\mathrm{K^+}\text{ (inward per ATP hydrolysed)} \]

As a result, the outer surface of the resting axonal membrane carries a net positive charge and the inner surface carries a net negative charge. This electrical potential difference across the resting plasma membrane is the resting potential.

2. Action Potential (Depolarisation) and Repolarisation

  1. Depolarisation: When a threshold stimulus is applied at a specific site (say, Site A), the membrane at Site A suddenly becomes freely permeable to \( \mathrm{Na^+} \). Rapid \( \mathrm{Na^+} \) influx reverses the local polarity: the outer surface becomes negative and the inner surface becomes positive. This reversed electrical potential difference is the action potential (nerve impulse).
  2. Local Current Flow & Propagation: At the adjacent resting site ahead (Site B), the outer surface is still positive and the inner surface is negative. Consequently, local current flows on the inner surface from Site A to Site B, and on the outer surface from Site B back to Site A, completing the circuit. This reverses polarity at Site B, generating a new action potential there and propagating the impulse along the axon.
  3. Repolarisation: The stimulus-induced rise in \( \mathrm{Na^+} \) permeability is extremely brief. It is immediately followed by a rise in \( \mathrm{K^+} \) permeability; \( \mathrm{K^+} \) diffuses outward within a fraction of a second, restoring the resting potential and making the membrane responsive to subsequent stimulation.

3. Synaptic Transmission

A synapse is the junction formed by the membranes of a pre-synaptic neuron and a post-synaptic neuron, which may or may not be separated by a gap called the synaptic cleft.

Feature Electrical Synapse Chemical Synapse
Membrane proximity Pre- and post-synaptic membranes lie in very close proximity Separated by a fluid-filled synaptic cleft
Mechanism Direct flow of electrical current across membranes Release of chemical neurotransmitters from vesicles
Speed & Occurrence Always faster; rare in the human neural system Slightly slower due to chemical diffusion; predominant in humans

Mechanism at a Chemical Synapse: Arrival of an action potential at the axon terminal stimulates synaptic vesicles to move toward and fuse with the pre-synaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. Neurotransmitters bind to specific receptors on the post-synaptic membrane, opening ion channels. The resulting ion entry generates a new post-synaptic potential, which may be either excitatory or inhibitory.

The Central Neural System and Reflex Arc

The brain is the central information-processing and command-and-control organ, governing voluntary movements, balance, vital involuntary functions (lungs, heart, kidneys), thermoregulation, hunger, thirst, circadian (24-hour) rhythms, endocrine gland activity, vision, hearing, speech, memory, intelligence, emotions, and thoughts. Inside the skull, it is protected by three cranial meninges:

  • Dura mater: Tough outer layer.
  • Arachnoid: Very thin middle layer.
  • Pia mater: Inner layer in direct contact with brain tissue.
Major Brain Division Sub-structures Key Functions & Features
Forebrain Cerebrum, Thalamus, Hypothalamus Cerebrum: Two hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum; outer folded grey matter (cerebral cortex with motor, sensory, and association areas for intersensory association, memory, communication); inner myelinated white matter.
Thalamus: Major coordinating centre for sensory and motor signalling.
Hypothalamus: Lies at base of thalamus; controls body temperature, eating, drinking; neurosecretory cells secrete hypothalamic hormones.
Limbic System: Inner cerebral hemispheres + amygdala, hippocampus, etc.; works with hypothalamus to regulate sexual behaviour, emotional expression (excitement, pleasure, rage, fear), and motivation.
Midbrain Cerebral aqueduct, Corpora quadrigemina Located between thalamus/hypothalamus and pons; traversed by the cerebral aqueduct; dorsal side bears four round swellings called corpora quadrigemina.
Hindbrain Pons, Cerebellum, Medulla oblongata Pons: Fibre tracts interconnecting brain regions.
Cerebellum: Highly convoluted surface providing extra space for neurons; coordinates balance and posture.
Medulla oblongata: Connects to spinal cord; controls respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, and gastric secretions.

Brain Stem: Formed collectively by the midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata, connecting the brain to the spinal cord.

Reflex Action and Reflex Arc

A reflex action is an entire involuntary response to peripheral nervous stimulation that occurs without conscious effort or thought and requires the involvement of a part of the CNS (typically the spinal cord). The reflex arc comprises at least one afferent neuron and one efferent neuron arranged in series:

\[ \text{Stimulus} \rightarrow \text{Receptor} \rightarrow \text{Afferent Neuron (Dorsal Root)} \rightarrow \text{CNS (Spinal Cord)} \rightarrow \text{Efferent Neuron} \rightarrow \text{Effector} \rightarrow \text{Response} \]

In a knee-jerk reflex, tapping the patellar tendon stretches the muscle spindle (receptor), sending impulses via the afferent pathway through the dorsal root ganglion into the spinal cord grey matter, which activates the motor (efferent) neuron to contract the effector muscle.

Sensory Reception and Processing

Olfaction and Gustation (Chemical Senses)

Both the nose and tongue detect dissolved chemicals and are functionally interrelated:

  • Nose (Olfaction): Mucus-coated olfactory receptors reside in the olfactory epithelium (composed of three cell types). Olfactory neurons extend directly into a pair of broad bean-sized olfactory bulbs, which are extensions of the brain's limbic system.
  • Tongue (Gustation): Detects tastes via taste buds containing gustatory receptors; the brain integrates differential inputs from taste buds and olfaction to perceive complex flavours.

The Human Eye and Mechanism of Vision

Paired eyes sit in skull sockets called orbits. The nearly spherical eyeball wall has three concentric layers:

  1. Sclera (Outer): Dense connective tissue; its transparent anterior portion is the cornea.
  2. Choroid (Middle): Highly vascular and bluish; thin over the posterior two-thirds, thickening anteriorly to form the ciliary body, which continues forward as the pigmented, opaque iris. The central aperture of the iris is the pupil (diameter regulated by iris muscle fibres). A transparent crystalline lens is held in place by ligaments attached to the ciliary body.
  3. Retina (Inner): Contains three layers of neural cells arranged from inside to outside as: ganglion cells → bipolar cells → photoreceptor cells (rods and cones).
Tutorial Diagram 5: Sagittal schematic of the human eye showing layers, lens, fovea, and blind spot.
  • Rods vs. Cones: Rods mediate twilight (scotopic) vision using rhodopsin (visual purple), a Vitamin A derivative. Cones mediate daylight (photopic) and colour vision via three photopigments responding to red, green, and blue light; equal stimulation of all three produces white light sensation.
  • Blind Spot vs. Fovea: The blind spot (medial and slightly above the posterior pole) is where optic nerves exit and blood vessels enter; it lacks photoreceptors. Lateral to it at the posterior pole lies the yellowish macula lutea with a central pit, the fovea—a thinned-out retinal region packed densely with cones only, offering the greatest visual acuity (resolution).
  • Chambers: The aqueous chamber (between cornea and lens) holds watery aqueous humor; the vitreous chamber (between lens and retina) holds gel-like vitreous humor.
  • Mechanism of Vision: Photopigments consist of opsin (protein) and retinal (an aldehyde of Vitamin A). Light induces dissociation of retinal from opsin, altering opsin's structure and membrane permeability. Resulting potential differences in photoreceptors generate signals transmitted through bipolar cells to ganglion cells, which fire action potentials along the optic nerve to the visual cortex of the brain.

The Human Ear: Hearing and Equilibrium

The ear performs two sensory roles: hearing and body balance. It comprises three anatomical sections:

  1. Outer Ear: Pinna (collects air vibrations) and external auditory meatus (canal) leading to the tympanic membrane (eardrum).
  2. Middle Ear: Houses three ear ossicles—malleus (attached to tympanic membrane), incus, and stapes (attached to the oval window of the cochlea)—which increase sound transmission efficiency. The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear cavity to the pharynx to equalise air pressure across the eardrum.
  3. Inner Ear (Labyrinth): Consists of a bony labyrinth containing perilymph, within which lies a membranous labyrinth filled with endolymph.
Tutorial Diagram 6: Sectional schematic of the cochlea showing the three fluid-filled scalae and Organ of Corti.
  • Cochlea & Hearing: Reissner's membrane and the basilar membrane divide the bony cochlea into an upper scala vestibuli (terminating at the oval window), a middle scala media (filled with endolymph), and a lower scala tympani (terminating at the round window). The Organ of Corti rests on the basilar membrane and contains auditory hair cells with apical stereocilia beneath an elastic tectorial membrane. Sound waves vibrate the eardrum → ossicles → oval window → cochlear lymph waves → basilar membrane ripples → bending of hair cells against the tectorial membrane → afferent impulses via auditory nerve to the auditory cortex.
  • Vestibular Apparatus & Equilibrium: Located above the cochlea, it comprises three mutually perpendicular semi-circular canals (swollen bases called ampullae contain crista ampullaris) and the otolith organ (saccule and utricle, each with a projecting ridge called macula). The crista and macula are the specific receptors for body balance and posture.

Worked Examples

1. Calculating Ion Transport Stoichiometry in the Sodium-Potassium Pump

Scenario (Assumed Values): Suppose a segment of a resting axonal membrane hydrolyses \( 150 \) molecules of ATP via its \( \mathrm{Na^+/K^+} \) pump to maintain ionic gradients.

Find: (a) The number of \( \mathrm{Na^+} \) ions exported, (b) the number of \( \mathrm{K^+} \) ions imported, and (c) the net positive charge moved outward across the membrane.

Solution:

  • Each cycle of the \( \mathrm{Na^+/K^+} \) pump hydrolyses \( 1\,\mathrm{ATP} \) to transport \( 3\,\mathrm{Na^+} \) outward and \( 2\,\mathrm{K^+} \) inward.
  • \( \mathrm{Na^+} \) exported \( = 150 \times 3 = 450\text{ ions} \).
  • \( \mathrm{K^+} \) imported \( = 150 \times 2 = 300\text{ ions} \).
  • Net positive charge moved outward \( = 450 - 300 = +150\text{ elementary charges} \) (reinforcing the negative intracellular resting potential).

2. Tracing the Sequence of Retinal Layers During Light Reception

Scenario: A student observes a distant green traffic signal in bright daylight. Trace the exact sequence of retinal cell layers traversed by (a) incoming light rays and (b) the resulting neural signal.

Solution:

  • Retinal anatomy (inside to outside): Ganglion cells → Bipolar cells → Photoreceptor cells (rods and cones).
  • Path of light (entering from the vitreous chamber): Light passes through the ganglion cell layer → bipolar cell layer → reaches the photoreceptor layer (specifically green-sensitive cones at the fovea for daylight colour vision).
  • Path of neural impulse: Photoreceptor cells (retinal dissociates from opsin, generating graded potential changes) → bipolar cells → ganglion cells (generate action potentials) → optic nerve → visual cortex of the cerebrum.

3. Analysing Pressure Equalisation and Ossicular Transmission in the Middle Ear

Scenario (Assumed Values): During an aeroplane descent, ambient cabin pressure rises by \( 12\,\mathrm{kPa} \) relative to a passenger's middle ear cavity before swallowing occurs. Meanwhile, sound waves exert a force \( F \) on the tympanic membrane, and the ossicular chain transmits this force to the oval window, whose area is assumed to be \( \frac{1}{15} \) of the tympanic membrane area.

Solution:

  • Pressure Equalisation: Swallowing opens the Eustachian tube connecting the pharynx to the middle ear cavity, allowing higher-pressure air to enter the middle ear and reduce the \( 12\,\mathrm{kPa} \) pressure differential across the tympanic membrane to zero.
  • Pressure Amplification: Because pressure \( P = \frac{F}{A} \), concentrating the transmitted force onto an oval window with \( \frac{1}{15} \) the area increases the effective pressure delivered to the cochlear perilymph (neglecting frictional losses), enhancing the efficiency of sound wave transmission to the inner ear.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements accurately distinguishes a non-myelinated nerve fibre from a myelinated nerve fibre in the human peripheral neural system?

  • (A) Non-myelinated fibres lack Schwann cells entirely, whereas myelinated fibres possess them.
  • (B) Non-myelinated fibres are enclosed by Schwann cells that do not form a myelin sheath around the axon.
  • (C) Non-myelinated fibres possess nodes of Ranvier at regular intervals to accelerate conduction.
  • (D) Non-myelinated fibres are restricted exclusively to cranial and spinal nerves.
Answer: (B)

Explanation: In the PNS, non-myelinated nerve fibres are still enclosed by Schwann cells, but these Schwann cells do not wrap around to form a myelin sheath. They lack nodes of Ranvier and are commonly found in the autonomic and somatic neural systems, whereas myelinated fibres are found in spinal and cranial nerves.

Practice Question 2 Statement-Based

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: During the resting state, the axonal membrane is comparatively more permeable to \( \mathrm{K^+} \) ions and nearly impermeable to \( \mathrm{Na^+} \) ions.
Statement II: Upon threshold stimulation at a site, rapid efflux of \( \mathrm{Na^+} \) ions causes depolarisation of the membrane.
Choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because depolarisation is caused by a rapid influx (entry) of \( \mathrm{Na^+} \) ions into the axoplasm, not efflux.

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

During impulse conduction along an axon from depolarised Site A to the adjacent resting Site B, what is the direction of local electrical current flow?

  • (A) From A to B on both the inner and outer surfaces of the membrane.
  • (B) From B to A on the inner surface and from A to B on the outer surface.
  • (C) From A to B on the inner surface and from B to A on the outer surface.
  • (D) From B to A on both the inner and outer surfaces of the membrane.
Answer: (C)

Explanation: At depolarised Site A, the inner surface is positive and the outer surface is negative; at resting Site B, the inner surface is negative and the outer surface is positive. Therefore, positive current flows from A to B on the inner surface and from B to A on the outer surface to complete the circuit.

Practice Question 4 Assertion-Reason

Assertion (A): Impulse transmission across an electrical synapse is always faster than that across a chemical synapse.
Reason (R): At electrical synapses, pre- and post-synaptic membranes lie in very close proximity, allowing direct flow of electrical current without neurotransmitter diffusion.

  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET Traps to Avoid
  • Retinal Layer Order: From inside to outside (vitreous side toward choroid), the order is Ganglion cells → Bipolar cells → Photoreceptor cells.
  • Unmyelinated Schwann Cells: Unmyelinated PNS fibres do have Schwann cells; those Schwann cells simply do not form a myelin sheath.
  • Brain Stem Composition: Midbrain + Pons + Medulla oblongata. Do not include the cerebellum.
  • Cochlear Fluids: Scala vestibuli and scala tympani contain perilymph; scala media contains endolymph.
  • Oval vs. Round Window: Scala vestibuli ends at the oval window (stapes); scala tympani ends at the round window.
  • \( \mathrm{Na^+/K^+} \) pump ratio: \( 3\,\mathrm{Na^+} \) out : \( 2\,\mathrm{K^+} \) in.
  • Meninges (outer to inner): Dura mater → Arachnoid → Pia mater.
  • Bipolar neurons: retina; Multipolar: cerebral cortex; Unipolar: embryonic stage.
  • Association areas in cerebral cortex: intersensory associations, memory, communication.
  • Limbic system + hypothalamus: sexual behaviour, emotional reactions, motivation.

Concise Final Recap

The neural system delivers rapid point-to-point coordination through excitable neurons arranged into the CNS (brain and spinal cord) and PNS (afferent/efferent, somatic, autonomic, and visceral pathways). Nerve impulses propagate as waves of \( \mathrm{Na^+} \)-driven depolarisation and \( \mathrm{K^+} \)-driven repolarisation, crossing chemical synapses via neurotransmitter release. Higher processing occurs across the forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain, while rapid involuntary responses travel through spinal reflex arcs. Specialised sensory organs—including the retina's rods and cones and the inner ear's Organ of Corti and vestibular apparatus—transduce light, sound, and gravitational cues into neural signals for perception and homeostasis.
