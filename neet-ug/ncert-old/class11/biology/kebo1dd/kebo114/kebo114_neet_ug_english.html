Cellular Respiration in Plants

Bioenergetics, Glycolysis, Fermentation, the Krebs Cycle, Oxidative Phosphorylation, Amphibolic Integration, and Respiratory Quotient

Learning Objectives and Overview

Every living cell requires a continuous supply of usable chemical energy to drive active transport, biosynthesis, growth, and reproduction. While photosynthetic organisms (green plants and cyanobacteria) capture light energy and store it within the covalent bonds of carbohydrates such as glucose, sucrose, and starch, non-green plant tissues (roots, woody stems, flowers, and developing seeds) as well as heterotrophs and saprophytes depend on translocated or ingested organic molecules. Cellular respiration is the enzyme-regulated, step-wise oxidation of these organic macromolecules—called respiratory substrates—within living cells to release energy and conserve it as adenosine triphosphate (ATP).

  • Why respiration is an amphibolic pathway and how to compute the respiratory quotient (RQ) for carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.

1. Gaseous Exchange in Plants and Controlled Oxidation

Although plants require \(\mathrm{O}_2\) for aerobic respiration and release \(\mathrm{CO}_2\), they lack lungs, gills, or circulatory transport for respiratory gases. Instead, gases diffuse through stomata (in leaves and young stems) and lenticels (in woody bark). Three structural and physiological features make this possible:

  1. Local autonomy: Each plant organ meets its own gas-exchange needs; long-distance gas transport between organs is minimal.
  2. Low metabolic demand: Roots, stems, and leaves respire at much lower rates than animal tissues. During active photosynthesis, leaves generate \(\mathrm{O}_2\) internally within chloroplasts, ensuring immediate availability.
  3. Short diffusion distances: Even in large, woody trees, living cells are arranged in thin layers just beneath the bark (while interior wood cells are dead mechanical elements). Loose packing of parenchyma cells creates an interconnected network of air spaces, keeping most living cells in direct contact with air.

Why Step-Wise Oxidation Beats Single-Step Combustion

Complete combustion of glucose in air releases energy all at once, primarily as heat:

\[\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow 6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O} + \text{Energy (mostly heat)}\]

A living cell cannot use uncontrolled heat to perform biological work. By breaking \(\mathrm{C–C}\) bonds through a series of small, enzyme-catalysed steps, the cell releases energy in discrete packets large enough to phosphorylate ADP into ATP. ATP then serves as the universal energy currency of the cell, while intermediate carbon skeletons serve as precursors for synthesizing other biomolecules.

2. Glycolysis (The EMP Pathway)

The term glycolysis derives from the Greek glycos (sugar) and lysis (splitting). Elucidated by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof, and J. Parnas, this 10-step pathway operates in the cytoplasm of all living organisms—both prokaryotes and eukaryotes—and does not require molecular oxygen. In anaerobic organisms, glycolysis is the sole energy-yielding pathway.

In plants, glucose entering glycolysis is typically derived from storage starch or from sucrose (the primary translocated sugar). The enzyme invertase hydrolyzes sucrose into glucose and fructose, both of which enter glycolysis.

Phase I: Energy Investment (Steps 1–5)

  • Phosphorylation of glucose/fructose: Glucose (6C) is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate by hexokinase, consuming 1 ATP. (Fructose is similarly phosphorylated by hexokinase.)
  • Isomerization: Glucose-6-phosphate isomerizes to fructose-6-phosphate.
  • Second phosphorylation: Fructose-6-phosphate is phosphorylated to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, consuming a second ATP.
  • Cleavage: The 6C fructose-1,6-bisphosphate splits into two interconvertible 3C triose phosphates: glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (PGAL) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). Both proceed through the payoff phase as 2 molecules of PGAL.

Phase II: Energy Payoff (Steps 6–10, occurring ×2 per glucose)

  • Oxidation and phosphorylation: Each PGAL is oxidized (two hydrogen atoms / redox equivalents are transferred to \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\)) and incorporates inorganic phosphate (\(\mathrm{P_i}\)) to form 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA), generating \(2\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\) per glucose.
  • First substrate-level phosphorylation: 1,3-BPGA transfers a high-energy phosphate to ADP, forming 3-phosphoglyceric acid (3-PGA) and 2 ATP per glucose.
  • Isomerization and dehydration: 3-PGA converts to 2-phosphoglycerate, which loses a water molecule to form phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP).
  • Second substrate-level phosphorylation: PEP transfers its phosphate to ADP, yielding 2 molecules of pyruvic acid (pyruvate, 3C) and 2 ATP per glucose.

3. Fermentation: Anaerobic Fates of Pyruvate

In the absence of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) (obligate or facultative anaerobes, germinating seeds under waterlogged soil, or vigorously contracting muscle cells), cells cannot oxidize \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) via the mitochondrial electron transport chain. To prevent glycolysis from halting due to \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\) depletion, pyruvate is reduced to regenerate \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\).

  • Alcoholic Fermentation (e.g., Yeast): Pyruvate is first decarboxylated to acetaldehyde and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) by pyruvic acid decarboxylase, and acetaldehyde is then reduced to ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase, oxidizing \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) back to \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\). Yeasts poison themselves to death once alcohol concentration reaches about 13%; therefore, naturally fermented beverages cannot exceed ~13% alcohol without distillation.
  • Lactic Acid Fermentation (e.g., Certain Bacteria, Exercising Muscles): Pyruvate is reduced directly to lactic acid by lactate dehydrogenase using \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), without releasing \(\mathrm{CO}_2\).
Tutorial Diagram 2: Anaerobic fermentation pathways regenerate NAD⁺ for glycolysis, whereas aerobic respiration channels pyruvate into mitochondria.
Key Limitation of Fermentation

Both lactic acid and alcoholic fermentation release less than 7% of the total energy stored in glucose, and not all of that is trapped in ATP. The net yield is only 2 ATP per glucose, and the end products (acid or alcohol) are toxic if accumulated.

4. Aerobic Respiration: Link Reaction, Krebs Cycle, and ETS

In eukaryotes, complete oxidation of pyruvate occurs inside the mitochondria and requires oxygen. Two distinct compartments carry out the two major phases:

  1. Mitochondrial Matrix: Stepwise removal of all hydrogen atoms from pyruvate, releasing \(3\,\mathrm{CO}_2\) per pyruvate (link reaction + TCA cycle).
  2. Inner Mitochondrial Membrane: Transfer of electrons from reduced coenzymes (\(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) and \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\)) to \(\mathrm{O}_2\) via the Electron Transport System (ETS), coupled to ATP synthesis.

4.1 Oxidative Decarboxylation (Link Reaction)

Pyruvate is transported from the cytosol into the mitochondrial matrix, where the multi-enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (requiring \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\), \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\), and Coenzyme A) converts it into acetyl CoA:

\[\mathrm{Pyruvic\;acid} + \mathrm{CoA} + \mathrm{NAD}^+ \xrightarrow[\mathrm{Pyruvate\;dehydrogenase}]{\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}} \mathrm{Acetyl\;CoA} + \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{NADH} + \mathrm{H}^+\]

Because 1 glucose yields 2 pyruvate molecules, the link reaction produces \(2\,\mathrm{Acetyl\;CoA}\), \(2\,\mathrm{CO_2}\), and \(2\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\) per glucose.

4.2 The Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA / Krebs) Cycle

Elucidated by Hans Krebs, the TCA cycle begins when the 2C acetyl group of acetyl CoA condenses with the 4C oxaloacetic acid (OAA) and water to form 6C citric acid, catalyzed by citrate synthase (releasing CoA). Citrate is isomerized to isocitrate, followed by two successive decarboxylations yielding 5C α-ketoglutaric acid and then 4C succinyl-CoA. Succinyl-CoA is subsequently oxidized back to OAA through succinic acid, fumaric acid, and malic acid.

During the conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinic acid, one molecule of GTP is synthesized by substrate-level phosphorylation, which in a coupled reaction transfers its phosphate to ADP to form ATP. Per turn of the cycle (1 pyruvate): 3 \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), 1 \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\), and 1 ATP (via GTP) are generated. Including the link reaction, the matrix summary per pyruvic acid is:

\[\mathrm{Pyruvic\;acid} + 4\,\mathrm{NAD}^+ + \mathrm{FAD}^+ + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{ADP} + \mathrm{P_i} \xrightarrow{\text{Mitochondrial Matrix}} 3\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 4\,\mathrm{NADH} + 4\,\mathrm{H}^+ + \mathrm{FADH_2} + \mathrm{ATP}\]

4.3 Electron Transport System (ETS) and Oxidative Phosphorylation

The energy stored in \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) and \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\) is harvested by passing their electrons through four protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane:

  • Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase): Oxidizes matrix \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) via FMN and Fe–S centers, transferring electrons to ubiquinone (UQ) within the membrane.
  • Complex II (Succinate dehydrogenase): Oxidizes succinate to fumarate in the TCA cycle, transferring electrons via FAD and Fe–S to ubiquinone (forming ubiquinol, \(\mathrm{UQH}_2\)).
  • Complex III (Cytochrome \(bc_1\) complex): Transfers electrons from \(\mathrm{UQH}_2\) to cytochrome \(c\), a small mobile protein attached to the outer surface of the inner mitochondrial membrane.
  • Complex IV (Cytochrome \(c\) oxidase): Contains cytochromes \(a\) and \(a_3\) and two copper centers. It transfers electrons to the terminal electron and hydrogen acceptor, \(\mathrm{O}_2\), forming \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\).

As electrons flow from Complex I to IV, protons (\(\mathrm{H}^+\)) are pumped from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical proton gradient. Protons flow back down this gradient into the matrix through Complex V (ATP synthase), which consists of an integral membrane channel (\(\mathrm{F}_0\)) and a peripheral catalytic headpiece (\(\mathrm{F}_1\)). For every \(2\,\mathrm{H}^+\) passing through \(\mathrm{F}_0\), 1 ATP is synthesized at \(\mathrm{F}_1\) from ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\).

Yields per coenzyme oxidized: \(1\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+} \rightarrow 3\,\mathrm{ATP}\); \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2 \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{ATP}\). Oxygen acts only at the terminal step, yet it is indispensable because it drives the entire chain by removing hydrogen/electrons.

5. The Respiratory Balance Sheet and Its Assumptions

By summing all direct (substrate-level) ATP and oxidative phosphorylation ATP generated from 1 molecule of glucose during complete aerobic respiration, we arrive at a theoretical net yield of 38 ATP:

Stage Direct ATP Reduced Coenzymes ATP via ETS Subtotal ATP
Glycolysis (Cytoplasm) 2 net ATP \(2\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) \(2 \times 3 = 6\) 8 ATP
Link Reaction (Matrix) 0 \(2\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) \(2 \times 3 = 6\) 6 ATP
TCA Cycle (Matrix, ×2) 2 ATP (via GTP) \(6\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), \(2\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\) \(18 + 4 = 22\) 24 ATP
Total Theoretical Yield 4 ATP \(10\,\mathrm{NADH}\), \(2\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\) 34 ATP 38 ATP

Why this calculation is purely theoretical: It relies on four idealized assumptions that do not strictly hold in living cells: (1) pathways operate sequentially rather than simultaneously; (2) cytosolic NADH from glycolysis is always shuttled into mitochondria for oxidative phosphorylation; (3) no pathway intermediates are withdrawn for biosynthesis; and (4) only glucose is respired with no alternative substrates entering mid-pathway.

Comparison: Aerobic Respiration vs. Fermentation

Feature Fermentation Aerobic Respiration
Extent of glucose breakdown Partial (to lactic acid or ethanol + \(\mathrm{CO}_2\)) Complete (to \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) and \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\))
Net ATP gain per glucose 2 ATP 38 ATP (theoretical)
Rate of NADH oxidation to \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\) Slow Vigorous (via ETS)
Oxygen requirement Absent (anaerobic) Essential (terminal acceptor)

6. Amphibolic Pathway and Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

6.1 Why Respiration Is Amphibolic

Although respiration breaks down substrates (catabolism), its intermediates are continuously withdrawn to synthesize cellular macromolecules (anabolism). For example, fats break down into glycerol (entering as PGAL) and fatty acids (entering as acetyl CoA), while proteins yield amino acids (entering as pyruvate, acetyl CoA, or TCA intermediates). Conversely, when a cell needs to synthesize fatty acids, acetyl CoA is withdrawn from the respiratory pathway; similarly, TCA intermediates are withdrawn for amino acid synthesis. Because the pathway participates in both breakdown and synthesis, it is termed an amphibolic pathway.

Tutorial Diagram 5: Intermediates such as PGAL, pyruvic acid, acetyl CoA, and Krebs cycle acids connect catabolism and anabolism.

6.2 Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

The respiratory quotient (RQ) is the ratio of the volume of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) evolved to the volume of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) consumed during respiration:

\[\mathrm{RQ} = \frac{\text{Volume of }\mathrm{CO_2}\text{ evolved}}{\text{Volume of }\mathrm{O_2}\text{ consumed}}\]
  • Carbohydrates: \(\mathrm{RQ} = 1.0\) (equal volumes of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) evolved and \(\mathrm{O}_2\) consumed: \(6\,\mathrm{CO_2} / 6\,\mathrm{O_2} = 1.0\)).
  • Fats: \(\mathrm{RQ} < 1.0\) because fats are poorer in oxygen and require more \(\mathrm{O}_2\) for complete oxidation. For tripalmitin: \(2(\mathrm{C_{51}H_{98}O_6}) + 145\,\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 102\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 98\,\mathrm{H_2O}\), giving \(\mathrm{RQ} = 102 / 145 \approx \mathbf{0.7}\).
  • Proteins: \(\mathrm{RQ} \approx \mathbf{0.9}\).

In living organisms, pure fats or pure proteins are rarely respired alone; typically, more than one substrate is utilized simultaneously.

Worked Examples

Calculating Net ATP Yield Under Partial Pathway Inhibition

Problem: Suppose a hypothetical plant root cell oxidizes 3 molecules of sucrose via aerobic respiration, but a specific inhibitor blocks Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) completely while leaving Complex I, III, IV, and V functional. Assuming standard theoretical ATP equivalents (\(1\,\mathrm{NADH} = 3\,\mathrm{ATP}\), \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2 = 2\,\mathrm{ATP}\)) and that the TCA cycle still completes one turn per acetyl CoA for the remaining steps, calculate the net ATP produced.

Solution:

  1. Sucrose to glucose equivalents: 1 sucrose yields 1 glucose + 1 fructose (2 hexose units). Thus, 3 sucrose molecules provide \(3 \times 2 = 6\) hexose units entering glycolysis.
  2. Normal theoretical ATP per hexose: \(38\,\mathrm{ATP}\). For 6 hexose units, normal yield = \(6 \times 38 = 228\,\mathrm{ATP}\).
  3. Effect of Complex II block: Each hexose generates \(2\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\) in the TCA cycle. Normally, \(2\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\) yield \(2 \times 2 = 4\,\mathrm{ATP}\) via Complex II. For 6 hexoses, the lost ATP = \(6 \times 4 = 24\,\mathrm{ATP}\).
  4. Net ATP produced: \(228 - 24 = \mathbf{204\,\mathrm{ATP}}\).

Determining Respiratory Quotient (RQ) for a Mixed Substrate

Problem: In a hypothetical respirometry experiment on germinating seeds, a tissue respires a mixture of carbohydrates and fats such that it consumes \(250\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) and evolves \(205\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) over 1 hour. Calculate the RQ and identify whether carbohydrates or fats contribute more to \(\mathrm{O}_2\) consumption.

Solution:

  1. Apply the RQ formula: \[\mathrm{RQ} = \frac{205\,\mathrm{mL}}{250\,\mathrm{mL}} = \mathbf{0.82}\]
  2. Interpretation: Pure carbohydrate has \(\mathrm{RQ} = 1.0\) and pure fat (tripalmitin) has \(\mathrm{RQ} = 0.7\). Since \(0.82\) lies closer to \(0.7\) than to \(1.0\) (specifically, \(0.82 - 0.70 = 0.12\) vs. \(1.00 - 0.82 = 0.18\)), fats account for a slightly larger share of the \(\mathrm{O}_2\) consumed (about 60% of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) uptake).

Tracing Carbon Atoms and Cofactors Through Link Reaction and TCA Cycle

Problem: If 4 molecules of pyruvic acid enter the mitochondrial matrix under aerobic conditions, how many molecules of (a) \(\mathrm{CO}_2\), (b) \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), (c) \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\), and (d) GTP are generated strictly inside the mitochondria before ETS?

Solution:

  1. Per pyruvic acid (3C), complete matrix oxidation (link reaction + 1 turn of TCA cycle) yields:
    • \(3\,\mathrm{CO}_2\) (1 in link reaction + 2 in TCA cycle)
    • \(4\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) (1 in link reaction + 3 in TCA cycle)
    • \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\) (in TCA cycle)
    • \(1\,\mathrm{GTP}\) (substrate-level phosphorylation in TCA cycle)
  2. Multiplying by 4 molecules of pyruvic acid:
    • (a) \(\mathrm{CO}_2\): \(4 \times 3 = \mathbf{12}\)
    • (b) \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\): \(4 \times 4 = \mathbf{16}\)
    • (c) \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\): \(4 \times 1 = \mathbf{4}\)
    • (d) GTP: \(4 \times 1 = \mathbf{4}\)

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

During glycolysis, at which specific step is \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\) reduced to \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\)?

  • (A) Glucose → Glucose-6-phosphate
  • (B) Fructose-6-phosphate → Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate
  • (C) 3-Phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL) → 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA)
  • (D) Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) → Pyruvic acid

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Oxidation of PGAL to 1,3-BPGA removes two hydrogen atoms (redox equivalents) from PGAL and transfers them to \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\), forming \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\). Steps (A) and (B) consume ATP, while step (D) synthesizes ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation.

Practice Question 2 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): In alcoholic fermentation by yeast, the net gain of ATP per molecule of glucose is only 2 ATP.

Reason (R): During conversion of pyruvic acid to ethanol and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\), \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) is reoxidized to \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\) without passing electrons through the mitochondrial ETS.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Answer: (A)

Explanation: Because \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) generated in glycolysis is reoxidized in the cytoplasm to reduce acetaldehyde to ethanol rather than entering the ETS, no oxidative phosphorylation occurs, leaving only the 2 net ATP from glycolytic substrate-level phosphorylation.

Statement I: Cytochrome \(c\) is a small mobile protein attached to the outer surface of the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Statement II: Cytochrome \(c\) transfers electrons directly from Complex I to Complex III.

  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Explanation: Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because cytochrome \(c\) acts as a mobile carrier between Complex III (cytochrome \(bc_1\)) and Complex IV (cytochrome \(c\) oxidase); ubiquinone (UQ) carries electrons from Complexes I and II to Complex III.

Practice Question 5 Numerical MCQ

How many protons (\(\mathrm{H}^+\)) must pass through the \(\mathrm{F}_0\) channel of ATP synthase from the intermembrane space to the mitochondrial matrix to synthesize 12 molecules of ATP at the \(\mathrm{F}_1\) headpiece?

  • (A) 6
  • (B) 12
  • (C) 24
  • (D) 36

Answer: (C)

Explanation: For each ATP molecule produced by ATP synthase, \(2\,\mathrm{H}^+\) pass through \(\mathrm{F}_0\) down the electrochemical proton gradient. Thus, \(12\,\mathrm{ATP} \times 2\,\mathrm{H}^+/\mathrm{ATP} = 24\,\mathrm{H}^+\).

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

During the complete oxidation of one molecule of glucose in aerobic respiration, how many molecules of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) are released in the mitochondrial matrix excluding the link reaction?

  • (A) 2
  • (B) 4
  • (C) 6
  • (D) 3

Answer: (B)

Explanation: One glucose produces 2 pyruvate molecules. The link reaction releases \(2\,\mathrm{CO}_2\), and two turns of the TCA cycle release \(2 \times 2 = 4\,\mathrm{CO}_2\) (total \(6\,\mathrm{CO}_2\)). Excluding the link reaction, 4 \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) molecules are released in the TCA cycle.

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

Why is the respiratory pathway best described as an amphibolic pathway rather than a purely catabolic pathway?

  • (A) It operates both in the cytoplasm and in the mitochondria.
  • (B) It can switch between aerobic and anaerobic modes depending on oxygen availability.
  • (C) Its intermediates are both generated by substrate breakdown and withdrawn for biomolecule synthesis.
  • (D) It consumes ATP during the investment phase and produces ATP during the payoff phase.

Answer: (C)

Explanation: An amphibolic pathway serves both catabolism (breakdown of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins) and anabolism (withdrawal of intermediates such as acetyl CoA for fatty acid synthesis or TCA intermediates for amino acid synthesis).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Gross vs. Net ATP in Glycolysis: Total ATP synthesized directly in glycolysis is 4 ATP, but 2 ATP are consumed, leaving a net gain of 2 ATP.
  • Location of Succinate Dehydrogenase (Complex II): Unlike other TCA cycle enzymes located freely in the mitochondrial matrix, succinate dehydrogenase is bound to the inner mitochondrial membrane.
  • \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) in Fermentation: Alcoholic fermentation releases \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) (via pyruvate decarboxylase), whereas lactic acid fermentation does not release \(\mathrm{CO}_2\).
  • Role of \(\mathrm{O}_2\): Oxygen is not used in glycolysis or the TCA cycle; it acts exclusively at Complex IV as the terminal electron/hydrogen acceptor to form \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\).
  • Cofactors in Link Reaction: Pyruvate dehydrogenase requires \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\), \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\), and CoA.

Pre-Exam Revision Checklist

  • I can trace all 10 steps of glycolysis and identify the exact steps of ATP consumption (steps 1, 3), NADH formation (step 6), and ATP synthesis (steps 7, 10).
  • I know the enzymes for alcoholic vs. lactic acid fermentation and the ~13% ethanol toxicity threshold in yeast.
  • I can count the carbon atoms in TCA intermediates: Citrate (6C) → α-Ketoglutarate (5C) → Succinyl-CoA / Succinate / Malate / OAA (4C).
  • I know the exact order of ETS carriers: Complex I/II → UQ → Complex III → Cyt \(c\) → Complex IV → \(\mathrm{O}_2\), plus Complex V (\(\mathrm{F}_0\text{–F}_1\)).
  • I can state the 4 assumptions behind the 38 ATP balance sheet and compute RQ for carbohydrates (1.0), fats (0.7), and proteins (0.9).

Concise Final Recap

Cellular respiration in plants is a controlled, enzyme-driven, step-wise oxidation of organic substrates that traps chemical energy as ATP. Glycolysis in the cytoplasm splits glucose into two molecules of pyruvate with a net gain of 2 ATP and \(2\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\). Without \(\mathrm{O}_2\), fermentation regenerates \(\mathrm{NAD}^+\) while yielding only 2 ATP. With \(\mathrm{O}_2\), pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix for oxidative decarboxylation and the TCA cycle, generating \(3\,\mathrm{CO}_2\), \(4\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\), \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\), and \(1\,\mathrm{ATP}\) per pyruvate. These reduced coenzymes drive the inner-membrane ETS and chemiosmotic ATP synthase to produce up to 38 ATP per glucose theoretically. Because its intermediates both receive breakdown products and supply precursors for biosynthesis, respiration functions as a central amphibolic pathway.
