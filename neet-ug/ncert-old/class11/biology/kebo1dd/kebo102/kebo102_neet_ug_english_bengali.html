Biological Classification

জৈবিক শ্রেণিবিন্যাস (Biological Classification) — NEET UG দ্বিভাষিক টিউটোরিয়াল

Learning Objectives & Exam Overview / শিখনের উদ্দেশ্য ও পরীক্ষার রূপরেখা

English

For NEET UG, biological classification is a high-yield foundational topic that tests your ability to compare cellular organization, cell wall chemistry, nutrition, and reproductive cycles across major groups of organisms. Rather than memorizing isolated names, focus on the exact diagnostic criteria that separate prokaryotes from eukaryotes, unicellular protists from multicellular fungi, and cellular life from acellular infectious agents.

  • Trace the shift from morphological groupings to Whittaker's five-kingdom phylogenetic framework.
  • Distinguish Archaebacteria, Eubacteria (including Cyanobacteria), and Mycoplasma within Kingdom Monera.
  • Compare the five major protistan lineages by habitat, cell covering, locomotion, and nutrition.
  • Master the hyphal structure, dikaryophase, and spore types across the four classes of Kingdom Fungi.
  • Identify key features of Plantae, Animalia, viruses, viroids, prions, and lichens.

Evolution of Classification Systems / শ্রেণিবিন্যাস পদ্ধতির বিবর্তন

English

Early human groupings were driven by practical utility such as food, shelter, and clothing. Aristotle introduced the first structured biological approach using simple observable traits: he divided plants into trees, shrubs, and herbs, and separated animals into those with red blood and those without it.

Later, Linnaeus established the Two-Kingdom system (Plantae and Animalia). Although straightforward, it failed on three critical fronts: it did not separate prokaryotes from eukaryotes, unicellular organisms from multicellular ones, or photosynthetic green algae from non-photosynthetic fungi. Placing all cell-walled organisms into Plantae artificially grouped prokaryotic bacteria and cyanobacteria alongside eukaryotic mosses and angiosperms, and ignored the chemical difference between fungal chitin walls and plant cellulose walls.

In 1969, R.H. Whittaker proposed the Five-Kingdom Classification—Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia—based on five core criteria: cell structure, body organization, mode of nutrition, reproduction, and phylogenetic (evolutionary) relationships. A subsequent three-domain system splits Kingdom Monera into two domains while grouping all eukaryotes into a third domain, forming a six-kingdom framework.

Kingdom Monera: Prokaryotic Diversity / মোনেরা রাজ্য: প্রোক্যারিওটিক বৈচিত্র্য

English

Bacteria are the sole members of Kingdom Monera. Although structurally simple, they exhibit the widest metabolic diversity of any group and inhabit nearly every environment, including hot springs, deserts, snow, and deep oceans. Based on cell shape, bacteria are categorized as spherical cocci (sing. coccus), rod-shaped bacilli (sing. bacillus), comma-shaped vibrio (sing. vibrium), and spiral spirilla (sing. spirillum).

  • Archaebacteria: Extremophiles that survive harsh conditions because of their unique cell wall structure. They include halophiles (extreme salt), thermoacidophiles (hot acidic springs), and methanogens (marshy areas and the gut of ruminants such as cows and buffaloes, where they produce methane/biogas from dung).
  • Eubacteria (True Bacteria): Possess a rigid cell wall and, if motile, a flagellum.
    • Photosynthetic autotrophs: Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) contain chlorophyll a similar to green plants. They may be unicellular, colonial, or filamentous, often encased in a gelatinous/mucilaginous sheath, and form blooms in polluted waters. Specialized cells called heterocysts in Nostoc and Anabaena fix atmospheric nitrogen.
    • Chemosynthetic autotrophs: Oxidize inorganic substances such as nitrates, nitrites, and ammonia to generate ATP, playing a vital role in recycling nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and sulfur.
    • Heterotrophic bacteria: The most abundant group; primarily decomposers. Useful roles include curdling milk, antibiotic production, and nitrogen fixation in legume roots. Pathogenic species cause cholera, typhoid, tetanus, and citrus canker.
  • Reproduction: Primarily by binary fission; under unfavourable conditions they form spores, and they also exchange DNA via a primitive form of sexual reproduction.
  • Mycoplasma: Completely lack a cell wall, represent the smallest known living cells, can survive without oxygen, and many are pathogenic in plants and animals.

  • Mycoplasma: এদের কোষপ্রাচীর সম্পূর্ণ অনুপস্থিত; এরা পরিচিত ক্ষুদ্রতম জীবিত কোষ, অক্সিজেন ছাড়াই বাঁচতে পারে এবং উদ্ভিদ ও প্রাণীতে রোগ সৃষ্টি করে।

Kingdom Protista: Unicellular Eukaryotes / প্রোটিস্টা রাজ্য: এককোষী ইউক্যারিওট

English

Kingdom Protista encompasses all single-celled eukaryotes, though its boundaries remain somewhat fluid (for instance, a photosynthetic protist may be treated as a plant by some biologists). Primarily aquatic, protists form an evolutionary link between prokaryotes and the multicellular kingdoms of plants, animals, and fungi. Their cells contain a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles, and they reproduce both asexually and sexually (via cell fusion and zygote formation).

Kingdom Fungi: Heterotrophic Absorbers / ছত্রাক রাজ্য: পরভোজী শোষক

English

Fungi form a cosmopolitan kingdom of heterotrophic organisms thriving in warm, humid habitats. Except for unicellular yeasts (Saccharomyces), fungal bodies consist of slender filaments called hyphae, which weave into a mycelium. Hyphae may be continuous multinucleated tubes (coenocytic) or divided by cross-walls (septate). Their cell walls contain chitin and polysaccharides. Ecologically, fungi act as saprophytes, parasites, or symbionts (forming lichens with algae and mycorrhiza with higher-plant roots).

Fungi reproduce vegetatively (fragmentation, fission, budding), asexually (conidia, sporangiospores, or zoospores), and sexually (oospores, ascospores, or basidiospores). The sexual cycle proceeds in three sequential steps: (1) Plasmogamy (fusion of protoplasms), (2) Karyogamy (fusion of two nuclei), and (3) Meiosis in the zygote to yield haploid spores. In Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes, an intervening dikaryophase (\(n + n\), two nuclei per cell) delays karyogamy.

Viruses, Viroids, Prions, and Lichens / ভাইরাস, ভিরয়েড, প্রিয়ন ও লাইকেন

English

Whittaker's five-kingdom system excludes acellular infectious agents (viruses, viroids, prions) and symbiotic lichens.

  • Viruses: Non-cellular obligate parasites that remain inert crystals outside a host cell but hijack host machinery upon infection. Key milestones: Dmitri Ivanowsky (1892) recognized microbes causing tobacco mosaic disease that passed through bacteria-proof filters; M.W. Beijerinck (1898) termed the infectious extract Contagium vivum fluidum; W.M. Stanley (1935) crystallized viruses and showed they consist largely of proteins. A virus is a nucleoprotein containing either RNA or DNA (never both). Plant viruses generally have single-stranded RNA; animal viruses have ssRNA, dsRNA, or dsDNA; bacteriophages usually have dsDNA. The protein coat (capsid) is built from subunits called capsomeres arranged in helical or polyhedral forms. Human viral diseases include mumps, smallpox, herpes, influenza, and AIDS; plant symptoms include mosaic formation, leaf rolling/curling, yellowing, vein clearing, and dwarfing.
  • Viroids: Discovered by T.O. Diener (1971) as agents smaller than viruses causing potato spindle tuber disease; they consist of free low-molecular-weight RNA without a protein coat.
  • Prions: Virus-sized agents composed solely of abnormally folded proteins; they cause neurological diseases such as bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE / mad cow disease) in cattle and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) in humans.
  • Lichens: Mutualistic symbioses between an autotrophic algal partner (phycobiont, provides food) and a heterotrophic fungal partner (mycobiont, provides shelter and absorbs water/minerals). They are sensitive bioindicators of air pollution and do not grow in polluted areas.

Worked Example 1: Assigning a Newly Isolated Microbe to Its Kingdom

English

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): A marine biologist isolates a unicellular organism from a coastal sample. Microscopy reveals a membrane-bound nucleus, mitochondria, and a rigid outer covering made of stiff cellulose plates. It possesses two flagella—one longitudinal and one transverse—and performs photosynthesis.

Task: Identify its kingdom and specific subgroup, and justify why it cannot be placed in Kingdom Monera or Kingdom Plantae.

Solution:

  1. Presence of a membrane-bound nucleus and mitochondria confirms it is eukaryotic, ruling out Kingdom Monera (all prokaryotes).
  2. Being unicellular and eukaryotic places it in Kingdom Protista rather than Kingdom Plantae.
  3. Stiff outer cellulose plates combined with two perpendicular flagella (one longitudinal, one transverse) are the diagnostic traits of Dinoflagellates.

Worked Example 2: Tracing Ploidy and Dikaryophase in Fungal Life Cycles

English

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): A mycologist cultures a saprophytic fungus with branched, septate hyphae. During sexual reproduction, two compatible haploid hyphae (\(n = 12\)) fuse their protoplasms, forming cells that temporarily contain two unfused nuclei per cell before nuclear fusion and meiosis occur inside a sac-like structure.

Task: Determine the chromosome count per cell during the dikaryophase, identify the fungal class, and state the ploidy of the resulting sexual spores.

Solution:

  1. After plasmogamy, each cell enters the dikaryophase (\(n + n\)), containing two haploid nuclei (\(12 + 12 = 24\) chromosomes total, still unfused as \(n + n\)).
  2. A branched, septate mycelium with a dikaryophase and sexual spores formed endogenously inside a sac-like ascus characterizes Ascomycetes (sac-fungi).
  3. Subsequent karyogamy produces a diploid nucleus (\(2n = 24\)), which undergoes meiosis to yield haploid (\(n = 12\)) ascospores.

Worked Example 3: Differentiating Acellular Infectious Agents

English

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Three infectious samples are analyzed in a lab: Sample X contains only an abnormally folded protein with no nucleic acid; Sample Y contains free low-molecular-weight RNA without a protein coat; Sample Z contains dsDNA enclosed within a polyhedral head and a contractile tail sheath.

Task: Classify Samples X, Y, and Z and name one disease or host associated with each.

Solution:

  • Sample X: Prion (abnormally folded protein; causes BSE in cattle and CJD in humans).
  • Sample Y: Viroid (free low-molecular-weight RNA lacking a capsid; causes potato spindle tuber disease).
  • Sample Z: Bacteriophage (a dsDNA virus that infects bacteria).

  • Slime mould vs. Malarial Plasmodium: Do not confuse the multinucleated aggregation of slime moulds (plasmodium) with the protozoan genus Plasmodium that causes malaria.
  • Virus genetic material: No virus contains both RNA and DNA; it contains either RNA or DNA.
  • Prions vs. Viroids: Viroids are pure RNA (no protein); prions are pure abnormally folded protein (no nucleic acid).
  • Basidiomycetes spores: Basidiospores are produced exogenously on the basidium, whereas ascospores are produced endogenously inside the ascus.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Whittaker's 5 criteria: cell structure, body organization, nutrition, reproduction, phylogenetic relationships.
  • Monera cell wall: non-cellulosic (polysaccharide + amino acid); Fungi: chitin + polysaccharides; Plantae: cellulose.
  • Heterocysts in Nostoc and Anabaena fix atmospheric nitrogen.
  • Neurospora is extensively used in biochemical and genetic work; Albugo causes white spots on mustard leaves.

  • প্রিয়ন বনাম ভিরয়েড: ভিরয়েড হলো কেবল মুক্ত RNA (প্রোটিন নেই); প্রিয়ন হলো কেবল অস্বাভাবিকভাবে ভাঁজ হওয়া প্রোটিন (নিউক্লিক অ্যাসিড নেই)।

Concise Final Recap / সংক্ষিপ্ত চূড়ান্ত সারসংক্ষেপ

English

Biological classification progressed from Aristotle's morphological groupings and Linnaeus's two-kingdom system to Whittaker's five-kingdom phylogenetic framework: Monera (all prokaryotes), Protista (unicellular eukaryotes), Fungi (chitin-walled absorptive heterotrophs), Plantae (cellulosic autotrophs with alternation of generations), and Animalia (wall-less holozoic heterotrophs). Acellular entities—viruses (nucleoprotein), viroids (free RNA), and prions (misfolded protein)—along with symbiotic lichens (phycobiont + mycobiont) sit outside the five kingdoms yet remain essential for mastering microbial diversity and disease biology.
