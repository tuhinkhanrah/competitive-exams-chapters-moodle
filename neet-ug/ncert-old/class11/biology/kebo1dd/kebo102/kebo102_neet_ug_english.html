Biological Classification: Systems, Kingdoms, and Acellular Entities

A structured, exam-oriented guide to Whittaker's Five-Kingdom framework, prokaryotic and protistan diversity, fungal life cycles, and acellular infectious agents.

Learning Objectives and Conceptual Overview

Biological classification organises Earth's vast biodiversity into logical, evolutionary groups. For NEET UG, mastery of this topic requires precise recall of diagnostic cellular features, nutritional modes, reproductive structures, and taxonomic exceptions.

Key Outcomes
  • Trace the transition from Aristotle's morphological grouping and Linnaeus's Two-Kingdom system to R.H. Whittaker's Five-Kingdom classification and the Three-Domain system.
  • Distinguish Kingdom Monera (Archaebacteria, Eubacteria, Cyanobacteria, and Mycoplasma) by cell wall chemistry, metabolism, and habitat extremes.
  • Classify unicellular eukaryotes in Kingdom Protista across Chrysophytes, Dinoflagellates, Euglenoids, Slime Moulds, and Protozoans.
  • Compare the four classes of Kingdom Fungi using hyphal septation, asexual spores, sexual spores, fruiting bodies, and the presence of a dikaryophase.
  • Differentiate acellular agents (viruses, viroids, prions) and symbiotic lichens from cellular organisms.

Evolution of Classification Systems

Early human attempts to group living organisms were driven by practical utility—food, shelter, and clothing—rather than scientific principles. Over centuries, taxonomy evolved from superficial morphology to cellular, biochemical, and evolutionary criteria.

  • Aristotle's Earliest Scientific Attempt: Grouped plants by simple morphological habit into trees, shrubs, and herbs. Divided animals into two broad categories: those possessing red blood and those lacking red blood.
  • Linnaeus's Two-Kingdom System: Placed all organisms into Plantae and Animalia. Although easy to apply, it failed to separate:
    • Prokaryotes (bacteria, cyanobacteria) from eukaryotes,
    • Unicellular organisms (e.g., Chlamydomonas) from multicellular organisms (e.g., Spirogyra), and
    • Photosynthetic green plants (cellulosic walls) from non-photosynthetic heterotrophic fungi (chitinous walls).
  • R.H. Whittaker's Five-Kingdom System (1969): Proposed five kingdoms—Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia—using five primary criteria:
    1. Cell structure (prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic),
    2. Body organisation (unicellular vs. multicellular/tissue/organ),
    3. Mode of nutrition (autotrophic vs. heterotrophic),
    4. Reproduction, and
    5. Phylogenetic (evolutionary) relationships.
  • Three-Domain System: Divides Kingdom Monera into two distinct domains while placing all eukaryotic kingdoms into a third domain, effectively creating a six-kingdom framework.
Taxonomic Reassignment Insight

Changing classification criteria reshuffles organisms across kingdoms. For instance, unicellular eukaryotic algae such as Chlamydomonas and Chlorella (possessing cell walls, once grouped under Plantae) were united inside Kingdom Protista alongside wall-less unicellular protozoans like Amoeba and Paramoecium (once placed in Animalia).

Kingdom Monera: The Prokaryotes

Bacteria are the sole members of Kingdom Monera and represent the most abundant microorganisms on Earth. A single handful of soil contains hundreds of bacteria, and they inhabit extreme environments—hot springs, deserts, snow, and deep oceans—as well as living as parasites inside or on other organisms.

Morphology and Metabolic Diversity

Based on cell shape, bacteria fall into four morphological categories:

  • Coccus (pl. cocci): Spherical.
  • Bacillus (pl. bacilli): Rod-shaped (some form endospores).
  • Vibrium (pl. vibrio): Comma-shaped.
  • Spirillum (pl. spirilla): Spiral-shaped (often bearing flagella).

Although structurally simple, bacteria exhibit the most extensive metabolic diversity of any group. A minority are autotrophic (synthesising organic food from inorganic substrates via photosynthesis or chemosynthesis), while the vast majority are heterotrophs.

Archaebacteria: Extremophiles

Archaebacteria survive in some of the harshest habitats on Earth because they possess a distinct cell wall structure that differs from true bacteria:

  • Halophiles: Thrive in extreme salty areas.
  • Thermoacidophiles: Inhabit hot, acidic springs.
  • Methanogens: Live in marshy areas and in the gut of ruminant animals (such as cows and buffaloes), where they produce methane (biogas) from animal dung.

Eubacteria: True Bacteria

Eubacteria are characterised by a rigid cell wall and, when motile, a flagellum. They include:

  • Cyanobacteria (Blue-green algae): Contain chlorophyll a (similar to green plants) and function as photosynthetic autotrophs. They may be unicellular, colonial, or filamentous, inhabiting freshwater, marine, or terrestrial environments. Colonies are typically enclosed in a gelatinous/mucilaginous sheath and often form dense blooms in polluted water bodies. Certain filamentous forms such as Nostoc and Anabaena fix atmospheric nitrogen inside specialised thick-walled cells called heterocysts.
  • Chemosynthetic Autotrophs: Oxidise inorganic substances—such as nitrates, nitrites, and ammonia—and use the released chemical energy for ATP synthesis. They play a crucial ecological role in recycling nutrients including nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and sulphur.
  • Heterotrophic Bacteria: The most abundant bacteria in nature; most act as decomposers. Beneficial roles include curd production from milk, antibiotic synthesis, and nitrogen fixation in legume roots. Pathogenic strains cause human, crop, and livestock diseases such as cholera, typhoid, tetanus, and citrus canker.

Bacterial Reproduction and Mycoplasma

Bacteria multiply primarily by binary fission. Under unfavourable conditions, they form spores. They also undergo a primitive form of sexual reproduction involving DNA transfer from one bacterium to another.

Mycoplasma: Unique prokaryotes that completely lack a cell wall. They are the smallest living cells known, can survive without oxygen, and many species are pathogenic in plants and animals.

Kingdom Protista: Unicellular Eukaryotes

Kingdom Protista encompasses all single-celled eukaryotes, though its boundaries are not rigidly defined (an organism viewed as a photosynthetic protist by one biologist may be treated as a plant by another). Primarily aquatic, protists form an evolutionary link with plants, animals, and fungi. Each cell possesses a well-defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; some bear cilia or flagella. Reproduction occurs both asexually and sexually (via cell fusion and zygote formation).

1. Chrysophytes (Diatoms and Golden Algae / Desmids)

  • Found in both freshwater and marine habitats; microscopic and float passively in water currents (plankton).
  • Most are photosynthetic; diatoms serve as the chief producers in the oceans.
  • In diatoms, the cell wall forms two thin, overlapping shells that fit together like a soap box. Because the walls are embedded with silica, they are indestructible.
  • Billions of years of accumulated diatom shells on ocean floors form diatomaceous earth, whose gritty texture is used in polishing and in the filtration of oils and syrups.

2. Dinoflagellates

  • Mostly marine and photosynthetic; appear yellow, green, brown, blue, or red depending on their dominant pigments.
  • Outer cell wall bears stiff cellulose plates.
  • Typically possess two flagella: one oriented longitudinally and the other transversely within a furrow between the wall plates.
  • Red dinoflagellates (e.g., Gonyaulax) multiply rapidly to produce red tides, releasing toxins that can kill marine fishes and other animals.

3. Euglenoids

  • Predominantly freshwater organisms inhabiting stagnant water (e.g., Euglena).
  • Lack a cell wall; instead, they have a flexible, protein-rich outer layer called a pellicle.
  • Possess two flagella—one short and one long.
  • Mixotrophic nutrition: Photosynthetic in sunlight (using pigments identical to those of higher plants), but behave as heterotrophic predators on smaller organisms when deprived of light.

4. Slime Moulds

  • Saprophytic protists whose body glides over decaying twigs and leaves, engulfing organic debris.
  • Under favourable conditions, they aggregate into a multinucleate mass called a plasmodium, which may grow and spread over several feet.
  • Under unfavourable conditions, the plasmodium differentiates into fruiting bodies bearing spores at their tips. These spores possess true walls, are extremely resistant to adverse conditions for years, and are dispersed by air currents.

5. Protozoans

All protozoans are heterotrophs (predators or parasites) and are regarded as primitive relatives of animals. They are divided into four major groups:

  • Amoeboid Protozoans: Inhabit freshwater, seawater, or moist soil. Move and capture prey using pseudopodia (false feet), as in Amoeba. Marine forms bear silica shells. Parasitic examples include Entamoeba.
  • Flagellated Protozoans: Free-living or parasitic, possessing flagella. Parasitic species such as Trypanosoma cause sleeping sickness.
  • Ciliated Protozoans: Aquatic and actively motile due to thousands of cilia. Coordinated ciliary beating sweeps food-laden water into a cavity called the gullet that opens to the cell surface (e.g., Paramoecium).
  • Sporozoans: Diverse parasites characterised by an infectious spore-like stage in their life cycle. The most notorious example is Plasmodium (malarial parasite), which causes malaria.

Kingdom Fungi: Heterotrophic Absorbers

Fungi constitute a unique kingdom of cosmopolitan, heterotrophic eukaryotes that thrive in warm and humid environments (which is why refrigeration slows food spoilage). Except for unicellular yeasts (Saccharomyces), fungal bodies are filamentous, composed of slender threads called hyphae that interweave into a mycelium.

  • Hyphal Types: Coenocytic hyphae are continuous, aseptate tubes filled with multinucleated cytoplasm; septate hyphae possess cross-walls (septae).
  • Cell Wall: Composed of chitin and polysaccharides.
  • Nutrition: Mostly saprophytes (absorbing soluble organic matter from dead substrates), parasites (on living plants/animals, e.g., Puccinia causing wheat rust, white spots on mustard leaves), or symbionts (with algae as lichens, and with roots of higher plants as mycorrhiza).

Fungal Reproduction and the Dikaryophase

Fungi reproduce vegetatively (fragmentation, fission, budding), asexually (via conidia, sporangiospores, or zoospores), and sexually (via oospores, ascospores, or basidiospores produced in fruiting bodies).

The fungal sexual cycle proceeds in three sequential steps:

  1. Plasmogamy: Fusion of protoplasms between two motile or non-motile gametes (or compatible haploid hyphae).
  2. Karyogamy: Fusion of the two parental nuclei to form a diploid nucleus (\(2n\)).
  3. Meiosis: Reduction division in the zygote, producing haploid spores (\(n\)).

In some fungi, plasmogamy is immediately followed by karyogamy to yield a diploid (\(2n\)) cell. However, in Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes, an intervening dikaryotic stage (\(n + n\), two unfused nuclei per cell) persists; this condition is called a dikaryon and the phase is the dikaryophase. Later, the nuclei fuse, and meiosis occurs inside fruiting bodies.

Overview of Kingdoms Plantae and Animalia

  • Kingdom Plantae: Eukaryotic, chlorophyll-containing organisms with prominent chloroplasts and cellulosic cell walls (including algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms, and angiosperms). A few are partially heterotrophic: insectivorous plants (Bladderwort, Venus flytrap) and parasitic plants (Cuscuta). Their life cycle exhibits alternation of generations between a diploid sporophytic phase (\(2n\)) and a haploid gametophytic phase (\(n\)).
  • Kingdom Animalia: Multicellular, heterotrophic eukaryotes lacking cell walls. They exhibit holozoic nutrition (ingesting food and digesting it in an internal cavity), store food reserves as glycogen or fat, follow a definite growth pattern, and show sensory, neuromotor, and locomotory abilities. Sexual reproduction involves copulation followed by embryological development.

Acellular Entities and Lichens

Whittaker's Five-Kingdom classification omits acellular infectious agents—viruses, viroids, and prions—as well as symbiotic lichens.

Viruses

Viruses are non-cellular, obligate intracellular parasites that exist as inert crystalline structures outside a host cell. Once inside, they hijack the host's cellular machinery to replicate, often killing the host.

  • Key Discoveries: Dmitri Ivanowsky (1892) identified microbes causing tobacco mosaic disease that passed through bacteria-proof filters (virus means venom/poisonous fluid). M.W. Beijerinck (1898) demonstrated that infected plant extract could infect healthy plants and termed it Contagium vivum fluidum (infectious living fluid). W.M. Stanley (1935) showed viruses could be crystallised, with crystals consisting largely of proteins.
  • Structure & Genetic Material: A virus is a nucleoprotein consisting of a protective protein coat (capsid, built from subunits called capsomeres arranged in helical or polyhedral forms) surrounding infectious nucleic acid. No virus contains both RNA and DNA.
    • Plant-infecting viruses generally have single-stranded RNA (ssRNA).
    • Animal-infecting viruses have ssRNA, dsRNA, or dsDNA.
    • Bacteriophages (viruses infecting bacteria) are usually double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses.
  • Diseases: Human diseases include mumps, smallpox, herpes, influenza, and AIDS. Plant symptoms include mosaic formation, leaf rolling and curling, yellowing and vein clearing, dwarfing, and stunted growth.

Viroids and Prions

  • Viroids: Discovered by T.O. Diener (1971) as infectious agents smaller than viruses causing potato spindle tuber disease. A viroid consists solely of free, low-molecular-weight RNA and completely lacks a protein coat.
  • Prions: Infectious neurological agents similar in size to viruses, composed exclusively of abnormally folded protein (no nucleic acid). Notable prion diseases include bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) or mad cow disease in cattle, and its human analogue Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD).

Lichens

Lichens are close mutualistic associations between algae and fungi. The algal partner (phycobiont) is autotrophic and synthesises food, while the fungal partner (mycobiont) is heterotrophic, providing shelter and absorbing water and mineral nutrients. Lichens are highly sensitive pollution indicators and do not grow in polluted areas.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Assigning an Unknown Microorganism to Its Kingdom

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): A newly isolated aquatic microorganism is unicellular, possesses a membrane-bound nucleus and mitochondria, lacks a cell wall, and has a flexible proteinaceous outer covering. It photosynthesises in bright light using chlorophylls typical of higher plants, but engulfs smaller microbes when kept in continuous darkness. Identify its kingdom and group.

Step-by-step Reasoning:

  1. Presence of a membrane-bound nucleus and mitochondria confirms the organism is eukaryotic (excluding Kingdom Monera).
  2. Being unicellular places it in Kingdom Protista rather than Plantae or Animalia.
  3. A flexible protein-rich covering (pellicle) instead of a rigid cell wall, combined with mixotrophic nutrition (photosynthetic in light, predatory heterotroph in darkness), is diagnostic of Euglenoids (e.g., Euglena).

Conclusion: Kingdom Protista; group Euglenoids.

Example 2: Tracing Fungal Ploidy Through the Sexual Cycle

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Suppose two compatible haploid mycelia (\(n\)) of a mushroom-forming fungus undergo fusion of their protoplasms inside soil. Describe the ploidy of the resulting cells before karyogamy, immediately after karyogamy, and in the final spores released from the fruiting body.

Step-by-step Reasoning:

  1. Mushroom-forming fungi belong to Basidiomycetes, which exhibit an intervening dikaryophase.
  2. After plasmogamy (fusion of somatic cells), the two nuclei do not fuse immediately; each cell contains two haploid nuclei, giving a dikaryotic state of \(n + n\).
  3. Inside the basidium, karyogamy occurs, uniting the two nuclei into a transient diploid nucleus (\(2n\)).
  4. Meiosis immediately follows karyogamy in the basidium, restoring the haploid condition (\(n\)) in four exogenously produced basidiospores.

Conclusion: Before karyogamy: \(n + n\) (dikaryon); after karyogamy: \(2n\) (diploid); final basidiospores: \(n\) (haploid).

Example 3: Distinguishing Acellular Infectious Agents by Biochemical Composition

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Three distinct infectious samples (X, Y, and Z) are treated with proteases (protein-digesting enzymes) and nucleases (nucleic-acid-digesting enzymes). Sample X loses infectivity only when its protein is destroyed (it contains no RNA or DNA). Sample Y loses infectivity when treated with RNase, even though it has no protein coat. Sample Z has a helical protein capsid protecting single-stranded RNA. Identify X, Y, and Z.

Step-by-step Reasoning:

  1. Sample X consists solely of abnormally folded protein without nucleic acid—the defining feature of a prion.
  2. Sample Y is naked, infectious, low-molecular-weight RNA lacking a protein coat—the defining feature of a viroid.
  3. Sample Z is a nucleoprotein with a helical capsid and ssRNA—characteristic of a plant virus (such as Tobacco Mosaic Virus).

Conclusion: X = Prion, Y = Viroid, Z = Helical plant virus.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding Kingdom Monera?

  1. (A) Methanogens are archaebacteria found in the gut of ruminant animals.
  2. (B) Mycoplasma are the smallest living cells and can survive without oxygen.
  3. (C) Cyanobacteria possess chlorophyll a and fix nitrogen in specialised cells called heterocysts.
  4. (D) Chemosynthetic autotrophic bacteria use light energy to oxidise nitrates and ammonia.
Answer: (D)

Explanation: Chemosynthetic autotrophs do not use light energy; they oxidise inorganic substances (nitrates, nitrites, ammonia) and use the released chemical energy for ATP synthesis. Statements (A), (B), and (C) are scientifically accurate.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In Whittaker's Five-Kingdom classification, Chlamydomonas and Chlorella were moved out of Plantae and placed together with Amoeba and Paramoecium under Kingdom Protista primarily because:

  1. (A) All four organisms lack a cell wall.
  2. (B) All four organisms are unicellular eukaryotes.
  3. (C) All four organisms are exclusively heterotrophic.
  4. (D) All four organisms reproduce only by binary fission.
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Kingdom Protista groups all single-celled eukaryotes regardless of whether they possess a cell wall (Chlamydomonas, Chlorella) or lack one (Amoeba, Paramoecium), and regardless of autotrophic vs. heterotrophic nutrition.

Practice Question 3 (Statement I & Statement II)

Read the two statements carefully and choose the correct option:

Statement I: Diatomaceous earth is gritty and used in polishing and filtration of oils and syrups.
Statement II: Diatom cell walls consist of two overlapping shells embedded with chitin, making them indestructible.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because diatom cell walls are embedded with silica, not chitin (chitin is characteristic of fungal cell walls).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Red tides in marine waters are caused by the rapid multiplication of:

  1. (A) Desmids
  2. (B) Gonyaulax
  3. (C) Euglena
  4. (D) Nostoc
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Gonyaulax is a red dinoflagellate whose rapid multiplication makes the sea appear red (red tide) and releases toxins lethal to marine fishes. Desmids are golden algae, Euglena is a freshwater euglenoid, and Nostoc is a cyanobacterium.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following combinations correctly matches a fungal class with its mycelium and sexual spore placement?

  1. (A) Phycomycetes — Septate mycelium — Exogenous zygospores
  2. (B) Ascomycetes — Septate mycelium — Endogenous ascospores
  3. (C) Basidiomycetes — Coenocytic mycelium — Endogenous basidiospores
  4. (D) Deuteromycetes — Aseptate mycelium — Endogenous conidia
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Ascomycetes have branched, septate mycelium and produce sexual ascospores endogenously inside sac-like asci. Phycomycetes have aseptate coenocytic mycelium; Basidiomycetes have septate mycelium and exogenous basidiospores; Deuteromycetes have septate mycelium and lack known sexual stages (conidia are asexual).

Practice Question 7 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Deuteromycetes are commonly known as imperfect fungi.
Reason (R): Only the asexual or vegetative phases of Deuteromycetes are known.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Fungi placed in Deuteromycetes are termed imperfect fungi precisely because their sexual (perfect) stages have not been observed; once a sexual stage is discovered, the species is transferred to Ascomycetes or Basidiomycetes.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Common Pitfalls and NEET Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Exogenous vs. Endogenous Fungal Spores: Conidia and basidiospores are exogenous; zoospores, aplanospores, and ascospores are endogenous.
  • Viral Nucleic Acid Rule: A virus contains either RNA or DNA, never both. Bacteriophages are typically dsDNA, whereas plant viruses are generally ssRNA.
  • Mycoplasma Wall Exception: Among Monerans, bacteria have a non-cellulosic cell wall, but Mycoplasma completely lack a cell wall.
  • Puccinia vs. Albugo: Albugo (mustard parasite) is a Phycomycete; Puccinia (wheat rust) and Ustilago (smut) are Basidiomycetes.
  • Order of Terms: Phycobiont = alga; Mycobiont = fungus. Always check which term is asked first.
  • Five-Kingdom Criteria: Cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction, and phylogenetic relationships.
  • Archaebacteria Survival: Unique cell wall structure enables survival in extreme salt (halophiles), heat/acid (thermoacidophiles), and anoxic guts/marshes (methanogens).
  • Protista Link: Unicellular eukaryotes; Chrysophytes (silica soap-box), Dinoflagellates (cellulose plates, 2 orthogonal flagella), Euglenoids (pellicle, mixotrophic), Slime moulds (plasmodium), Protozoans (4 groups).
  • Fungi Dikaryophase: Present in Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes (\(n + n\) stage between plasmogamy and karyogamy).
  • Acellular Trio: Viruses (nucleoprotein), Viroids (free RNA, no capsid), Prions (abnormal protein, no nucleic acid).

Concise Final Recap

Biological classification progressed from Aristotle's morphological division and Linnaeus's Two-Kingdom model to Whittaker's Five-Kingdom system (1969), which separates prokaryotes (Monera) from unicellular eukaryotes (Protista) and distinguishes chitinous heterotrophic Fungi from cellulosic autotrophic Plantae and wall-less holozoic Animalia. Acellular entities—viruses, viroids, and prions—along with mutualistic lichens fall outside the five kingdoms yet remain essential for understanding microbial diversity, disease mechanisms, and ecological indicators.
