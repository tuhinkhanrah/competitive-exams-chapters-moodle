NEET UG Biology • Plant Structural Organisation

Anatomy of Flowering Plants

Comprehensive conceptual tutorial on plant tissues, tissue systems, dicot–monocot organ anatomy, and secondary growth, tailored for NEET UG.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Plant anatomy examines the internal cellular architecture of plant organs and how structural specialization supports physiological roles such as conduction, mechanical support, photosynthesis, and protection. For NEET UG, mastery of this topic requires connecting cell wall chemistry and meristematic activity to the diagnostic cross-sectional features of roots, stems, and leaves in dicotyledons and monocotyledons.

Core Focus Areas:
  • Classification, structure, and functions of meristematic and permanent (simple and complex) tissues.
  • Organisation of the epidermal, ground, and vascular tissue systems.
  • Anatomical comparison of primary dicot and monocot roots, stems, and leaves.
  • Mechanism and products of secondary growth via vascular cambium and cork cambium.

1. Plant Tissues: Meristematic and Permanent

A tissue is an assemblage of cells sharing a common origin and typically performing a coordinated function. Based on mitotic capability, plant tissues are divided into meristematic tissues (actively dividing) and permanent tissues (structurally and functionally specialised, generally non-dividing).

1.1 Meristematic Tissues

Growth in higher plants is concentrated in discrete regions of active cell division called meristems. They are classified by position and developmental origin:

  • Apical Meristems: Located at root and shoot tips; they generate primary tissues and increase organ length. The root apical meristem occupies the sub-terminal root apex protected by a root cap, whereas the shoot apical meristem sits at the distal tip of the stem axis. During leaf formation and stem elongation, residual cells left behind by the shoot apical meristem form axillary buds in leaf axils, capable of developing into branches or flowers.
  • Intercalary Meristems: Situated between mature permanent tissues, notably at internodes and leaf bases of grasses; they rapidly regenerate parts removed by grazing herbivores.
  • Lateral (Secondary) Meristems: Cylindrical meristems appearing later in mature regions of roots and stems, particularly in woody plants, to produce secondary tissues and increase girth. Key examples are fascicular (intrafascicular) vascular cambium, interfascicular cambium, and cork cambium (phellogen).

Both apical and intercalary meristems are primary meristems because they appear early in the plant's life and construct the primary plant body.

1.2 Simple Permanent Tissues

Simple tissues consist of a single cell type uniform in structure and function.

Tissue Cell Wall & Living Status Arrangement & Forms Primary Functions
Parenchyma Living; thin cellulosic walls; isodiametric (spherical, oval, polygonal, or elongated). Closely packed or with small intercellular spaces; major component of plant organs. Photosynthesis (chlorenchyma), storage of food/water, and secretion.
Collenchyma Living; unevenly thickened corners due to cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin deposition. Intercellular spaces absent; occurs below epidermis in dicot stems (homogeneous layer or patches); often has chloroplasts. Mechanical support to growing organs (young stems, leaf petioles) and food assimilation when chloroplasts are present.
Sclerenchyma Usually dead without protoplasts at maturity; thick, lignified walls with pits. Two forms: Fibres (elongated, pointed, in groups) and Sclereids (spherical/oval/cylindrical with narrow lumen). Rigid mechanical support. Sclereids occur in nut shells, guava/pear/sapota pulp, legume seed coats, and tea leaves.

1.3 Complex Permanent Tissues

Complex tissues contain multiple cell types functioning together as a single conducting unit: xylem and phloem.

Xylem (Water and Mineral Conduction + Mechanical Support)

  • Tracheids: Elongated, tube-like dead cells with tapering ends and thick, lignified walls bearing varied inner thickenings.
  • Vessels: Long cylindrical tubes formed from aligned vessel members with lignified walls, large central cavities, and perforated end walls. Vessels are a characteristic feature of angiosperms and are absent in gymnosperms.
  • Xylem Fibres: Dead sclerenchymatous cells with highly thickened walls and obliterated lumens; may be septate or aseptate.
  • Xylem Parenchyma: Living, thin-walled cellulosic cells storing starch, fat, or tannins; ray parenchymatous cells perform radial water conduction.

Primary xylem differentiates into earlier-formed protoxylem (narrower elements) and later-formed metaxylem (wider elements). Their relative position defines two patterns:

  • Endarch (characteristic of stems): Protoxylem lies toward the centre (pith) and metaxylem lies toward the periphery.
  • Exarch (characteristic of roots): Protoxylem lies toward the periphery and metaxylem lies toward the centre.

Phloem (Food Translocation)

  • Sieve Tube Elements: Long, longitudinally arranged living tubes with end walls perforated as sieve plates. At maturity, they retain peripheral cytoplasm and a large vacuole but lack a nucleus; their metabolic activities are governed by the nucleus of adjacent companion cells.
  • Companion Cells: Specialised parenchymatous cells closely linked to sieve tubes via pit fields on common longitudinal walls; they maintain the pressure gradient needed for translocation.
  • Phloem Parenchyma: Elongated, tapering living cells with dense cytoplasm, nucleus, and cellulosic pitted walls (plasmodesmatal links); stores food, resins, latex, and mucilage. Absent in most monocotyledons.
  • Phloem Fibres (Bast Fibres): Unbranched, needle-pointed sclerenchymatous cells that lose protoplasm at maturity. Generally absent in primary phloem but present in secondary phloem (e.g., commercial fibres of jute, flax, and hemp).
Exam Alert — Gymnosperm Phloem: Gymnosperms lack both sieve tubes and companion cells; instead, they possess sieve cells and albuminous cells. Primary phloem also shows developmental grading from narrow-tubed protophloem to wider-tubed metaphloem.

2. The Three Tissue Systems

Based on structure and anatomical location, plant tissues are grouped into three systems:

2.1 Epidermal Tissue System

Forms the outermost protective boundary of the primary plant body, comprising:

  • Epidermis: Usually single-layered, compactly arranged parenchymatous cells with peripheral cytoplasm and a large central vacuole. Aerial parts are coated with a waxy cuticle to curb water loss (cuticle is absent in roots).
  • Stomatal Apparatus: Comprises the stomatal pore, two guard cells, and surrounding specialised subsidiary cells. Guard cells contain chloroplasts, have thin outer walls (away from the pore) and thick inner walls (facing the pore), and regulate transpiration and gas exchange. Dicots have bean-shaped guard cells, whereas grasses (monocots) have dumb-bell shaped guard cells.
  • Epidermal Appendages: Root hairs are unicellular epidermal elongations that absorb water and minerals. Shoot trichomes are usually multicellular (branched or unbranched, soft or stiff, sometimes secretory) and reduce transpirational water loss.

2.2 Ground Tissue System

All tissues excluding the epidermis and vascular bundles form the ground (fundamental) tissue system. It comprises parenchyma, collenchyma, and sclerenchyma. In primary stems and roots, parenchyma populates the cortex, pericycle, pith, and medullary rays. In leaves, the ground tissue consists of thin-walled, chloroplast-rich cells collectively termed mesophyll.

2.3 Vascular Tissue System

Composed of xylem and phloem organised into vascular bundles, classified by relative position and cambium presence:

  • Radial: Xylem and phloem patches alternate along separate radii (characteristic of roots).
  • Conjoint: Xylem and phloem lie together along the same radius, with phloem typically on the outer side (characteristic of stems and leaves).
  • Open vs. Closed Conjoint Bundles: Presence of cambium between xylem and phloem makes the bundle open to secondary growth (dicot stems); absence of cambium makes it closed (monocots).

3. Anatomy of Dicotyledonous and Monocotyledonous Organs

3.1 Dicotyledonous vs. Monocotyledonous Roots

In a young dicot root, the outermost epiblema bears unicellular root hairs. Beneath it lies a multilayered parenchymatous cortex with intercellular spaces, bounded internally by a single-layered, barrel-shaped endodermis lacking intercellular spaces. Endodermal radial and tangential walls contain waxy, water-impermeable suberin deposited as Casparian strips.

Internal to the endodermis lies the stele (all tissues inside the endodermis: pericycle, vascular bundles, and pith). The thick-walled parenchymatous pericycle initiates lateral roots and vascular cambium during secondary growth. Dicot roots typically have 2 to 4 (diarch to tetrarch) exarch xylem and phloem patches separated by parenchymatous conjunctive tissue, with a small or inconspicuous pith.

A monocot root shares the same basic layers (epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, vascular bundles, pith) but differs in two diagnostic ways: it is polyarch (usually more than six xylem bundles, i.e., \(> 6\)), has a large, well-developed pith, and undergoes no secondary growth.

3.2 Dicotyledonous vs. Monocotyledonous Stems

Anatomical Feature Dicotyledonous Stem Monocotyledonous Stem
Epidermis Thin cuticle; often bears multicellular trichomes and a few stomata. Cuticle present; trichomes generally absent.
Hypodermis Collenchymatous (few layers below epidermis); gives flexible strength. Sclerenchymatous; rigid peripheral support.
Cortex & Endodermis Differentiated into hypodermis, parenchymatous cortex, and starch-rich endodermis (starch sheath). No differentiation into cortex, endodermis, pericycle, or pith; large conspicuous parenchymatous ground tissue.
Pericycle & Rays Semi-lunar patches of sclerenchyma above phloem; radial parenchymatous medullary rays between bundles. Absent.
Vascular Bundles Arranged in a ring; uniform size; conjoint, open, endarch. Scattered in ground tissue; peripheral bundles smaller than central ones; conjoint, closed; surrounded by sclerenchymatous bundle sheath.
Special Bundle Traits Phloem parenchyma present; water cavities absent. Phloem parenchyma absent; water-containing cavities present within bundles.

3.3 Dorsiventral (Dicot) vs. Isobilateral (Monocot) Leaves

  • Dorsiventral (Dicot) Leaf: Covered by upper (adaxial) and lower (abaxial) epidermis with cuticle; stomata are more numerous on the abaxial surface (and may be absent adaxially). Mesophyll is differentiated into adaxial vertically elongated palisade parenchyma and abaxial loosely arranged spongy parenchyma with large air cavities. Reticulate venation produces vascular bundles of varying sizes, each encased by thick-walled bundle sheath cells (xylem faces adaxially, phloem faces abaxially).
  • Isobilateral (Monocot) Leaf: Stomata are distributed on both epidermal surfaces, and mesophyll is undifferentiated (no distinct palisade/spongy layers). Parallel venation yields near-uniform vascular bundle sizes (except main veins). In grasses, specialised adaxial epidermal cells along veins form large, empty, colourless bulliform cells: when turgid they expose the leaf blade; when flaccid under water stress they cause inward leaf curling to minimise water loss.

4. Secondary Growth in Woody Dicots and Roots

While primary growth elongates roots and stems via apical meristems, secondary growth increases organ girth through two lateral meristems: vascular cambium and cork cambium. Secondary growth occurs in dicots and gymnosperms, but not in monocotyledons.

4.1 Activity of the Vascular Cambium

  1. Cambial Ring Formation: In dicot stems, intrafascicular cambium (between primary xylem and primary phloem) joins with dedifferentiated medullary ray cells forming interfascicular cambium to establish a continuous cambial ring.
  2. Differential Production: The cambial ring cuts off secondary xylem inward (toward the pith) and secondary phloem outward (toward the periphery). Because the cambium is far more active on the inner side, secondary xylem accumulates as a compact mass that gradually crushes the primary and older secondary phloem, while primary xylem remains largely intact near the centre. Narrow radial bands of parenchyma formed at intervals across both secondary tissues constitute secondary medullary rays.

4.2 Spring Wood vs. Autumn Wood and Heartwood vs. Sapwood

  • Spring Wood (Early Wood) vs. Autumn Wood (Late Wood): In temperate climates, high spring cambial activity produces abundant xylary elements with wider vessel cavities, yielding lighter-coloured, lower-density spring wood. Reduced winter activity produces fewer elements with narrower vessels, yielding darker, higher-density autumn wood. One pair of alternating concentric rings constitutes an annual ring, used to estimate tree age.
  • Heartwood vs. Sapwood: In mature trees, central secondary xylem accumulates dark organic compounds (tannins, resins, oils, gums, aromatic substances), becoming hard, durable, and resistant to microbes and insects. This dead, non-conducting core is heartwood (provides mechanical support). The lighter, peripheral secondary xylem is sapwood, which conducts water and minerals from root to leaf.

4.3 Cork Cambium (Phellogen), Periderm, Bark, and Lenticels

As stem girth expands, the outer cortex and epidermis rupture. A new meristem, cork cambium (phellogen), arises usually in the cortex as a few layers of narrow, thin-walled rectangular cells. It divides bidirectionally:

\[ \mathrm{Periderm} = \mathrm{Phellem\ (Cork,\ outer,\ suberised/impervious)} + \mathrm{Phellogen\ (Cork\ cambium)} + \mathrm{Phelloderm\ (Secondary\ cortex,\ inner,\ parenchymatous)} \]
  • Bark: A non-technical term encompassing all tissues exterior to the vascular cambium (thus including both periderm and secondary phloem). Early-season bark is early/soft bark, whereas late-season bark is late/hard bark.
  • Lenticels: At certain sites, phellogen cuts off loosely arranged parenchymatous complementary cells outward instead of cork; these rupture the epidermis to create lens-shaped pores called lenticels for gaseous exchange in woody stems.

4.4 Secondary Growth in Dicot Roots

In dicot roots, the vascular cambium is completely secondary in origin. It develops from conjunctive tissue just below the phloem bundles and a portion of pericycle tissue just above the protoxylem, forming a continuous wavy ring that subsequently becomes circular. Thereafter, secondary xylem, secondary phloem, and periderm formation proceed similarly to the dicot stem.

Worked Examples

Identifying a Plant Organ from Microscopic Cross-Sectional Features

Scenario (Hypothetical Specimen): A student examines a stained transverse section of an angiosperm organ under a microscope and records four observations: (1) outermost epidermis lacks a cuticle and bears unicellular hairs, (2) endodermis displays distinct Casparian strips, (3) eight alternating xylem and phloem patches are arranged on separate radii, and (4) the central pith is broad and parenchymatous. Identify the organ and plant group.

Solution:

  • Absence of cuticle and presence of unicellular hairs identify the organ as a root (stems and leaves have a cuticle; shoot hairs are multicellular trichomes).
  • Radial vascular bundles with Casparian strips confirm root anatomy.
  • Presence of eight (more than six, i.e., polyarch) xylem bundles together with a broad, well-developed pith is diagnostic of a monocotyledonous root (dicot roots have 2 to 4 xylem patches and a small/inconspicuous pith).

Distinguishing Gymnosperm and Angiosperm Conducting Tissues

Scenario (Hypothetical Specimen): A woody stem sample (assumed to be an unknown seed plant) undergoes secondary growth and shows tracheids, sieve cells, and albuminous cells, but lacks both xylem vessels and companion cells. Classify the plant group and justify the physiological role of albuminous cells.

Solution:

  • Secondary growth occurs in both dicot angiosperms and gymnosperms, so secondary xylem alone does not separate them.
  • However, the absence of vessels in xylem and absence of sieve tubes and companion cells in phloem uniquely identify the specimen as a gymnosperm.
  • In gymnosperms, albuminous cells are functionally analogous to angiosperm companion cells, supporting the enucleate or specialised sieve cells in phloem translocation.

Interpreting Annual Rings and Wood Functional Zones

Scenario (Hypothetical Specimen): A transverse disc cut from the base of a temperate dicot tree (assumed to have grown for 18 years under uniform seasonal cycles) shows 18 concentric pairs of light and dark wood rings. If a fungal pathogen destroys only the innermost 10 dark-stained rings, will upward water transport from roots to leaves immediately halt?

Solution:

  • Each concentric pair of light spring wood (wide vessels, lower density) and dark autumn wood (narrow vessels, higher density) represents one annual ring, matching the tree's 18-year age.
  • The innermost dark-stained rings constitute heartwood, which consists of dead, lignified elements clogged with tannins, resins, oils, and gums. Heartwood provides mechanical support and does not conduct water.
  • Upward water and mineral transport occurs through the peripheral lighter-coloured sapwood (the outer rings). Therefore, water transport will not immediately halt.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following combinations of features is strictly characteristic of a monocotyledonous stem?

  1. (A) Collenchymatous hypodermis, open vascular bundles in a ring, phloem parenchyma present
  2. (B) Sclerenchymatous hypodermis, scattered closed vascular bundles, water-containing cavities present
  3. (C) Sclerenchymatous hypodermis, radial vascular bundles, well-developed pith
  4. (D) Parenchymatous hypodermis, conjoint open bundles, starch sheath present
Answer: (B)
Explanation: A monocot stem has a sclerenchymatous hypodermis, scattered conjoint and closed vascular bundles surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath, water-containing cavities within the bundles, and no phloem parenchyma. Option (A) describes a dicot stem, while radial bundles (C) occur in roots.
Practice Question 2 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: In a mature sieve tube element, the peripheral cytoplasm and large vacuole are present, but the nucleus is absent.
Statement II: Phloem parenchyma is commonly present in most monocotyledons to store resins and latex.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  3. (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Statement I is correct; a mature sieve tube element is enucleate and its functions are controlled by the nucleus of the associated companion cell. Statement II is incorrect because phloem parenchyma is absent in most monocotyledons.
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

During secondary growth in a typical dicotyledonous root, the vascular cambium originates from:

  1. (A) Intrafascicular cambium and medullary rays
  2. (B) Endodermis and cortical parenchyma
  3. (C) Tissue just below the phloem bundles and a portion of pericycle above the protoxylem
  4. (D) Phellogen and primary xylem parenchyma
Answer: (C)
Explanation: In dicot roots, the vascular cambium is completely secondary in origin, arising from conjunctive tissue located just below the phloem bundles and pericycle cells located above the protoxylem, forming a wavy ring that later becomes circular. Option (A) applies to dicot stems.
Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): In grasses, leaves curl inwards during periods of water stress.
Reason (R): Flaccid bulliform cells present in the adaxial epidermis along the veins cause the leaf surface to curl inwards to minimise water loss.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  3. (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
  4. (D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Answer: (A)
Explanation: Grasses possess large, empty, colourless bulliform cells in the adaxial epidermis. When turgid, they keep the leaf flat and exposed; when flaccid due to water deficit, they cause inward curling, reducing transpiration.
Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which tissue provides mechanical support specifically to growing parts of a dicot plant, such as a young stem and leaf petiole, without any intercellular spaces?

  1. (A) Parenchyma
  2. (B) Collenchyma
  3. (C) Sclereids
  4. (D) Xylem fibres
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Collenchyma consists of living cells thickened at the corners with cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, with no intercellular spaces. It provides flexible mechanical support to growing young stems and petioles.
Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Identify the incorrect statement regarding bark and periderm in woody dicot stems:

  1. (A) Periderm consists of phellogen, phellem, and phelloderm
  2. (B) Bark includes all tissues exterior to the vascular cambium, including secondary phloem
  3. (C) Cork (phellem) is impervious to water due to suberin deposition
  4. (D) Secondary cortex (phelloderm) is composed of dead, lignified sclerenchyma cells
Answer: (D)
Explanation: Phelloderm (secondary cortex) formed on the inner side of phellogen is composed of living parenchymatous cells, not dead lignified sclerenchyma. Statements (A), (B), and (C) are all accurate.
Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a dorsiventral leaf, the orientation of mesophyll and vascular tissues is such that:

  1. (A) Palisade parenchyma is abaxial and spongy parenchyma is adaxial
  2. (B) Stomata are strictly restricted to the adaxial epidermis
  3. (C) Palisade parenchyma is adaxial, and abaxial epidermis generally bears more stomata
  4. (D) Vascular bundles are all identical in size due to reticulate venation
Answer: (C)
Explanation: In a dorsiventral (dicot) leaf, vertically elongated palisade parenchyma lies adaxially (upper side), spongy parenchyma with air cavities lies abaxially (lower side), and the abaxial epidermis bears more stomata. Vein thickness varies in reticulate venation, so bundle sizes also vary.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid:
  • Endarch vs. Exarch: Never confuse stem and root xylem arrangement—stems are endarch (protoxylem central), roots are exarch (protoxylem peripheral).
  • Bark vs. Periderm: Bark is broader than periderm; bark = periderm + secondary phloem (all tissues outside vascular cambium).
  • Secondary Growth in Roots vs. Stems: In dicot stems, vascular cambium is partly primary (intrafascicular) and partly secondary (interfascicular); in dicot roots, it is completely secondary in origin.
  • Cuticle Distribution: Aerial epidermis has a waxy cuticle; root epiblema lacks a cuticle to allow water absorption.
  • Bast Fibres: Phloem fibres are generally absent in primary phloem and occur in secondary phloem.

Concise Final Recap

Plant anatomy integrates meristematic growth with specialised permanent tissues organised into epidermal, ground, and vascular systems. Dicots and monocots diverge sharply in root xylem bundle count (2–4 vs. polyarch), stem bundle arrangement (ring/open vs. scattered/closed), and leaf mesophyll differentiation (dorsiventral vs. isobilateral). Secondary growth via vascular cambium (producing wood and secondary phloem) and cork cambium (producing periderm) drives radial expansion exclusively in woody dicots and gymnosperms.
