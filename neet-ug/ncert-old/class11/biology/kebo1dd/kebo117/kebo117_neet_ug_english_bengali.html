Comparative Respiratory Organs & Human Respiratory Anatomy

Animal respiratory mechanisms reflect habitat (aquatic vs. terrestrial) and body complexity. Simple aquatic or moist-bodied invertebrates rely on direct surface diffusion, whereas larger, metabolically active animals evolve specialized vascularized surfaces.

Animal Group Primary Respiratory Structure Mode of Respiration
Sponges, Coelenterates, Flatworms Entire general body surface Simple diffusion
Earthworms (Annelida) Moist cuticle Cutaneous diffusion
Insects (Arthropoda) Network of tracheal tubes Tracheal respiration
Aquatic arthropods, Molluscs, Fishes Vascularized gills Branchial respiration
Amphibians (e.g., Frogs) Lungs and moist skin Pulmonary & cutaneous respiration
Reptiles, Birds, Mammals Vascularized lungs Pulmonary respiration

Human Respiratory Tract: Conducting vs. Exchange Zones

Air enters through paired external nostrils into the nasal passage and nasal chamber, then passes into the pharynx (common passage for air and food). The pharynx opens via the glottis into the larynx (a cartilaginous sound box). During swallowing, an elastic cartilaginous flap called the epiglottis covers the glottis to prevent food from entering the airway.

The larynx continues into the trachea, a straight tube extending to the mid-thoracic cavity, where it bifurcates at the level of the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left primary bronchi. Each primary bronchus branches into secondary and tertiary bronchi, followed by bronchioles and thin terminal bronchioles. Incomplete C-shaped cartilaginous rings prevent airway collapse in the trachea, primary, secondary, and tertiary bronchi, as well as the initial bronchioles.

  • Conducting Part (External nostrils up to terminal bronchioles): Transports atmospheric air to alveoli, clears foreign particles, humidifies air, and warms it to body temperature.
  • Respiratory / Exchange Part (Alveoli and alveolar ducts): Thin, irregular-walled, richly vascularized bag-like structures where actual diffusion of O2 and CO2 occurs between blood and alveolar air.

Pleural Membranes & Thoracic Chamber

The two lungs are enclosed by a double-layered pleura containing pleural fluid that minimizes friction during lung movement. The outer parietal pleura adheres to the thoracic wall, while the inner visceral pleura contacts the lung surface. Because the thoracic chamber is anatomically air-tight—bounded dorsally by the vertebral column, ventrally by the sternum, laterally by the ribs, and inferiorly by the dome-shaped diaphragm—any change in thoracic cavity volume directly alters pulmonary volume.

Five Sequential Steps of Respiration

  1. Pulmonary ventilation (Breathing): Drawing in atmospheric O2-rich air and expelling CO2-rich alveolar air.
  2. Alveolar gas diffusion: Exchange of O2 and CO2 across the alveolar-capillary membrane.
  3. Gas transport: Movement of O2 and CO2 via blood circulation.
  4. Tissue gas diffusion: Exchange of O2 and CO2 between systemic blood and body tissues.
  5. Cellular respiration: Intracellular utilization of O2 in catabolism and generation of CO2.

Mechanism of Breathing, Respiratory Volumes & Capacities

Airflow into and out of the lungs depends on creating a pressure gradient between the alveoli and the atmosphere. A healthy adult human breathes approximately 12–16 times per minute, and respiratory air volumes can be measured clinically using a spirometer.

Inspiration vs. Expiration

  • Inspiration (Active process): Initiated by contraction of the diaphragm (flattens downward, increasing thoracic volume along the antero-posterior axis) and contraction of the external intercostal muscles (lifting ribs and sternum outward and upward, increasing thoracic volume along the dorso-ventral axis). The resulting increase in pulmonary volume drops intra-pulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure (negative pressure), drawing air into the lungs.
  • Expiration (Passive during quiet breathing): Relaxation of the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles returns the diaphragm to its dome shape and lowers the ribs/sternum. Thoracic and pulmonary volumes decrease, raising intra-pulmonary pressure slightly above atmospheric pressure and expelling air.
  • Forced Breathing: Additional abdominal muscles and internal intercostal muscles can be recruited to increase the strength of inspiration and expiration.

Standard Respiratory Volumes and Capacities

Pulmonary capacities are clinical combinations of two or more basic respiratory volumes. Note that Residual Volume (RV) cannot be measured directly by simple spirometry because it remains in the lungs even after maximal forced expiration.

Term & Abbreviation Definition / Formula Average Adult Value
Tidal Volume (TV) Air inspired or expired during normal quiet breathing ~500 mL (6000–8000 mL/min)
Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV) Additional air inspired by forcible inspiration 2500–3000 mL
Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV) Additional air expired by forcible expiration 1000–1100 mL
Residual Volume (RV) Air remaining in lungs after forcible expiration 1100–1200 mL
Inspiratory Capacity (IC) Max air inspired after normal expiration: \(\mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV}\) 3000–3500 mL
Expiratory Capacity (EC) Max air expired after normal inspiration: \(\mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{ERV}\) 1500–1600 mL
Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) Air remaining after normal expiration: \(\mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{RV}\) 2100–2300 mL
Vital Capacity (VC) Max air breathed in after forced expiration (or out after forced inspiration): \(\mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV}\) 4000–4600 mL
Total Lung Capacity (TLC) Total air in lungs after forced inspiration: \(\mathrm{RV} + \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV} = \mathrm{VC} + \mathrm{RV}\) 5100–5800 mL

Exchange of Gases & Structure of the Diffusion Membrane

Gas exchange at both the alveoli and systemic tissues occurs by simple diffusion driven primarily by partial pressure gradients (\(p\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\)), where partial pressure is the pressure exerted by an individual gas in a mixture.

Respiratory Gas Atmospheric Air Alveoli Deoxygenated Blood Oxygenated Blood Tissues
\(\mathrm{O_2}\) (mm Hg) 159 104 40 95 40
\(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (mm Hg) 0.3 40 45 40 45

Three Key Factors Governing Diffusion Rate

  1. Partial Pressure Gradient: \(\mathrm{O_2}\) diffuses from alveoli (104 mm Hg) to deoxygenated blood (40 mm Hg), and from oxygenated blood (95 mm Hg) to tissues (40 mm Hg). \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffuses in the opposite direction: tissues (45 mm Hg) → blood (40 mm Hg) → alveoli (40 mm Hg).
  2. Gas Solubility: The solubility of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is 20–25 times higher than that of \(\mathrm{O_2}\), allowing rapid \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffusion even across a small partial pressure difference (45 vs. 40 mm Hg).
  3. Thickness of the Diffusion Membrane: Total thickness is much less than 1 mm (~0.2–0.5 µm), comprising three distinct layers:
    • Thin squamous epithelium of the alveoli (one-cell thick).
    • Basement substance (fused basement membranes of alveolar epithelium and capillary endothelium).
    • Endothelium of the alveolar capillaries.

Transport of Oxygen & Carbon Dioxide in Blood

Transport of Oxygen (O2)

  • 97% is transported bound to haemoglobin (Hb) inside RBCs as oxyhaemoglobin; 3% travels dissolved in plasma.
  • Each Hb tetramer binds a maximum of four O2 molecules reversibly.
  • Plotting percentage saturation of Hb against \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) yields a sigmoid Oxygen Dissociation Curve.
  • Alveolar Conditions (Association / Left-favoured): High \(p\mathrm{O_2}\), low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), lower \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration (higher pH), and lower temperature promote oxyhaemoglobin formation.
  • Tissue Conditions (Dissociation / Right-shift): Low \(p\mathrm{O_2}\), high \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), higher \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration (lower pH), and higher temperature promote O2 release from oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Under normal physiological conditions, every 100 mL of oxygenated blood delivers approximately 5 mL of O2 to the tissues.

Transport of Carbon Dioxide (CO2)

  • 70% as Bicarbonate (\(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\)): Catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase (abundant in RBCs, minute in plasma). At tissues (high \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\)), the reaction proceeds forward to form \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\); at alveoli (low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\)), it reverses to release \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\).
  • 20–25% as Carbamino-haemoglobin: \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) binds to amino groups of haemoglobin. High \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) and low \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) (tissues) favour binding; low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) and high \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) (alveoli) favour dissociation.
  • 7% dissolved in plasma.

Regulation of Respiration & Respiratory Disorders

Neural & Chemical Regulation

  • Respiratory Rhythm Centre (Medulla): Primarily responsible for generating and maintaining the basic respiratory rhythm.
  • Pneumotaxic Centre (Pons): Moderates the rhythm centre; neural signals from this centre reduce the duration of inspiration and thereby alter the respiratory rate.
  • Central Chemosensitive Area (Medulla, adjacent to rhythm centre): Highly sensitive to elevated CO2 and H+ ions; stimulates the rhythm centre to increase ventilation and eliminate excess CO2.
  • Peripheral Chemoreceptors (Aortic arch and Carotid artery): Also detect changes in arterial CO2 and H+ concentrations and signal the medulla.
  • Crucial NEET Fact: The role of oxygen (O2) in the regulation of respiratory rhythm is quite insignificant.

Common Respiratory Disorders

  • Asthma: Difficulty in breathing accompanied by wheezing due to inflammation of the bronchi and bronchioles.
  • Emphysema: A chronic disorder in which alveolar walls are damaged, drastically reducing the respiratory surface area. Major cause: cigarette smoking.
  • Occupational Respiratory Disorders: Prolonged exposure to heavy dust (e.g., grinding or stone-breaking industries) overwhelms lung defense mechanisms, leading to chronic inflammation and fibrosis (proliferation of fibrous tissue) causing serious lung damage.

Example 1: Calculating Pulmonary Capacities and Minute Ventilation

Scenario (Assumed Spirometry Values): Suppose a healthy adult athlete records the following lung volumes during a pulmonary function test: \(\mathrm{TV} = 480\,\mathrm{mL}\), \(\mathrm{IRV} = 2700\,\mathrm{mL}\), \(\mathrm{ERV} = 1050\,\mathrm{mL}\), \(\mathrm{RV} = 1150\,\mathrm{mL}\), and a resting respiratory rate of \(14\,\mathrm{breaths/min}\). Calculate (a) Vital Capacity (VC), (b) Functional Residual Capacity (FRC), (c) Total Lung Capacity (TLC), and (d) Minute Ventilation.

Solution:

  • (a) \(\mathrm{VC} = \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV} = 1050 + 480 + 2700 = 4230\,\mathrm{mL}\).
  • (b) \(\mathrm{FRC} = \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{RV} = 1050 + 1150 = 2200\,\mathrm{mL}\).
  • (c) \(\mathrm{TLC} = \mathrm{VC} + \mathrm{RV} = 4230 + 1150 = 5380\,\mathrm{mL}\).
  • (d) \(\text{Minute Ventilation} = \mathrm{TV} \times \text{Respiratory Rate} = 480\,\mathrm{mL} \times 14\,\mathrm{min^{-1}} = 6720\,\mathrm{mL/min}\).
Example 2: Estimating O2 Delivery and CO2 Removal at Rest

Scenario (Assumed Blood Flow): Under normal physiological conditions, assume a resting cardiac output of \(5.2\,\mathrm{L/min}\) (\(5200\,\mathrm{mL/min}\)). Using standard physiological gas exchange values, estimate how much O2 is delivered to tissues and how much CO2 is released at the alveoli per minute.

Solution:

  • Every \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of oxygenated blood delivers \(5\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to tissues: \[ \mathrm{O_2\ delivered} = \frac{5200}{100} \times 5\,\mathrm{mL} = 52 \times 5 = 260\,\mathrm{mL/min} \]
  • Every \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of deoxygenated blood delivers \(4\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to alveoli: \[ \mathrm{CO_2\ released} = \frac{5200}{100} \times 4\,\mathrm{mL} = 52 \times 4 = 208\,\mathrm{mL/min} \]
Example 3: Predicting Shifts in the Oxygen Dissociation Curve

Scenario (Assumed Physiological State): Compare blood flowing through (i) alveolar capillaries and (ii) vigorously exercising skeletal muscle. In which location does the oxygen dissociation curve shift to the right, and what happens to oxyhaemoglobin?

Solution:

In vigorously exercising skeletal muscle, cellular respiration produces abundant \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and lactic acid (raising \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration and lowering pH) alongside elevated temperature and low \(p\mathrm{O_2}\). These four conditions shift the oxygen dissociation curve to the right, lowering haemoglobin's affinity for \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and promoting rapid dissociation of oxyhaemoglobin to supply active muscle cells. Conversely, alveolar capillaries have high \(p\mathrm{O_2}\), low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), low \(\mathrm{H^+}\), and lower temperature, favouring oxyhaemoglobin formation.

Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid:
  • Confusing RV (1100–1200 mL) with ERV (1000–1100 mL): RV cannot be measured by a spirometer and is excluded from Vital Capacity (\(\mathrm{VC} = \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV}\)).
  • Assuming \(\mathrm{O_2}\) drives medullary breathing regulation: In reality, CO2 and H+ are the primary chemical regulators.
  • Forgetting that 70% of CO2 travels as bicarbonate (\(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\)), while only 20–25% travels as carbamino-haemoglobin.
  • Mixing up the tracheal bifurcation level: It occurs at the 5th thoracic vertebra (T5).
High-Yield Revision Checklist:
  • Conducting zone ends at terminal bronchioles; exchange zone begins at alveolar ducts and alveoli.
  • Normal breathing rate: 12–16/min; Tidal volume: ~500 mL; 100 mL blood delivers 5 mL O2 to tissues and 4 mL CO2 to alveoli.
  • Diffusion membrane (< 1 mm): alveolar squamous epithelium + basement substance + capillary endothelium.
  • Pons (pneumotaxic centre) moderates the medulla (rhythm centre) by shortening inspiration.

Final Recap: Pulmonary ventilation creates pressure gradients via the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, driving O2 into alveoli and CO2 out. Across the ultra-thin alveolar membrane, partial pressure gradients and high CO2 solubility ensure rapid gas exchange, while haemoglobin and carbonic anhydrase coordinate reversible gas transport between lungs and tissues under tight medullary-pontine control.
