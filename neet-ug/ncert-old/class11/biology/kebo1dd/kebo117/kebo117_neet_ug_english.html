Breathing and Exchange of Gases

A complete conceptual and assessment tutorial covering respiratory anatomy, ventilation mechanics, gas diffusion, blood transport, neural regulation, and clinical disorders.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Every living cell requires a continuous supply of oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)) to oxidize simple nutrients—such as glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids—for metabolic energy. These catabolic reactions simultaneously generate carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)), a harmful waste gas that must be eliminated. Breathing (commonly termed respiration) is the physiological exchange of atmospheric \(\mathrm{O_2}\) with cellular \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

What You Will Master

  • Comparative respiratory strategies across major animal phyla and vertebrate classes.
  • Structural organization of the human conducting and respiratory (exchange) zones.
  • Pressure-gradient mechanics of inspiration and expiration, plus spirometric volumes and capacities.
  • Partial pressure gradients and the three-layered alveolar-capillary diffusion membrane.
  • Blood transport of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) (oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (bicarbonate and carbamino-haemoglobin).
  • Neural and chemical regulation of respiratory rhythm, and key respiratory disorders.

Comparative Respiratory Strategies in Animals

The mechanism of gas exchange across the animal kingdom depends primarily on an organism's habitat and level of structural organization. Simple aquatic forms rely on direct diffusion, whereas complex terrestrial animals use specialized, vascularized internal surfaces.

Animal Group / Examples Respiratory Organ / Surface Mode of Respiration
Lower invertebrates (sponges, coelenterates, flatworms) Entire body surface Simple diffusion
Earthworms Moist cuticle Cutaneous diffusion
Insects Network of tracheal tubes Tracheal respiration (direct air transport)
Most aquatic arthropods and molluscs; Fishes Special vascularized gills Branchial respiration
Amphibians (e.g., frogs), reptiles, birds, mammals Vascularized lungs (plus moist skin in frogs) Pulmonary respiration (and cutaneous in frogs)

Architecture of the Human Respiratory System

The human respiratory system is organized into two functional divisions: a conducting part that conditions and channels air, and an exchange (respiratory) part where gas diffusion occurs.

Conducting and Exchange Zones

  • Upper Airway: A pair of external nostrils above the upper lip opens into the nasal passage and nasal chamber, which leads into the pharynx (a common passage for both food and air).
  • Larynx and Glottis: The pharynx opens through the glottis into the larynx, a cartilaginous box responsible for sound production (the sound box). During swallowing, a thin elastic cartilaginous flap called the epiglottis covers the glottis to prevent food from entering the airway.
  • Tracheobronchial Tree: The trachea is a straight tube extending into the mid-thoracic cavity, where it bifurcates at the level of the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left primary bronchi. Each primary bronchus divides repeatedly into secondary bronchi, tertiary bronchi, bronchioles, and finally very thin terminal bronchioles.
  • Cartilaginous Support: The trachea, primary, secondary, and tertiary bronchi, and initial bronchioles are supported by incomplete cartilaginous rings that keep the airway patent.
  • Alveoli: Each terminal bronchiole gives rise to thin, irregular-walled, heavily vascularized bag-like structures called alveoli. The branching network of bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli constitutes the lungs.
Functional Division: The conducting part extends from the external nostrils to the terminal bronchioles; it transports air, clears foreign particles, humidifies the air, and brings it to body temperature. The exchange part consists of the alveoli and their ducts, serving as the actual site of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffusion between blood and atmospheric air.

Thoracic Chamber and Pleural Protection

The two lungs lie within an anatomically air-tight thoracic chamber bounded dorsally by the vertebral column, ventrally by the sternum, laterally by the ribs, and inferiorly by the dome-shaped diaphragm. Because humans cannot directly alter pulmonary volume, this air-tight arrangement ensures that any change in thoracic cavity volume is immediately reflected in the lung (pulmonary) cavity.

Each lung is enclosed by a double-layered pleura containing pleural fluid between the membranes to minimize friction on the lung surface. The outer pleural membrane contacts the thoracic lining, while the inner pleural membrane adheres to the lung surface.

Five Sequential Steps of Respiration

  1. Pulmonary Ventilation (Breathing): Atmospheric air is drawn in and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-rich alveolar air is expelled.
  2. Alveolar Gas Diffusion: \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffuse across the alveolar membrane.
  3. Gas Transport: Blood carries \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to tissues and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) back to the lungs.
  4. Tissue Gas Diffusion: \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffuse between systemic blood and tissue cells.
  5. Cellular Respiration: Cells utilize \(\mathrm{O_2}\) for catabolic reactions, releasing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

Mechanics of Breathing and Pulmonary Volumes

Airflow into and out of the lungs depends on generating a pressure gradient between the alveoli (intra-pulmonary pressure) and the atmosphere. A healthy human breathes 12 to 16 times per minute on average, and respiratory air volumes are measured clinically using a spirometer.

Inspiration vs. Expiration

  • Inspiration (Active Process): Contraction of the diaphragm flattens its dome, increasing thoracic volume along the antero-posterior axis. Simultaneously, contraction of the external intercostal muscles lifts the ribs and sternum upward and outward, increasing thoracic volume along the dorso-ventral axis. The resulting increase in pulmonary volume drops intra-pulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure (negative pressure), drawing air into the lungs.
  • Expiration (Passive under Normal Conditions): Relaxation of the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles returns the diaphragm, sternum, and ribs to their resting positions. Thoracic and pulmonary volumes decrease, raising intra-pulmonary pressure slightly above atmospheric pressure and expelling air.
  • Forceful Breathing: Humans can amplify the strength of inspiration and expiration using additional abdominal muscles and internal intercostal muscles.

Respiratory Volumes and Pulmonary Capacities

Four primary respiratory volumes combine to form five clinically useful pulmonary capacities. Note that Residual Volume (RV) cannot be measured directly by simple spirometry because it never leaves the lungs.

Parameter Definition Standard Value / Formula
Tidal Volume (TV) Air inspired or expired during a normal quiet breath. ~500 mL (6000–8000 mL/min)
Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV) Additional air inspired by a forcible inspiration. 2500–3000 mL
Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV) Additional air expired by a forcible expiration. 1000–1100 mL
Residual Volume (RV) Air remaining in lungs even after a forcible expiration. 1100–1200 mL
Inspiratory Capacity (IC) Total air inspired after a normal expiration. \(\mathrm{IC = TV + IRV}\) (~3000–3500 mL)
Expiratory Capacity (EC) Total air expired after a normal inspiration. \(\mathrm{EC = TV + ERV}\) (~1500–1600 mL)
Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) Air remaining in lungs after a normal expiration. \(\mathrm{FRC = ERV + RV}\) (~2100–2300 mL)
Vital Capacity (VC) Max air breathed in after forced expiration (or out after forced inspiration). \(\mathrm{VC = ERV + TV + IRV}\) (~4000–4600 mL)
Total Lung Capacity (TLC) Total air accommodated in lungs at the end of a forced inspiration. \(\mathrm{TLC = VC + RV = RV + ERV + TV + IRV}\) (~5100–5800 mL)

Gaseous Exchange and the Diffusion Membrane

Alveoli are the primary sites of gas exchange, with secondary exchange occurring between systemic blood and body tissues. Both \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) move across membranes by simple diffusion driven by three factors: (1) partial pressure/concentration gradients, (2) gas solubility, and (3) thickness of the diffusion membrane.

Partial Pressure Gradients

The pressure contributed by an individual gas in a mixture is its partial pressure (\(p\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\)). Comparing values across compartments reveals why \(\mathrm{O_2}\) diffuses from alveoli to blood and then to tissues, while \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) moves in the exact opposite direction.

Respiratory Gas Atmospheric Air Alveoli Deoxygenated Blood Oxygenated Blood Tissues
\(p\mathrm{O_2}\) (mm Hg) 159 104 40 95 40
\(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) (mm Hg) 0.3 40 45 40 45
Why \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) Diffuses Efficiently Despite a Smaller Gradient: At the alveoli, the partial pressure gradient for \(\mathrm{O_2}\) is \(104 - 40 = 64\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\), whereas for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) it is only \(45 - 40 = 5\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\). However, the solubility of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is 20–25 times higher than that of \(\mathrm{O_2}\), so the volume of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffusing per unit pressure difference is far greater.

Structure of the Diffusion Membrane

The alveolar-capillary diffusion membrane has a total thickness of much less than 1 mm and consists of three major layers:

  1. Thin squamous epithelium of the alveolar wall (one-cell thick).
  2. Basement substance (a thin fused layer supporting both the alveolar epithelium and the capillary endothelium).
  3. Endothelium of the alveolar blood capillary.

Transport of Respiratory Gases in Blood

Blood serves as the transport medium for both gases, utilizing red blood cells (RBCs) and plasma in distinct proportions:

  • Oxygen Transport: ~97% is carried by RBCs as oxyhaemoglobin; ~3% is dissolved in plasma.
  • Carbon Dioxide Transport: ~70% is carried as bicarbonate ions (\(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\)); ~20–25% is bound to haemoglobin as carbamino-haemoglobin; ~7% is dissolved in plasma.

Oxygen Transport and the Oxygen Dissociation Curve

Haemoglobin (\(\mathrm{Hb}\)) is a red, iron-containing pigment in RBCs. Each \(\mathrm{Hb}\) molecule binds reversibly with up to four \(\mathrm{O_2}\) molecules to form oxyhaemoglobin. Plotting percentage saturation of haemoglobin against \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) yields a characteristic sigmoid curve (caused by cooperative binding, where binding of one \(\mathrm{O_2}\) increases affinity for the next).

  • In the Alveoli (Association / Left Shift): High \(p\mathrm{O_2}\), low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), lower \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration (higher pH), and lower temperature favour oxyhaemoglobin formation.
  • In the Tissues (Dissociation / Right Shift — Bohr Effect): Low \(p\mathrm{O_2}\), high \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), higher \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration (lower pH), and higher temperature promote \(\mathrm{O_2}\) release from oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Normal Physiological Delivery: Every 100 mL of oxygenated blood delivers approximately 5 mL of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to tissues under normal resting conditions.

Carbon Dioxide Transport

Carbamino-haemoglobin (20–25%): \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) binds to the amino groups of haemoglobin. Binding increases when \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) is high and \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) is low (tissues), and dissociates when \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) is low and \(p\mathrm{O_2}\) is high (alveoli).

Bicarbonate System (70%): RBCs contain a very high concentration of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (with minute amounts in plasma), catalyzing the reversible conversion of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) into bicarbonate:

\[ \mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O \xrightleftharpoons{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}} H_2CO_3 \xrightleftharpoons{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}} HCO_3^- + H^+} \]

At the tissues (high \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\)), the reaction proceeds rightward to form \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\). At the alveoli (low \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\)), it reverses to release \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) gas. Every 100 mL of deoxygenated blood delivers approximately 4 mL of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to the alveoli.

Regulation of Respiration and Respiratory Disorders

Neural and Chemical Control

  • Respiratory Rhythm Centre (Medulla): Primarily responsible for maintaining and moderating the basic breathing rhythm.
  • Pneumotaxic Centre (Pons): Moderates the medullary rhythm centre; neural signals from this centre reduce the duration of inspiration, thereby altering (increasing) the respiratory rate.
  • Central Chemosensitive Area: Located adjacent to the medullary rhythm centre; highly sensitive to increases in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ions, signaling the rhythm centre to accelerate elimination of these substances.
  • Peripheral Chemoreceptors: Receptors in the aortic arch and carotid artery also detect changes in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentrations and send corrective signals to the rhythm centre.
  • Role of Oxygen: The role of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) in regulating respiratory rhythm is quite insignificant.

Key Respiratory Disorders

Disorder Pathophysiology & Primary Cause Key Clinical Features
Asthma Inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles. Difficulty in breathing accompanied by wheezing.
Emphysema Chronic damage to alveolar walls (major cause: cigarette smoking). Decreased respiratory surface area for gas exchange.
Occupational Respiratory Disorders Prolonged inhalation of dust in grinding or stone-breaking industries overwhelms body defenses. Chronic inflammation leading to fibrosis (proliferation of fibrous tissue) and severe lung damage; prevented by protective masks.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Minute Ventilation and Alveolar Gas Exchange

Scenario (Assumed Values): Suppose a healthy adult athlete at rest has a breathing rate of \(14\,\mathrm{breaths/min}\) and a tidal volume of \(520\,\mathrm{mL}\). Assuming normal physiological blood delivery efficiency (\(5\,\mathrm{mL\,O_2}\) delivered per \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) oxygenated blood and \(4\,\mathrm{mL\,CO_2}\) delivered per \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) deoxygenated blood), calculate (a) the minute ventilation and (b) the volume of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) delivered if cardiac output is \(5000\,\mathrm{mL/min}\).

Solution:

(a) Minute ventilation is the product of tidal volume and respiratory rate:

\[ \text{Minute Ventilation} = 520\,\mathrm{mL/breath} \times 14\,\mathrm{breaths/min} = 7280\,\mathrm{mL/min} \approx 7.28\,\mathrm{L/min} \]

(b) Since \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of oxygenated blood delivers \(5\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\):

\[ \mathrm{O_2\ Delivered} = \frac{5\,\mathrm{mL\,O_2}}{100\,\mathrm{mL\ blood}} \times 5000\,\mathrm{mL/min} = 250\,\mathrm{mL\,O_2/min} \]
Example 2: Deriving Pulmonary Capacities from Spirometry Data

Scenario (Assumed Values): A clinical spirometry test records the following hypothetical volumes for a patient: \(\mathrm{TV} = 480\,\mathrm{mL}\), \(\mathrm{IRV} = 2700\,\mathrm{mL}\), \(\mathrm{ERV} = 1050\,\mathrm{mL}\), and an estimated \(\mathrm{RV} = 1150\,\mathrm{mL}\). Determine the patient's (a) Inspiratory Capacity (IC), (b) Functional Residual Capacity (FRC), (c) Vital Capacity (VC), and (d) Total Lung Capacity (TLC).

Solution:

  • (a) Inspiratory Capacity: \(\mathrm{IC = TV + IRV} = 480 + 2700 = 3180\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (b) Functional Residual Capacity: \(\mathrm{FRC = ERV + RV} = 1050 + 1150 = 2200\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (c) Vital Capacity: \(\mathrm{VC = ERV + TV + IRV} = 1050 + 480 + 2700 = 4230\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (d) Total Lung Capacity: \(\mathrm{TLC = VC + RV} = 4230 + 1150 = 5380\,\mathrm{mL}\)
Example 3: Predicting Shifts in the Oxygen Dissociation Curve

Scenario (Assumed Values): During intense sprinting, skeletal muscle temperature rises from \(37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(39\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), local \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\) increases from \(40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\) to \(58\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\), and blood pH drops from \(7.40\) to \(7.22\). Explain how these changes affect haemoglobin's affinity for \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and the position of the oxygen dissociation curve.

Solution:

Increased temperature, elevated \(p\mathrm{CO_2}\), and elevated \(\mathrm{H^+}\) concentration (lower pH) all reduce haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen. Consequently, the oxygen dissociation curve shifts to the right (the Bohr effect), promoting greater unloading of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) from oxyhaemoglobin to meet the heightened metabolic demand of active muscle fibres.

At which anatomical landmark does the human trachea bifurcate into right and left primary bronchi, and which structures lack incomplete cartilaginous rings?

Practice Question 3 (Numerical-Based MCQ)

A student measures an Expiratory Capacity (EC) of \(1550\,\mathrm{mL}\) and a Vital Capacity (VC) of \(4350\,\mathrm{mL}\) in a subject whose Residual Volume (RV) is \(1150\,\mathrm{mL}\). What is the subject's Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV)?

  • (A) \(2800\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (B) \(3200\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (C) \(1650\,\mathrm{mL}\)
  • (D) \(2650\,\mathrm{mL}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Vital Capacity is \(\mathrm{VC = ERV + TV + IRV}\). Since \(\mathrm{EC = TV + ERV} = 1550\,\mathrm{mL}\), we can substitute directly: \(\mathrm{IRV = VC - EC} = 4350 - 1550 = 2800\,\mathrm{mL}\). (RV is not needed for this calculation.)

Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): Even though the partial pressure gradient for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) across the alveolar membrane is much smaller than that for \(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffuses across rapidly.

Reason (R): The solubility of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is 20–25 times higher than that of \(\mathrm{O_2}\).

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: At the alveoli, \(\Delta p\mathrm{CO_2}\) is \(45 - 40 = 5\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\), whereas \(\Delta p\mathrm{O_2}\) is \(104 - 40 = 64\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\). Because \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) has 20–25 times higher solubility than \(\mathrm{O_2}\), a far greater amount of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) diffuses per unit partial pressure difference.

  • (B) \(15\,\mathrm{mL}\)

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid

  • Mixing up axes of thoracic expansion: Diaphragm contraction expands the antero-posterior axis; external intercostal contraction expands the dorso-ventral axis.
  • Confusing \(\mathrm{O_2}\) vs. \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) delivery numbers: \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) oxygenated blood delivers 5 mL \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to tissues; \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) deoxygenated blood delivers 4 mL \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to alveoli.
  • Overestimating \(\mathrm{O_2}\) in respiratory control: Breathing rate is driven primarily by \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\) levels, not \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  • Forgetting Residual Volume in Spirometry: Simple spirometers cannot measure RV, FRC, or TLC directly because RV remains in the lungs even after forced expiration.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Trachea bifurcates at the 5th thoracic vertebra; incomplete cartilaginous rings extend down to initial bronchioles.
  • Partial pressures (\(p\mathrm{O_2} / p\mathrm{CO_2}\) in mm Hg): Atmosphere (159 / 0.3), Alveoli (104 / 40), Deoxygenated blood (40 / 45), Oxygenated blood (95 / 40), Tissues (40 / 45).
  • Transport percentages: \(\mathrm{O_2}\) (97% RBC, 3% plasma); \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (70% \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\), 20–25% carbamino-Hb, 7% plasma).
  • Medulla = respiratory rhythm centre + chemosensitive area; Pons = pneumotaxic centre.

Concise Final Recap

Human respiration integrates mechanical ventilation (negative-pressure inspiration and elastic expiration) with passive, gradient-driven gas diffusion across a sub-millimetre alveolar-capillary membrane. Haemoglobin's sigmoid binding curve optimizes \(\mathrm{O_2}\) loading in the lungs and unloading in metabolically active tissues, while carbonic anhydrase enables rapid \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) buffering as bicarbonate. Finally, medullary and pontine neural centres continuously tune ventilation to blood \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H^+}\) levels, preserving acid-base and metabolic homeostasis.
