Body Fluids and Circulation

Target Examination: NEET UG (Human Physiology)  |  Scope: Composition of Blood & Lymph, Blood Grouping & Coagulation, Circulatory Pathways, Cardiac Cycle, ECG, Double Circulation, Regulation & Disorders

Learning Objectives and Overview

Every living cell requires a continuous supply of oxygen, nutrients, and regulatory molecules, alongside uninterrupted removal of carbon dioxide and metabolic wastes. While simple aquatic animals such as sponges and coelenterates circulate surrounding water directly through their body cavities to exchange substances, complex multicellular animals rely on specialized internal transport fluids.

By mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish the cellular and non-cellular fractions of blood and recall standard physiological counts, lifespans, and protein functions.
  • Predict ABO and Rh transfusion compatibility and explain the immunological basis of erythroblastosis foetalis.
  • Trace the enzymatic cascade of blood coagulation and understand how tissue fluid (lymph) mediates exchange between capillaries and cells.
  • Compare open versus closed circulatory systems and trace the evolutionary progression of vertebrate hearts from two to four chambers.
  • Map the electrical conduction pathway of the human heart, correlate mechanical events of the cardiac cycle with heart sounds and ECG waves, and calculate cardiac output.
  • Differentiate pulmonary, systemic, hepatic portal, and coronary circulations, and explain neural, hormonal, and clinical aspects of cardiovascular regulation.

1. Blood: Fluid Matrix and Formed Elements

Blood is a specialized fluid connective tissue comprising a liquid extracellular matrix called plasma (nearly 55% of total blood volume) and cellular components collectively termed formed elements (nearly 45% of total blood volume).

Plasma and Serum

Plasma is a straw-colored, viscous fluid made up of 90–92% water and 6–8% plasma proteins. It also carries dissolved mineral ions (such as Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, HCO₃⁻, and Cl⁻) and nutrients in transit (glucose, amino acids, and lipids), along with inactive clotting factors.

  • Fibrinogen: Essential for blood coagulation (clotting) upon conversion to insoluble fibrin.
  • Globulins: Primarily mediate immune and defense mechanisms of the body.
  • Albumins: Maintain osmotic balance (colloid osmotic pressure) between blood and interstitial fluid.
Key Distinction (NEET UG High-Yield): Serum is plasma minus its clotting factors. When blood clots in a test tube, the clear straw-colored fluid left behind is serum, not plasma.

Formed Elements: Erythrocytes, Leucocytes, and Platelets

Formed elements arise primarily in the red bone marrow in adults and fall into three functional classes:

Component Normal Count (per mm³ blood) Key Structural & Functional Features
Erythrocytes (RBCs) 5.0 × 10⁶ to 5.5 × 10⁶ (healthy adult male) Biconcave, enucleated in most mammals; contain iron-bearing haemoglobin (12–16 g per 100 mL blood) for O₂ and CO₂ transport. Average lifespan: 120 days; destroyed in the spleen ("graveyard of RBCs").
Leucocytes (WBCs) 6,000 – 8,000 Colourless (lack haemoglobin), nucleated, generally short-lived; execute immune defense and phagocytosis. Divided into granulocytes and agranulocytes.
Platelets (Thrombocytes) 1,50,000 – 3,50,000 Anucleate cell fragments pinched off from bone marrow megakaryocytes; release coagulation factors. Severe reduction causes clotting disorders and excessive bleeding.

White blood cells (leucocytes) are further classified into five distinct types based on cytoplasmic granules and nuclear morphology:

  • Neutrophils (60–65% of WBCs): Most abundant; multilobed nucleus; actively phagocytic cells that engulf invading microbes.
  • Lymphocytes (20–25% of WBCs): Agranulocytes with large round nuclei; exist as B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes responsible for adaptive immune responses.
  • Monocytes (6–8% of WBCs): Largest agranulocytes with kidney-shaped nuclei; phagocytic cells that destroy foreign organisms.
  • Eosinophils (2–3% of WBCs): Granulocytes that resist parasitic infections and are associated with allergic reactions.
  • Basophils (0.5–1% of WBCs): Least abundant WBCs; secrete histamine, serotonin, and heparin, mediating inflammatory reactions.

2. Blood Grouping and Coagulation

ABO Blood Grouping

The ABO system classifies human blood based on the presence or absence of two surface antigens (A and B) on the RBC membrane and corresponding natural antibodies (anti-A and anti-B) in the plasma. Safe blood transfusion requires matching donor RBC antigens against recipient plasma antibodies to prevent agglutination (clumping and destruction of RBCs).

Blood Group Antigen on RBCs Antibody in Plasma Compatible Donor Groups
A A anti-B A, O
B B anti-A B, O
AB (Universal Recipient) A, B Nil AB, A, B, O
O (Universal Donor) Nil anti-A, anti-B O

Rh Grouping and Erythroblastosis Foetalis

Nearly 80% of humans carry another surface antigen on their RBCs called the Rh antigen (similar to that in Rhesus monkeys) and are termed Rh positive (Rh⁺); individuals lacking it are Rh negative (Rh⁻). If an Rh⁻ person is exposed to Rh⁺ blood, their immune system generates specific anti-Rh antibodies.

Clinical Scenario — Rh Incompatibility in Pregnancy: When an Rh⁻ mother carries an Rh⁺ foetus, the placenta separates maternal and foetal blood during the first pregnancy, preventing sensitization. However, during delivery of the first child, small amounts of Rh⁺ foetal blood may enter the maternal circulation, stimulating the mother to produce anti-Rh antibodies. In subsequent pregnancies with an Rh⁺ foetus, maternal anti-Rh antibodies can cross the placenta and destroy foetal RBCs, causing erythroblastosis foetalis (severe foetal anaemia, jaundice, and potential fatality). This condition is prevented by administering anti-Rh antibodies to the Rh⁻ mother immediately after the first delivery.

Coagulation of Blood (Clotting Cascade)

Blood coagulation prevents excessive blood loss following vascular injury by forming a dark reddish-brown clot composed of an insoluble fibrin meshwork trapping dead and damaged formed elements:

  1. Initiation: Trauma stimulates platelets and injured tissues to release clotting factors that trigger a cascade of linked enzymatic reactions involving inactive plasma factors.
  2. Thrombokinase Formation: The cascade generates an enzyme complex called thrombokinase.
  3. Prothrombin Activation: In the presence of calcium ions (Ca²⁺), thrombokinase converts inactive plasma prothrombin into active thrombin.
  4. Fibrin Polymerization: Thrombin converts soluble plasma fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads, forming the clot.
\[\text{Prothrombin (inactive)} \xrightarrow[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]{\text{Thrombokinase}} \text{Thrombin (active)}\] \[\text{Fibrinogen (soluble)} \xrightarrow{\text{Thrombin}} \text{Fibrin (insoluble meshwork)}\]

3. Lymph (Tissue Fluid)

As blood flows through tissue capillaries, hydrostatic filtration pushes water and small water-soluble solutes into the intercellular spaces, leaving larger plasma proteins and most formed elements inside the blood vessels. This extravascular fluid is called interstitial fluid or tissue fluid, and it shares the same mineral distribution as plasma. All exchange of respiratory gases and nutrients between blood and tissue cells occurs across this fluid.

An elaborate network of vessels—the lymphatic system—collects excess interstitial fluid (now termed lymph) and drains it back into major veins. Lymph is a colourless fluid rich in specialized lymphocytes that mediate immune responses. It also transports hormones and nutrients; notably, dietary fats are absorbed into lymph via lacteals present inside intestinal villi.

4. Circulatory Pathways and the Human Heart

Animal circulatory systems follow either an open pattern (arthropods and molluscs, where blood pumped by the heart enters open body cavities called sinuses) or a closed pattern (annelids and chordates, where blood remains confined within vessels, enabling precise regulation of flow).

Vertebrate Heart Evolution

  • Fishes (2-chambered heart): One atrium and one ventricle. The heart pumps deoxygenated blood to the gills for oxygenation, and oxygenated blood travels directly to body tissues before returning to the heart (single circulation).
  • Amphibians and most Reptiles (3-chambered heart): Two atria and one ventricle. Oxygenated blood (from gills/lungs/skin) enters the left atrium and deoxygenated blood enters the right atrium, but both mix in the single ventricle (incomplete double circulation).
  • Crocodiles, Birds, and Mammals (4-chambered heart): Two atria and two ventricles. Oxygenated and deoxygenated blood remain completely separated, establishing complete double circulation.

Anatomy and Conduction System of the Human Heart

The human heart is a mesodermally derived, muscular organ roughly the size of a clenched fist, located in the thoracic cavity between the lungs and tilted slightly to the left. It is enclosed in a double-walled membranous sac, the pericardium, containing pericardial fluid.

  • Chambers and Septa: Two smaller upper atria are separated by a thin, muscular inter-atrial septum; two larger lower ventricles (with much thicker walls than atria) are separated by a thick-walled inter-ventricular septum. A thick fibrous atrio-ventricular (AV) septum separates each atrium from its corresponding ventricle.
  • Valves (Unidirectional Flow): The right AV opening is guarded by the tricuspid valve (three muscular cusps), and the left AV opening is guarded by the bicuspid (mitral) valve. The exits of the right ventricle (into the pulmonary artery) and left ventricle (into the aorta) are guarded by semilunar valves.
  • Nodal Tissue (Autoexcitable Pacemaker System):
    • Sino-atrial node (SAN): Located in the right upper corner of the right atrium; generates the maximum action potentials (70–75 min⁻¹) and acts as the pacemaker, setting the heart's rhythm (average 72 beats min⁻¹).
    • Atrio-ventricular node (AVN): Located in the lower left corner of the right atrium near the AV septum.
    • AV Bundle (Bundle of His) & Purkinje Fibres: The AV bundle emerges from the AVN, crosses the AV septum to the top of the inter-ventricular septum, divides into right and left bundle branches, and ramifies into minute Purkinje fibres throughout the ventricular walls.

5. Cardiac Cycle, Heart Sounds, and Electrocardiogram (ECG)

Phases of the Cardiac Cycle

The sequential contraction (systole) and relaxation (diastole) of the atria and ventricles repeated cyclically is called the cardiac cycle. At a normal resting heart rate of 72 beats min⁻¹, one cardiac cycle lasts 0.8 seconds.

  1. Joint Diastole: All four chambers are relaxed. Tricuspid and bicuspid valves are open while semilunar valves are closed. Blood flows passively from the pulmonary veins and vena cava into the atria and ventricles.
  2. Atrial Systole: The SAN generates an action potential, causing simultaneous contraction of both atria. This increases blood flow into the ventricles by about 30%.
  3. Ventricular Systole: The impulse conducts via the AVN, bundle of His, and Purkinje fibres to the ventricular musculature. Ventricles contract while atria relax. Rising ventricular pressure closes the tricuspid and bicuspid valves (producing the first heart sound, "lub") and then forces the semilunar valves open, ejecting blood into the pulmonary artery and aorta.
  4. Ventricular Diastole: Ventricles relax and their internal pressure drops, causing the semilunar valves to snap shut (producing the second heart sound, "dub") to prevent backflow. As ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, the AV valves are pushed open and passive filling resumes, returning the heart to joint diastole.
\[\text{Cardiac Output} = \text{Stroke Volume} \times \text{Heart Rate}\] \[\text{Normal Resting Value} \approx 70\,\mathrm{mL} \times 72\,\mathrm{min}^{-1} \approx 5000\,\mathrm{mL}\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}\;(5\,\mathrm{L}\,\mathrm{min}^{-1})\]

Stroke volume is the volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per cardiac cycle (~70 mL). Both stroke volume and heart rate can be modulated—for instance, trained athletes achieve a substantially higher cardiac output.

Electrocardiogram (ECG)

An electrocardiograph records the electrical activity of the heart from the body surface, producing a trace called an electrocardiogram (ECG). In a standard recording, three electrical leads are attached (one to each wrist and one to the left ankle). Each deflection corresponds to a specific electrical event:

  • P-wave: Represents electrical excitation (depolarisation) of the atria, leading to atrial contraction.
  • QRS complex: Represents depolarisation of the ventricles, initiating ventricular contraction (systole begins shortly after Q). Counting QRS complexes over a known interval gives the heart rate.
  • T-wave: Represents repolarisation of the ventricles (return from excited to normal state). The end of the T-wave marks the end of ventricular systole.

6. Double Circulation and Vascular Architecture

Arteries and veins share a three-layered wall structure: an inner squamous endothelium (tunica intima), a middle layer of smooth muscle and elastic fibres (tunica media), and an outer fibrous connective tissue coat with collagen fibres (tunica externa). However, the tunica media is comparatively thin in veins, giving veins a wider lumen than arteries of similar outer diameter.

Pulmonary, Systemic, Portal, and Coronary Pathways

  • Pulmonary Circulation: Deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle enters the pulmonary artery, passes through the lungs for oxygenation, and returns via pulmonary veins to the left atrium.
  • Systemic Circulation: Oxygenated blood from the left ventricle enters the aorta, branches through arteries, arterioles, and tissue capillaries (delivering O₂ and nutrients and picking up CO₂ and wastes), and returns via venules, veins, and the vena cava to the right atrium.
  • Hepatic Portal System: A specialized vascular link between the digestive tract and the liver; the hepatic portal vein carries nutrient-rich blood from the intestine to the liver before it enters the systemic circulation.
  • Coronary System: An exclusive network of blood vessels supplying and draining the cardiac musculature itself.

7. Regulation of Cardiac Activity and Circulatory Disorders

Because the human heart generates its own electrical rhythm via nodal tissue, it is termed myogenic. Nevertheless, a neural centre in the medulla oblongata moderates cardiac function via the autonomic nervous system (ANS):

  • Sympathetic Neural Signals: Increase heart rate, strength of ventricular contraction, and consequently cardiac output.
  • Parasympathetic Neural Signals: Decrease heart rate, speed of action potential conduction, and consequently cardiac output.
  • Adrenal Medullary Hormones: Increase cardiac output during stress or exercise.

Major Disorders of the Circulatory System

Disorder Key Diagnostic / Pathological Features
Hypertension (High Blood Pressure) Normal BP is 120/80 mm Hg (120 systolic/pumping, 80 diastolic/resting). Repeated readings of 140/90 mm Hg or higher indicate hypertension, damaging the heart, brain, and kidneys.
Coronary Artery Disease (CAD / Atherosclerosis) Narrowing of coronary artery lumens due to deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol, and fibrous tissues, restricting blood supply to heart muscle.
Angina (Angina Pectoris) Acute chest pain caused by insufficient oxygen reaching the cardiac muscle due to compromised blood flow.
Heart Failure Inability of the heart to pump blood effectively enough to meet body demands; often called congestive heart failure because lung congestion is a primary symptom. Distinct from cardiac arrest (heart stops beating) and heart attack (sudden myocardial damage from inadequate blood supply).

Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Calculating Cardiac Output and Cycle Duration

Problem (Assumed Physiological Scenario): During moderate exercise, an athlete's heart rate rises from a resting value of 72 beats min⁻¹ to 120 beats min⁻¹, and the stroke volume increases from 70 mL to 95 mL. Calculate (a) the duration of one cardiac cycle during exercise, and (b) the percentage increase in cardiac output compared to rest.

Solution:

(a) Duration of one cardiac cycle at 120 beats min⁻¹:

\[T = \frac{60\,\mathrm{s}}{120} = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}\]

(b) Resting cardiac output:

\[\mathrm{CO}_{\text{rest}} = 70\,\mathrm{mL} \times 72\,\mathrm{min}^{-1} = 5040\,\mathrm{mL}\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}\]

Exercise cardiac output:

\[\mathrm{CO}_{\text{exercise}} = 95\,\mathrm{mL} \times 120\,\mathrm{min}^{-1} = 11400\,\mathrm{mL}\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}\]

Percentage increase:

\[\frac{11400 - 5040}{5040} \times 100\% = \frac{6360}{5040} \times 100\% \approx 126.2\%\]

Answer: Each cardiac cycle lasts 0.5 s, and cardiac output increases by approximately 126.2%.

Worked Example 2: Predicting Transfusion and Rh Compatibility

Problem (Assumed Clinical Scenario): A patient with blood group B⁻ requires an emergency RBC transfusion. Which of the following donor units are safe: O⁻, O⁺, B⁺, or AB⁻? Explain your reasoning.

Solution:

  • ABO Check: A group B recipient has B antigens on RBCs and anti-A antibodies in plasma. Therefore, donor RBCs must lack the A antigen. This rules out AB⁻ (carries A and B antigens) and leaves O and B groups.
  • Rh Check: An Rh⁻ recipient lacks the Rh antigen and will form anti-Rh antibodies if exposed to Rh⁺ RBCs. Therefore, Rh⁺ donor blood (O⁺ and B⁺) is unsafe.

Answer: Only O⁻ blood (among the given options) is safe for this patient.

Worked Example 3: Interpreting ECG Heart Rate

Problem (Assumed Diagnostic Scenario): A standard ECG strip recorded over a 10-second interval displays 15 complete QRS complexes. Calculate the patient's heart rate in beats min⁻¹ and determine the approximate duration of each cardiac cycle.

Solution:

Each QRS complex represents one ventricular depolarisation (one heartbeat). In 10 s, there are 15 beats:

\[\text{Heart Rate} = \frac{15\,\text{beats}}{10\,\mathrm{s}} \times 60\,\mathrm{s}\,\mathrm{min}^{-1} = 90\,\text{beats}\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}\]

Duration of one cardiac cycle:

\[T = \frac{60\,\mathrm{s}}{90} \approx 0.67\,\mathrm{s}\]

Answer: The heart rate is 90 beats min⁻¹ and each cardiac cycle lasts approximately 0.67 s.

Practice Question 1 Which of the following correctly pairs a plasma protein with its primary physiological role?

Answer: (C)

  • (A) The first foetus lacks Rh antigens on its RBCs until birth.

Practice Question 5 Which heart sound is produced by the closure of the semilunar valves at the onset of ventricular diastole?

Explanation: The T-wave represents ventricular repolarisation (return from excited to normal state). Ventricular systole begins shortly after the Q-wave and ends at the end of the T-wave.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid:
  • Plasma vs. Serum: Never confuse plasma with serum; serum lacks clotting factors (such as fibrinogen).
  • Pulmonary Vessels Exception: The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood (RV → lungs), and pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood (lungs → LA).
  • Heart Failure vs. Heart Attack vs. Cardiac Arrest: Heart failure = ineffective pumping; heart attack = sudden myocardial damage from blocked blood supply; cardiac arrest = heart stops beating.
  • Crocodile Exception: Among reptiles, crocodiles have a 4-chambered heart, not a 3-chambered heart.
Quick Revision Checklist:
  • Blood = 55% Plasma + 45% Formed Elements (RBCs: 5–5.5 million mm⁻³; WBCs: 6,000–8,000 mm⁻³; Platelets: 1.5–3.5 lakh mm⁻³).
  • Universal Donor = Group O (no A/B antigens); Universal Recipient = Group AB (no anti-A/anti-B antibodies).
  • Cardiac Cycle = 0.8 s at 72 beats min⁻¹; Stroke Volume = 70 mL; Cardiac Output ≈ 5 L min⁻¹.
  • ECG: P (atrial depolarisation) → QRS (ventricular depolarisation) → T (ventricular repolarisation).
  • Normal BP = 120/80 mm Hg; Hypertension threshold = ≥ 140/90 mm Hg.

Concise Final Recap

Blood and lymph form the dual transport media of human physiology: blood delivers oxygen, nutrients, and clotting/immune factors through a closed double-circulatory network, while lymph returns interstitial fluid, lymphocytes, and absorbed dietary fats to the venous system. Driven by the autoexcitable SAN and coordinated through the AVN, bundle of His, and Purkinje fibres, the four-chambered human heart maintains separated pulmonary and systemic loops, while autonomic and hormonal inputs continuously match cardiac output to metabolic demand.
