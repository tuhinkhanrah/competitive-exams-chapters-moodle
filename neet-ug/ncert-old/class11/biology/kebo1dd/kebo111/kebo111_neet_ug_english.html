Transport in Plants

Short-Distance Membrane Transport, Plant–Water Relations, Xylem Ascent, Transpiration, Mineral Uptake, and Phloem Translocation

Learning Objectives and Overview

Flowering plants lack a muscular heart or circulatory fluid, yet they move water, inorganic ions, organic food, and growth regulators across distances ranging from micrometres within a single cell to tens of metres in tall trees. This tutorial builds the physical and physiological framework tested in NEET UG:

  • Distinguish passive diffusion, facilitated transport (channel proteins, porins, aquaporins, uniport, symport, antiport), and ATP-driven active transport.
  • Apply the water potential equation \(\Psi_w = \Psi_s + \Psi_p\) to predict osmosis, turgor, flaccidity, and plasmolysis.
  • Trace water absorption from soil through root hairs, apoplast, symplast, and the Casparian strip into xylem conduits.
  • Evaluate root pressure, guttation, and the cohesion–tension–transpiration pull mechanism for upward xylem transport.
  • Explain stomatal opening and closing, factors governing transpiration, and the transpiration–photosynthesis trade-off in \(\mathrm{C_3}\) versus \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants.
  • Connect active mineral uptake, nutrient remobilisation, and the pressure-flow hypothesis of bidirectional phloem translocation.
Directionality of Plant Transport
  • Short-distance movement: Occurs across membranes and from cell to cell via diffusion, cytoplasmic streaming, and active transport.
  • Long-distance translocation (vascular tissues): Xylem transport of water and minerals is essentially unidirectional (roots to aerial shoots). Phloem transport of organic solutes and remobilised minerals is multidirectional / bidirectional, depending on seasonal source–sink shifts.
  • Hormones (plant growth regulators): Transported in minute quantities, often in a strictly polarised or unidirectional manner from synthesis sites to target tissues.

Cellular Means of Transport

Simple Diffusion

Diffusion is the spontaneous, passive movement of molecules or ions down a concentration gradient (from higher to lower concentration) driven by random kinetic motion. It requires no metabolic energy (ATP) and does not depend on a living membrane. Within plants, diffusion is slow—crossing a typical \(50\,\mathrm{µm}\) plant cell takes roughly \(2.5\,\mathrm{s}\)—so it suffices only for short distances, though it remains the sole mechanism for gaseous movement inside the plant body. Diffusion rates increase with steeper concentration gradients, higher membrane permeability, elevated temperature, and higher pressure.

Facilitated Diffusion

Hydrophilic substances cannot easily cross the lipid bilayer on their own. Integral membrane proteins provide specific binding sites or channels that allow such molecules to cross down an existing concentration gradient without ATP expenditure.

  • Key properties: Highly selective, displays saturation kinetics (maximum transport rate when all carrier sites are occupied), sensitive to inhibitors that react with protein side chains, and subject to hormonal regulation.
  • Porins and Aquaporins: Porins form wide channels in the outer membranes of plastids, mitochondria, and some bacteria, permitting passage of molecules up to the size of small proteins. Water channels are constructed from eight distinct types of aquaporins.
  • Coupled Passive Carriers: A uniport moves a single solute independently; a symport moves two different molecules together in the same direction; an antiport moves two molecules simultaneously in opposite directions.

Active Transport

Active transport uses energy (ATP) and specialised membrane protein pumps to move solutes uphill, against a concentration gradient (from low to high concentration). Like facilitated diffusion, active pumps are highly specific, saturable, inhibitor-sensitive, and hormonally regulated.

Plant–Water Relations: Water Potential, Osmosis, and Plasmolysis

Water forms the medium of protoplasm: a watermelon contains over 92% water, while herbaceous plants typically have only 10–15% of fresh weight as dry matter. Even apparently dry seeds retain small amounts of water necessary for respiration. Terrestrial plants absorb large volumes daily (a mature corn plant absorbs nearly \(3\,\mathrm{L/day}\); a mustard plant absorbs its own weight in about \(5\,\mathrm{hours}\)), making water a frequent limiting factor in agriculture and natural ecosystems.

Water Potential (\(\Psi_w\)) and Its Components

Water potential measures the free energy (kinetic energy) of water molecules in a system, expressed in pressure units such as pascals (\(\mathrm{Pa}\)) or megapascals (\(\mathrm{MPa}\)). By convention, pure water at standard temperature and atmospheric pressure has the maximum water potential: \(\Psi_w = 0\).

\[ \Psi_w = \Psi_s + \Psi_p \]
  • Solute Potential (\(\Psi_s\)): Also called osmotic potential, it represents the lowering of water potential caused by dissolving solutes. Because solutes reduce the concentration of free water molecules, \(\Psi_s\) is always negative. Adding more solute makes \(\Psi_s\) more negative. For an open solution at atmospheric pressure, \(\Psi_p = 0\), so \(\Psi_w = \Psi_s\).
  • Pressure Potential (\(\Psi_p\)): Applying pressure above atmospheric pressure raises water potential. Inside a turgid plant cell, osmotic water entry presses the protoplast against the rigid cell wall, generating a positive turgor pressure (\(\Psi_p > 0\)). Conversely, inside actively transpiring xylem vessels, water is under tension, producing a negative pressure potential.

Osmosis and Osmotic Pressure

Osmosis is the net diffusion of water across a differentially or selectively permeable membrane (in plant cells, the plasma membrane and the vacuolar membrane or tonoplast; the cell wall itself is freely permeable). Net water movement occurs spontaneously from higher \(\Psi_w\) to lower \(\Psi_w\) until equilibrium is reached, governed by both the concentration gradient and the pressure gradient.

Osmotic pressure (\(\Pi\)) is the external positive hydrostatic pressure that must be applied to a solution to prevent inward osmotic flow of pure water across a semipermeable membrane. Numerically, osmotic pressure equals solute potential in magnitude, but carries the opposite (positive) sign: \(\Pi = -\Psi_s\).

Plasmolysis, Flaccidity, and Turgidity

Cell behaviour depends on the osmotic concentration of the surrounding medium relative to the cell sap:

  • Hypertonic external solution (lower \(\Psi_w\) than cell): Water exits first from the cytoplasm and then from the central vacuole. The protoplast shrinks and pulls away from the cell wall—a reversible state called plasmolysis. The external hypertonic solution fills the gap between the cell wall and the shrunken protoplast.
  • Isotonic external solution (equal \(\Psi_w\)): Water moves in and out at equal rates with no net change. The cell is flaccid, and its pressure potential is zero (\(\Psi_p = 0\), so \(\Psi_w = \Psi_s\)).
  • Hypotonic external solution (higher \(\Psi_w\) than cell): Water enters the cell, building positive turgor pressure (\(\Psi_p > 0\)) against the rigid cell wall without bursting. Turgor pressure drives cell enlargement and extension growth.

Imbibition

Imbibition is a special type of diffusion in which hydrophilic solids (colloids such as dry seeds and dry wood) absorb water and swell considerably. Two prerequisites are essential: (1) a steep water potential gradient between the dry absorbent and the liquid, and (2) chemical affinity between the adsorbent and the imbibed liquid. Imbibition pressure enables germinating seedlings to break through soil crusts and was historically used to split rocks using dry wooden wedges.

Long-Distance Water Transport: Root Uptake to Xylem Ascent

Because diffusion is far too slow for metre-scale transport, vascular plants rely on mass (bulk) flow—the en masse movement of water and dissolved or suspended substances along a pressure gradient, either positive (like water pushed through a hose) or negative (like liquid pulled through a straw).

Apoplast and Symplast Pathways in the Root

Millions of thin-walled root hairs absorb water and minerals from the soil by diffusion. Water then moves radially across the root cortex toward the xylem via two distinct routes:

  • Apoplast Pathway: Movement through the continuous system of non-living adjacent cell walls and intercellular spaces without crossing any plasma membrane. It offers minimal resistance and proceeds by mass flow driven by transpiration tension, cohesion, and adhesion. However, at the endodermis, a suberised band called the Casparian strip blocks apoplastic flow, forcing water and solutes to cross a plasma membrane into the symplast.
  • Symplast Pathway: Movement through the living interconnected protoplasts linked by cytoplasmic strands called plasmodesmata. Because water enters across the plasma membrane, symplastic flow is slower and may be assisted by cytoplasmic streaming (visible, for example, in Hydrilla leaf cells). Inside the endodermis, water movement is strictly symplastic before entering the non-living xylem vessels and tracheids.
Mycorrhizal Association

Many plants form a symbiotic partnership with soil fungi called mycorrhizae. Fungal hyphae dramatically expand the absorbing surface area to supply water and mineral ions from soil volumes inaccessible to roots alone, while the host roots supply sugars and nitrogen-containing compounds to the fungus. Certain species—such as Pinus—have an obligate requirement for mycorrhizae; their seeds cannot germinate and establish without fungal partners.

Root Pressure and Guttation

Active transport of mineral ions into root vascular tissues lowers xylem water potential, drawing water in by osmosis and building a positive hydrostatic pressure known as root pressure. Root pressure can push water up short distances in herbaceous stems (demonstrated by sap oozing from a freshly cut stem) and causes guttation—the loss of liquid water droplets from specialised vein openings near leaf tips (such as grass blades) at night or early morning when evaporation is minimal. However, root pressure is too weak to lift water to the tops of tall trees; its primary physiological value is re-establishing continuous water columns in the xylem when cavitation breaks them under high tension.

Cohesion–Tension–Transpiration Pull Model

Less than \(1\%\) of the water reaching leaves is consumed in photosynthesis and growth; the vast majority evaporates through stomata. This evaporative loss generates a negative hydrostatic pressure (tension) that pulls water upward through xylem conduits at speeds reaching \(15\,\mathrm{m/h}\) and to heights exceeding \(130\,\mathrm{m}\). The continuous water column is sustained by three physical properties of water:

  • Cohesion: Mutual hydrogen-bonding attraction between adjacent water molecules.
  • Adhesion: Attraction of water molecules to the polar hydrophilic walls of tracheids and vessel elements.
  • Surface Tension: Stronger attraction of water molecules to one another in the liquid phase than to air in the gas phase.

Together, these properties confer high tensile strength (resistance to snapping under pull) and high capillarity (ability to rise in narrow-diameter tracheids and vessels).

Transpiration, Stomatal Mechanics, and Photosynthetic Trade-Off

Transpiration is the evaporative loss of water vapour primarily through leaf stomata, which simultaneously serve as ports for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) exchange. In dorsiventral (dicot) leaves, stomata are more abundant on the lower (abaxial) surface, whereas in isobilateral (monocot) leaves they are distributed nearly equally on both surfaces.

Mechanism of Stomatal Opening and Closing

Stomatal aperture changes are driven directly by changes in the turgidity of the two flanking guard cells:

  • Each guard cell has a thick, elastic inner wall facing the pore and a thin outer wall.
  • Cellulose microfibrils in guard cell walls are arranged radially (like bicycle spokes) rather than longitudinally.
  • When guard cells absorb water and become turgid, the thin outer walls bulge outward, pulling the thick inner walls into a crescent shape and opening the stoma.
  • When guard cells lose turgor (under water stress or darkness), the elastic inner walls snap back to their original straighter shape, closing the pore.

Factors and Physiological Significance

  • External factors: Temperature, light, relative humidity, and wind speed.
  • Plant factors: Number and distribution of stomata, percentage of open stomata, plant water status, and canopy structure.
  • Functions of transpiration: Generates transpiration pull for water and mineral ascent, supplies water for photosynthesis, cools leaf surfaces by \(10\text{–}15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) via evaporative cooling, and maintains cell turgidity and plant form.
  • \(\mathrm{C_4}\) vs \(\mathrm{C_3}\) compromise: Photosynthesis demands water, yet transpiration rapidly depletes it. \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants evolved to maximise \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixation while conserving water: a \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plant fixes roughly twice as much \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) as a \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plant while losing only half as much water per unit of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed.

Mineral Nutrient Uptake and Phloem Translocation

Mineral Uptake and Remobilisation

Most soil minerals cannot enter roots passively because (1) they exist as charged ions unable to cross the lipid bilayer freely, and (2) their concentration in soil is typically lower than inside root cells. Hence, epidermal cells use ATP-driven active transport (alongside some passive uptake) to pump ions inward, which also helps establish the osmotic gradient for water entry. Endodermal transport proteins act as selective control points regulating which solutes reach the xylem, and the suberin layer permits active ion transport in only one direction.

Once in the xylem, ions travel upward in the transpiration stream to growing sinks (apical and lateral meristems, young leaves, developing flowers, fruits, seeds, and storage organs). Before leaf senescence or abscission, mobile elements—notably phosphorus (\(\mathrm{P}\)), sulphur (\(\mathrm{S}\)), nitrogen (\(\mathrm{N}\)), and potassium (\(\mathrm{K}\))—are remobilised to younger tissues. Structural elements such as calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca}\)) are not remobilised. Note also that xylem sap carries much of its nitrogen in organic form (amino acids and related compounds) alongside inorganic ions, plus small amounts of organic \(\mathrm{P}\) and \(\mathrm{S}\), and limited exchange occurs between xylem and phloem.

Phloem Transport: Pressure-Flow (Mass Flow) Hypothesis

Phloem translocates food primarily as sucrose (plus water, amino acids, and hormones) from a source (photosynthetic leaf or mobilised storage organ) to a sink (growing or storage region). Because source–sink roles reverse with season (for instance, roots act as a sink in summer and as a source for sprouting buds in early spring), phloem transport is bidirectional.

  1. Phloem Loading at Source: Glucose produced by photosynthesis is converted to sucrose and actively transported into companion cells and then into living sieve tube elements.
  2. Osmotic Water Influx: High sucrose concentration creates a hypertonic condition in the sieve tube, drawing water from adjacent xylem vessels by osmosis and raising hydrostatic (turgor) pressure.
  3. Bulk Flow: Sap flows en masse through sieve plates (whose pores are traversed by continuous cytoplasmic filaments) toward regions of lower hydrostatic pressure.
  4. Phloem Unloading at Sink: Sucrose is actively transported out of the sieve tube into sink cells and metabolised or stored as starch or cellulose. Lowered solute concentration raises phloem water potential, so water exits back to the xylem.

Girdling Experiment: Removing a ring of bark down to the phloem layer on a tree trunk blocks downward sugar translocation. Over several weeks, the bark immediately above the ring swells due to accumulated photosynthates, demonstrating that phloem is the tissue responsible for downward food transport.

Worked Examples

1. Calculating Cellular Water Potential and Net Osmotic Flow

Problem (Assumed values): A root cortical cell X has a solute potential \(\Psi_s = -1.4\,\mathrm{MPa}\) and a pressure potential \(\Psi_p = +0.5\,\mathrm{MPa}\). An adjacent cell Y has \(\Psi_s = -1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}\) and \(\Psi_p = +0.1\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Determine the water potential of each cell and the direction of net water movement.

Solution:

  • For cell X: \[\Psi_w(X) = \Psi_s + \Psi_p = -1.4\,\mathrm{MPa} + 0.5\,\mathrm{MPa} = -0.9\,\mathrm{MPa}\]
  • For cell Y: \[\Psi_w(Y) = \Psi_s + \Psi_p = -1.2\,\mathrm{MPa} + 0.1\,\mathrm{MPa} = -1.1\,\mathrm{MPa}\]
  • Since \(-0.9\,\mathrm{MPa} > -1.1\,\mathrm{MPa}\), cell X has the higher water potential. Water moves passively from cell X to cell Y until their water potentials equilibrate.

2. Estimating Diffusion Time Over Macroscopic Distances

Problem (Assumed values): Suppose a sucrose molecule diffuses across a \(50\,\mathrm{µm}\) plant cell in \(2.5\,\mathrm{s}\), and diffusion time scales with the square of distance (\(t \propto d^2\)). How long would pure diffusion take to move the molecule across a stem height of \(0.5\,\mathrm{m}\)?

Solution:

  • Convert distance to micrometres: \(0.5\,\mathrm{m} = 5 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{µm}\).
  • Distance ratio: \[\frac{d_2}{d_1} = \frac{5 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{µm}}{50\,\mathrm{µm}} = 10^4\]
  • Using \(t_2 = t_1 \left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2\): \[t_2 = 2.5\,\mathrm{s} \times (10^4)^2 = 2.5 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{s} \approx 7.9\,\mathrm{years}\]
  • This confirms why long-distance transport in plants requires bulk flow through vascular tissues rather than diffusion.

3. Comparing Flaccid and Turgid States in an Osmometer

Problem (Assumed values): A flaccid plant cell with internal solute potential \(\Psi_s = -0.85\,\mathrm{MPa}\) is immersed in pure water (\(\Psi_w = 0\,\mathrm{MPa}\)) at atmospheric pressure. What will its \(\Psi_w\) and \(\Psi_p\) be at equilibrium?

Solution:

  • Initially, because the cell is flaccid, \(\Psi_p = 0\), so \(\Psi_w = -0.85\,\mathrm{MPa}\).
  • Water enters osmotically from pure water (\(\Psi_w = 0\)) into the cell until \(\Psi_w(\text{cell}) = 0\,\mathrm{MPa}\).
  • Assuming negligible dilution of cell sap (\(\Psi_s \approx -0.85\,\mathrm{MPa}\)), at equilibrium: \[0 = -0.85\,\mathrm{MPa} + \Psi_p \implies \Psi_p = +0.85\,\mathrm{MPa}\]
  • Thus, the cell becomes fully turgid with a pressure potential equal in magnitude to its solute potential.

NEET UG Practice Questions

The following practice questions are designed in NEET UG format. Attempt all questions before checking the answers and worked solutions.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following transport features is shared by BOTH facilitated diffusion and active transport, but NOT by simple diffusion?

  1. (A) Dependence on ATP hydrolysis
  2. (B) Uphill movement against a concentration gradient
  3. (C) Saturation of transport rate and sensitivity to protein-binding inhibitors
  4. (D) Movement of gases across the leaf intercellular spaces
Answer: (C)

Solution: Both facilitated diffusion and active transport rely on specific membrane proteins, so both exhibit selectivity, saturation kinetics, inhibitor sensitivity, and hormonal regulation. Only active transport requires ATP and moves solutes uphill. Simple diffusion does not use membrane proteins and does not saturate.

Practice Question 2 (Numerical MCQ): A plant cell A has \(\Psi_s = -1.6\,\mathrm{MPa}\) and \(\Psi_p = +0.4\,\mathrm{MPa}\). An adjacent cell B has \(\Psi_s = -1.1\,\mathrm{MPa}\) and \(\Psi_p = +0.2\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Which statement correctly describes water movement between A and B?

  1. (A) Water moves from A to B because A has higher \(\Psi_w\)
  2. (B) Water moves from B to A because B has higher \(\Psi_w\)
  3. (C) No net water movement occurs because both are turgid
  4. (D) Water moves from A to B because A has lower \(\Psi_s\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: \(\Psi_w(A) = -1.6 + 0.4 = -1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}\), whereas \(\Psi_w(B) = -1.1 + 0.2 = -0.9\,\mathrm{MPa}\). Because \(-0.9 > -1.2\), cell B has the higher water potential, so net water movement occurs from B to A.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ): When a living plant cell is placed in a hypertonic sucrose solution and becomes completely plasmolysed, what occupies the space between the cell wall and the shrunken plasma membrane?

  1. (A) Pure water
  2. (B) Air
  3. (C) Hypertonic sucrose solution
  4. (D) Vacuolar sap
Answer: (C)

Solution: The plant cell wall is freely permeable to water and dissolved solutes. Therefore, the external hypertonic sucrose solution passes freely through the cell wall and occupies the space between the wall and the shrunken protoplast.

Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ): Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: In the root cortex, most water moves through the apoplast pathway because cortical cells are loosely packed.
Statement II: Across the root endodermis, water movement becomes strictly symplastic due to the suberised Casparian strip.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  3. (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
Answer: (A)

Solution: Loosely packed cortical cells offer negligible resistance to apoplastic flow through cell walls and intercellular spaces. At the endodermis, the impervious suberin band of the Casparian strip blocks the apoplast, forcing all water and solutes through the plasma membrane into the symplast.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which combination of guard cell features directly causes a stoma to open when turgidity increases?

  1. (A) Thin inner wall, thick outer wall, and longitudinal cellulose microfibrils
  2. (B) Thick elastic inner wall, thin outer wall, and radial cellulose microfibrils
  3. (C) Uniformly thickened walls and longitudinal microfibrils
  4. (D) Thick outer wall and absence of cellulose microfibrils
Answer: (B)

Solution: Guard cells have a thick, elastic inner wall facing the stomatal pore and a thin outer wall, reinforced by radially oriented cellulose microfibrils. Rising turgor bulges the thin outer wall outward and pulls the inner wall into a crescent, opening the pore.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following elements is NOT readily remobilised from older, senescing leaves to younger growing regions?

  1. (A) Nitrogen
  2. (B) Phosphorus
  3. (C) Potassium
  4. (D) Calcium
Answer: (D)

Solution: Nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur, and potassium are readily remobilised from senescing organs to young meristems and developing leaves. Calcium forms structural components of cell walls and membranes and is immobile.

Practice Question 7 (Assertion–Reason MCQ):
Assertion (A): Root pressure alone cannot account for water transport to the canopy of tall trees.
Reason (R): Root pressure is a positive hydrostatic push that can lift water only to modest heights, whereas tall trees rely on negative tension generated by transpiration pull.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  3. (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
  4. (D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Answer: (A)

Solution: Root pressure provides only a small positive pressure capable of pushing water up short distances (and repairing broken xylem columns). Tall trees depend on the cohesion–tension–transpiration pull mechanism to lift water over \(100\,\mathrm{m}\).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which statement accurately compares \(\mathrm{C_4}\) and \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants with respect to the transpiration–photosynthesis compromise?

  1. (A) \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants fix half as much \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and lose twice as much water as \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants
  2. (B) \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants are twice as efficient at fixing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) while losing only half as much water per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed
  3. (C) \(\mathrm{C_4}\) and \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants lose identical amounts of water per molecule of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants close their stomata permanently during daylight to prevent transpiration
Answer: (B)

Solution: The \(\mathrm{C_4}\) photosynthetic pathway maximises \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) availability at Rubisco while minimising water loss: \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants are twice as efficient at fixing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) as \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants and lose only half as much water for the same amount of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ): According to the pressure-flow hypothesis, what directly causes water to enter phloem sieve tubes at a source leaf?

  1. (A) Active pumping of water into sieve tubes by companion cells
  2. (B) Active loading of sucrose into sieve tubes, creating a hypertonic condition
  3. (C) Breakdown of starch into cellulose inside the sieve plates
  4. (D) Negative tension generated by root pressure
Answer: (B)

Solution: Active loading of sucrose into companion cells and sieve tube elements lowers water potential (creates a hypertonic condition) inside the phloem, drawing water osmotically from the adjacent xylem and building turgor pressure for mass flow.

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ): In a girdling experiment where a ring of bark including the phloem is removed from a woody stem, which immediate result is observed after a few weeks?

  1. (A) The leaves wilt immediately because xylem is destroyed
  2. (B) The bark just below the ring swells due to upward water accumulation
  3. (C) The bark just above the ring swells due to accumulation of downward-moving organic solutes
  4. (D) Guttation increases dramatically at the shoot apex
Answer: (C)

Solution: Removing the bark up to the phloem layer interrupts downward phloem translocation while leaving the deeper xylem intact. Consequently, sugars accumulate above the girdle, causing the bark above the cut to swell.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Sign confusion in water potential: Pure water has \(\Psi_w = 0\) (the maximum). A value of \(-0.4\,\mathrm{MPa}\) is higher than \(-1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}\), so water flows from \(-0.4\,\mathrm{MPa}\) to \(-1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}\).
  • Osmotic pressure vs. osmotic potential: Both have the same numerical magnitude for a solution, but osmotic pressure (\(\Pi\)) is positive whereas solute/osmotic potential (\(\Psi_s\)) is negative.
  • Facilitated diffusion direction: Even though membrane proteins are involved, facilitated diffusion cannot move solutes from low to high concentration; a gradient must already exist.
  • Xylem contents: Xylem does not carry exclusively inorganic ions—much nitrogen travels in xylem sap as organic amino acids and related compounds, alongside small amounts of organic \(\mathrm{P}\) and \(\mathrm{S}\).
  • Microfibril orientation in guard cells: Cellulose microfibrils are radial, not longitudinal.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • \(\Psi_w = \Psi_s + \Psi_p\); flaccid cell: \(\Psi_p = 0 \implies \Psi_w = \Psi_s\).
  • Porins: outer membranes of plastids, mitochondria, some bacteria; Aquaporins: 8 types.
  • Imbibition requires: water potential gradient + affinity between adsorbent and liquid.
  • Casparian strip: suberised endodermal band blocking apoplast, forcing symplastic entry.
  • Guttation: liquid water loss via vein endings at night/early morning due to positive root pressure.
  • Remobilised minerals: \(\mathrm{N, P, S, K}\); non-remobilised structural mineral: \(\mathrm{Ca}\).
  • Phloem sap: mainly water + sucrose; loading and unloading at source/sink are active (ATP-dependent), while bulk flow between them is passive along a turgor pressure gradient.

Concise Final Recap

Plant transport integrates short-distance membrane processes (diffusion, facilitated carriers, and ATP-driven pumps) with long-distance bulk flow through vascular tissues. Water movement follows gradients in water potential (\(\Psi_w = \Psi_s + \Psi_p\)) from soil through root hairs, apoplast, and symplast across the endodermal Casparian strip into xylem vessels, where cohesion, adhesion, and transpiration pull lift sap to the canopy. Simultaneously, active loading of sucrose at sources and unloading at sinks drive bidirectional osmotic pressure flow through living phloem sieve tubes, coordinating nutrition and growth across the entire plant.
