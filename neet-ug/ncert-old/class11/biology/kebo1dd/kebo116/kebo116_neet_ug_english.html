NEET UG Biology Human Physiology

Human Digestion and Absorption

Anatomy of the Alimentary Canal, Enzymatic Hydrolysis, Nutrient Transport, and Clinical Disorders

Learning Objectives and Overview

Complex dietary biomacromolecules—carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids—cannot cross the intestinal barrier or enter cellular metabolism in their native polymeric forms. Digestion converts these macromolecules into small, water-soluble or micelle-transportable monomers through coordinated mechanical disruption and enzymatic hydrolysis. Absorption then moves these end products, along with water, vitamins, and electrolytes, across the mucosal epithelium into blood or lymph.

Core Competencies for NEET UG
  • Map the regional anatomy of the alimentary canal, dentition, sphincters, and accessory digestive glands with their ductal junctions.
  • Distinguish the four histological layers of the gut wall and their regional specializations (rugae, Brunner's glands, villi, crypts of Lieberkühn).
  • Trace substrate-specific enzymatic reactions, optimal pH values, and zymogen activation cascades across the oral cavity, stomach, and small intestine.
  • Compare passive, facilitated, active, and lymphatic (chylomicron) absorption pathways and contrast gross versus physiologic calorific values.
  • Identify the causes and distinguishing clinical features of major digestive disorders and protein-energy malnutrition (Marasmus and Kwashiorkor).

Structural Organization of the Digestive System

The human digestive apparatus comprises a continuous muscular tube—the alimentary canal (from mouth to anus)—and three sets of associated glands: salivary glands, the liver, and the pancreas.

1. Oral Cavity, Dentition, and Pharynx

Food enters the buccal (oral) cavity, where the teeth and muscular tongue perform mastication. The tongue attaches to the floor of the mouth via a fold called the frenulum and carries dorsal projections called papillae, several of which house taste buds.

Human dentition exhibits three defining traits:

  • Thecodont: Each tooth sits embedded within a bony socket of the jaw.
  • Diphyodont: Most mammals, including humans, develop two successive generations of teeth—a temporary set of milk (deciduous) teeth replaced by permanent adult teeth.
  • Heterodont: Adult dentition consists of four morphologically distinct tooth classes: incisors (I), canines (C), premolars (PM), and molars (M).
Adult Human Dental Formula (one half of upper and lower jaws): \[ \text{Dental Formula} = \frac{2\,1\,2\,3}{2\,1\,2\,3} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 2 \times (8 + 8) = 32 \text{ permanent teeth} \]

The hard outer chewing surface is composed of enamel. Posteriorly, the oral cavity opens into the pharynx, a shared passage for both food and inspired air. During swallowing, a cartilaginous flap—the epiglottis—covers the glottis (the tracheal opening) to prevent food from entering the respiratory tract.

2. Oesophagus, Stomach, and Intestines

The oesophagus is a slender muscular tube descending through the neck, thorax, and diaphragm to join a J-shaped stomach in the upper-left abdominal cavity. The gastro-oesophageal sphincter regulates entry into the stomach.

  • Stomach Regions: Divided into four anatomical zones: cardiac (receives oesophagus), fundic (upper dome), body (central main chamber), and pyloric (distal region opening into the duodenum via the pyloric sphincter).
  • Small Intestine: Comprises a C-shaped duodenum (primary site of chemical digestion), a long coiled middle jejunum, and a highly coiled distal ileum (primary sites of nutrient absorption).
  • Large Intestine: The ileum opens into the caecum through the ileo-caecal valve, which prevents faecal backflow. The caecum hosts symbiotic microorganisms and gives off a narrow vestigial projection, the vermiform appendix. The caecum continues into the colon (ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid segments), followed by the rectum and anus.

3. Histology of the Alimentary Canal Wall

From the oesophagus to the rectum, the gut wall exhibits four concentric tissue layers (listed from outermost to innermost):

  1. Serosa: The outermost visceral layer formed by thin mesothelium supported by connective tissue.
  2. Muscularis: Smooth muscle arranged as an inner circular and an outer longitudinal layer (the stomach additionally possesses an oblique muscle layer for vigorous churning).
  3. Sub-mucosa: Loose connective tissue housing blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerve plexuses. In the duodenum, it uniquely contains mucous-secreting Brunner's glands.
  4. Mucosa: The innermost epithelial lining bordering the lumen. It forms irregular gastric folds (rugae) in the stomach, finger-like projections (villi) in the small intestine, and microscopic apical extensions (microvilli) on enterocytes creating a brush border. Mucosa also forms gastric glands in the stomach, mucus-secreting goblet cells, and intestinal pits called crypts of Lieberkühn between villus bases. Each villus contains a dense capillary network and a central lymphatic vessel called a lacteal.

4. Digestive Glands and Ductal Connections

  • Salivary Glands: Three paired glands situated outside the buccal cavity—parotids (cheek), sub-maxillary/sub-mandibular (lower jaw), and sub-linguals (beneath the tongue)—secrete saliva into the mouth.
  • Liver: The largest gland in the human body (mass approximately \(1.2\text{–}1.5\text{ kg}\) in adults), located beneath the diaphragm and divided into two lobes. Its structural and functional units are hepatic lobules containing cords of hepatic cells, each lobule wrapped in a thin connective tissue sheath called Glisson's capsule. Bile from hepatic ducts is stored and concentrated in the gall bladder. The cystic duct (from gall bladder) merges with the common hepatic duct to form the common bile duct.
  • Pancreas: A compound (heterocrine) elongated gland nestled in the loop of the duodenum. Its exocrine acini secrete alkaline enzyme-rich pancreatic juice, while its endocrine islets secrete insulin and glucagon. The common bile duct and pancreatic duct unite to open into the duodenum as the common hepato-pancreatic duct, regulated by the sphincter of Oddi.

Chemical and Mechanical Digestion Across the Gut

Food mixed with saliva forms a lubricated bolus, propelled down the oesophagus by peristalsis (rhythmic muscular waves). In the stomach, food is stored for 4–5 hours and churned with acidic gastric juice into an acidic semi-fluid paste called chyme.

Gastric Gland Cell Types

The gastric mucosa contains three major secretory cell populations:

  • Mucus neck cells: Secrete mucus, which together with bicarbonates shields the stomach lining from HCl-induced excoriation.
  • Peptic (chief) cells: Secrete the inactive zymogen pepsinogen (and small amounts of gastric lipase; infants also secrete rennin for milk protein digestion).
  • Parietal (oxyntic) cells: Secrete hydrochloric acid (HCl) and intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein essential for intestinal absorption of vitamin \(\text{B}_{12}\).

Zymogen Activation and Enzymatic Summary

Inactive proenzymes protect secretory cells from autodigestion. In the stomach, \(\text{HCl}\) (\(\text{pH } 1.8\)) converts pepsinogen into active pepsin. In the duodenum, an intestinal brush-border enzyme—enterokinase—cleaves pancreatic trypsinogen into active trypsin, which subsequently activates chymotrypsinogen, procarboxypeptidases, and additional trypsinogen.

Role of Bile: Bile contains bile pigments (bilirubin and biliverdin), bile salts, cholesterol, and phospholipids, but no digestive enzymes. Nevertheless, bile salts are indispensable because they emulsify fats into microscopic droplets (increasing surface area) and activate lipases.

Regulation: Gastrointestinal motility and secretions are coordinated by both neural signals (sight, smell, or food presence stimulating salivary/gastric secretions via local and CNS pathways) and local hormones produced by the gastric and intestinal mucosa.

Absorption Mechanisms and Calorific Values

Maximum nutrient absorption occurs in the jejunum and ileum of the small intestine. Absorbed nutrients subsequently reach tissues for metabolic utilization, a process termed assimilation.

Transport Mechanisms and Regional Absorption

  • Simple Diffusion: Small amounts of glucose, amino acids, and \(\text{Cl}^-\) ions move down their concentration gradients.
  • Facilitated Transport: Glucose and amino acids cross via specific carrier proteins without direct ATP expenditure.
  • Osmosis: Water moves across the mucosa strictly along osmotic gradients.
  • Active Transport: Nutrients such as \(\text{Na}^+\), glucose, and amino acids are pumped against concentration gradients using metabolic energy.
  • Site Summary: Mouth (certain sublingual drugs); Stomach (water, simple sugars, alcohol); Small Intestine (principal site for all digested nutrients); Large Intestine (water, some minerals, and drugs).

Gross vs. Physiologic Calorific Values

Gross calorific value is the total heat released by complete combustion of \(1\text{ g}\) of food in an oxygen-filled bomb calorimeter. Physiologic value is the actual usable energy yielded by \(1\text{ g}\) of food inside the human body.

Nutrient Class Gross Calorific Value (\(\mathrm{kcal/g}\)) Physiologic Value (\(\mathrm{kcal/g}\))
Carbohydrates \(4.1\) \(4.0\)
Proteins \(5.65\) \(4.0\)
Fats \(9.45\) \(9.0\)

Disorders of the Digestive System and Malnutrition

Intestinal tract inflammation commonly arises from bacterial or viral infections, or intestinal parasites (tapeworm, roundworm, threadworm, hookworm, pinworm).

  • Jaundice: Liver dysfunction causes bile pigments to accumulate, yellowing the skin and eyes.
  • Vomiting: Forceful ejection of stomach contents through the mouth, preceded by nausea and coordinated by the vomit centre in the medulla.
  • Diarrhoea: Abnormally frequent, watery bowel movements that impair nutrient absorption.
  • Constipation: Irregular bowel movements causing retention of faeces in the colon.
  • Indigestion: Incomplete digestion producing fullness, triggered by inadequate enzyme secretion, anxiety, food poisoning, overeating, or spicy food.

Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Calculating Physiologic Energy Yield of a Meal

Problem (Assumed Meal Composition): Suppose a student consumes a hypothetical balanced meal containing \(50\text{ g}\) of digestible carbohydrates, \(25\text{ g}\) of protein, and \(15\text{ g}\) of fat. Calculate (a) the physiologic energy available to the body and (b) the gross calorific energy if the same meal were combusted in a bomb calorimeter.

Solution:

Using the standard physiologic values (\(4.0\text{ kcal/g}\) for carbohydrates, \(4.0\text{ kcal/g}\) for proteins, and \(9.0\text{ kcal/g}\) for fats):

\[ E_{\text{phys}} = (50 \times 4.0) + (25 \times 4.0) + (15 \times 9.0) = 200 + 100 + 135 = 435\text{ kcal} \]

Using the gross calorific values (\(4.1\text{ kcal/g}\) for carbohydrates, \(5.65\text{ kcal/g}\) for proteins, and \(9.45\text{ kcal/g}\) for fats):

\[ E_{\text{gross}} = (50 \times 4.1) + (25 \times 5.65) + (15 \times 9.45) = 205 + 141.25 + 141.75 = 488\text{ kcal} \]

Notice that the largest discrepancy (\(5.65 - 4.0 = 1.65\text{ kcal/g}\)) occurs in proteins because nitrogenous groups are excreted as urea rather than oxidized to \(\text{CO}_2\) and \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) in vivo.

Worked Example 2: Predicting Consequences of Selective Enzyme or Acid Deficiency

Problem: In a hypothetical clinical scenario, a patient has autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal (oxyntic) cells, resulting in negligible \(\text{HCl}\) and intrinsic factor secretion. Predict three distinct physiological consequences.

Solution:

  1. Impaired Gastric Proteolysis: Without \(\text{HCl}\), gastric \(\text{pH}\) fails to drop to \(1.8\), so inactive pepsinogen cannot be efficiently converted to active pepsin, halting protein breakdown into proteoses and peptones in the stomach.
  2. Vitamin \(\text{B}_{12}\) Deficiency: Absence of intrinsic factor prevents absorption of vitamin \(\text{B}_{12}\) in the small intestine, which over time impairs red blood cell maturation.
  3. Reduced Microbial Barrier: Lack of strongly acidic gastric juice weakens the chemical barrier against ingested pathogens.

Worked Example 3: Counting Teeth by Class in Adult Dentition

Problem: Using the human adult dental formula \(\frac{2123}{2123}\), determine the total number of premolars and molars across the entire mouth.

Solution:

The formula represents one quadrant (half of one jaw): \(2\) incisors, \(1\) canine, \(2\) premolars, and \(3\) molars. Multiplying by \(4\) quadrants (upper-left, upper-right, lower-left, lower-right):

\[ \text{Total Premolars} = 2 \times 4 = 8, \qquad \text{Total Molars} = 3 \times 4 = 12 \]

Combined premolars and molars equal \(8 + 12 = 20\) grinding teeth out of \(32\) total permanent teeth.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following correctly matches the gastric gland cell type with its primary secretory product?

  • (A) Peptic cells — Hydrochloric acid and intrinsic factor
  • (B) Oxyntic cells — Pepsinogen and mucus
  • (C) Chief cells — Pepsinogen
  • (D) Goblet cells — Enterokinase
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Peptic (chief) cells secrete the proenzyme pepsinogen. Parietal (oxyntic) cells secrete \(\text{HCl}\) and intrinsic factor, mucus neck cells secrete mucus, and intestinal mucosal cells secrete enterokinase.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If the intestinal mucosa fails to secrete enterokinase, which of the following enzymes will still be secreted in its active form or remain unaffected in its activation pathway?

  • (A) Trypsin
  • (B) Chymotrypsin
  • (C) Carboxypeptidase
  • (D) Salivary amylase
Answer: (D)
Explanation: Enterokinase activates trypsinogen into trypsin, and trypsin in turn activates chymotrypsinogen and procarboxypeptidases. Salivary amylase is secreted directly in active form in the oral cavity and does not depend on enterokinase.
Practice Question 3 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Bile juice is essential for efficient fat digestion in the duodenum.
Reason (R): Bile contains potent lipolytic enzymes that hydrolyse triglycerides directly into fatty acids and glycerol.

  • (A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true but R is false.
  • (D) A is false but R is true.
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Bile contains no digestive enzymes (so R is false). However, bile salts emulsify fats into tiny micelles and activate pancreatic/intestinal lipases, making bile essential for fat digestion (A is true).
Practice Question 4 (Match the Following)

Match Column I (Anatomical Structure) with Column II (Characteristic Feature):

Column I Column II
(a) Sphincter of Oddi (i) Connects ileum to caecum
(b) Glisson's capsule (ii) Guards common hepato-pancreatic duct
(c) Brunner's glands (iii) Sheath covering hepatic lobules
(d) Ileo-caecal valve (iv) Sub-mucosal glands in duodenum
  • (A) (a)–(ii), (b)–(iii), (c)–(iv), (d)–(i)
  • (B) (a)–(iii), (b)–(ii), (c)–(iv), (d)–(i)
  • (C) (a)–(ii), (b)–(iv), (c)–(iii), (d)–(i)
  • (D) (a)–(i), (b)–(iii), (c)–(iv), (d)–(ii)
Answer: (A)
Explanation: The sphincter of Oddi guards the opening of the common hepato-pancreatic duct into the duodenum; Glisson's capsule covers each hepatic lobule; Brunner's glands lie in the duodenal sub-mucosa; and the ileo-caecal valve prevents backflow from the caecum into the ileum.
Practice Question 5 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Long-chain fatty acids and glycerol are absorbed directly into the blood capillaries of intestinal villi.
Statement II: Chylomicrons are protein-coated fat globules transported into lacteals before entering the bloodstream.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (D)
Explanation: Because fatty acids and glycerol are water-insoluble, they cannot enter blood capillaries directly. Instead, they form micelles, enter enterocytes, are re-packaged into protein-coated chylomicrons, and enter lacteals (lymph vessels) before reaching the bloodstream.
Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A 2-year-old child weaned onto a diet rich in starchy porridge (adequate calories) but severely deficient in protein presents with extensive oedema and some retained subcutaneous fat. This clinical picture is characteristic of:

  • (A) Marasmus
  • (B) Kwashiorkor
  • (C) Jaundice
  • (D) Indigestion
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Kwashiorkor occurs in children over 1 year of age due to isolated protein deficiency with adequate calories, and is distinguished from marasmus by extensive oedema and preserved subcutaneous fat.
Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which pair of digestive enzymes acts on disaccharides in the succus entericus to yield glucose and galactose, and glucose and fructose, respectively?

  • (A) Maltase and Sucrase
  • (B) Lactase and Maltase
  • (C) Lactase and Sucrase
  • (D) Amylase and Lactase
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Lactase hydrolyses lactose into glucose + galactose, while sucrase hydrolyses sucrose into glucose + fructose. Maltase yields two molecules of glucose from maltose.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Bile vs. Enzymes: Never attribute enzymatic activity to bile; its digestive role is strictly physical emulsification (via bile salts) and lipase activation.
  • Brunner's Glands Location: Brunner's glands reside in the sub-mucosa of the duodenum, whereas crypts of Lieberkühn and gastric glands are derivatives of the mucosa.
  • Rennin vs. Renin: Rennin (double 'n') is the gastric milk-protein enzyme in infants; renin (single 'n') is a renal hormone/enzyme involved in blood pressure regulation.
  • Calorific Values: Do not confuse gross calorific values (\(4.1, 5.65, 9.45\text{ kcal/g}\)) with physiologic values (\(4.0, 4.0, 9.0\text{ kcal/g}\)).
  • Marasmus vs. Kwashiorkor: Remember that oedema and subcutaneous fat retention point to Kwashiorkor (>1 year, protein-only deficit), whereas severe emaciation without oedema points to Marasmus (<1 year, protein + calorie deficit).

Concise Final Recap

Human digestion integrates mechanical breakdown (mastication, churning, peristalsis) with pH-specific enzymatic hydrolysis across three major compartments: the oral cavity (\(\text{pH } 6.8\), starch \(\to\) maltose), the stomach (\(\text{pH } 1.8\), proteins \(\to\) proteoses/peptones), and the small intestine (\(\text{pH } 7.8\), complete breakdown to monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids, glycerol, and nitrogenous bases/sugars). Monosaccharides and amino acids enter villus capillaries via diffusion, facilitated transport, or active transport, whereas lipids assemble into micelles and chylomicrons for lymphatic transport via lacteals.
