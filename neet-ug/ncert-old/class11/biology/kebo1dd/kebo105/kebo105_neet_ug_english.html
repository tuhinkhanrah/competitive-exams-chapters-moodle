Morphology of Flowering Plants

A complete, concept-driven guide to angiosperm vegetative and reproductive structures, floral formulas, and plant families.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Plant morphology is the study of the external form and visible structural features of plants. In flowering plants (angiosperms), external structures are organized into an underground root system and an above-ground shoot system comprising stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits. Mastering morphological terminology is essential for plant classification and for solving high-yield questions in NEET UG.

Key Exam Scope
  • Distinguishing root systems, root zones, and specialized root modifications.
  • Identifying stem versus root traits and categorizing underground, aerial, and sub-aerial stem modifications.
  • Classifying leaf venation, compound leaves, phyllotaxy, and leaf modifications.
  • Analyzing inflorescence types, floral symmetry, ovary position, aestivation, and placentation.
  • Understanding fruit pericarp layers and comparing dicotyledonous and monocotyledonous seeds.
  • Interpreting floral formulas, floral diagrams, and diagnostic features of Fabaceae, Solanaceae, and Liliaceae.

1. The Root System

The root is the descending, non-green, positively geotropic organ of the plant axis that anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals, stores reserve food, and synthesizes plant growth regulators.

Types of Root Systems

Root System Origin & Structure Typical Examples
Tap Root System Direct elongation of the embryonic radicle forms a persistent primary root bearing secondary and tertiary lateral roots. Mustard (most dicots)
Fibrous Root System The primary root is short-lived and replaced by numerous thin roots originating from the base of the stem. Wheat (most monocots)
Adventitious Roots Roots arising from any plant part other than the radicle (such as stem nodes or leaves). Grass, Monstera, Banyan tree

Regions of the Root

Moving from the tip upward toward the stem, a growing root displays four distinct functional zones:

  • Root Cap: A thimble-like protective covering at the extreme apex that shields delicate meristematic cells as the root pushes through soil particles.
  • Region of Meristematic Activity: Located a few millimetres above the root cap; consists of small, thin-walled cells with dense protoplasm that divide repeatedly.
  • Region of Elongation: Lies proximal to the meristematic zone; cells undergo rapid elongation and enlargement, driving the lengthwise growth of the root.
  • Region of Maturation: Proximal to the elongation zone; cells differentiate and mature. Certain epidermal cells here form delicate, thread-like root hairs that greatly increase the surface area for water and mineral absorption.

Modifications of the Root

Roots frequently alter their structure to perform specialized physiological roles beyond water absorption:

Function Modification Type Examples
Food Storage Swollen tap roots; swollen adventitious roots (tuberous or fasciculated). Tap: Carrot, Turnip.
Adventitious: Sweet potato, Asparagus.
Mechanical Support Prop roots hanging vertically from horizontal branches; Stilt roots emerging from lower stem nodes. Prop: Banyan tree.
Stilt: Maize, Sugarcane.
Respiration Pneumatophores growing vertically upward out of waterlogged, oxygen-poor swampy soil to absorb oxygen. Rhizophora (mangrove).

2. The Stem

The stem is the ascending part of the plant axis that develops from the plumule of the germinating embryo. Unlike roots, stems bear nodes (points where leaves arise) and internodes (segments between two nodes), along with terminal or axillary buds. Young stems are typically green and photosynthetic, later becoming woody and brown.

Modifications of the Stem

Category Modification & Role Examples
Underground Storage & Perennation Fleshy underground stems store food and help plants survive unfavourable seasons. Potato (tuber), Ginger & Turmeric (rhizome), Zaminkand & Colocasia (corm).
Climbing Support Stem tendrils: slender, spirally coiled structures arising from axillary buds. Gourds (cucumber, pumpkin, watermelon), Grapevines.
Defence / Protection Thorns: woody, straight, pointed structures developed from axillary buds to deter browsing animals. Citrus, Bougainvillea.
Photosynthesis (Arid Habitat) Chlorophyll-containing stems modified into flattened or fleshy cylindrical structures to reduce water loss. Opuntia (flattened), Euphorbia (fleshy cylindrical).
Vegetative Propagation
  • Runner: underground/creeping stems spreading to new niches.
  • Stolon: slender lateral branch from main axis base that arches down to touch ground.
  • Offset: aquatic lateral branch with short internodes, rosette of leaves, and tuft of roots.
  • Sucker: basal underground branch growing horizontally then obliquely upward into leafy shoots.
 Runner: Grass, Strawberry, Oxalis.

3. The Leaf

The leaf is a lateral, flattened photosynthetic organ borne exogenously at a stem node. Leaves originate from the shoot apical meristem in an acropetal order (older leaves at the base, younger toward the apex) and always bear an axillary bud in their axil, which can develop into a branch.

Parts of a Typical Leaf

  • Leaf Base: Attaches the leaf to the stem. It may bear two small lateral leaf-like appendages called stipules. In monocots, the leaf base expands into a sheath covering the stem partially or wholly. In some leguminous plants, the leaf base swells into a pulvinus.
  • Petiole: The stalk that holds the leaf blade to sunlight and allows it to flutter in the wind, cooling the surface and bringing fresh air.
  • Lamina (Leaf Blade): The green expanded part containing a prominent central midrib, veins, and veinlets. Veins provide mechanical rigidity and transport water, minerals, and photosynthates.

Venation and Leaf Complexity

  • Reticulate Venation: Veinlets form an interconnected network; characteristic of most dicotyledons.
  • Parallel Venation: Veins run parallel to one another within the lamina; characteristic of most monocotyledons.
  • Simple Leaf: The lamina is entire, or if incised, the incisions do not reach the midrib.
  • Compound Leaf: Incisions reach the midrib, breaking the lamina into distinct leaflets. Key rule: A bud is present in the axil of the petiole in both simple and compound leaves, but never in the axil of individual leaflets.
    • Pinnately compound: Leaflets are arranged along a common central axis called the rachis (e.g., neem).
    • Palmately compound: Leaflets radiate from a common point at the tip of the petiole (e.g., silk cotton).

Phyllotaxy (Arrangement of Leaves on the Stem)

Phyllotaxy optimizes light capture and minimizes self-shading:

  • Alternate: A single leaf arises at each node in an alternating spiral (e.g., china rose, mustard, sunflower).
  • Opposite: A pair of leaves arises at each node, facing opposite each other (e.g., Calotropis, guava).
  • Whorled: More than two leaves arise at a single node, forming a ring or whorl (e.g., Alstonia).

Modifications of Leaves

  • Climbing (Leaf Tendrils): Found in pea plants.
  • Defence (Spines): Leaves modified into sharp spines in cacti to deter herbivores and curb transpiration.
  • Food Storage: Fleshy scale leaves in onion and garlic.
  • Phyllode: In Australian acacia, leaves are small and short-lived; the petiole expands, turns green, and performs photosynthesis.
  • Insectivory: Modified traps in pitcher plant and Venus-fly trap.

4. The Inflorescence and The Flower

A flower is a specialized modified shoot in which the shoot apical meristem transforms into a floral meristem, internodes fail to elongate, and the axis condenses to produce floral appendages laterally at successive nodes. The arrangement of flowers on the floral axis is called inflorescence.

  • Racemose Inflorescence: The main axis continues to grow indefinitely; flowers are borne laterally in an acropetal succession (older flowers at the base, younger toward the apex).
  • Cymose Inflorescence: The main axis terminates in a flower, limiting its growth; flowers are borne in a basipetal order (oldest flower at the apex, younger ones below).

Floral Symmetry, Merosity, and Sexuality

  • Actinomorphic (Radial Symmetry): Can be divided into two equal radial halves in any radial plane passing through the centre (e.g., mustard, Datura, chilli).
  • Zygomorphic (Bilateral Symmetry): Can be divided into two similar halves only in one particular vertical plane (e.g., pea, gulmohur, bean, Cassia).
  • Asymmetric (Irregular): Cannot be divided into two similar halves by any vertical plane (e.g., canna).
  • Bisexual vs. Unisexual: Bisexual flowers possess both androecium and gynoecium; unisexual flowers have only stamens or only carpels.
  • Bracteate vs. Ebracteate: Flowers with reduced leaf-like bracts at the pedicel base are bracteate; those without are ebracteate.
  • Merosity: Floral appendages in multiples of 3 (trimerous), 4 (tetramerous), or 5 (pentamerous).

Position of Floral Parts on Thalamus

The four whorls sit on the swollen end of the pedicel, called the thalamus or receptacle. Based on ovary position relative to calyx, corolla, and androecium:

  • Hypogynous (Superior Ovary): Gynoecium occupies the highest position; other whorls arise below it (e.g., mustard, china rose, brinjal).
  • Perigynous (Half-Inferior Ovary): Gynoecium lies in the centre while other parts sit on the rim of a cup-shaped thalamus at nearly the same level (e.g., plum, rose, peach).
  • Epigynous (Inferior Ovary): The thalamus margin grows upward, completely enclosing and fusing with the ovary; other floral parts arise above the ovary (e.g., guava, cucumber, ray florets of sunflower).

The Four Floral Whorls

  • Calyx (Sepals): Outermost protective whorl, usually green. May be gamosepalous (united) or polysepalous (free).
  • Corolla (Petals): Brightly coloured to attract pollinators; gamopetalous (united) or polypetalous (free). When calyx and corolla are indistinct (as in lily), the whorl is termed perianth (units: tepals).

Aestivation (Arrangement of Sepals/Petals in Bud)

  • Valvate: Margins just touch one another without overlapping (e.g., Calotropis).
  • Twisted: One margin of each appendage overlaps the next in a regular direction (e.g., china rose, lady's finger, cotton).
  • Imbricate: Margins overlap one another, but not in any particular direction (e.g., Cassia, gulmohur).
  • Vexillary (Papilionaceous): Five petals where the largest posterior standard overlaps two lateral wings, which in turn overlap two smallest anterior keel petals (e.g., pea, bean).

Tutorial Diagram 4: Cross-sectional patterns of floral aestivation.

  • Androecium (Stamens): Male reproductive whorl; each stamen has a filament and a typically bilobed anther (two pollen-sacs per lobe). A sterile stamen is a staminode.
    • Adhesion: Epipetalous (attached to petals, e.g., brinjal) or epiphyllous (attached to perianth, e.g., lily).
    • Cohesion: Free (polyandrous), or united into one bundle (monoadelphous, e.g., china rose), two bundles (diadelphous, e.g., pea), or more than two bundles (polyadelphous, e.g., Citrus).
    • Filament length variation: Seen in Salvia and mustard.
  • Gynoecium (Carpels): Female reproductive whorl composed of stigma, style, and ovary (with ovules attached to a cushion-like placenta). Multiple carpels may be free (apocarpous, e.g., lotus, rose) or fused (syncarpous, e.g., mustard, tomato).

5. The Fruit and The Seed

The Fruit

A fruit is a mature, ripened ovary developed after fertilisation. A fruit formed without fertilisation of the ovary is called a parthenocarpic fruit. The fruit wall (pericarp) may be dry or fleshy; when thick and fleshy, it differentiates into an outer epicarp, middle mesocarp, and inner endocarp.

  • Drupe (Mango & Coconut): Develops from a monocarpellary superior ovary and is one-seeded. In mango, the epicarp is thin, the mesocarp is fleshy and edible, and the endocarp is stony hard. In coconut, the mesocarp is fibrous.

Dicotyledonous vs. Monocotyledonous Seeds

After fertilisation, ovules mature into seeds consisting of a seed coat and an embryo (radicle, embryonal axis, and one or two cotyledons).

  • Dicotyledonous Seed: Seed coat has two layers—outer testa and inner tegmen. The hilum is the scar marking attachment to the fruit, with a small pore (micropyle) just above it. Cotyledons are often fleshy with reserve food. Seeds may be endospermic (e.g., castor) or non-endospermous (e.g., bean, gram, pea).
  • Monocotyledonous Seed: Mostly endospermic (exception: orchids are non-endospermic). In cereals such as maize, the membranous seed coat is fused with the fruit wall. The bulky endosperm is bordered externally by a proteinous aleurone layer. The small embryo has one large shield-shaped cotyledon (scutellum), a plumule enclosed in a coleoptile, and a radicle enclosed in a coleorhiza.

6. Semi-Technical Description and Important Angiosperm Families

A floral formula summarizes floral symmetry, sexuality, whorl counts, cohesion (parentheses around numbers), and adhesion (an arc or line connecting symbols above). A floral diagram plots these whorls concentrically around the gynoecium, with a dot at the top marking the mother axis.

Worked Examples

Distinguishing Underground Stems from Storage Roots

Scenario (Hypothetical): A student excavates two swollen, fleshy underground organs (Sample X and Sample Y) from a botanical garden. Sample X shows distinct horizontal rings (nodes), small scale leaves, and axillary buds. Sample Y lacks nodes, internodes, and buds, and has fine lateral rootlets.

Task: Identify which sample is a modified stem and which is a modified root, and justify based on morphological criteria.

Solution:

  1. Sample X is an underground modified stem (such as a rhizome or corm) because it possesses nodes, internodes, scale leaves, and axillary buds—defining features of a stem.
  2. Sample Y is a storage root (such as a swollen tap root or adventitious tuberous root) because roots lack nodes, internodes, leaves, and buds.

Decoding a Floral Formula and Predicting Family Traits

Scenario (Hypothetical): A botanist records the floral formula of an unknown angiosperm as:

\[\oplus\,⚥\,\mathrm{K}_{(5)}\,\overset{\frown}{\mathrm{C}_{(5)}\,\mathrm{A}_5}\,\underline{\mathrm{G}}_{(2)}\]

Task: Determine the flower's symmetry, stamen attachment, ovary position, and likely family, plus predict whether its seed is endospermic.

Solution:

  1. \(\oplus\) indicates actinomorphic (radial) symmetry; ⚥ indicates a bisexual flower.
  2. \(\mathrm{K}_{(5)}\) and \(\mathrm{C}_{(5)}\) denote 5 united sepals (gamosepalous) and 5 united petals (gamopetalous).
  3. The arc over \(\mathrm{C}_{(5)}\) and \(\mathrm{A}_5\) shows epipetalous stamens (5 free stamens attached to petals).
  4. \(\underline{\mathrm{G}}_{(2)}\) denotes a bicarpellary, syncarpous, superior ovary.
  5. This combination is diagnostic of Solanaceae, whose seeds are endospermous.

Identifying Ovary Position and Placentation from Dissection

Scenario (Hypothetical): During dissection of a flower, the thalamus forms a hollow cup around the ovary without fusing with the ovary wall, and the sepals, petals, and stamens emerge from the rim of the cup. A transverse section of the unilocular ovary reveals ovules attached along a single peripheral wall, divided into two chambers by a thin false septum.

Task: Classify the flower by thalamus insertion and identify its placentation.

Solution:

  1. Because the thalamus forms a cup with other floral parts on its rim at nearly the same level as the central gynoecium, the flower is perigynous (half-inferior ovary).
  2. Ovules developing on the inner peripheral wall of a unilocular ovary that becomes bilocular due to a false septum represent parietal placentation (seen in mustard and Argemone).

Practice Questions (NEET UG)

Practice Question 1

Which of the following plants exhibits both underground stem modification for food storage and perennation, rather than root modification?

  • (A) Sweet potato and Carrot
  • (B) Turnip and Asparagus
  • (C) Ginger and Colocasia
  • (D) Rhizophora and Banyan
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Ginger (rhizome) and Colocasia (corm) are underground modified stems that store food and serve as organs of perennation. Carrot, turnip (tap roots), and sweet potato, Asparagus (adventitious roots) are root modifications for storage.
Practice Question 2

In a compound leaf, how can you reliably distinguish a leaflet from a simple leaf?

  • (A) Leaflets always show parallel venation.
  • (B) A bud is present in the axil of the petiole of the compound leaf, but never in the axil of individual leaflets.
  • (C) Leaflets are always arranged in a whorled phyllotaxy.
  • (D) Simple leaves never have incisions on their lamina.
Answer: (B)
Explanation: An axillary bud is found in the axil of the petiole in both simple and compound leaves, whereas individual leaflets of a compound leaf lack axillary buds.

Practice Question 4

Statement II: Ray florets of sunflower and flowers of cucumber are examples of epigynous flowers.

  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.

Practice Question 5

Answer: (C)
Explanation: Axile placentation is found in china rose, tomato, and lemon. Dianthus and Primrose show free central placentation, sunflower and marigold show basal placentation, and pea shows marginal placentation.
Practice Question 6

Answer: (B)
Explanation: Coconut is a drupe with a fibrous mesocarp and a stony hard endocarp. The aleurone layer is part of a monocot seed, not the pericarp.
Practice Question 7

Practice Question 9

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Stem Tendrils vs. Leaf Tendrils: Gourds and grapevines have stem tendrils (from axillary buds); pea has leaf tendrils.
  • Thorns vs. Spines: Citrus and Bougainvillea bear thorns (modified stems); cacti bear spines (modified leaves).
  • Orchids Exception: Monocot seeds are generally endospermic, but orchids are non-endospermic. Conversely, dicot seeds are mostly non-endospermic, but castor is endospermic.
  • Apocarpous vs. Syncarpous: Lotus and rose have free carpels (apocarpous); mustard and tomato have fused carpels (syncarpous).
  • Epipetalous vs. Epitepalous: Solanaceae has epipetalous stamens; Liliaceae has epitepalous (epiphyllous) stamens.

Concise Final Recap

  • Roots develop from the radicle (tap/fibrous) or other parts (adventitious) and grow via meristematic division, elongation, and maturation.
  • Stems bear nodes, internodes, and buds, enabling modifications for storage, climbing, defence, photosynthesis, and vegetative spread.
  • Leaves show reticulate (dicot) or parallel (monocot) venation, alternate/opposite/whorled phyllotaxy, and simple/compound forms.
  • Flowers are modified shoots classified by inflorescence (racemose/cymose), symmetry, ovary insertion (hypogynous/perigynous/epigynous), aestivation, and placentation.
  • Fabaceae, Solanaceae, and Liliaceae are distinguished by symmetry, whorl fusion/adhesion, carpel number, and seed endosperm status.
