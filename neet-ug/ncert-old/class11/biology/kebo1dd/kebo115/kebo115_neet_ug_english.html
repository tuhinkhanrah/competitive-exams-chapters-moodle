NEET UG Biology • Plant Physiology

Plant Growth, Differentiation, and Hormonal Regulation

A complete conceptual and assessment guide to growth kinetics, cellular plasticity, plant growth regulators, photoperiodism, vernalisation, and seed dormancy.

Learning Objectives and Overview

In flowering plants, the transition from a single-celled zygote to a mature, reproductive organism follows a tightly regulated sequence of cell division, expansion, specialization, and programmed senescence. Both intrinsic factors (genetic programming and chemical messengers) and extrinsic environmental cues (water, oxygen, nutrients, temperature, light, and gravity) coordinate these events.

  • Distinguish true metabolic growth from reversible physical swelling and explain why plant growth is indeterminate and open.
  • Identify the anatomical zones of growth along the plant axis and apply mathematical models for arithmetic and geometric growth.
  • Differentiate between absolute growth rate (AGR) and relative growth rate (RGR).
  • Trace cellular fate across differentiation, dedifferentiation, redifferentiation, and developmental plasticity (heterophylly).
  • Compare the chemical nature, discovery, physiological roles, and agricultural uses of the five major plant growth regulators (PGRs).
  • Explain how photoperiodism, vernalisation, and seed dormancy mechanisms synchronize plant reproduction with seasonal environments.

Mechanics and Kinetics of Plant Growth

Defining Growth and Open Indeterminate Development

Biological growth is an irreversible, permanent increase in the size of an organ, a tissue, or an individual cell. It is an energy-requiring process driven by coupled anabolic and catabolic metabolism. For example, the expansion of a leaf lamina represents true growth, whereas the temporary swelling of a dry piece of wood soaked in water is merely reversible physical imbibition and does not qualify as growth.

Unlike most animals, higher plants exhibit indeterminate (unlimited) growth throughout their life cycle because they retain localized pockets of actively dividing cells called meristems. Meristematic cells divide and self-perpetuate; some daughter cells remain meristematic while others exit the cell cycle to build mature tissues. This continuous addition of new cells is known as the open form of growth.

  • Primary growth: Driven by the root apical meristem (RAM) and shoot apical meristem (SAM) (often aided by intercalary meristems), producing elongation along the longitudinal axis.
  • Secondary growth: Occurs later in dicotyledons and gymnosperms via lateral meristems—specifically the vascular cambium and cork cambium—which increase the girth (diameter) of roots and stems.

Measurable Parameters of Growth

At the cellular level, growth reflects an increase in protoplasmic content. Because protoplasm cannot be measured directly in intact organs, biologists track proportional parameters: fresh weight, dry weight, length, surface area, volume, or cell number. Different organs require different metrics:

  • Cell number: A single maize root apical meristem can generate more than \(17{,}500\) new cells per hour.
  • Cell volume/size: Developing cells in a watermelon fruit can expand up to \(3{,}50{,}000\) times their initial size.
  • Length: Used to track pollen tube extension or root elongation.
  • Surface area: Used to measure the expansion of a dorsiventral leaf.

Three Anatomical Phases of Growth

Moving proximally (away from the tip) along a growing root or shoot apex, cells pass through three sequential developmental zones:

Growth Phase Location Relative to Apex Key Cellular Characteristics
Meristematic Phase At the extreme tip (root/shoot apex) Constantly dividing cells; dense protoplasm; large, conspicuous nuclei; thin, primary cellulosic cell walls; abundant plasmodesmatal connections.
Elongation Phase Just proximal (next) to the meristematic zone Rapid cell enlargement driven by increased vacuolation; active deposition of new cell wall material.
Maturation Phase Further proximal to the elongation zone Cells attain maximal size; secondary wall thickening and protoplasmic modifications occur to form specialized permanent tissues.

Growth Rates: Arithmetic vs. Geometric

The increase in growth per unit time is the growth rate. Depending on how daughter cells behave after mitosis, growth follows either an arithmetic or a geometric pattern:

  • Arithmetic growth: Following each mitotic division, only one daughter cell retains the capacity to divide, while the other differentiates and matures. A root elongating at a steady pace exemplifies this pattern. Plotting length against time yields a straight line:
\[L_t = L_0 + rt\]
where \(L_t\) is length at time \(t\), \(L_0\) is initial length at \(t = 0\), and \(r\) is the constant elongation rate per unit time.
  • Geometric growth: Following mitosis, both daughter cells retain the ability to divide. Initially, growth is slow (lag phase), then accelerates rapidly at an exponential rate (log or exponential phase). In natural environments, nutrient and space limitations eventually slow division, leading to a plateau (stationary phase). Plotting size against time produces a characteristic sigmoid (S-shaped) curve.
\[W_1 = W_0 e^{rt}\]
where \(W_1\) is final size, \(W_0\) is initial size, \(r\) is the relative growth rate (efficiency index), \(t\) is time, and \(e\) is the base of natural logarithms.

Note that during early embryogenesis, the zygote initially undergoes geometric divisions (all cells divide), and later transitions into an arithmetic pattern as meristems establish localized division.

Absolute Growth Rate (AGR) vs. Relative Growth Rate (RGR)

Quantitative comparisons between two growing systems use two distinct metrics:

  • Absolute Growth Rate (AGR): Total growth achieved per unit time: \[\text{AGR} = \frac{\text{Final value} - \text{Initial value}}{\text{Time interval}}\]
  • Relative Growth Rate (RGR): Growth per unit time expressed on a common basis (per unit initial parameter): \[\text{RGR} = \frac{\text{Final value} - \text{Initial value}}{\text{Initial value} \times \text{Time interval}}\]

Two organs may show identical AGR, yet the smaller organ will have a substantially higher RGR because its proportional gain relative to its starting size is much larger.

Essential Conditions for Growth

Plant growth depends on adequate water (maintains cell turgidity for extension and provides the aqueous medium for enzymes), oxygen (supports aerobic respiration to release metabolic energy), and macro- and micronutrients (raw materials for protoplasm synthesis and energy metabolism). Additionally, every species requires an optimum temperature range, and external signals such as light and gravity orient and regulate specific developmental stages.

Cellular Fate: Differentiation, Dedifferentiation, Redifferentiation, and Plasticity

Every cell in a plant descends from the same zygote, yet mature tissues show striking structural and functional diversity. Three interrelated processes govern this cellular specialization:

  • Differentiation: The process by which cells derived from root apical, shoot apical, or cambial meristems mature to perform specific functions, losing the capacity to divide. Cells undergo major structural changes in both protoplasm and cell wall. For instance, to form a tracheary element (xylem vessel/tracheid), a cell loses its protoplasm and deposits a strong, elastic, lignocellulosic secondary cell wall capable of withstanding extreme negative tension during long-distance water transport.
  • Dedifferentiation: The phenomenon in which living, fully differentiated cells that had lost the ability to divide regain meristematic activity under specific conditions. Key examples include the formation of interfascicular cambium and cork cambium from mature parenchyma cells during secondary growth, or callus formation from differentiated explants in tissue culture.
  • Redifferentiation: The process by which cells produced by dedifferentiated meristems once again lose their capacity to divide and mature into specialized permanent tissues (such as secondary xylem, secondary phloem, cork/phellem, and secondary cortex/phelloderm).
Open Differentiation: Just as plant growth is open, differentiation in plants is also open. Cells arising from the exact same apical meristem adopt different mature structures depending on their final position. For example, cells displaced distally from the root apical meristem differentiate into root-cap cells, whereas cells pushed to the periphery mature into epidermis.

Development and Plasticity

Development encompasses all progressive changes an organism undergoes throughout its life cycle, from seed germination to senescence. Broadly, it is the sum of growth and differentiation:

\[\text{Development} = \text{Growth} + \text{Differentiation}\]

Plants exhibit plasticity—the ability to follow alternative developmental pathways in response to internal life stages or external environments, producing different structures. A classic example is heterophylly (occurrence of different leaf shapes on the same plant):

  • Developmental heterophylly: Juvenile leaves differ in shape from adult leaves, seen in cotton, coriander, and larkspur.
  • Environmental heterophylly: Terrestrial (aerial) leaves are broad and lobed, whereas submerged aquatic leaves are finely dissected, seen in buttercup (Ranunculus).

Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs)

Plant growth regulators (also called phytohormones or plant growth substances) are small, simple, chemically diverse molecules that act as intercellular intrinsic signals. Functionally, they fall into two broad categories:

  • Plant Growth Promoters: Mediate cell division, cell enlargement, pattern formation, tropic growth, flowering, fruiting, and seed formation. Examples: Auxins, Gibberellins, and Cytokinins.
  • Plant Growth Inhibitors: Mediate responses to biotic/abiotic stress and wounds, dormancy, and abscission. Example: Abscisic acid (ABA).
  • Dual-role gaseous PGR: Ethylene fits aspects of both groups, though it functions predominantly as a growth inhibitor.

Physiological Effects and Applications

1. Auxins

Natural auxins include IAA (indole-3-acetic acid) and IBA (indole butyric acid), synthesized mainly in growing shoot and root apices. Synthetic auxins include NAA (naphthalene acetic acid) and 2,4-D (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid).

  • Apical dominance: The growing apical bud inhibits the outgrowth of lateral (axillary) buds. Removing the shoot tip (decapitation) releases lateral buds to grow into branches—a technique exploited in tea plantations and hedge-making.
  • Rooting & propagation: Initiates adventitious rooting in stem cuttings.
  • Parthenocarpy & flowering: Induces seedless fruit development (parthenocarpy) in tomatoes; promotes flowering in pineapples.
  • Abscission control: Prevents premature drop of young leaves and fruits, but promotes the abscission of older, mature leaves and fruits.
  • Selective herbicide: 2,4-D selectively kills broad-leaved dicotyledonous weeds without harming mature monocotyledonous turf, making it valuable for maintaining weed-free lawns.
  • Cellular roles: Controls xylem differentiation and assists in cell division.

2. Gibberellins

Over 100 gibberellins (\(\text{GA}_1, \text{GA}_2, \text{GA}_3,\) etc.) are known from fungi and higher plants; gibberellic acid (\(\text{GA}_3\)) is the most extensively studied.

  • Axis & stem elongation: Increases grape stalk length; elongates apple fruits and improves their shape; spraying sugarcane increases stem length and sugar yield by up to \(20\text{ tonnes per acre}\).
  • Bolting: Promotes rapid internode elongation just prior to flowering in rosette-habit plants such as beet and cabbage.
  • Delayed senescence: Allows fruits to remain on trees longer, extending their market window.
  • Juvenility & brewing: Hastens maturity in juvenile conifers for early seed production; \(\text{GA}_3\) accelerates the malting process in the brewing industry.

3. Cytokinins

While kinetin does not occur naturally in plants, zeatin is a natural cytokinin isolated from corn kernels and coconut milk. Natural cytokinins are synthesized in regions of rapid cell division (root apices, developing shoot buds, young fruits).

  • Cytokinesis & organogenesis: Promotes cell division, new leaf production, chloroplast biogenesis in leaves, lateral shoot growth, and adventitious shoot formation.
  • Overcoming apical dominance: Acts antagonistically to auxin by stimulating axillary bud growth.
  • Richmond–Lang effect (nutrient mobilisation): Promotes nutrient mobilisation toward treated tissues, thereby delaying leaf senescence.

4. Ethylene

Synthesized in large quantities by senescing tissues and ripening fruits, ethylene is the most widely used agricultural PGR, commonly supplied as an aqueous solution of ethephon (which is absorbed, transported, and slowly releases ethylene).

  • Fruit ripening & respiratory climactic: Accelerates ripening (e.g., tomatoes, apples) accompanied by a sharp rise in respiration rate known as the respiratory climactic.
  • Triple response in dicot seedlings: Induces horizontal growth of seedlings, swelling of the axis, and apical hook formation.
  • Senescence, abscission & thinning: Promotes leaf/flower senescence and abscission; ethephon accelerates fruit/flower thinning in cotton, cherry, and walnut.
  • Breaking dormancy & elongation: Breaks seed/bud dormancy, initiates peanut seed germination and potato tuber sprouting; stimulates rapid internode/petiole elongation in deep-water rice so leaves stay above water.
  • Rooting & flowering: Enhances root growth and root hair formation (increasing absorptive surface); synchronizes flowering and fruit-set in pineapple; induces flowering in mango; promotes female flowers in cucumbers to boost yield.

5. Abscisic Acid (ABA)

ABA acts as a general inhibitor of plant growth and metabolism and is widely termed the stress hormone because it increases plant tolerance to environmental stresses.

  • Stomatal closure: Rapidly triggers stomatal closure under water stress to minimize transpirational water loss.
  • Seed development & dormancy: Promotes seed maturation and induces dormancy, enabling seeds to withstand desiccation and unfavourable conditions.
  • Antagonism to gibberellins: In most physiological contexts (especially seed germination), ABA acts as a direct antagonist to \(\text{GA}_3\).

Environmental Regulation of Flowering and Seed Dormancy

Photoperiodism

Photoperiodism is the developmental response of plants to the relative durations of light and dark periods. Based on their critical photoperiod (a species-specific threshold duration of light), plants are grouped into three classes:

  • Long-Day Plants (LDPs): Flower only when exposed to light periods exceeding their critical duration (and correspondingly short uninterrupted dark periods).
  • Short-Day Plants (SDPs): Flower only when exposed to light periods shorter than their critical duration (requiring an uninterrupted dark period longer than a critical length).
  • Day-Neutral Plants (DNPs): Show no correlation between day/night duration and flowering; they flower after completing vegetative maturity.

Crucially, the site of perception of the light/dark cycle is the leaves—not the shoot apices. Upon receiving an inductive photoperiod, leaves synthesize a hypothetical flowering hormone that migrates to the shoot apices, transforming them into flowering apices. Consequently, a completely defoliated plant cannot perceive photoperiodic cycles.

Vernalisation

Vernalisation is the qualitative or quantitative promotion of flowering by exposure to a period of low temperature. It prevents precocious reproductive development late in a growing season, ensuring the plant attains sufficient vegetative maturity before flowering.

  • Winter vs. spring cereals: Crops such as wheat, barley, and rye exist in spring and winter varieties. Spring varieties are planted in spring and flower within the same season. Winter varieties, if planted in spring, fail to flower; they are sown in autumn, germinate into small seedlings over winter (receiving cold treatment), resume growth in spring, and are harvested in mid-summer.
  • Biennials: Monocarpic plants that flower and die in their second season—such as sugarbeet, cabbage, and carrot. Subjecting a growing biennial to cold treatment stimulates a subsequent photoperiodic flowering response.

Seed Dormancy and Methods to Break It

Seed dormancy occurs when viable seeds fail to germinate even under favourable external conditions of moisture, oxygen, and temperature. It is controlled by endogenous factors within the seed:

  • Causes: (1) Hard, impermeable seed coat; (2) Chemical inhibitors such as abscisic acid (ABA), phenolic acids, and para-ascorbic acid; (3) Immature embryos.
  • Natural dormancy-breaking mechanisms: Microbial action in soil and passage through the digestive tract of animals abrade hard seed coats.
  • Artificial dormancy-breaking methods: Mechanical scarification (knives, sandpaper, vigorous shaking) to breach the seed coat; chilling treatment (stratification) or chemical application of gibberellic acid and nitrates to overcome inhibitors; and altering light or temperature regimes.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Comparing Absolute and Relative Growth Rates

Scenario (Hypothetical Values): Suppose two leaves on a plant—Leaf P with an initial area of \(4\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) and Leaf Q with an initial area of \(40\,\mathrm{cm^2}\)—are measured over \(5\text{ days}\). After \(5\text{ days}\), Leaf P reaches \(9\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) and Leaf Q reaches \(45\,\mathrm{cm^2}\). Calculate and compare their Absolute Growth Rates (AGR) and Relative Growth Rates (RGR).

Solution:

  1. Absolute Growth Rate (AGR): \[\text{AGR}_P = \frac{9 - 4}{5} = \frac{5}{5} = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm^2/day}\] \[\text{AGR}_Q = \frac{45 - 40}{5} = \frac{5}{5} = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm^2/day}\] Both leaves exhibit the exact same AGR (\(1.0\,\mathrm{cm^2/day}\)).
  2. Relative Growth Rate (RGR): \[\text{RGR}_P = \frac{9 - 4}{4 \times 5} = \frac{5}{20} = 0.25\,\mathrm{cm^2\,cm^{-2}\,day^{-1}}\text{ (or }25\%\text{ per day)}\] \[\text{RGR}_Q = \frac{45 - 40}{40 \times 5} = \frac{5}{200} = 0.025\,\mathrm{cm^2\,cm^{-2}\,day^{-1}}\text{ (or }2.5\%\text{ per day)}\]

Conclusion: Although both leaves gained the same absolute area, Leaf P has a 10-fold higher RGR because its growth relative to its initial size is much greater.

Example 2: Applying the Arithmetic Growth Equation

Scenario (Hypothetical Values): A young radicle (root) elongates arithmetically. At the start of observation (\(t = 0\)), its length is \(L_0 = 2.4\,\mathrm{cm}\). After \(6\text{ hours}\), its length reaches \(L_6 = 4.2\,\mathrm{cm}\). Determine the constant elongation rate \(r\) and predict the root length at \(t = 10\text{ hours}\).

Solution:

Using the arithmetic growth equation \(L_t = L_0 + rt\):

\[4.2 = 2.4 + r(6) \implies 6r = 1.8 \implies r = 0.3\,\mathrm{cm/h}\]

At \(t = 10\text{ hours}\):

\[L_{10} = 2.4 + (0.3)(10) = 2.4 + 3.0 = 5.4\,\mathrm{cm}\]
Example 3: Diagnosing PGR Applications in Horticulture

Scenario: A horticulturist faces four practical challenges: (i) inducing roots on woody stem cuttings, (ii) obtaining bushier tea plants, (iii) accelerating tomato fruit ripening, and (iv) elongating internodes in a rosette cabbage crop prior to flowering. Identify the appropriate PGR for each task.

Solution:

  • (i) Rooting in stem cuttings: Auxin (e.g., IBA or NAA), which initiates adventitious root formation.
  • (ii) Bushy tea plants: Decapitation (removing auxin-producing apical buds) or applying Cytokinin to overcome apical dominance and stimulate axillary shoot growth.
  • (iii) Tomato ripening: Ethylene (applied as aqueous ethephon), which triggers the respiratory climactic and hastens ripening.
  • (iv) Bolting in cabbage: Gibberellic acid (\(\text{GA}_3\)), which promotes rapid internode elongation (bolting) in rosette plants.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Cells located in the elongation zone of a growing root tip are characteristically distinguished from cells in the meristematic zone by:

  • (A) Thin cellulosic primary walls with abundant plasmodesmata and dense protoplasm
  • (B) Increased vacuolation, cell enlargement, and new cell wall deposition
  • (C) Loss of protoplasm and deposition of lignocellulosic secondary walls
  • (D) Large conspicuous nuclei and continuous mitotic division
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Cells proximal to the meristematic zone enter the phase of elongation, characterized by increased vacuolation, cell enlargement, and new cell wall deposition. Options (A) and (D) describe the meristematic phase, while (C) describes mature differentiated tracheary elements.

Practice Question 2 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully and select the correct option:

Statement I: In arithmetic growth, following mitotic cell division, both daughter cells retain the capacity to divide continuously.
Statement II: When a living organism grows in a natural environment with limited nutrients, plotting its size against time yields a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
Correct Answer: (D)

Explanation: Statement I is incorrect because in arithmetic growth, only one daughter cell continues to divide while the other differentiates and matures (both daughters dividing occurs in geometric growth). Statement II is correct: geometric growth under natural resource limits progresses through lag, exponential, and stationary phases, forming a sigmoid curve.

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

During secondary growth in a woody dicotyledonous stem, fully differentiated medullary ray parenchyma cells regain the capacity to divide and form the interfascicular cambium. This event is an example of:

  • (A) Redifferentiation
  • (B) Dedifferentiation
  • (C) Environmental heterophylly
  • (D) Open differentiation
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: When living, fully differentiated cells (such as parenchyma) regain the capacity to divide to form a secondary meristem (interfascicular cambium or cork cambium), the phenomenon is called dedifferentiation. The subsequent maturation of cells produced by that cambium is redifferentiation.

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): A completely defoliated plant fails to flower even when exposed to its inductive photoperiodic cycle.
Reason (R): The site of perception of light/dark duration in photoperiodism is the leaves, not the shoot apices.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Shoot apices modify into flowering apices, yet they cannot perceive photoperiods directly. Leaves perceive the light/dark duration and produce a transmittable hormonal signal that migrates to the shoot apex. Without leaves (defoliation), no signal is generated, so flowering fails.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following combinations of physiological effects is mediated by ethylene (or its aqueous source ethephon)?

  • (A) Bolting in beet, malting in brewing, and delay of fruit senescence
  • (B) Stomatal closure, induction of seed dormancy, and inhibition of germination
  • (C) Rapid petiole elongation in deep-water rice, respiratory climactic in fruits, and promotion of female flowers in cucumber
  • (D) Eradication of dicot weeds, parthenocarpy in tomatoes, and apical dominance
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Ethylene promotes rapid internode/petiole elongation in deep-water rice, causes the respiratory climactic during fruit ripening, and increases female flowers in cucumbers. Option (A) lists gibberellin effects, (B) lists ABA effects, and (D) lists auxin effects.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If a farmer sows a winter variety of wheat in the spring season instead of autumn, what is the most likely outcome?

  • (A) It will flower earlier than the spring variety due to longer days
  • (B) It will fail to flower or produce mature grain within that flowering season
  • (C) It will undergo immediate bolting without vegetative growth
  • (D) It will behave as a day-neutral plant and yield double grain
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Winter varieties of wheat, barley, and rye require vernalisation (exposure to low winter temperatures) to induce flowering later in life. If planted in spring, they miss the cold treatment and fail to flower or set mature grain within that growing season.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following treatments is least likely to break endogenous seed dormancy caused by chemical inhibitors or a hard seed coat?

  • (A) Mechanical scarification using sandpaper
  • (B) Application of gibberellic acid (\(\text{GA}_3\)) or nitrates
  • (C) Subjecting seeds to chilling conditions
  • (D) Spraying seeds with abscisic acid (ABA)
Correct Answer: (D)

Explanation: Abscisic acid (ABA) is an endogenous chemical inhibitor that induces and maintains seed dormancy and inhibits germination. Mechanical scarification breaches hard seed coats, while chilling and \(\text{GA}_3\) / nitrates overcome inhibitory substances to break dormancy.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Kinetin vs. Zeatin: Kinetin is a synthetic cytokinin (isolated from autoclaved herring sperm DNA) and does not occur naturally in plants; Zeatin is a natural cytokinin.
  • Auxin on young vs. mature organs: Auxin prevents abscission of young leaves and fruits, but promotes abscission of older, mature leaves and fruits.
  • 2,4-D selectivity: 2,4-D kills dicotyledonous weeds and spares mature monocotyledonous plants (grasses).
  • Photoperiodism perception: Leaves—not shoot apices—perceive photoperiods; both light duration and dark duration are critical.
  • Imbibition vs. Growth: Swelling of dry wood in water is reversible physical imbibition, not metabolic growth.

High-Yield Revision Checklist

  • Arithmetic growth formula \(L_t = L_0 + rt\) (linear); Geometric growth formula \(W_1 = W_0 e^{rt}\) (sigmoid curve).
  • Meristematic → Elongation → Maturation phases along the root/shoot axis.
  • Differentiation (tracheary element), Dedifferentiation (interfascicular & cork cambium), Redifferentiation (secondary xylem/phloem/cork).
  • Plasticity examples: Cotton, coriander, larkspur (developmental heterophylly); Buttercup (environmental heterophylly).
  • Auxins (apical dominance, rooting, parthenocarpy); Gibberellins (bolting, sugarcane +20 tonnes/acre, malting); Cytokinins (overcome apical dominance, delay senescence).
  • Ethylene (respiratory climactic, deep-water rice elongation, cucumber female flowers, ethephon); ABA (stress hormone, stomatal closure, GA antagonist).
  • Vernalisation in winter cereals (wheat, barley, rye) and biennials (sugarbeet, cabbage, carrot).

Concise Final Recap

Plant development integrates open, indeterminate meristematic growth with position-dependent cellular differentiation, dedifferentiation, and plasticity. Quantitative growth follows either linear arithmetic kinetics or nutrient-limited sigmoid geometric kinetics. Internally, five major PGR classes—auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, ethylene, and abscisic acid—act synergistically or antagonistically to coordinate every developmental milestone. Externally, day-length perception by leaves (photoperiodism), low-temperature exposure (vernalisation), and seed dormancy controls ensure that germination, vegetative expansion, and flowering occur in harmony with seasonal conditions.
