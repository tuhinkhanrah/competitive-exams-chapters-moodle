Locomotion and Movement

A complete, standalone tutorial covering cellular movements, muscle ultrastructure, contraction physiology, skeletal anatomy, joints, and musculoskeletal disorders.

Learning Objectives and Conceptual Foundation

By mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between general cellular movements and whole-organism locomotion.
  • Classify human muscle tissues by location, microscopic appearance, and neural regulation.
  • Explain the molecular architecture of a sarcomere, including thin (actin) and thick (myosin) filaments.
  • Trace the neuromuscular excitation–contraction coupling pathway and the sliding filament cross-bridge cycle.
  • Account for all 206 bones of the human axial and appendicular skeletons and classify body joints structurally.
  • Identify the physiological basis of major muscular and skeletal disorders tested in NEET UG.
Core Principle: Movement vs. Locomotion Movement is a fundamental property of living cells and organisms, ranging from cytoplasmic streaming in unicellular protozoans to blinking eyelids or chewing food in humans. Locomotion is a specific subset of voluntary movements that results in a change of place or position (such as walking, running, swimming, or flying). Organisms perform locomotion to find food, shelter, mates, breeding grounds, or favourable climates, and to escape predators. Because the same structures can serve both functions—for instance, cilia in Paramoecium sweep food into the cytopharynx and propel the cell, while tentacles in Hydra capture prey and assist in movement—we summarize the relationship as: all locomotions are movements, but all movements are not locomotions.

Types of Movement in Human Cells

Effective locomotion in humans requires seamless coordination among three organ systems: the muscular system (force generation), the skeletal system (rigid levers and fulcrums), and the neural system (signal initiation and timing). At the cellular level, human cells exhibit three primary modes of movement, alongside flagellar propulsion in male gametes.

Movement Type Structural Mechanism Human Examples
Amoeboid Formation of pseudopodia via protoplasmic streaming and cytoskeletal microfilaments. Macrophages and leucocytes (white blood cells) migrating through tissues.
Ciliary Coordinated beating of hair-like membrane outgrowths (cilia) lining internal tubular organs. Clearing inhaled dust and foreign particles in the trachea; propelling ova along the female reproductive tract.
Flagellar Whip-like undulation of a flagellum (cell membrane outgrowth). Swimming of spermatozoa (also maintains water currents in sponges and propels Euglena).
Muscular Active shortening of specialized contractile cells containing actin and myosin filaments. Movement of limbs, jaws, tongue, and posture maintenance.

Classification and Structure of Muscle Tissue

Muscle is a specialized tissue of mesodermal origin that accounts for approximately 40–50% of an adult human's body weight. Muscles possess four defining physiological properties: excitability (responsiveness to stimuli), contractility (ability to shorten forcefully), extensibility (ability to be stretched), and elasticity (ability to recoil to resting length).

Feature Skeletal Muscle Visceral (Smooth) Muscle Cardiac Muscle
Location Attached to skeletal bones Inner walls of hollow visceral organs (alimentary canal, reproductive tract) Wall of the heart
Microscopic Appearance Striated (alternating light and dark bands) Non-striated (smooth appearance) Striated, with cells arranged in a branching pattern
Neural Control Voluntary (under conscious nervous control) Involuntary (not under conscious control) Involuntary (not directly controlled by CNS)
Primary Role Locomotion and posture changes Transport of food through gut and gametes through genital tract Rhythmic pumping of blood

Hierarchical Organisation of Skeletal Muscle

An intact skeletal muscle consists of numerous cylindrical muscle bundles (fascicles) bound together by a common collagenous connective tissue sheath called fascia. Each fascicle houses many elongated muscle fibres (muscle cells).

  • Sarcolemma: The plasma membrane enclosing a muscle fibre.
  • Sarcoplasm: The cytoplasm of a muscle fibre; it is a syncytium because it contains multiple nuclei.
  • Sarcoplasmic Reticulum (SR): The specialized endoplasmic reticulum of muscle fibres that serves as the primary intracellular storehouse of calcium ions (\(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)).
  • Myofibrils (Myofilaments): Numerous parallel protein rods running longitudinally within the sarcoplasm, responsible for the striated banding pattern.

Sarcomere Architecture

Under polarized light, each myofibril displays alternating light and dark bands formed by the precise overlap of two contractile proteins: thin actin filaments and thick myosin filaments.

  • I-band (Isotropic / Light band): Contains only thin actin filaments. An elastic fibre called the Z-line runs right through the centre of each I-band, anchoring the thin filaments.
  • A-band (Anisotropic / Dark band): Contains the full length of thick myosin filaments, along with overlapping ends of thin actin filaments. Thick filaments are held together at the centre of the A-band by a thin fibrous membrane called the M-line.
  • H-zone: The central region of the A-band in a resting sarcomere where thin filaments do not overlap the thick filaments.
  • Sarcomere: The segment of a myofibril between two successive Z-lines. It is the functional unit of contraction.

Molecular Structure of Contractile Proteins

Muscle contraction depends on the interaction between two polymerized protein assemblies:

  • Thin (Actin) Filament: Composed of two F-actin (filamentous) strands helically wound around each other. Each F-actin is a polymer of monomeric G-actin (globular) subunits. Two filamentous strands of tropomyosin run lengthwise alongside the F-actin helix. A trimeric regulatory protein complex called troponin sits at regular intervals on tropomyosin; in the resting state, a subunit of troponin masks the myosin-binding active sites on actin.
  • Thick (Myosin) Filament: A polymer of monomeric proteins called meromyosins. Each meromyosin has two functional regions:
    • Heavy Meromyosin (HMM): Consists of a globular head and a short arm that project outward from the filament surface at regular distances and angles, forming the cross arm. The globular head functions as an active ATPase enzyme and carries specific binding sites for both ATP and actin.
    • Light Meromyosin (LMM): Forms the elongated tail that bundles with other LMM tails to build the backbone of the thick filament.

Mechanism of Muscle Contraction and Fibre Types

Muscle contraction is explained by the Sliding Filament Theory, which states that a muscle fibre contracts when thin actin filaments slide over thick myosin filaments.

Excitation–Contraction Coupling

  1. Neural Signal: The central nervous system (CNS) sends an impulse along a motor neuron. A motor neuron together with all the muscle fibres it innervates forms a motor unit.
  2. Neuromuscular Junction: The synapse between the motor neuron and the sarcolemma is called the neuromuscular junction (motor-end plate). Arrival of a neural signal triggers the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine.
  3. Action Potential & \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) Release: Acetylcholine generates an action potential in the sarcolemma that spreads across the fibre, stimulating the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) ions into the sarcoplasm.
  4. Unmasking of Active Sites: Elevated sarcoplasmic \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) binds to a subunit of troponin on the thin filaments, causing a conformational shift that removes the masking of myosin-binding sites on actin.

The Cross-Bridge Cycle

Once actin's active sites are exposed, the myosin head drives filament sliding through a repeating ATP-dependent cycle:

  • Cross-Bridge Formation: Utilizing energy from ATP hydrolysis (into \(\mathrm{ADP} + \mathrm{P_i}\)), the energized myosin head binds to the exposed active site on actin, forming a cross bridge.
  • Sliding (Power Stroke): The myosin head rotates, pulling the attached actin filaments toward the centre of the A-band. Because actin is anchored to Z-lines, the Z-lines are pulled inward, shortening the sarcomere.
  • Cross-Bridge Detachment: Myosin releases \(\mathrm{ADP}\) and \(\mathrm{P_i}\) and returns to its relaxed orientation. Binding of a new ATP molecule is required to break the cross bridge between myosin and actin.
  • Relaxation: The cycle repeats as long as \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) remains elevated. When neural stimulation stops, \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic cisternae, troponin re-masks the actin sites, and the Z-lines return to their resting positions.

Muscle Fatigue, Red Fibres, and White Fibres

Repeated, intense activation of skeletal muscles depletes oxygen, forcing anaerobic breakdown of glycogen. This produces lactic acid accumulation, leading to muscle fatigue. Skeletal muscle fibres are categorized into two physiological types based on their content of myoglobin (a red oxygen-storing pigment):

Characteristic Red Muscle Fibres (Aerobic) White Muscle Fibres (Anaerobic)
Myoglobin Content High (reddish appearance) Very low (pale or whitish appearance)
Mitochondria Abundant (utilizes stored \(\mathrm{O_2}\) for ATP synthesis) Few
Sarcoplasmic Reticulum Moderate High
Primary Energy Pathway Aerobic respiration (sustained contractions) Anaerobic glycolysis (rapid, short bursts)

The Human Skeletal System

The skeletal system provides structural support, organ protection, and rigid levers for muscle action. It consists of bone (specialized connective tissue with a very hard matrix enriched in calcium salts) and cartilage (specialized connective tissue with a slightly pliable matrix due to chondroitin salts). An adult human skeleton comprises 206 bones organized into two principal divisions:

\[\text{Total Human Bones (206)} = \text{Axial Skeleton (80)} + \text{Appendicular Skeleton (126)}\]

1. Axial Skeleton (80 Bones)

Distributed along the central longitudinal axis of the body, the axial skeleton includes the skull, vertebral column, sternum, and ribs.

  • Skull (22 Cranial & Facial Bones; 29 Total in Skull Region):
    • Cranium (8 bones): Forms the hard protective braincase.
    • Facial region (14 bones): Forms the anterior framework of the skull.
    • Associated bones: A single U-shaped hyoid bone at the base of the buccal cavity, plus 6 ear ossicles (3 in each middle ear: Malleus, Incus, and Stapes).
    • Articulation: The skull is dicondylic, articulating with the first vertebra (atlas) via two occipital condyles.
  • Vertebral Column (26 Serially Arranged Units): Dorsally placed from the skull base down the trunk, each vertebra encloses a central hollow neural canal through which the spinal cord passes. From superior to inferior, the regions are: Cervical (7) (nearly all mammals, including humans, have 7 cervical vertebrae), Thoracic (12), Lumbar (5), Sacral (1 fused), and Coccygeal (1 fused).
  • Sternum (1 Bone): A flat bone positioned along the ventral midline of the thorax.
  • Ribs (12 Pairs = 24 Bones): Thin, flat, bicephalic bones (having two articulation surfaces on their dorsal end for attachment to thoracic vertebrae). Based on ventral attachment:
    • True ribs (Pairs 1–7): Attach dorsally to thoracic vertebrae and ventrally directly to the sternum via hyaline cartilage.
    • Vertebrochondral / False ribs (Pairs 8–10): Do not reach the sternum directly; instead, they join the 7th rib via hyaline cartilage.
    • Floating ribs (Pairs 11–12): Not connected ventrally at all.
    Together, the thoracic vertebrae, ribs, and sternum form the protective rib cage.

2. Appendicular Skeleton (126 Bones)

The appendicular skeleton consists of the bones of the four limbs (30 bones per limb = 120 bones) and the two girdles (6 bones) that articulate the limbs with the axial skeleton.

Division / Region Component Bones Count
Forelimb (each = 30; total = 60) Humerus (1), Radius (1), Ulna (1), Carpals/wrist (8), Metacarpals/palm (5), Phalanges/digits (14) 60
Hindlimb (each = 30; total = 60) Femur/thigh — longest bone (1), Tibia (1), Fibula (1), Patella/knee cap (1), Tarsals/ankle (7), Metatarsals (5), Phalanges/digits (14) 60
Pectoral Girdle (2 halves) Each half: 1 Clavicle (slender collar bone with two curvatures) + 1 Scapula (large triangular flat dorsal bone spanning ribs 2–7; has a dorsal spine projecting as the acromion for clavicle articulation, and a glenoid cavity below it for the humerus head) 4
Pelvic Girdle (2 halves) 2 Coxal bones; each formed by fusion of ilium, ischium, and pubis. At their junction lies the acetabulum (articulates with femur head). Ventrally, the two halves meet at the pubic symphysis (fibrous cartilage) 2

Joints and Musculoskeletal Disorders

Joints are points of contact between bones, or between bones and cartilages. Muscle-generated force acts through joints, where the joint serves as a mechanical fulcrum. Structurally, joints fall into three major classes:

Joint Class Structural Feature & Mobility Key Examples
Fibrous Joints Bones fused end-to-end by dense fibrous connective tissue; immovable. Sutures between flat cranial bones of the skull.
Cartilaginous Joints Bones joined by cartilage; permits limited movement. Joints between adjacent vertebrae (intervertebral discs); pubic symphysis.
Synovial Joints Fluid-filled synovial cavity between articulating surfaces; allows considerable movement. • Ball & socket: Humerus & glenoid cavity; Femur & acetabulum.
• Hinge: Knee joint; elbow joint.
• Pivot: Between atlas and axis vertebrae.
• Gliding: Between carpals.
• Saddle: Between carpal and metacarpal of the thumb.

Disorders of the Muscular and Skeletal Systems

Disorder Primary Cause / Mechanism Key Clinical Manifestation
Myasthenia gravis Autoimmune disorder targeting the neuromuscular junction. Progressive fatigue, weakening, and paralysis of skeletal muscles.
Muscular dystrophy Mostly genetic disorder. Progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle tissue.
Tetany Low \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) concentration in body fluids. Rapid spasms (wild contractions) in muscles.
Arthritis Inflammatory joint condition. Painful inflammation of joints.
Osteoporosis Age-related disorder; decreased estrogen levels are a common cause. Decreased bone mass and increased risk of fractures.
Gout Accumulation of uric acid crystals in joint spaces. Inflammation and severe pain in joints.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Analyzing Sarcomere Band Changes During Contraction

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumed Values): Suppose a resting skeletal muscle sarcomere has a total length of \(2.6\,\mathrm{µm}\), an A-band width of \(1.6\,\mathrm{µm}\), and a central H-zone of \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\). During an isotonic contraction, the sarcomere shortens by \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\). Calculate the new A-band width, the new H-zone width, and the total change in I-band width within one sarcomere.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. A-band width: The A-band corresponds to the length of the thick myosin filaments. Because filaments slide without changing length, the A-band remains constant at \(1.6\,\mathrm{µm}\).
  2. Sarcomere shortening: The sarcomere shortens from \(2.6\,\mathrm{µm}\) to \(2.6 - 0.4 = 2.2\,\mathrm{µm}\). Each Z-line moves inward by \(0.2\,\mathrm{µm}\) toward the M-line.
  3. H-zone width: Because each side's thin filaments advance inward by \(0.2\,\mathrm{µm}\), the central non-overlapped H-zone shrinks from both ends by \(0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\). Thus, the new H-zone width is \(0.4 - 0.4 = 0\,\mathrm{µm}\) (it disappears at this contraction).
  4. I-band width: In one sarcomere, the total I-band contribution (two half I-bands) equals \(\text{Sarcomere length} - \text{A-band length}\). Initially it was \(2.6 - 1.6 = 1.0\,\mathrm{µm}\); after contraction it is \(2.2 - 1.6 = 0.6\,\mathrm{µm}\), a reduction of \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\).

Example 2: Auditing Bone Counts in the Appendicular Skeleton

Scenario: A medical student is assembling a complete human appendicular skeleton model and needs to verify the count of distal limb bones (wrist, palm, ankle, sole, and digits) versus long/girdle bones.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. One Forelimb (30 bones): Humerus (1) + Radius (1) + Ulna (1) + Carpals (8) + Metacarpals (5) + Phalanges (14) \(= 30\). For both forelimbs: \(2 \times 30 = 60\) bones.
  2. One Hindlimb (30 bones): Femur (1) + Tibia (1) + Fibula (1) + Patella (1) + Tarsals (7) + Metatarsals (5) + Phalanges (14) \(= 30\). Notice that the hindlimb has 7 tarsals (one fewer than the 8 carpals) because the patella accounts for the extra bone, keeping the limb total at 30. For both hindlimbs: \(2 \times 30 = 60\) bones.
  3. Girdles (6 bones): Pectoral girdle has 2 clavicles + 2 scapulae \(= 4\); Pelvic girdle has 2 coxal bones \(= 2\).
  4. Total Appendicular Count: \(60 + 60 + 4 + 2 = 126\) bones.

Example 3: Predicting Effects of ATP Depletion on the Cross-Bridge Cycle

Scenario: Consider a muscle fibre in which \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) ions are still bound to troponin, and myosin heads are firmly attached to actin filaments after completing their power stroke, but intracellular ATP is suddenly exhausted.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. In the cross-bridge cycle, myosin heads detach from actin only when a new ATP molecule binds to the ATP-binding site on the HMM globular head.
  2. Without ATP, the cross bridges cannot break, locking the actin and myosin filaments together in a rigid state (the physiological basis of rigor mortis).
  3. Furthermore, active pumping of \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum requires ATP, so relaxation cannot occur.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All items below are original NEET UG-aligned practice questions designed for self-assessment.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements accurately describes the changes in a sarcomere during maximal skeletal muscle contraction?

  • (A) Both the A-band and the I-band shorten equally.
  • (B) The A-band retains its length, while the I-band and H-zone decrease.
  • (C) The H-zone widens as thin filaments slide away from the M-line.
  • (D) The Z-lines move farther apart, increasing the distance between thick filaments.
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: According to the sliding filament theory, thin actin filaments slide inward over thick myosin filaments toward the M-line. Because the length of thick filaments does not change, the A-band remains constant. Meanwhile, the I-band shortens and the central H-zone narrows or disappears.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the resting state of a skeletal muscle fibre, the active binding sites for myosin on the actin filament are masked by:

  • (A) Light meromyosin (LMM)
  • (B) A subunit of troponin
  • (C) G-actin monomers
  • (D) The M-line fibrous membrane
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Troponin is a regulatory protein distributed at regular intervals on tropomyosin. In the resting state, a subunit of troponin masks the myosin-binding sites on F-actin. Binding of \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) to troponin removes this masking.

Practice Question 3 (Match-the-Following MCQ)

Match the joints in Column I with their correct anatomical locations in Column II:

Column I (Joint Type) Column II (Location)
(a) Pivot joint (i) Between carpal and metacarpal of thumb
(b) Saddle joint (ii) Between flat cranial bones
(c) Fibrous joint (iii) Between atlas and axis
(d) Cartilaginous joint (iv) Between adjacent vertebrae
  • (A) (a)–(iii), (b)–(i), (c)–(ii), (d)–(iv)
  • (B) (a)–(i), (b)–(iii), (c)–(iv), (d)–(ii)
  • (C) (a)–(iii), (b)–(iv), (c)–(ii), (d)–(i)
  • (D) (a)–(ii), (b)–(i), (c)–(iii), (d)–(iv)
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Atlas–axis forms a pivot joint (a–iii); the thumb's carpometacarpal joint is a saddle joint (b–i); cranial sutures are immovable fibrous joints (c–ii); and intervertebral discs form cartilaginous joints (d–iv).

Practice Question 4 (Statement I & II MCQ)

Read the two statements carefully and select the correct option:

Statement I: The 8th, 9th, and 10th pairs of ribs are called vertebrochondral ribs because they attach directly to the sternum via hyaline cartilage.
Statement II: The 11th and 12th pairs of ribs are called floating ribs because they lack any ventral attachment.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Statement I is incorrect because the 8th, 9th, and 10th pairs (false/vertebrochondral ribs) do not articulate directly with the sternum; instead, they join the 7th rib via hyaline cartilage. Statement II is correct.

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): White muscle fibres fatigue more rapidly than red muscle fibres during prolonged exercise.
Reason (R): White muscle fibres possess high myoglobin content and abundant mitochondria, relying primarily on aerobic respiration.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) Both (A) and (R) are false.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: White muscle fibres rely on anaerobic glycolysis and accumulate lactic acid quickly, so (A) is true. However, (R) describes red muscle fibres, not white fibres (white fibres have very little myoglobin and few mitochondria). Thus, (R) is false.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which anatomical structure of the pectoral girdle articulates directly with the head of the humerus to form the shoulder joint?

  • (A) Acromion process
  • (B) Acetabulum
  • (C) Glenoid cavity
  • (D) Pubic symphysis
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Below the acromion process of the scapula lies a depression called the glenoid cavity, which receives the head of the humerus. (The acromion articulates with the clavicle; the acetabulum is in the pelvic girdle for the femur.)

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A patient presents with severe joint inflammation caused by the deposition of uric acid crystals, while another patient experiences rapid, uncontrolled muscle spasms due to hypocalcaemia (low \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) in body fluids). These two conditions are respectively:

  • (A) Osteoporosis and Myasthenia gravis
  • (B) Gout and Tetany
  • (C) Arthritis and Muscular dystrophy
  • (D) Gout and Muscular dystrophy
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Accumulation of uric acid crystals in joints causes gout, whereas low \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) in body fluids triggers rapid, wild muscle contractions known as tetany.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following is NOT a function of the globular head of heavy meromyosin (HMM)?

  • (A) Acting as an active ATPase enzyme
  • (B) Providing a binding site for ATP
  • (C) Providing an active site for binding actin
  • (D) Storing \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) ions for excitation–contraction coupling
Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: The globular head of HMM is an active ATPase with binding sites for both ATP and actin. Calcium ions are stored in the sarcoplasmic reticulum, not in the myosin head.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Carpals vs. Tarsals: The wrist has 8 carpals, whereas the ankle has 7 tarsals (the 30th bone in the hindlimb is the patella).
  • False Ribs Attachment: Rib pairs 8, 9, and 10 attach to the 7th rib's hyaline cartilage, not directly to the sternum.
  • Myasthenia gravis vs. Muscular dystrophy: Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction; muscular dystrophy is a genetic degeneration of skeletal muscle.
  • Role of ATP in Contraction: ATP hydrolysis energizes the myosin head, and binding of a new ATP molecule is essential to break the cross bridge.
  • Osteoporosis Cause: Decreased estrogen levels (especially post-menopause) are a common cause of age-related osteoporosis.

Concise Final Recap

  • Human cells show amoeboid, ciliary, flagellar, and muscular movements; coordinated muscular, skeletal, and neural activity drives locomotion.
  • Skeletal muscles are striated and voluntary; visceral muscles are smooth and involuntary; cardiac muscles are striated, branched, and involuntary.
  • A sarcomere (Z-line to Z-line) contracts via \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)-triggered sliding of actin over myosin, shortening the I-band and H-zone while keeping the A-band constant.
  • The 206 human bones divide into 80 axial (skull, vertebral column, sternum, ribs) and 126 appendicular (limbs and girdles) bones, connected via fibrous, cartilaginous, and synovial joints.
