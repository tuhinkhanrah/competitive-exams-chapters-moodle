NEET UG • Human Physiology

Chemical Coordination and Integration

A Complete Exam-Oriented Guide to the Human Endocrine System, Hormonal Homeostasis, and Receptor Signaling

Learning Objectives and Overview

While the nervous system delivers rapid, point-to-point electrical signals to specific muscles and glands, many physiological processes—such as growth, metabolism, electrolyte balance, and reproduction—require sustained, body-wide regulation. The endocrine system fulfills this role by secreting chemical messengers into the bloodstream to reach cells that lack direct nerve innervation. Together, the neural and endocrine systems form an integrated neuroendocrine network that maintains internal homeostasis.

After mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between classical ductless endocrine glands, exocrine glands, and non-classical hormone-secreting organs.
  • Explain the anatomical and functional relationship between the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary, and posterior pituitary.
  • Map every major human hormone to its source tissue, target organ, physiological action, and clinical disorder of hypo- or hypersecretion.
  • Analyze antagonistic hormonal pairs regulating blood glucose and blood calcium homeostasis.
  • Contrast membrane-bound receptor signaling (second messengers) with intracellular nuclear receptor signaling (gene regulation).

1. Endocrine Glands and the Nature of Hormones

Glands in the human body are broadly categorized by whether they use ducts to transport their secretions:

  • Exocrine glands possess ducts that carry secretions (such as digestive enzymes, sweat, or saliva) to an epithelial surface or body cavity.
  • Endocrine glands lack ducts and are therefore called ductless glands. They release their secretions directly into the surrounding interstitial fluid and bloodstream.
  • Composite (heterocrine) glands, such as the pancreas, contain both exocrine and endocrine tissues.

In classical physiology, a hormone was defined strictly as a substance secreted by an endocrine gland into the blood to act on a distant target organ. Modern endocrinology uses a broader functional definition:

Modern Scientific Definition of a Hormone

Hormones are non-nutrient chemicals that act as intercellular messengers and are produced in trace amounts. This definition encompasses both classical gland secretions and local or tissue-derived signaling molecules (such as gastrointestinal peptides and growth factors).

Across the animal kingdom, invertebrates possess relatively simple endocrine systems with a small repertoire of hormones, whereas vertebrates—especially humans—rely on a rich network of organized endocrine glands and diffused hormone-producing cells.

2. The Hypothalamic–Pituitary Axis

The hypothalamus, located at the basal part of the diencephalon in the forebrain, serves as the primary bridge between the nervous system and the endocrine system. Specialized neurosecretory cells grouped into clusters called nuclei synthesize two classes of hypothalamic hormones:

  • Releasing hormones, which stimulate the synthesis and secretion of specific anterior pituitary hormones (for example, Gonadotrophin-releasing hormone, GnRH, stimulates gonadotrophin release).
  • Inhibiting hormones, which suppress anterior pituitary secretion (for example, somatostatin inhibits growth hormone release).

The pituitary gland sits inside a bony depression of the sphenoid bone called the sella tursica and is connected to the hypothalamus by a stalk. Anatomically, it comprises two major divisions with distinct embryonic origins and regulatory mechanisms:

  • Adenohypophysis: Divided into the pars distalis (commonly called the anterior pituitary) and the pars intermedia (which in humans is almost completely merged with the pars distalis). Hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting hormones travel down axons to nerve endings in the median eminence and enter the hypophyseal portal circulatory system to reach the anterior pituitary.
  • Neurohypophysis (pars nervosa): Commonly called the posterior pituitary. It does not synthesize hormones of its own; instead, it stores and releases two hormones—oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH)—that are synthesized by hypothalamic neurons and transported directly down their axons.

3. Metabolic, Immune, and Homeostatic Endocrine Glands

The Pineal Gland

Situated on the dorsal side of the forebrain, the pineal gland secretes melatonin. Melatonin governs the body's 24-hour (diurnal/circadian) rhythm, including the sleep–wake cycle and daily body temperature fluctuations. Beyond circadian timing, melatonin also influences metabolism, skin pigmentation, the menstrual cycle, and immune defense capability.

The Thyroid Gland

The thyroid gland consists of two lobes flanking the trachea, bridged by a thin band of connective tissue called the isthmus. Histologically, it contains stromal tissue and numerous thyroid follicles—spherical cavities lined by follicular cells. These cells synthesize two iodine-containing hormones: tetraiodothyronine (thyroxine, T4) and triiodothyronine (T3).

  • Functions of T3 and T4: Regulate the basal metabolic rate (BMR), support red blood cell formation (erythropoiesis), control carbohydrate, protein, and lipid metabolism, and help maintain water and electrolyte balance.
  • Thyrocalcitonin (TCT): Secreted by the thyroid gland (parafollicular cells); it is a protein hormone that lowers blood calcium levels.
Thyroid Clinical Correlations (High-Yield for NEET UG)
  • Simple Goitre (Hypothyroidism): Dietary iodine deficiency prevents normal T3/T4 synthesis, causing compensatory enlargement of the thyroid gland.
  • Cretinism: Hypothyroidism during pregnancy impairs fetal development, leading to stunted physical growth, mental retardation, low IQ, abnormal skin, and deaf-mutism. In adult women, hypothyroidism can cause irregular menstrual cycles.
  • Exophthalmic Goitre (Graves' Disease): A form of hyperthyroidism caused by thyroid nodules or cancer, characterized by an enlarged thyroid, protrusion of the eyeballs (exophthalmos), elevated BMR, and weight loss.

The Parathyroid Glands

Humans possess four small parathyroid glands embedded on the posterior (dorsal) surface of the thyroid lobes—one pair per lobe. They secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH), a peptide hormone whose release is directly triggered by low circulating Ca2+ levels.

PTH is a hypercalcemic hormone (raises blood Ca2+) via three coordinated actions: (1) stimulating bone resorption (demineralization), (2) enhancing Ca2+ reabsorption by renal tubules, and (3) increasing Ca2+ absorption from digested food in the gut. PTH and thyrocalcitonin (TCT) act antagonistically to maintain tight calcium homeostasis.

The Thymus

The thymus is a lobular organ situated between the lungs, behind the sternum, on the ventral side of the aorta. It secretes peptide hormones called thymosins, which are essential for the maturation and differentiation of T-lymphocytes (mediating cell-mediated immunity) and also promote antibody production (supporting humoral immunity). Because the thymus gradually degenerates with age, thymosin output drops in elderly individuals, weakening their immune responses.

The Adrenal Glands

A pair of adrenal glands caps the anterior (superior) pole of each kidney. Each gland has two functionally distinct regions: an inner adrenal medulla and an outer adrenal cortex.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Structural zonation of the adrenal gland showing the three cortical layers (GFR: Glomerulosa, Fasciculata, Reticularis) surrounding the central adrenal medulla.
  • Adrenal Medulla: Secretes adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine), collectively termed catecholamines. Released rapidly during stress or emergencies ("Fight or Flight" hormones), they elevate alertness, pupillary dilation, piloerection, sweating, heart rate, cardiac contraction strength, and respiratory rate, while promoting glycogenolysis, lipolysis, and proteolysis to mobilize blood glucose.
  • Adrenal Cortex: Organized from outside to inside into zona glomerulosa, zona fasciculata, and zona reticularis. It secretes steroid hormones called corticoids:
    • Glucocorticoids (mainly cortisol): Stimulate gluconeogenesis, lipolysis, and proteolysis; inhibit cellular amino acid uptake; maintain cardiovascular and kidney function; stimulate RBC production; and exert anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects.
    • Mineralocorticoids (mainly aldosterone): Act on renal tubules to promote Na+ and water reabsorption and K+ and phosphate excretion, regulating fluid volume, osmotic pressure, and blood pressure.
    • Androgenic steroids: Secreted in small quantities to support axial, pubic, and facial hair growth at puberty.
  • Addison's Disease: Hyposecretion of adrenal cortical hormones disrupts carbohydrate metabolism, causing acute weakness and fatigue.

The Endocrine Pancreas

The pancreas is a composite gland whose endocrine component consists of 1 to 2 million Islets of Langerhans (comprising only 1–2% of total pancreatic tissue). Two key cell types regulate blood glucose homeostasis:

  • α-cells secrete glucagon (a peptide hormone). Glucagon acts primarily on hepatocytes (liver cells) to stimulate glycogenolysis (glycogen breakdown) and gluconeogenesis, while reducing cellular glucose uptake. Thus, glucagon is a hyperglycemic hormone.
  • β-cells secrete insulin (a peptide hormone). Insulin acts on hepatocytes and adipocytes to enhance cellular glucose uptake and utilization and stimulate glycogenesis (conversion of glucose to glycogen). Thus, insulin is a hypoglycemic hormone.

Prolonged hyperglycemia caused by insulin deficiency or resistance results in diabetes mellitus, characterized by loss of glucose in urine (glycosuria) and formation of harmful ketone bodies. Patients are managed with insulin therapy.

4. Gonadal Endocrine Functions

Testis (Male Gonad)

Located in the scrotal sac outside the abdominal cavity, the testes serve as both primary sex organs and endocrine glands. They consist of seminiferous tubules and interstitial (stromal) tissue. Leydig cells (interstitial cells) in the intertubular spaces produce androgens, principally testosterone.

Androgens regulate the development and maturation of male accessory sex organs (epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate, urethra), drive secondary sexual characteristics (muscular growth, facial and axillary hair, low-pitched voice, aggressiveness), stimulate spermatogenesis, influence male sexual behavior (libido) via the central neural system, and exert anabolic effects on protein and carbohydrate metabolism.

Ovary (Female Gonad)

Females have a pair of ovaries in the abdomen that produce one ovum per menstrual cycle along with two groups of steroid hormones:

  • Estrogen: Synthesized mainly by growing ovarian follicles. It stimulates female secondary sex organ growth, ovarian follicle development, female secondary sexual characteristics (such as a high-pitched voice), mammary gland development, and female sexual behavior.
  • Progesterone: Secreted mainly by the corpus luteum (formed from the ruptured Graafian follicle after ovulation). Progesterone supports pregnancy and acts on mammary glands to stimulate the formation of milk-storing alveoli and milk secretion.

6. Chemical Classification and Mechanism of Hormone Action

Hormones act exclusively on target tissues that express specific binding proteins called hormone receptors. Binding forms a hormone–receptor complex, triggering biochemical changes that alter cellular metabolism. Based on chemical structure, hormones fall into four groups:

  1. Peptide, polypeptide, and protein hormones (e.g., insulin, glucagon, pituitary hormones, hypothalamic hormones).
  2. Steroids (e.g., cortisol, testosterone, estradiol, progesterone).
  3. Iodothyronines (thyroid hormones: T3, T4).
  4. Amino-acid derivatives (e.g., epinephrine).

The chemical nature of a hormone determines where its receptor is located and how it signals:

  • Membrane-Bound Receptors: Water-soluble peptide/protein hormones and catecholamines cannot cross the lipid bilayer. They bind to cell-surface receptors and generate intracellular second messengers—such as cyclic AMP (\(\mathrm{cAMP}\)), inositol trisphosphate (\(\mathrm{IP}_3\)), or \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\)—which cascade into biochemical and physiological responses.
  • Intracellular (Nuclear) Receptors: Lipid-soluble steroid hormones and iodothyronines diffuse across the plasma membrane and bind to receptors inside the cell (mostly inside the nucleus). The resulting hormone–receptor complex interacts directly with the genome to regulate gene expression (mRNA transcription and protein synthesis), producing slower but sustained developmental and physiological effects.

Worked Examples

Diagnosing Calcium Homeostasis Imbalance

Scenario (Assumed Clinical Case): A patient presents with a serum \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) level of \(7.2\,\mathrm{mg/dL}\) (assumed below the normal physiological range) following accidental damage to tissue on the dorsal surface of the thyroid gland during neck surgery.

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  1. Identify the anatomical structure on the dorsal surface of the thyroid: the four parathyroid glands.
  2. Recall the hormone secreted: parathyroid hormone (PTH), which is a hypercalcemic peptide hormone.
  3. Loss of PTH reduces bone resorption, decreases renal tubular \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) reabsorption, and lowers intestinal \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) absorption, explaining the hypocalcemia.

Conclusion: Damage to the parathyroid glands caused PTH deficiency, lowering blood \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) levels.

Tracing the Hypothalamic–Pituitary Portal Pathway

Scenario (Assumed Experimental Case): Suppose a selective vascular block interrupts blood flow in the hypophyseal portal system while leaving hypothalamic axons to the neurohypophysis intact. Which hormones will be directly affected, and which will continue to be released normally?

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  1. The hypophyseal portal system carries hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting hormones (e.g., GnRH, somatostatin) to the anterior pituitary (pars distalis). Blocking it disrupts the regulation of GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, and FSH.
  2. The posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) receives oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH) via direct axonal transport from hypothalamic neurons, not via the portal blood vessels.

Conclusion: Anterior pituitary hormone regulation fails, whereas oxytocin and ADH release remains intact.

Distinguishing Peptide vs. Steroid Hormone Signaling

Scenario (Assumed Assay): Two unknown hormones, \(X\) and \(Y\), are tested on target cells. Hormone \(X\) triggers a rapid rise in intracellular \(\mathrm{cAMP}\) within seconds without entering the cell, whereas Hormone \(Y\) crosses the plasma membrane and increases specific mRNA synthesis over several hours. Classify \(X\) and \(Y\).

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  1. Hormone \(X\) binds a membrane-bound receptor and uses \(\mathrm{cAMP}\) as a second messenger—characteristic of peptide/protein hormones (e.g., FSH, glucagon, insulin) or catecholamines.
  2. Hormone \(Y\) enters the cell, binds an intracellular nuclear receptor, and alters transcription—characteristic of steroid hormones (e.g., cortisol, estrogen, testosterone) or iodothyronines.

Conclusion: \(X\) acts via a membrane-bound receptor (second messenger pathway), and \(Y\) acts via an intracellular receptor (genomic pathway).

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following pairs of hormones is synthesized in the hypothalamus and transported axonally to the neurohypophysis for storage and release?
  • (1) Growth hormone and Prolactin
  • (2) Oxytocin and Vasopressin
  • (3) Gonadotrophin-releasing hormone and Somatostatin
  • (4) Luteinizing hormone and Follicle stimulating hormone
Correct Answer: (2)
Worked Solution: Oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH) are synthesized by neurosecretory cells of the hypothalamus and transported down their axons to the neurohypophysis (posterior pituitary) for storage and release. GnRH and somatostatin travel via the hypophyseal portal system to the anterior pituitary, while GH, PRL, LH, and FSH are synthesized by the pars distalis itself.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ): A patient exhibits protrusion of the eyeballs, an enlarged thyroid gland, elevated basal metabolic rate, and significant weight loss. This clinical presentation is characteristic of:
  • (1) Cretinism due to fetal hypothyroidism
  • (2) Addison's disease due to adrenal cortex hyposecretion
  • (3) Exophthalmic goitre (Graves' disease) due to hyperthyroidism
  • (4) Simple goitre due to dietary iodine deficiency
Correct Answer: (3)
Worked Solution: Exophthalmic goitre (Graves' disease) is a form of hyperthyroidism marked by thyroid enlargement, protrusion of eyeballs (exophthalmos), increased BMR, and weight loss. Simple goitre and cretinism are associated with hypothyroidism, while Addison's disease results from underproduction of adrenal cortical hormones.
Practice Question 3 (Statement-Based MCQ): Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is a hypercalcemic hormone that increases blood \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) levels.
Statement II: Thyrocalcitonin (TCT) acts synergistically with PTH to further elevate blood \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) levels.
Choose the correct option:
  • (1) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (2) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (3) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  • (4) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
Correct Answer: (3)
Worked Solution: Statement I is correct because PTH raises blood \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) via bone resorption, renal reabsorption, and intestinal absorption. Statement II is incorrect because thyrocalcitonin (TCT) lowers blood \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) levels, acting antagonistically—not synergistically—to PTH.

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ):
Assertion (A): Insulin and glucagon jointly maintain blood glucose homeostasis.
Reason (R): Insulin stimulates glycogenolysis in hepatocytes to raise blood glucose, whereas glucagon stimulates glycogenesis to lower blood glucose.
  • (1) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  • (2) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  • (3) (A) is true but (R) is false
  • (4) (A) is false but (R) is true
Correct Answer: (3)
Worked Solution: Assertion (A) is true: insulin and glucagon act as an antagonistic pair to maintain glucose homeostasis. Reason (R) is false because the roles are reversed: glucagon stimulates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis (hyperglycemic), whereas insulin stimulates cellular glucose uptake and glycogenesis (hypoglycemic).
Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following gastrointestinal hormones acts on both the exocrine pancreas and the gall bladder to stimulate the secretion of pancreatic enzymes and bile juice, respectively?
  • (1) Gastrin
  • (2) Secretin
  • (3) Cholecystokinin (CCK)
  • (4) Gastric Inhibitory Peptide (GIP)
Correct Answer: (3)
Worked Solution: Cholecystokinin (CCK) targets both the pancreas (stimulating pancreatic enzyme release) and the gall bladder (stimulating bile juice release). Gastrin stimulates HCl and pepsinogen; secretin stimulates pancreatic water and bicarbonate secretion; GIP inhibits gastric secretion and motility.
Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following hormones binds to intracellular receptors and regulates gene expression rather than generating second messengers like \(\mathrm{cAMP}\)?
  • (1) Follicle stimulating hormone (FSH)
  • (2) Epinephrine
  • (3) Glucagon
  • (4) Cortisol
Correct Answer: (4)
Worked Solution: Cortisol is a lipid-soluble steroid hormone that crosses the plasma membrane, binds to an intracellular (nuclear) receptor, and interacts with the genome to regulate gene expression. FSH and glucagon are peptide/glycoprotein hormones, and epinephrine is an amino-acid derivative; all three act via membrane-bound receptors and second messengers.
Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ): Degeneration of the thymus gland in elderly individuals primarily leads to weakened immunity because of reduced secretion of:
  • (1) Melatonin, impairing sleep–wake cycles
  • (2) Thymosins, impairing T-lymphocyte differentiation and antibody production
  • (3) Aldosterone, impairing sodium reabsorption
  • (4) Erythropoietin, impairing red blood cell formation
Correct Answer: (2)
Worked Solution: Thymosins from the thymus are essential for T-lymphocyte differentiation (cell-mediated immunity) and also promote antibody production (humoral immunity). Age-related thymic degeneration lowers thymosin levels and weakens immune responses.

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Diabetes Mellitus vs. Diabetes Insipidus: Diabetes mellitus is caused by insulin deficiency/resistance (glycosuria + ketone bodies). Diabetes insipidus is caused by ADH (vasopressin) deficiency (excessive water loss in urine, no glucose loss).
  • Origin of Posterior Pituitary Hormones: Oxytocin and ADH are released by the posterior pituitary, but they are synthesized in the hypothalamus.
  • Adrenal Cortex Layers (Outside → Inside): Remember the order G-F-R: Zona Glomerulosa (outer, mineralocorticoids/aldosterone), Zona Fasciculata (middle, glucocorticoids/cortisol), Zona Reticularis (inner, androgenic steroids).
  • Secretin vs. CCK: Secretin stimulates pancreatic water and bicarbonate; CCK stimulates pancreatic enzymes and gall bladder bile.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Iodine is essential for \(\mathrm{T}_3\) and \(\mathrm{T}_4\) synthesis; deficiency causes hypothyroidism and simple goitre.
  • PTH raises blood \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) (hypercalcemic); TCT lowers blood \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) (hypocalcemic).
  • Islets of Langerhans (α-cells → glucagon; β-cells → insulin) make up only 1–2% of pancreatic tissue.
  • Leydig cells secrete testosterone; ovarian follicles secrete estrogen; corpus luteum secretes progesterone.
  • Membrane-bound receptors use second messengers (\(\mathrm{cAMP}\), \(\mathrm{IP}_3\), \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\)); intracellular receptors (steroids, iodothyronines) regulate genome transcription.

Concise Final Recap

Chemical coordination relies on trace, non-nutrient intercellular messengers called hormones. The hypothalamus orchestrates the endocrine system by regulating the anterior pituitary via the hypophyseal portal system and supplying the posterior pituitary with oxytocin and ADH via direct neural axons. Peripheral glands—including the pineal, thyroid, parathyroids, thymus, adrenals, pancreas, and gonads—along with non-classical tissues in the heart (ANF), kidney (erythropoietin), and GI tract (gastrin, secretin, CCK, GIP), maintain metabolic, electrolyte, immune, and reproductive homeostasis. Ultimately, every hormone acts with target specificity either by triggering rapid second-messenger cascades at the cell membrane or by modulating gene expression inside the nucleus.
