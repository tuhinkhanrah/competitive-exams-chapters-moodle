NEET UG Biology Animal Physiology & Morphology

Structural Organisation in Animals

Animal Tissues, Organ Systems, and Comparative Anatomy of Earthworm, Cockroach, and Frog

Learning Objectives and Overview

Multicellular animals achieve physiological efficiency through division of labour. Rather than expecting a single cell to perform digestion, respiration, excretion, and reproduction simultaneously, complex animals partition these tasks across specialised structural levels: cells, tissues, organs, and organ systems.

Core Mastery Goals for NEET UG
  • Classify the four primary animal tissue types—epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural—and match each subtype to its exact anatomical location and function.
  • Distinguish between the three types of cell junctions (tight, adhering, gap) and between exocrine and endocrine glands.
  • Master the segmental anatomy, alimentary canal, circulatory pattern, excretory organs, and reproductive landmarks of the earthworm (Pheretima), cockroach (Periplaneta americana), and frog (Rana tigrina).
  • Apply comparative morphology (such as sexual dimorphism, nitrogenous waste products, and respiratory modes) to solve assertion–reason, statement-based, and matching questions accurately.

1. Animal Tissues: Structure, Classification, and Function

A tissue is a group of similar cells, together with their surrounding intercellular substances, organised to perform a specific function. Remarkably, all complex animals are constructed from only four fundamental tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural.

1.1 Epithelial Tissue

Epithelial tissue (epithelium) forms a continuous sheet with a free surface exposed either to the external environment or to an internal body fluid. Its cells are tightly packed with minimal intercellular matrix, resting on a supportive framework.

Epithelium Type Cellular Architecture Key Locations Primary Functions
Simple Squamous Single thin layer of flattened cells with irregular boundaries Walls of blood vessels (endothelium), air sacs (alveoli) of lungs Forms a selective diffusion boundary
Simple Cuboidal Single layer of cube-like cells; may bear microvilli (e.g., in PCT) Ducts of glands, tubular parts of nephrons (PCT of kidney) Secretion and absorption (microvilli increase surface area)
Simple Columnar Single layer of tall, slender cells with basal nuclei; free surface may have microvilli Inner lining of stomach and intestine Secretion and nutrient absorption
Ciliated Epithelium Cuboidal or columnar cells bearing motile cilia on the free surface Inner surface of hollow organs: bronchioles and fallopian tubes Propels mucus, particles, or ova in a specific direction
Compound Epithelium Multi-layered (two or more cell layers) Dry surface of skin; moist surface of buccal cavity, pharynx, inner lining of salivary & pancreatic ducts Protection against chemical and mechanical stress; limited secretion/absorption

Glandular Epithelium and Cell Junctions

Certain columnar or cuboidal cells specialise for secretion to form glandular epithelium:

  • By cell number: Unicellular glands consist of isolated secretory cells (such as mucus-secreting goblet cells of the alimentary canal), whereas multicellular glands consist of clusters of cells (such as the salivary gland).
  • By mode of secretion: Exocrine glands release products (mucus, saliva, earwax, oil, milk, digestive enzymes) through ducts or tubes. Endocrine glands are ductless and secrete hormones directly into the fluid bathing the gland.

Because epithelial cells lie tightly apposed, specialised cell junctions link neighbouring cells both structurally and functionally:

  • Tight junctions: Seal intercellular spaces to prevent substances from leaking across a tissue.
  • Adhering junctions: Perform mechanical cementing to hold adjacent cells firmly together.
  • Gap junctions: Connect the cytoplasm of adjoining cells directly, enabling rapid communication and transfer of ions, small molecules, and occasionally larger molecules.

1.2 Connective Tissue

Connective tissues are the most abundant and widely distributed tissues in complex animals, serving to link, support, protect, and transport. In all connective tissues except blood, cells secrete structural protein fibres (collagen or elastin) that impart strength, elasticity, and flexibility, along with modified polysaccharides that form the intercellular ground substance (matrix).

  • Loose Connective Tissue: Cells and fibres are loosely arranged in a semi-fluid ground substance.
    • Areolar tissue: Lies beneath the skin and acts as a support framework for epithelium; contains fibroblasts (fibre-secreting cells), macrophages (phagocytic), and mast cells.
    • Adipose tissue: Also located mainly beneath the skin; its cells are specialised to convert excess unused nutrients into fat and store it.
  • Dense Connective Tissue: Fibres and fibroblasts are compactly packed.
    • Dense regular: Collagen fibres lie in parallel rows between bundles; examples are tendons (attach skeletal muscles to bones) and ligaments (attach bone to bone).
    • Dense irregular: Fibroblasts and fibres (mostly collagen) are oriented in different directions; found in the skin.
  • Specialised Connective Tissue:
    • Cartilage: Matrix is solid, pliable, and resists compression. Cells (chondrocytes) sit in small cavities (lacunae) within their own secreted matrix. Found in the tip of the nose, outer ear joints, between adjacent vertebrae, and in limbs and hands of adults. Most embryonic vertebrate cartilage is later replaced by bone.
    • Bone: Matrix is hard and non-pliable, rich in calcium salts and collagen fibres. Bone cells (osteocytes) occupy spaces called lacunae. Provides the structural frame, protects soft organs, interacts with skeletal muscles for movement, and houses bone marrow (site of blood cell production in some bones).
    • Blood: A fluid connective tissue lacking fibres, composed of plasma, red blood cells (RBCs), white blood cells (WBCs), and platelets. Functions as the primary circulating transport medium.

1.3 Muscular Tissue

Muscles consist of long, cylindrical fibres arranged in parallel arrays, each packed with fine contractile fibrils called myofibrils. Muscle fibres shorten (contract) upon stimulation and lengthen (relax) in a coordinated manner to generate movement and maintain posture.

1.4 Neural Tissue

Neural tissue exerts the greatest control over the body's responsiveness to changing internal and external conditions. Neurons are the excitable functional units: when suitably stimulated, an electrical disturbance travels swiftly along the neuron's plasma membrane to its output zone, triggering stimulation or inhibition of adjacent neurons or effector cells. Non-excitable neuroglial cells protect and support neurons and constitute more than half (\(>50\%\)) of the total volume of neural tissue in the body.

2. Organ and Organ System Organisation

Tissues combine in specific proportions and patterns to form an organ. For instance, the human heart contains all four basic tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural. Two or more organs interacting physically or chemically to execute a shared physiological role form an organ system (such as the digestive or respiratory system).

In zoological study, morphology refers to externally visible features (form and external appearance), whereas anatomy conventionally denotes the study of internal organs.

3. Earthworm (Pheretima posthuma)

Earthworms are reddish-brown terrestrial invertebrates inhabiting the upper layers of moist soil. Nocturnal in habit, they remain in burrows by day, boring and swallowing soil and leaving behind faecal deposits called worm castings. Common Indian genera include Pheretima and Lumbricus.

3.1 External Morphology

  • Segmentation: Cylindrical body divided into \(100\text{–}120\) similar short segments called metameres.
  • Orientation: The dorsal surface bears a dark median mid-dorsal line (representing the dorsal blood vessel). The ventral surface is distinguished by genital pores.
  • Anterior End: The first body segment is the peristomium (buccal segment), which contains the mouth. Overhanging the mouth is the prostomium, a sensory lobe used as a wedge to force open soil cracks.
  • Clitellum: In mature worms, segments 14–16 are covered by a prominent dark glandular band called the clitellum, dividing the body into preclitellar, clitellar, and postclitellar regions.
  • Locomotion (Setae): Every segment except the first, last, and clitellum bears rows of S-shaped chitinous setae embedded in epidermal pits; these can be extended or retracted for gripping soil.
  • External Apertures: Four pairs of spermathecal apertures lie ventro-laterally in the intersegmental grooves between segments 5/6, 6/7, 7/8, and 8/9. A single median female genital pore opens mid-ventrally on segment 14, and a pair of male genital pores open ventro-laterally on segment 18. Numerous minute nephridiopores dot the body surface.

3.2 Internal Anatomy

  • Body Wall: Covered externally by a thin non-cellular cuticle, followed by a single-layered columnar epidermis (with secretory gland cells), circular and longitudinal muscle layers, and an innermost coelomic epithelium.
  • Digestive System: A straight tube from first to last segment: mouth \(\rightarrow\) buccal cavity (1–3) \(\rightarrow\) muscular pharynx \(\rightarrow\) narrow oesophagus (5–7) \(\rightarrow\) muscular gizzard (8–9, grinds soil and decaying leaves) \(\rightarrow\) stomach (9–14, contains calciferous glands that neutralise humic acid in humus) \(\rightarrow\) intestine (15 to last segment). A pair of short conical intestinal caecae project at segment 26. After segment 26 (excluding the last 23–25 segments), the dorsal intestinal wall folds inward as the typhlosole, expanding the absorptive surface area.
  • Circulatory & Respiratory Systems: Closed blood vascular system with blood vessels, capillaries, and contractile hearts ensuring unidirectional flow. Blood glands in segments 4, 5, and 6 produce phagocytic blood cells and haemoglobin (dissolved directly in blood plasma, not inside corpuscles). Specialised breathing organs are absent; gaseous exchange occurs across the moist body surface.
  • Excretory System (Nephridia): Segmentally arranged coiled tubules of three types:
    1. Septal nephridia: On both sides of intersegmental septa from segment 15 to the last; open into the intestine.
    2. Integumentary nephridia: Attached to the body wall lining from segment 3 to the last; open onto the body surface.
    3. Pharyngeal nephridia: Three paired tufts in segments 4, 5, and 6; open into the buccal cavity/pharynx.
  • Nervous & Sensory Systems: Segmental ganglia on a paired ventral nerve cord. In segments 3–4, the cord bifurcates around the pharynx to join dorsal cerebral ganglia, forming a nerve ring. Eyes are absent, but anterior photoreceptors, tactile receptors, and chemoreceptors detect light intensity, ground vibrations, and chemicals.
  • Reproductive System: Earthworms are hermaphrodites (bisexual) and protandrous (testes mature earlier), undergoing cross-fertilisation. Two pairs of testes (segments 10, 11) send vasa deferentia to segment 18, joining prostatic ducts to open as male pores; accessory glands lie in segments 17 and 19. One pair of ovaries attaches to the 12/13 septum, opening via oviducts at the single female pore on segment 14. During mating, worms exchange sperm packets (spermatophores) stored in four pairs of spermathecae (segments 6–9). The clitellum secretes a cocoon containing ova, sperm, and nutritive fluid; fertilisation and direct development (no larva) occur inside the cocoon in soil, yielding 2–20 (average 4) young worms after about 3 weeks.

4. Cockroach (Periplaneta americana)

Cockroaches belong to Class Insecta of Phylum Arthropoda. Periplaneta americana is a nocturnal omnivore measuring \(34\text{–}53\text{ mm}\) in length, with wings extending beyond the abdominal tip in males.

4.1 External Morphology and Sexual Dimorphism

  • Exoskeleton: Hardened brown chitinous plates called sclerites—dorsal tergites and ventral sternites—joined by flexible arthrodial membranes.
  • Head: Triangular, formed by fusion of six segments, held at right angles to the body axis on a flexible neck. Bears thread-like sensory antennae, a pair of compound eyes, and biting and chewing mouthparts: labrum (upper lip), a pair of mandibles, a pair of maxillae, labium (lower lip), and a median flexible hypopharynx (tongue).
  • Thorax: Divided into prothorax, mesothorax, and metathorax, each bearing one pair of walking legs (6 legs total). Forewings (tegmina, mesothoracic) are opaque, dark, and leathery, covering the transparent, membranous metathoracic hindwings used in flight.
  • Abdomen (10 segments): In both sexes, segment 10 bears a pair of jointed filamentous anal cerci. Males are distinguished by a pair of short, thread-like anal styles (absent in females). In females, the boat-shaped 7th sternum combines with the 8th and 9th sterna to form a brood/genital pouch.

4.2 Internal Anatomy

Tutorial Diagram 4: Regional organisation of the cockroach alimentary canal and its junctional appendages.
  • Digestive System: Divided into foregut (entirely lined by cuticle; mouth \(\rightarrow\) pharynx \(\rightarrow\) oesophagus \(\rightarrow\) crop for storage \(\rightarrow\) gizzard/proventriculus with 6 chitinous teeth for grinding), midgut, and hindgut (ileum, colon, rectum). A ring of 6–8 hepatic/gastric caeca at the foregut–midgut junction secretes digestive juice, while 100–150 yellow Malpighian tubules at the midgut–hindgut junction remove excretory wastes.
  • Circulatory & Respiratory Systems: Open circulatory system; poorly developed vessels open into a haemocoel filled with colourless haemolymph (plasma + haemocytes, no respiratory pigment). The elongated, mid-dorsal tubular heart has funnel-shaped chambers with lateral ostia. Respiration occurs via a tracheal network opening through 10 pairs of lateral spiracles (regulated by sphincters); gas exchange takes place by diffusion at tracheoles.
  • Excretion: Uricotelic (excretes uric acid). Malpighian tubules lined by glandular and ciliated cells absorb nitrogenous wastes from haemolymph and convert them into uric acid, discharged via the hindgut. Fat body, nephrocytes, and urecose glands also assist.
  • Nervous System & Vision: Paired ventral nerve cord with 3 thoracic and 6 abdominal ganglia. The head holds only the supra-oesophageal ganglion (brain), supplying antennae and compound eyes; consequently, a decapitated cockroach can survive for up to a week. Each compound eye contains roughly \(2000\) hexagonal ommatidia, producing mosaic vision characterised by high sensitivity but low resolution (adapted for nocturnal life).
  • Reproductive System & Development: Dioecious. Males possess testes in abdominal segments 4–6, a mushroom-shaped accessory gland in segments 6–7, seminal vesicles (forming spermatophores), and asymmetrical chitinous external genitalia (phallomeres). Females have two ovaries (segments 2–6), each composed of 8 ovarioles, plus a pair of spermathecae in segment 6. Fertilised eggs are enclosed in dark capsules called oothecae (~8 mm long; 9–10 produced per female, each holding 14–16 eggs). Development is paurometabolous (gradual metamorphosis through nymphs that moult about 13 times; the penultimate nymphal stage bears wing pads).

5. Frog (Rana tigrina)

Rana tigrina (Indian bullfrog) belongs to Class Amphibia of Phylum Chordata. Frogs are poikilotherms (cold-blooded) capable of protective colour change (camouflage/mimicry). They survive extreme summer heat by burrowing into deep shelter (aestivation) and extreme winter cold through winter sleep (hibernation).

5.1 Morphology and Sexual Dimorphism

  • Skin & Body Plan: Smooth, slippery, mucus-rich skin (olive-green dorsally with dark spots, pale yellow ventrally). Frogs never drink water; they absorb it through the skin. Body consists of head and trunk (neck and tail absent).
  • Sense & Locomotion: Bulged eyes protected underwater by a nictitating membrane; membranous tympanum receives sound. Forelimbs have 4 digits; larger muscular hindlimbs have 5 webbed digits for swimming and leaping.
  • Sexual Dimorphism: Male frogs possess sound-producing vocal sacs and a copulatory pad on the first digit of each forelimb, both absent in females.

5.2 Internal Organ Systems

  • Digestive System: Short alimentary canal reflecting a carnivorous diet. Prey is captured by a bilobed tongue. Stomach (HCl and gastric juice) produces chyme, which enters the duodenum to receive bile (from gall bladder) and pancreatic juice via a common bile duct. Final digestion and absorption occur in the intestine (villi and microvilli); undigested waste exits through the cloaca.
  • Respiration: Aquatic respiration is strictly cutaneous (dissolved \(\mathrm{O_2}\) diffuses across moist skin). On land, frogs use buccal cavity, skin, and paired pink lungs (pulmonary respiration). During aestivation and hibernation, respiration is exclusively cutaneous.
  • Vascular System: Closed blood vascular system with a 3-chambered heart (2 atria, 1 ventricle) enclosed in a pericardium. A triangular sinus venosus joins the right atrium, and the ventricle opens ventrally into a sac-like conus arteriosus. Both hepatic portal and renal portal systems are present. RBCs are nucleated and contain haemoglobin. Lymph lacks certain proteins and RBCs.
  • Excretory System: Paired dark-red kidneys composed of nephrons; ureotelic (excretes urea). In males, ureters act as urinogenital ducts opening into the cloaca; in females, ureters and oviducts open separately into the cloaca. A thin-walled urinary bladder lies ventral to the rectum.
  • Neural & Endocrine Control: 10 pairs of cranial nerves. Brain (enclosed in cranium) comprises forebrain (olfactory lobes, paired cerebral hemispheres, unpaired diencephalon), midbrain (pair of optic lobes), and hindbrain (cerebellum, medulla oblongata passing through the foramen magnum into the spinal cord). Simple eyes; tympanum and internal ear serve both hearing and equilibrium.
  • Reproductive System: Male testes attach to kidneys via a peritoneal fold (mesorchium); 10–12 vasa efferentia enter the kidney and open into Bidder's canal, communicating with the urinogenital duct. Female ovaries have no functional connection with kidneys; a mature female lays \(2500\text{–}3000\) ova at a time. Fertilisation is external in water; development is indirect via a tadpole larva undergoing metamorphosis.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Identifying Epithelial Tissues by Micro-anatomical Location

Problem: A histology student examines three unlabelled slides taken from (i) the lining of a human bronchiole, (ii) the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) of a nephron, and (iii) the moist lining of the buccal cavity. Assuming standard mammalian tissue architecture, identify the specific epithelium in each slide and justify your answer.

Solution:

  • (i) Bronchiole: Ciliated columnar epithelium. Cilia on the free surface beat in a coordinated direction to clear mucus and trapped dust particles from the airway.
  • (ii) PCT of nephron: Simple cuboidal epithelium with microvilli (brush border). Cube-like cells specialised for active secretion and reabsorption, with microvilli expanding the absorptive area.
  • (iii) Moist buccal cavity: Compound (multi-layered) epithelium. Because the oral cavity experiences mechanical abrasion during chewing and swallowing, multiple cell layers are required for protection rather than absorption.

Example 2: Mapping Segmental Structures in Pheretima

Problem: Suppose a researcher examines a dissected mature earthworm and isolates (a) a muscular grinding organ, (b) blood-cell-forming glands, and (c) the single median female genital pore. In which segment numbers should each structure be located?

Solution:

  • (a) Muscular gizzard: Segments 8–9 (grinds soil particles and decaying leaves).
  • (b) Blood glands: Segments 4, 5, and 6 (produce phagocytic blood cells and plasma-dissolved haemoglobin).
  • (c) Female genital pore: Mid-ventral line of segment 14 (within the clitellar region, segments 14–16).

Example 3: Distinguishing Male and Female Cockroaches

Problem: A biology student collects two adult Periplaneta americana specimens (Specimen X and Specimen Y). Specimen X has wings extending beyond the tip of the abdomen and bears a pair of short, unjointed thread-like structures at the posterior end in addition to jointed cerci. Specimen Y has a boat-shaped 7th abdominal sternum and lacks short unjointed posterior styles. Identify the sex of each specimen.

Solution:

  • Specimen X is Male: Short, thread-like anal styles are exclusive to male cockroaches, and male wings characteristically extend beyond the abdominal tip. Jointed anal cerci on the 10th segment occur in both sexes.
  • Specimen Y is Female: Females lack anal styles and possess a boat-shaped 7th sternum that forms the anterior floor of the brood/genital pouch together with the 8th and 9th sterna.

Practice Question 1

Which of the following connective tissues lacks structural protein fibres secreted by its resident cells?

  1. (A) Areolar tissue
  2. (B) Dense irregular connective tissue
  3. (C) Blood
  4. (D) Cartilage
Answer: (C)

Explanation: In all connective tissues except blood, resident cells (such as fibroblasts or chondroblasts) secrete collagen or elastin fibres into the matrix. Blood is a fluid connective tissue whose plasma lacks these structural protein fibres.

Practice Question 2

Select the correct match between the type of cell junction and its primary physiological role:

  1. (A) Tight junction — Facilitates rapid cytoplasmic transfer of ions between adjoining cells
  2. (B) Adhering junction — Performs cementing to keep neighbouring cells together
  3. (C) Gap junction — Stops substances from leaking across an epithelial sheet
  4. (D) Desmosome — Secretes mucus directly into the alimentary lumen
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Adhering junctions cement neighbouring cells together. Tight junctions prevent leakage across tissues, whereas gap junctions connect the cytoplasm of adjoining cells for rapid ion and small-molecule transfer.

Practice Question 3

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Smooth muscle fibres are fusiform, lack striations, and are involuntary in action.
Statement II: Cardiac muscle cells possess intercalated discs that allow them to contract as a single coordinated unit.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  3. (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Smooth muscle fibres taper at both ends (fusiform), lack striations, and are under involuntary control in visceral organs. Cardiac muscle cells are linked at fusion points by communication junctions called intercalated discs, enabling simultaneous contraction when one cell is stimulated.

Practice Question 4

Which of the following structures in the earthworm Pheretima are correctly paired with their segmental locations?

  1. (A) Clitellum: 14–16; Male genital pores: 14th segment
  2. (B) Spermathecae: 6–9; Female genital pore: 18th segment
  3. (C) Testes: 10–11; Intestinal caecae: 26th segment
  4. (D) Gizzard: 5–7; Blood glands: 8–9
Answer: (C)

Explanation: In Pheretima, two pairs of testes lie in segments 10 and 11, and a pair of intestinal caecae project from the intestine at segment 26. The female genital pore is on segment 14, male genital pores on segment 18, blood glands in segments 4–6, and the gizzard in segments 8–9.

Practice Question 5

In earthworms, which type of nephridia discharges nitrogenous wastes directly into the lumen of the intestine rather than onto the external body surface?

  1. (A) Integumentary nephridia
  2. (B) Septal nephridia
  3. (C) Pharyngeal nephridia
  4. (D) Both integumentary and pharyngeal nephridia
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Septal nephridia (present on intersegmental septa from segment 15 to the last) open into the intestine (enteronephric). Integumentary nephridia open onto the body surface, while pharyngeal nephridia open into the buccal cavity and pharynx.

Practice Question 6

Assertion (A): A cockroach can survive for as long as one week even if its head is severed.
Reason (R): The nervous system of a cockroach is spread along the ventral side of the body, with only a small portion (supra-oesophageal ganglion) located in the head.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  3. (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
  4. (D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Answer: (A)

Explanation: In Periplaneta americana, three thoracic and six abdominal ganglia lie along the ventral nerve cord, controlling most body functions. Because the head houses only the supra-oesophageal ganglion, decapitation does not immediately halt vital ventral-ganglion activity.

Practice Question 7

In Periplaneta americana, where are the 100–150 yellow filamentous Malpighian tubules located, and what is their primary function?

  1. (A) Foregut–midgut junction; secretion of digestive enzymes
  2. (B) Midgut–hindgut junction; removal of excretory products from haemolymph
  3. (C) Posterior to crop; mechanical grinding of food
  4. (D) Dorsal haemocoel; pumping of colourless haemolymph
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Malpighian tubules form a ring of 100–150 yellow filaments at the junction of the midgut and hindgut, absorbing nitrogenous wastes from haemolymph and converting them to uric acid for excretion. (6–8 hepatic caeca sit at the foregut–midgut junction.)

Practice Question 8

Which statement accurately describes the wings and posterior abdominal appendages of Periplaneta americana?

  1. (A) Forewings (tegmina) arise from the prothorax and are used for flight
  2. (B) Hindwings are opaque, leathery, and cover the forewings at rest
  3. (C) Anal cerci are present in both sexes on the 10th abdominal segment
  4. (D) Anal styles are present in both sexes on the 9th abdominal segment
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Jointed anal cerci arise from the 10th abdominal segment in both males and females. Anal styles are exclusive to males. Forewings (tegmina) arise from the mesothorax and are dark/leathery, whereas transparent membranous hindwings arise from the metathorax and mediate flight.

Practice Question 9

During peak summer aestivation and winter hibernation, how does Rana tigrina carry out gaseous exchange?

  1. (A) Exclusively through the lungs (pulmonary respiration)
  2. (B) Exclusively through the moist skin (cutaneous respiration)
  3. (C) Through the buccal cavity and lungs
  4. (D) Through external gills
Answer: (B)

Explanation: While buried in deep burrows during aestivation or hibernation, frogs rely solely on cutaneous respiration across their moist, highly vascularised skin.

Practice Question 10

Trace the correct sequence of sperm transport in a male frog from the site of production to the exterior:

  1. (A) Testes \(\rightarrow\) Vasa efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Bidder's canal \(\rightarrow\) Urinogenital duct \(\rightarrow\) Cloaca
  2. (B) Testes \(\rightarrow\) Bidder's canal \(\rightarrow\) Vasa efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Ureter \(\rightarrow\) Urinary bladder
  3. (C) Testes \(\rightarrow\) Mesorchium \(\rightarrow\) Oviduct \(\rightarrow\) Cloaca
  4. (D) Testes \(\rightarrow\) Vasa deferentia \(\rightarrow\) Prostate duct \(\rightarrow\) Cloaca
Answer: (A)

Explanation: In male frogs, 10–12 vasa efferentia arise from the testes, enter the kidney, and open into Bidder's canal, which then communicates with the urinogenital duct (ureter) and opens into the cloaca.

Practice Question 11

Which of the following correctly differentiates frog red blood cells (RBCs) from human RBCs, and frog heart chambers from mammalian heart chambers?

  1. (A) Frog RBCs are enucleated; frog heart has 4 chambers
  2. (B) Frog RBCs are nucleated; frog heart has 3 chambers (2 atria, 1 ventricle)
  3. (C) Frog RBCs lack haemoglobin; frog heart has 2 chambers
  4. (D) Frog RBCs are nucleated; frog heart has 1 atrium and 2 ventricles
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Frog RBCs are nucleated and contain haemoglobin. The frog heart is three-chambered, consisting of two atria and a single ventricle (supplemented by sinus venosus and conus arteriosus).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Tendons vs. Ligaments: Both are dense regular connective tissues, but tendons join muscle to bone, whereas ligaments join bone to bone.
  • Setae vs. Clitellum in Earthworm: Setae are absent from the first segment, last segment, and the clitellum (14–16).
  • Anal Cerci vs. Anal Styles in Cockroach: Anal cerci (10th segment) are present in both sexes; anal styles (9th sternum) are present only in males.
  • Uricotelic vs. Ureotelic: Cockroaches are uricotelic (uric acid); frogs are ureotelic (urea).
  • Male vs. Female Frog Ureters: In male frogs, ureters function as urinogenital ducts (carrying both urine and sperm); in females, ureters carry only urine and open separately from oviducts into the cloaca.

Quick-Scan Revision Checklist

  • Four primary tissues: Epithelial, Connective, Muscular, Neural (neuroglia \(>50\%\) of neural volume).
  • Earthworm: Clitellum (14–16), Gizzard (8–9), Typhlosole (after 26th), Closed circulation, 3 nephridial types, Hermaphrodite with direct development in cocoon.
  • Cockroach: 3 thoracic legs pairs, 2 wing pairs (mesothoracic tegmina + metathoracic flight wings), 6–8 hepatic caeca, 100–150 Malpighian tubules, 10 pairs spiracles, Open circulation, 9–10 oothecae (14–16 eggs each), Paurometabolous development.
  • Frog: Cutaneous + buccal + pulmonary respiration, 3-chambered heart, Hepatic & renal portal systems, Nucleated RBCs, 10 pairs cranial nerves, External fertilisation & tadpole metamorphosis.

Concise Final Recap

Complex animals achieve coordinated survival by integrating four tissue families into organs and organ systems. Across the three representative organisms—Pheretima (annelid), Periplaneta (arthropod), and Rana tigrina (amphibian)—evolutionary adaptations in body covering, circulatory design (closed vs. open), gas exchange (cutaneous, tracheal, pulmonary), nitrogenous excretion (nephridia, Malpighian tubules, kidneys), and reproductive strategies directly reflect their terrestrial, burrowing, or amphibious habitats.
