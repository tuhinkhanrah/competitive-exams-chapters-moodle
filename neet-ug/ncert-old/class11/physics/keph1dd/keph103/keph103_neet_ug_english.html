Motion in a Plane: Vectors, Projectile Motion & Uniform Circular Motion

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide to two-dimensional kinematics.

Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Motion along a straight line requires only positive and negative signs to specify direction. However, real-world trajectories—such as a cricket ball flying through the air, a boat crossing a flowing river, or a vehicle rounding a curved track—occur in two or three dimensions. Describing such motion requires the mathematical language of vectors.

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between scalar and vector quantities and apply vector algebra rules accurately.
  • Add, subtract, and resolve vectors both graphically (triangle and parallelogram laws) and analytically (orthogonal components).
  • Express position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration in two-dimensional unit-vector notation.
  • Analyze planar motion with constant acceleration by decomposing it into independent perpendicular one-dimensional motions.
  • Derive and apply the trajectory equation, time of flight, maximum height, and horizontal range of a projectile.
  • Determine linear speed, angular speed, and centripetal acceleration in uniform circular motion, and avoid common conceptual traps.

Scalars and Vectors

Every measurable physical quantity in mechanics falls into one of two foundational categories based on whether direction is required for its complete specification.

  • Scalar Quantity: A physical quantity completely specified by a single numerical value (magnitude) and an appropriate unit. Scalars have no associated direction and combine according to ordinary arithmetic. Examples include mass, distance, speed, time, density, temperature, work, energy, and electric current.
  • Vector Quantity: A physical quantity that possesses both magnitude and direction, and strictly obeys the triangle law (or equivalently, the parallelogram law) of vector addition. Examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, impulse, and linear momentum.

In print, a vector is denoted by a boldface symbol such as \(\mathbf{A}\) or \(\mathbf{v}\), while its magnitude (a non-negative scalar) is written as \(|\mathbf{A}| = A\) or \(|\mathbf{v}| = v\). In handwritten work, an arrow is placed above the letter, such as \(\vec{A}\).

Equality, Scaling, and Special Vectors

  • Equality of Vectors: Two free vectors \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are equal (\(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{B}\)) if and only if they have identical magnitudes and identical directions. Shifting a free vector parallel to itself leaves it unchanged.
  • Multiplication by a Real Number: Multiplying \(\mathbf{A}\) by a positive real number \(\lambda\) scales its magnitude to \(\lambda A\) without altering its direction. Multiplying by a negative number \(-\lambda\) (\(\lambda > 0\)) scales the magnitude to \(\lambda A\) and reverses the direction by \(180^\circ\). If the scalar carries physical dimensions (for example, multiplying constant velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) by time \(t\)), the resulting vector carries the product of their dimensions (displacement).
  • Null (Zero) Vector: Represented by \(\mathbf{0}\), a null vector has zero magnitude (\(|\mathbf{0}| = 0\)) and an undefined direction. It arises when two equal and opposite vectors are added (\(\mathbf{A} + (-\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{0}\)) or when a body returns to its starting point (zero net displacement). Key properties: \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{A}\), \(\lambda \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\), and \(0\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{0}\).
  • Unit Vector: A dimensionless vector of magnitude \(1\) used purely to specify direction. Any non-zero vector \(\mathbf{A}\) can be written as \(\mathbf{A} = A\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{A}/A\) is the unit vector along \(\mathbf{A}\). Along the mutually perpendicular \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-axes of a right-handed Cartesian system, unit vectors are denoted by \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), and \(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\), respectively, with \(|\hat{\mathbf{i}}| = |\hat{\mathbf{j}}| = |\hat{\mathbf{k}}| = 1\).

Vector Addition, Subtraction, and Resolution

Graphical Methods: Triangle and Parallelogram Laws

To add two vectors \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) graphically using the triangle (head-to-tail) method, place the tail of \(\mathbf{B}\) at the head of \(\mathbf{A}\); the resultant \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\) runs from the tail of \(\mathbf{A}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{B}\). Equivalently, in the parallelogram method, join the tails of \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) at a common origin and complete the parallelogram; the diagonal passing through that common origin represents \(\mathbf{R}\).

Vector addition is both commutative (\(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A}\)) and associative (\((\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}) + \mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A} + (\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{C})\)).

Vector Subtraction: Subtracting \(\mathbf{B}\) from \(\mathbf{A}\) is defined as adding the reversed vector \(-\mathbf{B}\) to \(\mathbf{A}\):

\[ \mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A} + (-\mathbf{B}) \]
Tutorial Diagram 1: (a) Parallelogram law showing resultant \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\) at angle \(\alpha\) to \(\mathbf{A}\). (b) Resolving a vector \(\mathbf{A}\) into orthogonal scalar components \(A_x\) and \(A_y\).

Analytical Method: Magnitude and Direction of Resultant

For two vectors \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) inclined at an angle \(\theta\) (\(0^\circ \le \theta \le 180^\circ\)), applying the law of cosines and right-triangle geometry gives the magnitude \(R\) of \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\) and the angle \(\alpha\) that \(\mathbf{R}\) makes with \(\mathbf{A}\):

\[ R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + 2AB\cos\theta}, \qquad \tan\alpha = \frac{B\sin\theta}{A + B\cos\theta} \]

For the difference \(\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}\), replace \(\theta\) with \(180^\circ - \theta\), yielding \(D = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2 - 2AB\cos\theta}\).

Triangle Inequalities: For any two vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), the resultant magnitude is bounded between their difference and sum:

\[ \bigl|\,|\mathbf{a}| - |\mathbf{b}|\,\bigr| \le |\mathbf{a} \pm \mathbf{b}| \le |\mathbf{a}| + |\mathbf{b}| \]

Rectangular Resolution in 2D and 3D

Any vector \(\mathbf{A}\) in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane making an angle \(\theta\) with the positive \(x\)-axis can be expressed as \(\mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + A_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), where \(A_x = A\cos\theta\) and \(A_y = A\sin\theta\). Conversely, given components \(A_x\) and \(A_y\):

\[ A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x} \]

In three dimensions, \(\mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + A_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + A_z\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) with \(A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}\). Adding or subtracting vectors analytically reduces to adding or subtracting corresponding components: if \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\), then \(R_x = A_x + B_x\), \(R_y = A_y + B_y\), and \(R_z = A_z + B_z\).

Kinematics in a Plane

Position, Displacement, Velocity, and Acceleration

Choose an origin \(O\) in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane. At time \(t\), a particle at coordinates \((x, y)\) has position vector \(\mathbf{r} = x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y\hat{\mathbf{j}}\). If it moves to \((x', y')\) at time \(t'\), its displacement vector is:

\[ \Delta\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}' - \mathbf{r} = \Delta x\,\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \Delta y\,\hat{\mathbf{j}} \]

The displacement depends solely on the initial and final endpoints, whereas path length depends on the actual curve traversed. Consequently, \(\text{path length} \ge |\Delta\mathbf{r}|\), with equality holding only when the particle moves in a fixed straight direction without reversing.

  • Average Velocity: \(\bar{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf{r}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), directed along \(\Delta\mathbf{r}\).
  • Instantaneous Velocity: Taking the limit as \(\Delta t \to 0\) gives \(\mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t} = v_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + v_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), where \(v_x = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\) and \(v_y = \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t}\). Its magnitude is \(v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}\), and its direction is always tangent to the path at that instant.
  • Average and Instantaneous Acceleration: \(\bar{\mathbf{a}} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf{v}}{\Delta t}\) and \(\mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t} = a_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + a_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), where \(a_x = \frac{\mathrm{d}v_x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 x}{\mathrm{d}t^2}\) and \(a_y = \frac{\mathrm{d}v_y}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 y}{\mathrm{d}t^2}\).

Unlike one-dimensional motion, where velocity and acceleration are strictly collinear (\(0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\) apart), in two or three dimensions \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\) can make any angle between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\).

Motion in a Plane with Constant Acceleration

When acceleration \(\mathbf{a}\) is constant in both magnitude and direction, the kinematic equations mirror those of one dimension in vector form:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t, \qquad \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2 \]

Writing these in component form reveals a powerful principle: two-dimensional motion with constant acceleration is equivalent to two independent, simultaneous one-dimensional motions along perpendicular \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes:

\[ x = x_0 + v_{0x}t + \frac{1}{2}a_x t^2, \qquad v_x = v_{0x} + a_x t \] \[ y = y_0 + v_{0y}t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2, \qquad v_y = v_{0y} + a_y t \]

Relative Velocity in Two Dimensions

If objects 1 and 2 have velocities \(\mathbf{v}_1\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2\) measured relative to a common reference frame (such as the ground), the velocity of object 1 relative to object 2 is \(\mathbf{v}_{12} = \mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2\). Conversely, if an object moves with velocity \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{obj,med}}\) relative to a moving medium (such as a boat in a river or an aircraft in wind) whose velocity is \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{med}}\), the resultant velocity relative to the ground is \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_{\text{obj,med}} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{med}}\).

Projectile Motion

An object launched into flight and moving solely under the influence of gravity (neglecting air resistance) is called a projectile. Choosing the launch point as the origin \((0, 0)\), the positive \(x\)-axis horizontally forward, and the positive \(y\)-axis vertically upward, the acceleration components are \(a_x = 0\) and \(a_y = -g\).

If the projectile is launched with initial speed \(v_0\) at an angle \(\theta_0\) above the horizontal, its initial velocity components are \(v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta_0\) and \(v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta_0\). Because \(a_x = 0\), the horizontal velocity remains constant throughout flight (\(v_x = v_0\cos\theta_0\)), while the vertical motion behaves like free fall under gravity (\(v_y = v_0\sin\theta_0 - gt\)).

Tutorial Diagram 2: Parabolic path of a projectile launched at angle \(\theta_0\) with speed \(v_0\). At maximum height \(H\), vertical velocity \(v_y = 0\) and velocity is purely horizontal.

Key Projectile Equations (Level Ground Launch)

  • Position at time \(t\): \(x = (v_0\cos\theta_0)t\) and \(y = (v_0\sin\theta_0)t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2\).
  • Equation of Trajectory: Eliminating \(t\) gives a parabola:
    \[ y = (\tan\theta_0)x - \frac{g}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta_0}x^2 \]
  • Time to Maximum Height (\(t_m\)) and Time of Flight (\(T_f\)): Setting \(v_y = 0\) gives \(t_m = \frac{v_0\sin\theta_0}{g}\). By symmetry of the parabola over level ground, \(T_f = 2t_m = \frac{2v_0\sin\theta_0}{g}\).
  • Maximum Height (\(H\)):
    \[ H = \frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta_0}{2g} \]
  • Horizontal Range (\(R\)): The horizontal distance covered during \(T_f\) is:
    \[ R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta_0}{g} \]
High-Yield NEET UG Insights for Projectiles:
  • Maximum Range: For a fixed launch speed \(v_0\), \(R\) is maximum when \(\sin 2\theta_0 = 1\), i.e., at \(\theta_0 = 45^\circ\), giving \(R_{\max} = \frac{v_0^2}{g}\).
  • Complementary Angles: Angles \((45^\circ + \alpha)\) and \((45^\circ - \alpha)\)—or any two angles summing to \(90^\circ\)—produce the exact same horizontal range for a given \(v_0\), because \(\sin(180^\circ - 2\theta_0) = \sin 2\theta_0\).
  • At the Highest Point: Speed is minimum (\(v_{\min} = v_0\cos\theta_0 \ne 0\)), and the velocity vector is perpendicular to the acceleration vector (\(\mathbf{v} \perp \mathbf{g}\)).
  • Range–Height Relation: \(\frac{R}{H} = \frac{4}{\tan\theta_0}\), or \(R\tan\theta_0 = 4H\).

Uniform Circular Motion (UCM)

When an object travels along a circular path of radius \(R\) at a constant speed \(v\), its motion is called uniform circular motion. Even though speed is constant, the direction of velocity changes continuously, meaning the velocity vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is changing—so the object is continuously accelerating.

As the time interval \(\Delta t \to 0\), the change in velocity \(\Delta \mathbf{v}\) becomes perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\) and points directly towards the center of the circle. This inward acceleration is called centripetal acceleration (\(a_c\)):

\[ a_c = \frac{v^2}{R} = \omega^2 R = 4\pi^2 \nu^2 R \]

Here, \(\omega = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}\) is the angular speed (in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\)), related to linear speed by \(v = \omega R\). If \(T\) is the time period for one revolution and \(\nu = 1/T\) is the frequency, then \(\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi\nu\) and \(v = \frac{2\pi R}{T} = 2\pi R\nu\).

Tutorial Diagram 3: In uniform circular motion, instantaneous velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) is always tangent to the circle and perpendicular to centripetal acceleration \(\mathbf{a}_c\), which points towards center \(C\).
Crucial Distinctions in Circular Motion:
  • In UCM, the magnitude of \(\mathbf{a}_c\) is constant, but its direction changes continuously. Therefore, \(\mathbf{a}_c\) is not a constant vector.
  • Because \(\mathbf{a}_c\) is not a constant vector, constant-acceleration kinematic equations (\(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t\)) cannot be applied to UCM.
  • If speed varies along a circular path (non-uniform circular motion), there is also a tangential acceleration \(a_t = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}\), so the net acceleration is not directed purely towards the center.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Resultant of Two Non-Perpendicular Forces

Problem (Assumed Values): Two tugboats pull a barge with horizontal forces of magnitudes \(F_1 = 12\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(F_2 = 16\,\mathrm{N}\) inclined at an angle of \(60^\circ\) to each other. Calculate the magnitude of the resultant force and the angle it makes with \(\mathbf{F}_1\).

Solution: Using the analytical formula for vector addition with \(\theta = 60^\circ\):

\[ R = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2 + 2F_1F_2\cos 60^\circ} = \sqrt{12^2 + 16^2 + 2(12)(16)(0.5)} = \sqrt{144 + 256 + 192} = \sqrt{592} \approx 24.33\,\mathrm{N} \]

The angle \(\alpha\) with \(\mathbf{F}_1\) is given by:

\[ \tan\alpha = \frac{F_2\sin 60^\circ}{F_1 + F_2\cos 60^\circ} = \frac{16 \times (\sqrt{3}/2)}{12 + 16 \times 0.5} = \frac{8\sqrt{3}}{20} = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{5} \approx 0.693 \implies \alpha \approx 34.7^\circ \]

Example 2: Rain and Wind Relative Velocity

Problem (Assumed Values): Raindrops fall vertically downward at a steady speed of \(v_r = 8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). A horizontal wind starts blowing from west to east at \(v_w = 6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). At what angle to the vertical should a stationary pedestrian tilt an umbrella to stay dry?

Solution: The resultant velocity of the rain relative to the ground is \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_r + \mathbf{v}_w\). Since \(\mathbf{v}_r\) (downward) and \(\mathbf{v}_w\) (eastward) are perpendicular, the magnitude is:

\[ v = \sqrt{v_r^2 + v_w^2} = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

The angle \(\theta\) made with the vertical satisfies:

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{v_w}{v_r} = \frac{6}{8} = 0.75 \implies \theta \approx 36.9^\circ \]

Because the rain strikes from the west while moving eastward, the pedestrian must tilt the umbrella at \(36.9^\circ\) with the vertical towards the west.

Example 3: Two-Dimensional Motion with Constant Acceleration

Problem (Assumed Values): A particle leaves the origin at \(t = 0\) with an initial velocity \(\mathbf{v}_0 = 4\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and moves in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane under a constant acceleration \(\mathbf{a} = (2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{j}})\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Find (a) the \(y\)-coordinate when \(x = 21\,\mathrm{m}\), and (b) the particle's speed at that instant.

Solution: Since \(\mathbf{r}_0 = \mathbf{0}\), the position components are:

\[ x(t) = 4t + \frac{1}{2}(2)t^2 = 4t + t^2, \qquad y(t) = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(3)t^2 = 1.5t^2 \]

(a) Setting \(x(t) = 21\,\mathrm{m}\) gives \(t^2 + 4t - 21 = 0 \implies (t + 7)(t - 3) = 0\). Taking \(t > 0\), we get \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\). Substituting \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\) into \(y(t)\):

\[ y = 1.5(3)^2 = 13.5\,\mathrm{m} \]

(b) Velocity at time \(t\) is \(\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t = (4 + 2t)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3t\hat{\mathbf{j}}\). At \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\), \(\mathbf{v} = 10\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 9\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), so the speed is:

\[ v = \sqrt{10^2 + 9^2} = \sqrt{181} \approx 13.45\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Example 4: Horizontal Projection from an Elevated Cliff

Problem (Assumed Values): A rescue drone flying horizontally at \(25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at a height of \(80\,\mathrm{m}\) above flat ground releases a supply packet. Neglecting air resistance and taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find the time of fall, horizontal distance covered, and impact speed.

Solution: Choosing the release point as the origin with \(v_{0x} = 25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(v_{0y} = 0\), and \(a_y = -10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\):

Vertical displacement at impact is \(y = -80\,\mathrm{m}\), so:

\[ -80 = -\frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 \implies t^2 = 16 \implies t = 4\,\mathrm{s} \]

Horizontal distance covered: \(x = v_{0x}t = 25 \times 4 = 100\,\mathrm{m}\).

At \(t = 4\,\mathrm{s}\), \(v_x = 25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(v_y = 0 - 10(4) = -40\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Impact speed is:

\[ v = \sqrt{25^2 + (-40)^2} = \sqrt{625 + 1600} = \sqrt{2225} \approx 47.17\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Example 5: Oblique Projectile Range and Maximum Height

Problem (Assumed Values): A football is kicked at \(v_0 = 20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at an elevation of \(30^\circ\) above level ground. Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), calculate its maximum height, time of flight, and horizontal range.

Solution:

\[ H = \frac{(v_0\sin 30^\circ)^2}{2g} = \frac{(20 \times 0.5)^2}{2 \times 10} = \frac{100}{20} = 5\,\mathrm{m} \]

\[ T_f = \frac{2v_0\sin 30^\circ}{g} = \frac{2 \times 20 \times 0.5}{10} = 2\,\mathrm{s} \]

\[ R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 60^\circ}{g} = \frac{400 \times (\sqrt{3}/2)}{10} = 20\sqrt{3} \approx 34.64\,\mathrm{m} \]

Example 6: Centripetal Acceleration in Uniform Circular Motion

Problem (Assumed Values): A small stone tied to a \(0.5\,\mathrm{m}\) string is whirled in a horizontal circle at a steady rate of \(120\) revolutions per minute. Find its angular speed, linear speed, and centripetal acceleration.

Solution: Frequency \(\nu = \frac{120}{60} = 2\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Thus:

\[ \omega = 2\pi\nu = 2\pi(2) = 4\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}} \approx 12.57\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}} \]

\[ v = \omega R = (4\pi)(0.5) = 2\pi\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 6.28\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

\[ a_c = \omega^2 R = (4\pi)^2(0.5) = 8\pi^2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \approx 78.96\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \]

The acceleration vector is directed radially inward towards the center of the circle at every instant.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Each practice question below is designed in the single-correct MCQ format of NEET UG. Attempt each problem before checking its worked solution.

Practice Question 1: Which of the following sets of three concurrent forces CANNOT produce a zero resultant (equilibrium)?
  • (A) \(4\,\mathrm{N},\ 6\,\mathrm{N},\ 8\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(5\,\mathrm{N},\ 5\,\mathrm{N},\ 10\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(3\,\mathrm{N},\ 5\,\mathrm{N},\ 9\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(7\,\mathrm{N},\ 10\,\mathrm{N},\ 12\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: By the triangle inequality, three vectors can sum to \(\mathbf{0}\) only if the sum of the two smaller magnitudes is greater than or equal to the largest magnitude. For set (C), \(3 + 5 = 8 < 9\), so they cannot form a closed triangle and their resultant can never be zero.

Practice Question 2: A cyclist rides along a semicircular track of radius \(140\,\mathrm{m}\) from one end of a diameter to the opposite end in \(20\,\mathrm{s}\). Taking \(\pi = 22/7\), what is the ratio of the cyclist's average speed to the magnitude of average velocity?
  • (A) \(11 : 7\)
  • (B) \(7 : 11\)
  • (C) \(22 : 7\)
  • (D) \(1 : 1\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Path length \(= \pi R = \frac{22}{7} \times 140 = 440\,\mathrm{m}\), while displacement magnitude \(= 2R = 280\,\mathrm{m}\). Since the time interval \(20\,\mathrm{s}\) is identical for both, the ratio is \(\frac{440}{280} = \frac{11}{7}\).

Practice Question 3: A particle moves due east at \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and then turns smoothly to move due north at \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) over a time interval of \(3\,\mathrm{s}\). What is its average acceleration during this interval?
  • (A) \(5\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) towards north-east
  • (B) \(5\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) towards north-west
  • (C) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) towards north-west
  • (D) \(5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) towards south-west
Answer: (B)

Solution: Let east be \(+\hat{\mathbf{i}}\) and north be \(+\hat{\mathbf{j}}\). Then \(\mathbf{v}_i = 15\hat{\mathbf{i}}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_f = 15\hat{\mathbf{j}}\). The change in velocity is \(\Delta\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_f - \mathbf{v}_i = -15\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 15\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), which points north-west with magnitude \(15\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Dividing by \(\Delta t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\) gives \(|\bar{\mathbf{a}}| = 5\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) towards north-west.

Practice Question 4: Given \(\mathbf{A} = 3\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{j}}\) and \(\mathbf{B} = 7\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 24\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), find a vector that has the same magnitude as \(\mathbf{B}\) and is parallel to \(\mathbf{A}\).
  • (A) \(15\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 20\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)
  • (B) \(20\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 15\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)
  • (C) \(7\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 24\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)
  • (D) \(12\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 16\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: \(|\mathbf{A}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \frac{3\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{j}}}{5}\). Also, \(|\mathbf{B}| = \sqrt{7^2 + 24^2} = \sqrt{625} = 25\). The required vector is \(|\mathbf{B}|\hat{\mathbf{A}} = 25\left(\frac{3\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{j}}}{5}\right) = 15\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 20\hat{\mathbf{j}}\).

Practice Question 5: The position vector of a particle is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = 4t^2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (3t^2 - 2t)\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m}\), where \(t\) is in seconds. What is the magnitude of its acceleration at \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\)?
  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (B) \(14\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (C) \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (D) \(5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Differentiating twice with respect to \(t\): \(\mathbf{v}(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t} = 8t\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (6t - 2)\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(\mathbf{a}(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t} = 8\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 6\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The acceleration is constant with magnitude \(a = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

Practice Question 6: A projectile is launched from level ground such that its horizontal range is \(4\sqrt{3}\) times its maximum height. What is the angle of projection with the horizontal?
  • (A) \(30^\circ\)
  • (B) \(45^\circ\)
  • (C) \(60^\circ\)
  • (D) \(75^\circ\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Using the identity \(\frac{R}{H} = \frac{4}{\tan\theta_0}\), we have \(\frac{4}{\tan\theta_0} = 4\sqrt{3} \implies \tan\theta_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \implies \theta_0 = 30^\circ\).

Practice Question 7: A ball is projected with an initial speed of \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at an angle of \(60^\circ\) above the horizontal. Neglecting air resistance, what is its speed at the highest point of its trajectory?
  • (A) \(0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(10\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: At the highest point, \(v_y = 0\), while the horizontal component remains \(v_x = v_0\cos 60^\circ = 20 \times \frac{1}{2} = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Thus the speed at the peak is \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 8: Two projectiles A and B are launched from level ground with identical initial speeds \(v_0\) at angles \(35^\circ\) and \(55^\circ\) above the horizontal, respectively. Neglecting air resistance, which physical quantity is identical for both?
  • (A) Maximum height reached
  • (B) Total time of flight
  • (C) Horizontal range
  • (D) Vertical component of initial velocity
Answer: (C)

Solution: Since \(35^\circ + 55^\circ = 90^\circ\), the launch angles are complementary, giving \(\sin(2 \times 35^\circ) = \sin 70^\circ = \sin 110^\circ = \sin(2 \times 55^\circ)\). Therefore, both projectiles achieve the same horizontal range \(R = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta_0}{g}\).

Practice Question 9: A car travels around a flat circular track of radius \(50\,\mathrm{m}\) at a constant speed of \(72\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\). What is the magnitude of its centripetal acceleration?
  • (A) \(4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (B) \(8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (C) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (D) \(103.7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Convert speed to SI units: \(v = 72 \times \frac{5}{18} = 20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Then \(a_c = \frac{v^2}{R} = \frac{20^2}{50} = \frac{400}{50} = 8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

Practice Question 10: Consider the following two statements regarding Uniform Circular Motion (UCM):
Statement I: The centripetal acceleration vector of a particle in UCM is a constant vector because both speed and radius remain constant.
Statement II: The acceleration vector of a particle in UCM averaged over one complete revolution is a null vector.
Choose the correct option:
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
Answer: (C)

Solution: Statement I is incorrect because, although \(a_c = v^2/R\) has constant magnitude, its direction continuously changes (always pointing towards the center), so \(\mathbf{a}_c\) is not a constant vector. Statement II is correct because over one full cycle the initial and final velocities are identical (\(\Delta\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\)), making \(\bar{\mathbf{a}} = \mathbf{0}\).

Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Components vs. Component Vectors: \(A_x\) and \(A_y\) are scalar numbers (which can be negative), whereas \(A_x\hat{\mathbf{i}}\) and \(A_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}\) are vectors.
  • Zero Speed at Projectile Peak: Only the vertical velocity \(v_y\) vanishes at maximum height; the total speed is \(v_0\cos\theta_0\) (non-zero for \(\theta_0 < 90^\circ\)).
  • Applying 1D Kinematic Formulas to UCM: Never use \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t\) for circular motion, because \(\mathbf{a}_c\) is not constant in direction.
  • Unit Conversions: Always convert \(\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) to \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (multiply by \(\frac{5}{18}\)) and \(\mathrm{rpm}\) to \(\mathrm{Hz}\) (divide by \(60\)) before substituting into \(v^2/R\) or \(\omega^2 R\).
Quick Revision Checklist:
  • Can you resolve any vector into \(x\)- and \(y\)-components and reconstruct its magnitude and angle?
  • Do you remember \(R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + 2AB\cos\theta}\) and the triangle inequality limits?
  • Can you treat \(x\)- and \(y\)-motions independently for constant-acceleration problems and projectiles?
  • Do you know \(T_f\), \(H\), \(R\), and \(R/H = 4/\tan\theta_0\) by heart?
  • Can you interconvert \(v = \omega R\), \(\omega = 2\pi\nu\), and \(a_c = v^2/R = \omega^2 R\)?

Concise Final Recap

Planar motion is governed by vector kinematics: position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration obey vector algebra, and any constant-acceleration trajectory splits cleanly into two independent 1D motions. Projectile motion combines uniform horizontal motion with free-fall vertical motion to trace a parabola, while uniform circular motion demonstrates that changing direction alone produces an inward centripetal acceleration \(v^2/R\) even at constant speed.
