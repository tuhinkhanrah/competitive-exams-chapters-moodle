Laws of Motion

Conceptual Foundations, Free-Body Analysis, Friction, and Circular Dynamics

Learning Objectives and Overview

Kinematics describes how objects move using position, velocity, and acceleration. Dynamics explains why motion changes. For NEET UG, this chapter is one of the highest-yield pillars of mechanics because its principles—force, momentum, free-body diagrams, friction, and circular motion—recur across work-energy, rotational dynamics, and gravitation.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between Aristotle's misconception and Galileo's law of inertia.
  • Apply Newton's three laws of motion to single particles and coupled multi-body systems.
  • Compute impulse and average impulsive force from momentum changes.
  • Use the conservation of linear momentum for isolated systems such as explosions and collisions.
  • Construct accurate free-body diagrams (FBDs) for equilibrium, lifts, pulleys, and inclined planes.
  • Analyze static, limiting, kinetic, and rolling friction, including the angle of repose.
  • Determine safe and optimum speeds for vehicles negotiating level and banked circular tracks.

1. From Everyday Intuition to Inertia and the First Law

In daily terrestrial life, a rolling ball slows down and a pushed cart stops once you let go. Observing this, Aristotle concluded that an external push is continuously needed to keep a body moving at constant speed. The hidden flaw in this reasoning is the presence of opposing resistive forces—such as surface friction and air drag. When you push a cart at steady speed, your applied force merely balances friction so that the net external force is zero.

Galileo Galilei uncovered the true law of nature by idealizing motion without friction. Using smooth inclined planes, he reasoned that a ball rolling down an incline accelerates, a ball rolling up decelerates, and a ball moving on a frictionless horizontal plane experiences neither acceleration nor retardation. In his double-inclined plane thought experiment, a ball released from height \(h\) climbs to the same vertical height \(h\) on an opposing smooth slope, regardless of how gentle that second slope is. In the limiting case where the second slope becomes horizontal, the ball must travel forever at constant velocity to "reach" height \(h\).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Galileo's double-inclined plane reasoning. Without friction, a ball reaches its initial vertical height \(h\) on any upward slope; if the slope becomes zero, motion continues uniformly without stopping.

Newton's First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia)

Isaac Newton formalized Galileo's insight as the First Law: Every body continues in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled by a net external force to change that state.

  • Inertia: The inherent property of a body to resist any change in its state of rest or uniform linear motion. Mass is the quantitative measure of translational inertia—a heavier body has greater inertia.
  • Mathematical Equivalence: \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{0} \iff \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\). Both rest (\(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\)) and uniform linear motion (\(\mathbf{v} = \text{constant}\)) are dynamically equivalent states of zero acceleration.

2. Momentum, Impulse, and Newton's Second Law

When a net external force acts on a body, its velocity—and hence its momentum—changes. Linear momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) of a particle of mass \(m\) moving with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) is defined as:

Linear Momentum and Newton's Second Law
\[ \mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v} \qquad \text{(SI unit: } \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}} \text{ or } \mathrm{N\,s}\text{)} \] \[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt} \] For a body of constant mass \(m\): \[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m\mathbf{a} \qquad \text{(SI unit: } 1\,\mathrm{N} = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\text{)} \]

Key features of Newton's Second Law essential for problem-solving:

  • Vector Component Form: Since \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m\mathbf{a}\) is a vector equation, it splits into three independent scalar equations: \(F_x = ma_x\), \(F_y = ma_y\), and \(F_z = ma_z\). A force perpendicular to velocity changes only the direction of motion, not the speed.
  • Local Relation: The acceleration \(\mathbf{a}\) at any instant is determined solely by the net force \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}}\) acting at that exact instant. A particle carries no "memory" of prior forces once released (for instance, a stone dropped from an accelerating train has zero horizontal acceleration immediately after release if air drag is neglected).
  • System Applicability: For a system of particles or a rigid body, \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = M\mathbf{a}_{\text{cm}}\), where internal forces cancel in pairs.

Impulse and Impulsive Forces

When a large force acts for a very brief duration \(\Delta t\) (such as a bat hitting a ball or a hammer striking a nail), the force and contact time are often hard to measure separately, but their product—impulse (\(\mathbf{J}\))—equals the net change in momentum:

Impulse–Momentum Theorem
\[ \mathbf{J} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf{F}\,dt = \mathbf{F}_{\text{avg}}\,\Delta t = \Delta \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{p}_f - \mathbf{p}_i \]

Graphically, the area under a Force–Time (\(F\text{–}t\)) curve gives the magnitude of the impulse imparted to the body. Drawing hands backward while catching a fast ball increases \(\Delta t\), which reduces the average stopping force \(F_{\text{avg}} = \Delta p / \Delta t\) for the same change in momentum.

3. Newton's Third Law and Conservation of Momentum

Forces in nature never occur in isolation; they always arise from mutual interactions between pairs of bodies. Newton's Third Law states: To every action, there is always an equal and opposite reaction.

Action–Reaction Pair
\[ \mathbf{F}_{AB} = -\mathbf{F}_{BA} \] where \(\mathbf{F}_{AB}\) is the force on body \(A\) due to body \(B\), and \(\mathbf{F}_{BA}\) is the force on body \(B\) due to body \(A\).
Critical Rules for Action–Reaction Pairs:
  • Simultaneous Occurrence: Action and reaction act at the exact same instant; neither is the cause nor the effect of the other.
  • Different Bodies: Action acts on one body and reaction acts on the other. Therefore, they never cancel each other out when analyzing the motion of a single body.

Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum

Consider an isolated system of two interacting bodies \(A\) and \(B\) (net external force \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}\)). During an interaction lasting time \(\Delta t\), the impulses on \(A\) and \(B\) are \(\mathbf{F}_{AB}\Delta t = \mathbf{p}'_A - \mathbf{p}_A\) and \(\mathbf{F}_{BA}\Delta t = \mathbf{p}'_B - \mathbf{p}_B\). Since \(\mathbf{F}_{AB} = -\mathbf{F}_{BA}\), adding these gives:

Conservation of Linear Momentum
\[ \mathbf{p}_A + \mathbf{p}_B = \mathbf{p}'_A + \mathbf{p}'_B \qquad \text{(when } \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}\text{)} \]

This principle holds for both elastic and inelastic collisions, as well as for gun recoil and radioactive decay or shell explosions, provided no net external force acts along the direction considered.

4. Equilibrium, Free-Body Diagrams, and Apparent Weight

A particle is in translational equilibrium when the net external force acting on it is zero:

\[ \sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \implies \sum F_x = 0,\quad \sum F_y = 0,\quad \sum F_z = 0 \]

For three concurrent forces \(\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \mathbf{F}_3\) in equilibrium, their vector arrows form a closed triangle taken in the same rotational sense.

Systematic Free-Body Diagram (FBD) Method

  1. Sketch the complete assembly of bodies, strings, pulleys, and surfaces.
  2. Isolate one body (or a chosen subsystem) mentally from its surroundings.
  3. Draw all external forces acting ON the chosen body (weight \(mg\) downward, normal reaction \(N\) perpendicular to contact surface, tension \(T\) pulling along a taut string, friction \(f\) along the surface).
  4. Never include forces exerted by the body on its surroundings, and never include internal forces if treating multiple bodies as one combined system.
  5. Choose convenient perpendicular axes (typically along and perpendicular to the acceleration) and apply \(\sum F = ma\) along each axis.
Tutorial Diagram 2: Isolating each mass in a coupled pulley-block system produces clean free-body diagrams with tension \(T\) along the string and kinetic friction \(f_k\) opposing sliding.

Apparent Weight in an Accelerated Lift

A weighing scale reads the normal force \(N\) exerted on the person by the floor of the lift:

  • Lift at rest or moving with constant velocity (\(a = 0\)): \(N = mg\).
  • Lift accelerating upward with \(a\): \(N - mg = ma \implies N = m(g + a)\) (feels heavier).
  • Lift accelerating downward with \(a\): \(mg - N = ma \implies N = m(g - a)\) (feels lighter).
  • Free fall (\(a = g\) downward): \(N = 0\) (apparent weightlessness).

5. Common Contact Forces and Friction

Besides gravity (a non-contact force), mechanics problems primarily involve contact forces: normal reaction (perpendicular to the surface), friction (parallel to the surface), tension in a taut massless string, and spring force \(F = -kx\) (where \(k\) is the spring constant and \(x\) is displacement from natural length). At the microscopic level, contact forces originate from electromagnetic interactions between atoms at the interface.

Friction opposes impending or actual relative motion between two surfaces in contact—not motion itself.

  • Static Friction (\(f_s\)): Acts when there is no relative sliding. It is a self-adjusting force that matches the tendency to slide up to a maximum limiting value: \[ f_s \le (f_s)_{\max} = \mu_s N \] where \(\mu_s\) is the coefficient of static friction, independent of apparent contact area.
  • Kinetic (Sliding) Friction (\(f_k\)): Acts once relative sliding begins. It is nearly independent of speed and contact area: \[ f_k = \mu_k N \qquad (\text{typically } \mu_k < \mu_s) \]
  • Angle of Repose (\(\theta_{\max}\)): The maximum angle of an inclined plane at which a block placed on it just begins to slide down. At this angle, \(mg \sin\theta_{\max} = \mu_s mg \cos\theta_{\max}\), giving: \[ \mu_s = \tan\theta_{\max} \]
  • Rolling Friction: Arises from slight momentary deformation at the contact zone of a rolling wheel or sphere. For a given normal load, rolling friction is 2 to 3 orders of magnitude smaller than sliding friction, which is why ball bearings and wheels drastically reduce energy loss.

6. Dynamics of Circular Motion

A particle moving in a circle of radius \(R\) at speed \(v\) has a centripetal acceleration \(a_c = v^2/R\) directed toward the center. By Newton's Second Law, a net inward radial force—the centripetal force—must act on it:

\[ F_c = \frac{mv^2}{R} \]

Centripetal force is not a separate physical force; it is simply the role played by tension, gravity, normal reaction, friction, or their resultant.

Vehicle on a Level Circular Road

On a flat road, vertical equilibrium gives \(N = mg\). The inward centripetal force is provided entirely by static friction \(f_s \le \mu_s N = \mu_s mg\):

Maximum Safe Speed on a Level Curve
\[ \frac{mv^2}{R} \le \mu_s mg \implies v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s R g} \]

Vehicle on a Banked Circular Road

Raising the outer edge of a curved road by a banking angle \(\theta\) allows the horizontal component of the normal reaction \(N\sin\theta\) to assist friction in providing centripetal force. Resolving forces vertically and radially (when the car is at its maximum speed, so friction \(f = \mu_s N\) acts down the incline):

\[ N\cos\theta = mg + f\sin\theta \qquad \text{and} \qquad N\sin\theta + f\cos\theta = \frac{mv^2}{R} \]
Banked Road Speeds
Maximum safe speed (with friction): \[ v_{\max} = \sqrt{R g \left( \frac{\mu_s + \tan\theta}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta} \right)} \] Optimum speed (zero friction needed, minimum tyre wear): \[ v_o = \sqrt{R g \tan\theta} \]

If \(v < v_o\), static friction acts up the slope to prevent the car from sliding inward.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Forces on a vehicle negotiating a banked circular curve at high speed (\(v > v_o\)). Both \(N\sin\theta\) and \(f\cos\theta\) point horizontally toward the center of the turn.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Impulse and Average Force During an Oblique Wall Impact

A rubber ball of mass \(0.20\,\mathrm{kg}\) strikes a rigid vertical wall at a speed of \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) making an angle of \(60^\circ\) with the normal to the wall, and rebounds symmetrically with the same speed. If the ball remains in contact with the wall for \(0.02\,\mathrm{s}\), find the magnitude of the impulse imparted to the ball and the average force exerted by the wall on the ball.

Solution:

Take the \(x\)-axis along the outward normal to the wall (pointing toward the wall) and the \(y\)-axis parallel to the wall.

  • Initial momentum components: \(p_{x,i} = m u \cos 60^\circ = 0.20 \times 10 \times 0.5 = +1.0\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(p_{y,i} = -m u \sin 60^\circ\).
  • Final momentum components: \(p_{x,f} = -m u \cos 60^\circ = -1.0\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(p_{y,f} = -m u \sin 60^\circ\).
  • Change in momentum: \(\Delta p_x = p_{x,f} - p_{x,i} = -1.0 - 1.0 = -2.0\,\mathrm{N\,s}\), while \(\Delta p_y = 0\).

Thus, the impulse has magnitude \(J = 2.0\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) directed normally away from the wall. The average force on the ball is: \[ F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{2.0}{0.02} = 100\,\mathrm{N} \]

Example 2: Determining Apparent Weight in an Accelerating Elevator

A student of mass \(60\,\mathrm{kg}\) stands on a weighing machine inside an elevator. Assume \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Calculate the reading on the scale (in \(\mathrm{N}\)) when the elevator: (a) accelerates upward at \(2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), (b) accelerates downward at \(3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and (c) descends at a constant speed of \(5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Solution:

The scale measures the normal reaction \(N\) exerted on the student:

  • (a) Upward acceleration (\(a = 2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)): \(N - mg = ma \implies N = m(g + a) = 60(10 + 2) = 720\,\mathrm{N}\).
  • (b) Downward acceleration (\(a = 3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)): \(mg - N = ma \implies N = m(g - a) = 60(10 - 3) = 420\,\mathrm{N}\).
  • (c) Constant downward velocity (\(a = 0\)): \(N = mg = 60 \times 10 = 600\,\mathrm{N}\).

Example 3: Analyzing a Coupled Pulley–Block System with Kinetic Friction

A block of mass \(m_1 = 10\,\mathrm{kg}\) rests on a rough horizontal table (\(\mu_k = 0.2\)) and is attached by a light inextensible string over a smooth pulley to a hanging block of mass \(m_2 = 5\,\mathrm{kg}\). Assuming \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string once released.

Solution:

The normal reaction on \(m_1\) is \(N = m_1 g = 10 \times 10 = 100\,\mathrm{N}\), so kinetic friction is \(f_k = \mu_k N = 0.2 \times 100 = 20\,\mathrm{N}\). Since \(m_2 g = 50\,\mathrm{N} > 20\,\mathrm{N}\), the system accelerates with \(m_2\) moving downward:

  • For \(m_2\): \(50 - T = 5a\)
  • For \(m_1\): \(T - 20 = 10a\)

Adding both equations eliminates \(T\): \(30 = 15a \implies a = 2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Substituting back gives \(T = 20 + 10(2) = 40\,\mathrm{N}\).

Example 4: Finding the Angle of Repose and Limiting Static Friction on an Inclined Plane

A wooden crate of mass \(8\,\mathrm{kg}\) is placed on a rough adjustable ramp. It just begins to slide downward when the ramp is tilted to \(37^\circ\) with the horizontal. Assuming \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(\sin 37^\circ = 0.6\), \(\cos 37^\circ = 0.8\), and \(\tan 37^\circ = 0.75\), find \(\mu_s\) and the maximum static friction force.

Solution:

At the verge of sliding, \(\theta_{\max} = 37^\circ\) is the angle of repose, so: \[ \mu_s = \tan 37^\circ = 0.75 \] The normal reaction is \(N = mg \cos 37^\circ = 8 \times 10 \times 0.8 = 64\,\mathrm{N}\). Therefore, the limiting static friction is: \[ (f_s)_{\max} = \mu_s N = 0.75 \times 64 = 48\,\mathrm{N} \]

Example 5: Evaluating Optimum and Maximum Speeds on a Banked Circular Track

A circular racetrack of radius \(R = 160\,\mathrm{m}\) is banked at an angle \(\theta\) such that \(\tan\theta = 0.25\). If the coefficient of static friction between the tyres and the track is \(\mu_s = 0.20\) and \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find (a) the optimum speed to avoid tyre wear, and (b) the maximum permissible speed without skidding.

Solution:

  • (a) Optimum speed: \[ v_o = \sqrt{R g \tan\theta} = \sqrt{160 \times 10 \times 0.25} = \sqrt{400} = 20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]
  • (b) Maximum permissible speed: \[ v_{\max} = \sqrt{R g \frac{\mu_s + \tan\theta}{1 - \mu_s \tan\theta}} = \sqrt{1600 \times \frac{0.20 + 0.25}{1 - (0.20)(0.25)}} = \sqrt{1600 \times \frac{0.45}{0.95}} \approx 27.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A passenger sitting in a train facing the direction of motion tosses a coin vertically upward. If the coin falls behind the passenger, the train is:

  • (A) Moving with uniform velocity
  • (B) Accelerating forward
  • (C) Retarding (decelerating)
  • (D) At rest
Answer: (B)
Solution: Once tossed, the coin retains the horizontal velocity the train had at the instant of release (by inertia). While the coin is in the air, an accelerating train increases its speed and covers more horizontal distance than the coin, causing the coin to land behind the passenger.
Practice Question 2 (Numerical Application MCQ)

A constant net force acts on a body of mass \(2.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) initially at rest, moving it along a straight line. If the body covers \(40\,\mathrm{m}\) in \(4\,\mathrm{s}\), the magnitude of the applied force is:

  • (A) \(5.0\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(10.0\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(12.5\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(20.0\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (C)
Solution: From \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\) with \(u = 0\), \(40 = \frac{1}{2} a (4)^2 \implies 40 = 8a \implies a = 5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). By Newton's Second Law, \(F = ma = 2.5 \times 5 = 12.5\,\mathrm{N}\).
Practice Question 3 (Numerical Application MCQ)

A cricket ball of mass \(150\,\mathrm{g}\) moving horizontally at \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) is hit straight back along the same line at \(30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). If the bat and ball are in contact for \(0.015\,\mathrm{s}\), the average force exerted by the bat on the ball is:

  • (A) \(100\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(250\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(500\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(750\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (C)
Solution: Taking the rebound direction as positive, \(u = -20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(v = +30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). With \(m = 0.15\,\mathrm{kg}\), the impulse is \(J = m(v - u) = 0.15 \times (30 - (-20)) = 7.5\,\mathrm{N\,s}\). Thus, \(F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{7.5}{0.015} = 500\,\mathrm{N}\).
Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): When a book rests on a horizontal table, the weight of the book and the normal reaction exerted by the table on the book do not constitute an action–reaction pair.
Reason (R): Action and reaction forces always act on two different bodies, whereas the weight and normal reaction here act on the same body (the book).

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)
Solution: Both gravitational weight (by Earth on the book) and normal reaction (by the table on the book) act on the same object—the book. They are equal and opposite only because the book is in equilibrium, not because of Newton's Third Law.
Practice Question 5 (Conservation of Momentum MCQ)

A stationary shell of mass \(12\,\mathrm{kg}\) explodes into two fragments of masses \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(8\,\mathrm{kg}\). If the \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) fragment moves with a speed of \(18\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the speed of the \(8\,\mathrm{kg}\) fragment is:

  • (A) \(4.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(9.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(18.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(36.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)
Solution: Since the shell is initially at rest and no external force acts during the explosion, total momentum remains zero: \(m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = 0 \implies 4(18) = 8 v_2 \implies v_2 = 9.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) in the opposite direction.
Practice Question 6 (Equilibrium MCQ)

A block of mass \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\) is suspended from a ceiling by a light string. A horizontal force \(F\) is applied to the block so that the string makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the vertical in equilibrium. Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The magnitude of \(F\) is:

  • (A) \(10\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(15\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(15\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(30\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (A)
Solution: In equilibrium, \(T\cos 30^\circ = mg = 30\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(T\sin 30^\circ = F\). Dividing the two equations gives \(F = mg\tan 30^\circ = 30 \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = 10\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{N}\).
Practice Question 7 (Static Friction MCQ)

A block of mass \(5\,\mathrm{kg}\) rests on a rough horizontal floor with \(\mu_s = 0.4\) and \(\mu_k = 0.3\). A horizontal force of \(12\,\mathrm{N}\) is applied to the block. Assuming \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), the frictional force acting on the block is:

  • (A) \(20\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(15\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(12\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(0\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (C)
Solution: The maximum static friction is \((f_s)_{\max} = \mu_s mg = 0.4 \times 5 \times 10 = 20\,\mathrm{N}\). Since the applied force (\(12\,\mathrm{N}\)) is less than \(20\,\mathrm{N}\), the block does not move, and static friction adjusts to equal the applied force: \(f_s = 12\,\mathrm{N}\).
Practice Question 8 (Pulley–Block System MCQ)

Two blocks of masses \(6\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) are connected by a light inextensible string passing over a smooth, frictionless pulley. When released from rest, the tension in the string is (take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)):

  • (A) \(24\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(40\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(48\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(50\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (C)
Solution: Acceleration \(a = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}g = \frac{6 - 4}{6 + 4} \times 10 = 2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Using \(T = m_2(g + a) = 4(10 + 2) = 48\,\mathrm{N}\).
Practice Question 9 (Circular Motion MCQ)

A car negotiates a flat circular turn of radius \(50\,\mathrm{m}\). If the coefficient of static friction between the tyres and the road is \(0.5\), the maximum safe speed of the car without skidding is (take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)):

  • (A) \(5\sqrt{5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(5\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)
Solution: On a level road, \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s R g} = \sqrt{0.5 \times 50 \times 10} = \sqrt{250} = 5\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
Practice Question 10 (Statement-I / Statement-II)

Statement I: A box placed on the floor of an accelerating truck can remain stationary relative to the truck because static friction accelerates the box forward.
Statement II: Rolling friction between two surfaces is generally 2 to 3 orders of magnitude smaller than sliding friction for the same normal load.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
  • (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  • (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
Answer: (A)
Solution: Both statements are true. Friction opposes relative motion between surfaces in contact, so static friction accelerates the box along with the truck up to \(a_{\max} = \mu_s g\). Rolling friction is much smaller than sliding friction because contact deformation is minimal.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET Exam Traps to Avoid:
  • Blindly setting \(f_s = \mu_s N\): Static friction equals \(\mu_s N\) only at the verge of slipping. Always compare the driving force with \((f_s)_{\max}\) first.
  • Assuming \(N = mg\) everywhere: Normal reaction equals \(mg\) only on a horizontal surface with zero vertical acceleration and no other vertical forces.
  • Treating \(ma\) as a physical force: In \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m\mathbf{a}\), \(m\mathbf{a}\) is the effect of the net force, not an extra force to draw on an FBD in an inertial frame.
  • Zero velocity implies zero force: At the highest point of a vertical throw, \(v = 0\), yet \(F = mg \neq 0\) and \(a = g \neq 0\).
  • Confusing equilibrium forces with Third Law pairs: Forces acting on the same body cannot be an action–reaction pair.

Concise Final Recap

  • First Law: Zero net external force means zero acceleration (rest or uniform straight-line motion).
  • Second Law & Impulse: \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = d\mathbf{p}/dt = m\mathbf{a}\), and impulse \(\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{avg}}\Delta t = \Delta\mathbf{p}\).
  • Third Law & Momentum: Mutual forces \(\mathbf{F}_{AB} = -\mathbf{F}_{BA}\) act on different bodies; in an isolated system, total linear momentum is conserved.
  • Friction: \(f_s \le \mu_s N\) (self-adjusting) and \(f_k = \mu_k N\), with \(\mu_s = \tan\theta_{\max}\) at the angle of repose.
  • Circular Turns: Centripetal force is \(mv^2/R\); \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s R g}\) on a level road, and \(v_o = \sqrt{R g \tan\theta}\) on a banked road without friction.
