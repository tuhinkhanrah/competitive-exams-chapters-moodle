Work, Energy, and Power

A comprehensive, exam-oriented study guide covering scalar products, the work-energy theorem, conservative forces, mechanical energy conservation, power, and collisions.

1. Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

In competitive medical entrance examinations such as NEET UG, Work, Energy, and Power bridges Newton's laws of motion with rotational dynamics and gravitation. Mastery of energy methods allows you to solve complex motion problems without tracking time or intermediate accelerations.

  • Compute the scalar (dot) product of vectors and apply it to mechanical work and instantaneous power.
  • Distinguish between work done by constant forces and variable forces (both analytically and graphically).
  • Apply the Work-Energy Theorem to systems involving gravity, springs, and non-conservative forces like friction.
  • Identify conservative forces, relate force to potential energy gradients, and apply conservation of mechanical energy—including vertical circular motion.
  • Analyze one-dimensional and two-dimensional collisions using momentum and kinetic energy conservation.

2. Mathematical Prerequisite: The Scalar (Dot) Product

Physical quantities such as force (\(\mathbf{F}\)), displacement (\(\mathbf{d}\)), and velocity (\(\mathbf{v}\)) are vectors, whereas work, energy, and power are scalars. The scalar product (or dot product) combines two vectors to yield a scalar quantity.

\[\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A B \cos\theta\]

Here, \(A = |\mathbf{A}|\) and \(B = |\mathbf{B}|\) are the magnitudes of the vectors, and \(\theta\) is the angle between them when placed tail-to-tail (\(0° \le \theta \le 180°\)).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Geometric meaning of the scalar product as the magnitude of one vector multiplied by the orthogonal projection of the other along it.

Key Algebraic Properties

  • Commutative & Distributive: \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{A} \cdot (\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{C}\).
  • Orthogonal Unit Vectors: \(\hat{i} \cdot \hat{i} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{j} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{k} = 1\), while \(\hat{i} \cdot \hat{j} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{k} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{i} = 0\).
  • Component Form: For \(\mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{i} + A_y\hat{j} + A_z\hat{k}\) and \(\mathbf{B} = B_x\hat{i} + B_y\hat{j} + B_z\hat{k}\), \[\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z\]
  • Perpendicularity Test: If two non-zero vectors are mutually perpendicular (\(\theta = 90°\)), then \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\).

3. Mechanical Work: Constant and Variable Forces

In physics, work is done only when a force produces a displacement and has a non-zero component along that displacement. Simply holding a heavy load stationary or pushing against a rigid wall does zero mechanical work, even though internal metabolic energy is expended.

3.1 Work Done by a Constant Force

\[W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = (F \cos\theta)\, d\]
  • Positive Work (\(0° \le \theta < 90°\)): The force has a component in the direction of displacement (e.g., gravity acting on a freely falling stone).
  • Negative Work (\(90° < \theta \le 180°\)): The force opposes the displacement (e.g., kinetic friction acting on a sliding block, where \(\theta = 180°\) and \(W = -fd\)).
  • Zero Work (\(\theta = 90°\), or \(F = 0\), or \(d = 0\)): Gravity does zero work on a block sliding along a level table; centripetal force does zero work in uniform circular motion because it is always perpendicular to instantaneous velocity.
Action-Reaction Note: By Newton's third law, forces between two bodies A and B are equal and opposite (\(\mathbf{F}_{AB} = -\mathbf{F}_{BA}\)). However, their displacements need not be equal, so the work done by A on B is not necessarily the negative of the work done by B on A (\(W_{AB} + W_{BA} \neq 0\) in general).

3.2 Work Done by a Variable Force

When a force \(F(x)\) varies with position \(x\) in one dimension, the total displacement from \(x_i\) to \(x_f\) is divided into infinitesimal intervals \(dx\). Summing the elementary work \(dW = F(x)\,dx\) gives the definite integral:

\[W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,dx = \text{Signed Area under the } F\text{-}x \text{ curve}\]

Tutorial Diagram 2: Graphical evaluation of work done by a variable force. Areas above the position axis represent positive work; areas below represent negative work.

3.3 Units and Dimensions of Work and Energy

Work and energy share the dimensional formula \([\mathrm{M L^2 T^{-2}}]\) and the SI unit joule (J), where \(1\,\mathrm{J} = 1\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\).

Unit Context / Usage Equivalent in Joules (J)

4. Kinetic Energy and the Work-Energy Theorem

Kinetic energy (\(K\)) is the energy possessed by an object due to its motion. It is always a non-negative scalar quantity:

\[K = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}\]

where \(p = mv\) is the magnitude of linear momentum. The relation \(K = \frac{p^2}{2m}\) (or \(p = \sqrt{2mK}\)) is frequently tested in NEET UG for comparing momenta or kinetic energies of different particles.

The Work-Energy Theorem: The change in kinetic energy of a particle equals the net work done on it by all forces (conservative, non-conservative, external, and internal) acting over the displacement: \[W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = K_f - K_i = \frac{1}{2}m v_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}m v_i^2\]

Key points for problem solving:

  • It is an integral form of Newton's second law (\(\int F\,dx = \int mv\,dv\)) and holds for both constant and variable forces in 1D, 2D, and 3D.
  • It is valid in all inertial reference frames. In a non-inertial frame, it remains valid provided the work done by the pseudo-force is included in \(W_{\text{net}}\).
  • Note that kinetic energy and work are frame-dependent quantities because displacement and velocity depend on the observer's frame of reference.

5. Conservative Forces, Potential Energy, and Mechanical Energy

Forces in mechanics are classified into two categories based on whether the work they do depends on the path taken:

Property Conservative Force Non-Conservative Force
Path dependence Work depends only on initial and final positions. Work depends on the actual path followed.
Closed-loop work \(\oint \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0\) for any closed path. \(\oint \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} \neq 0\) in general.
Potential energy A potential energy function \(V\) can be defined. No potential energy function can be defined.
Examples Gravitational force, electrostatic force, ideal spring force. Kinetic friction, viscous drag, air resistance.

5.1 Potential Energy and Force Gradient

Potential energy \(V(x)\) is stored energy associated with the position or configuration of a system under a conservative force. The change in potential energy is the negative of the work done by the conservative force:

\[\Delta V = V_f - V_i = -\int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,dx \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad F(x) = -\frac{dV}{dx}\]
  • Equilibrium Condition: At equilibrium, \(F = -\frac{dV}{dx} = 0\). If \(\frac{d^2V}{dx^2} > 0\) (local minimum of \(V\)), equilibrium is stable; if \(\frac{d^2V}{dx^2} < 0\) (local maximum), it is unstable.
  • Reference Zero: The absolute value of \(V\) is arbitrary up to an additive constant; only \(\Delta V\) is physically meaningful. Near Earth's surface (\(h \ll R_E\)), choosing \(V = 0\) at ground level gives \(V(h) = mgh\).

5.2 Elastic Potential Energy of a Spring

An ideal massless spring obeys Hooke's law, \(F_s = -kx\), where \(k\) is the spring constant (\(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\)) and \(x\) is the displacement from the natural (unstretched) length. Choosing \(V(0) = 0\), the elastic potential energy stored for extension or compression \(x\) is:

\[V(x) = \frac{1}{2}k x^2\]

Tutorial Diagram 3: Spring-mass system and the complementary parabolic variation of potential energy (V) and kinetic energy (K) between turning points ±xₘ.

5.3 Conservation of Mechanical Energy

The sum of kinetic and potential energies is the total mechanical energy, \(E = K + V\). If only conservative forces do work on a system, total mechanical energy remains constant:

\[K_i + V_i = K_f + V_f \qquad (\text{when } W_{nc} = 0)\]

When non-conservative forces (such as friction) or external forces also perform work \(W_{nc}\), the change in total mechanical energy equals that work:

\[E_f - E_i = W_{nc}\]

5.4 Vertical Circular Motion (High-Yield NEET Application)

Consider a bob of mass \(m\) attached to a light inextensible string of length \(L\) moving in a vertical circle. Tension \(T\) is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does zero work. Taking \(V = 0\) at the lowest point A, mechanical energy is conserved:

  • Condition to just complete the vertical loop: At the topmost point C, the string is on the verge of going slack (\(T_C = 0\)), so gravity alone provides the centripetal force: \[mg = \frac{m v_C^2}{L} \implies v_{C,\min} = \sqrt{gL}\]
  • Minimum speed at the horizontal position B (height \(L\)): \[\frac{1}{2}m v_B^2 + mgL = \frac{1}{2}m v_C^2 + 2mgL \implies v_{B,\min} = \sqrt{3gL}\]
  • Minimum speed at the lowest point A (height \(0\)): \[\frac{1}{2}m v_A^2 = \frac{1}{2}m v_C^2 + 2mgL \implies v_{A,\min} = \sqrt{5gL}\]
  • Tension Difference: For any complete vertical circle with the string remaining taut, the difference in tension between the lowest and highest points is always \(T_A - T_C = 6mg\).

6. Power

Power measures how rapidly work is done or energy is transferred. It is a scalar quantity with dimensions \([\mathrm{M L^2 T^{-3}}]\) and SI unit watt (W), where \(1\,\mathrm{W} = 1\,\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}\). In automobiles and machinery, horsepower is also used: \(1\,\mathrm{hp} = 746\,\mathrm{W}\).

\[P_{\text{av}} = \frac{W}{\Delta t} \qquad \text{and} \qquad P_{\text{inst}} = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = Fv\cos\theta\]
  • Constant Acceleration vs. Constant Power: If a body starts from rest under constant acceleration \(a\), then \(v = at\) and \(P = mav = ma^2t\), so \(P \propto t\). Conversely, if a body starts from rest under a source of constant power \(P\), then \(v \propto t^{1/2}\) and displacement \(x \propto t^{3/2}\).

7. Collisions in One and Two Dimensions

During a collision, two bodies exert strong mutual impulsive forces over a very short contact interval \(\Delta t\). Because these forces are internal and obey Newton's third law (\(\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21}\)), the total linear momentum of the system is conserved at every instant of any collision (provided no net external impulsive force acts).

Collision Type Linear Momentum Kinetic Energy (Before vs. After) Post-Collision Behavior
Elastic Conserved Conserved (\(K_f = K_i\)) Bodies separate; no permanent deformation.
Inelastic Conserved Not conserved (\(K_f < K_i\)) Part of KE converts to heat, sound, or deformation.
Completely Inelastic Conserved Maximum KE loss Bodies stick together and move with a common velocity.

7.1 One-Dimensional (Head-On) Collisions

Consider a mass \(m_1\) moving with initial velocity \(v_{1i}\) colliding head-on with a stationary mass \(m_2\) (\(v_{2i} = 0\)):

  • Completely Inelastic Collision: The common final velocity is \(v_f = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i}\), and the loss in kinetic energy is: \[\Delta K = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right)v_{1i}^2\]
  • Elastic Collision: Solving momentum conservation and kinetic energy conservation simultaneously yields: \[v_{1f} = \left(\frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right)v_{1i}, \qquad v_{2f} = \left(\frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\right)v_{1i}\]
  • Special Elastic Cases:
    1. Equal masses (\(m_1 = m_2\)): The bodies exchange velocities (\(v_{1f} = 0\), \(v_{2f} = v_{1i}\)).
    2. Heavy target (\(m_2 \gg m_1\)): The light body rebounds with nearly the same speed (\(v_{1f} \approx -v_{1i}\), \(v_{2f} \approx 0\)).
    3. Fractional energy transfer to target: \(\frac{K_{2f}}{K_{1i}} = \frac{4m_1 m_2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2}\), which is maximum (\(100\%\)) when \(m_1 = m_2\).

7.2 Two-Dimensional (Glancing) Collisions

When a moving mass \(m_1\) strikes a stationary mass \(m_2\) off-center, the bodies scatter at angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) relative to the initial line of motion.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Glancing collision in the x-y plane. Momentum is conserved independently along both the x and y axes.

Conservation of momentum along the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes gives:

\[m_1 v_{1i} = m_1 v_{1f}\cos\theta_1 + m_2 v_{2f}\cos\theta_2, \qquad 0 = m_1 v_{1f}\sin\theta_1 - m_2 v_{2f}\sin\theta_2\]

Key Result: If two equal masses (\(m_1 = m_2\)) undergo a glancing elastic collision with one initially at rest, they always fly off at right angles to each other (\(\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 90°\)).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Work and Angle Using the Dot Product

A hypothetical particle is displaced from position \(\mathbf{r}_1 = (2\hat{i} - \hat{j} + 3\hat{k})\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(\mathbf{r}_2 = (5\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 3\hat{k})\,\mathrm{m}\) under a constant force \(\mathbf{F} = (8\hat{i} + 6\hat{j})\,\mathrm{N}\). Determine the work done by the force and the angle between \(\mathbf{F}\) and the displacement.

Solution:

First, find the displacement vector \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1\):

\[ \mathbf{d} = (5 - 2)\hat{i} + (3 - (-1))\hat{j} + (3 - 3)\hat{k} = (3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j})\,\mathrm{m} \]

The work done is the scalar product \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d}\):

\[ W = (8)(3) + (6)(4) + (0)(0) = 24 + 24 = 48\,\mathrm{J} \]

Next, compute the magnitudes \(F = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(d = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\,\mathrm{m}\). Using \(W = Fd\cos\theta\):

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{W}{Fd} = \frac{48}{10 \times 5} = 0.96 \implies \theta = \cos^{-1}(0.96) \]
Example 2: Work Done by a Variable Force and Speed Gain

A block of mass \(m = 3\,\mathrm{kg}\), initially at rest at \(x = 0\) on a smooth horizontal track, is pushed by a position-dependent force \(F(x) = (4x + 6)\,\mathrm{N}\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 3\,\mathrm{m}\). Thereafter, the force decreases linearly from \(18\,\mathrm{N}\) at \(x = 3\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(0\,\mathrm{N}\) at \(x = 5\,\mathrm{m}\). Find the total work done and the final speed of the block at \(x = 5\,\mathrm{m}\).

Solution:

From \(x = 0\) to \(x = 3\,\mathrm{m}\), integrate \(F(x)\):

\[ W_1 = \int_0^3 (4x + 6)\,dx = \left[2x^2 + 6x\right]_0^3 = 2(9) + 6(3) = 36\,\mathrm{J} \]

From \(x = 3\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(x = 5\,\mathrm{m}\), the \(F\)-\(x\) graph forms a right triangle of base \(2\,\mathrm{m}\) and height \(18\,\mathrm{N}\):

\[ W_2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 18 = 18\,\mathrm{J} \]

Total work done: \(W_{\text{total}} = 36 + 18 = 54\,\mathrm{J}\). By the work-energy theorem (\(K_i = 0\)):

\[ \frac{1}{2} m v_f^2 = 54\,\mathrm{J} \implies v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 54}{3}} = \sqrt{36} = 6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]
Example 3: Applying the Work-Energy Theorem with Friction and a Spring

A block of mass \(m = 2\,\mathrm{kg}\) slides along a rough horizontal floor (\(\mu_k = 0.5\), assuming \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)) with an initial speed \(v_i = 5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and strikes a light horizontal spring of spring constant \(k = 160\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\). Calculate the maximum compression \(x_m\) of the spring.

Solution:

At maximum compression, the block comes momentarily to rest (\(K_f = 0\)). Both the spring force and kinetic friction oppose the motion:

\[ W_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{spring}} + W_{\text{friction}} = -\frac{1}{2}k x_m^2 - \mu_k m g x_m \]

Applying the work-energy theorem, \(W_{\text{net}} = K_f - K_i\):

\[ -\frac{1}{2}(160)x_m^2 - (0.5)(2)(10)x_m = 0 - \frac{1}{2}(2)(5)^2 \] \[ 80 x_m^2 + 10 x_m - 25 = 0 \implies 16 x_m^2 + 2 x_m - 5 = 0 \]

Solving the quadratic equation for \(x_m > 0\):

\[ x_m = \frac{-2 + \sqrt{4 + 320}}{32} = \frac{-2 + 18}{32} = 0.5\,\mathrm{m} \]
Example 4: Vertical Circular Motion and String Tension

A small stone of mass \(m = 0.4\,\mathrm{kg}\) is attached to a light string of length \(L = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}\) and given the minimum horizontal speed at its lowest point A required to complete a vertical circle (assuming \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)). Find (a) the speed at A, (b) the speed at the horizontal position B, and (c) the tension in the string at A and B.

Solution:

(a) The critical speed at the lowest point A is:

\[ v_A = \sqrt{5gL} = \sqrt{5 \times 10 \times 2.0} = \sqrt{100} = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

(b) At the horizontal position B (height \(L\) above A), the speed is:

\[ v_B = \sqrt{3gL} = \sqrt{3 \times 10 \times 2.0} = \sqrt{60} = 2\sqrt{15}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 7.75\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

(c) Applying Newton's second law radially at A and B:

\[ T_A = mg + \frac{mv_A^2}{L} = 6mg = 6 \times 0.4 \times 10 = 24\,\mathrm{N} \] \[ T_B = \frac{mv_B^2}{L} = 3mg = 3 \times 0.4 \times 10 = 12\,\mathrm{N} \]
Example 5: Calculating Pump Power and Efficiency

A motor-driven pump lifts \(1200\,\mathrm{kg}\) of water per minute from a well \(15\,\mathrm{m}\) deep and ejects it at a speed of \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (assuming \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)). If the pump operates at \(80\%\) efficiency, find the electrical input power consumed.

Solution:

The mass flow rate is \(\frac{dm}{dt} = \frac{1200\,\mathrm{kg}}{60\,\mathrm{s}} = 20\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}\). The useful mechanical power output supplies both gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy:

\[ P_{\text{out}} = \frac{dm}{dt}\left(gh + \frac{1}{2}v^2\right) = 20\left(10 \times 15 + \frac{1}{2}(10)^2\right) = 20(150 + 50) = 4000\,\mathrm{W} \]

With efficiency \(\eta = 0.80\), the electrical input power is:

\[ P_{\text{in}} = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{\eta} = \frac{4000}{0.80} = 5000\,\mathrm{W} = 5\,\mathrm{kW} \]
Example 6: One-Dimensional Elastic Collision and Energy Transfer

A sphere of mass \(m_1 = 2\,\mathrm{kg}\) moving at \(6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) collides head-on and elastically with a stationary sphere of mass \(m_2 = 4\,\mathrm{kg}\). Find their velocities after collision and the fraction of initial kinetic energy transferred to \(m_2\).

Solution:

Using the 1D elastic collision formulas with \(v_{2i} = 0\):

\[ v_{1f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i} = \frac{2 - 4}{2 + 4}(6) = -2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \quad (\text{rebounds}) \] \[ v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}v_{1i} = \frac{2(2)}{2 + 4}(6) = 4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

The fraction of initial kinetic energy transferred to \(m_2\) is:

\[ \frac{K_{2f}}{K_{1i}} = \frac{4m_1 m_2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2} = \frac{4(2)(4)}{(2 + 4)^2} = \frac{32}{36} = \frac{8}{9} \approx 88.9\% \]

Practice Question 1

A particle moves from \((1, 2, -1)\,\mathrm{m}\) to \((3, 0, 2)\,\mathrm{m}\) under the action of a constant force \(\mathbf{F} = (4\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 2\hat{k})\,\mathrm{N}\). The work done by this force is:

  • (A) \(4\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(-4\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(10\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(-10\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Displacement \(\mathbf{d} = (3 - 1)\hat{i} + (0 - 2)\hat{j} + (2 - (-1))\hat{k} = (2\hat{i} - 2\hat{j} + 3\hat{k})\,\mathrm{m}\). Work \(W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = 4(2) + 3(-2) + (-2)(3) = 8 - 6 - 6 = -4\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 2

If the linear momentum of a moving body is increased by \(20\%\), the percentage increase in its kinetic energy is:

  • (A) \(20\%\)
  • (B) \(40\%\)
  • (C) \(44\%\)
  • (D) \(10\%\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Kinetic energy \(K = \frac{p^2}{2m}\), so \(K \propto p^2\). When \(p' = 1.20 p\), the new kinetic energy is \(K' = (1.20)^2 K = 1.44 K\). Therefore, the percentage increase is \((1.44 - 1) \times 100\% = 44\%\).

Practice Question 3

A force \(F = (3x^2 - 2x + 5)\,\mathrm{N}\) acts on a particle along the \(x\)-axis. The work done by this force in displacing the particle from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 2\,\mathrm{m}\) is:

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(14\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(18\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(12\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: \(W = \int_0^2 (3x^2 - 2x + 5)\,dx = \left[x^3 - x^2 + 5x\right]_0^2 = (8 - 4 + 10) - 0 = 14\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 4

A bullet of mass \(20\,\mathrm{g}\) strikes a wooden block at \(100\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and comes to rest after penetrating \(5\,\mathrm{cm}\) into it. The average resistive force offered by the block is:

  • (A) \(1000\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(2000\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(4000\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(500\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Using the work-energy theorem, \(-F_{\text{av}} d = 0 - \frac{1}{2}mv^2\). With \(m = 0.02\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(v = 100\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(d = 0.05\,\mathrm{m}\): \(F_{\text{av}} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}(0.02)(100)^2}{0.05} = \frac{100}{0.05} = 2000\,\mathrm{N}\).

Practice Question 5

Two springs A and B have spring constants \(k_A = 200\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) and \(k_B = 400\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\). If both springs are stretched by the same force \(F\), the ratio of the elastic potential energy stored in A to that in B (\(U_A : U_B\)) is:

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(2 : 1\)
  • (C) \(1 : 4\)
  • (D) \(4 : 1\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Since \(F = kx\), potential energy \(U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{F^2}{2k}\). For the same applied force \(F\), \(U \propto \frac{1}{k}\). Thus, \(\frac{U_A}{U_B} = \frac{k_B}{k_A} = \frac{400}{200} = 2 : 1\).

Practice Question 6

The potential energy of a particle in a conservative force field is given by \(U(x) = (4x^2 - 16x + 20)\,\mathrm{J}\), where \(x\) is in meters. The equilibrium position of the particle is at:

  • (A) \(x = 1\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(x = 2\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(x = 4\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(x = 0\,\mathrm{m}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: At equilibrium, the net force is zero: \(F = -\frac{dU}{dx} = -(8x - 16) = 0 \implies x = 2\,\mathrm{m}\). Since \(\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} = 8 > 0\), this is a stable equilibrium position.

Practice Question 7

A bob of mass \(m\) is whirled in a vertical circle of radius \(R\) using a light inextensible string. Provided the string remains taut throughout, the difference between the tension at the lowest point and the tension at the highest point is:

  • (A) \(2mg\)
  • (B) \(4mg\)
  • (C) \(5mg\)
  • (D) \(6mg\)
Answer: (D)

Solution: At the lowest point, \(T_L = \frac{mv_L^2}{R} + mg\); at the highest point, \(T_H = \frac{mv_H^2}{R} - mg\). Subtracting gives \(T_L - T_H = \frac{m}{R}(v_L^2 - v_H^2) + 2mg\). By conservation of mechanical energy, \(v_L^2 - v_H^2 = 4gR\), so \(T_L - T_H = 4mg + 2mg = 6mg\).

Practice Question 8

A particle of mass \(m\) starts from rest and moves in a straight line under the influence of a source delivering constant power \(P\). Its displacement \(x\) in time \(t\) is proportional to:

  • (A) \(t^{1/2}\)
  • (B) \(t\)
  • (C) \(t^{3/2}\)
  • (D) \(t^2\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Since \(P = \frac{dK}{dt} = \text{constant}\), integrating gives \(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = Pt \implies v = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m}}\,t^{1/2}\). Integrating velocity with respect to time gives \(x = \int v\,dt \propto t^{3/2}\).

Practice Question 9 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): In an elastic collision between two billiard balls, total linear momentum is conserved at every instant during the collision, whereas total kinetic energy is not constant during the brief contact interval.
Reason (R): During the contact interval, part of the kinetic energy is temporarily stored as elastic potential energy of deformation.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Internal impulsive forces always obey Newton's third law, conserving linear momentum at every instant. However, kinetic energy is temporarily converted into elastic potential energy while the balls are deformed during contact, and is fully recovered only after separation.

Practice Question 10

A \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) block moving with a speed of \(6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) collides head-on with a stationary \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) block, and the two blocks stick together after the impact. The loss in kinetic energy during the collision is:

  • (A) \(12\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(24\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(36\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(48\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: For a completely inelastic collision with \(v_{2i} = 0\), the kinetic energy lost is \(\Delta K = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right)v_{1i}^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4 \times 2}{4 + 2}\right)(6)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{8}{6}\right)(36) = 24\,\mathrm{J}\).

9. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Confusing kW and kWh: Kilowatt (\(\mathrm{kW}\)) is a unit of power, whereas kilowatt-hour (\(\mathrm{kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J}\)) is a unit of energy.
  • Spring energy comparison trap: If two springs are stretched by the same distance \(x\), \(U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 \propto k\) (stiffer spring stores more energy). If stretched by the same force \(F\), \(U = \frac{F^2}{2k} \propto \frac{1}{k}\) (softer spring stores more energy).
  • Applying \(K_i + V_i = K_f + V_f\) blindly: Mechanical energy conservation fails if friction, air drag, or an external non-conservative push does work. Use \(W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K\) or \(E_f - E_i = W_{nc}\) instead.
  • Momentum vs. Kinetic Energy in Collisions: Total linear momentum is conserved in all isolated collisions; kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions (and only when comparing before and after contact).

10. Concise Final Recap

Concept Core Equation Key Condition / Remark
Work (Constant / Variable) \(W = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d} = \int F(x)\,dx\) Signed area under \(F\)-\(x\) curve; scalar.
Kinetic Energy \(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}\) Always \(\ge 0\); frame-dependent.
Work-Energy Theorem \(W_{\text{net}} = K_f - K_i\) Includes all forces (and pseudo-forces if non-inertial).
Potential Energy \(F(x) = -\frac{dV}{dx}\), \(V_{\text{spring}} = \frac{1}{2}kx^2\) Defined only for conservative forces.
Instantaneous Power \(P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) \(1\,\mathrm{hp} = 746\,\mathrm{W}\).
1D Elastic Collision (\(v_{2i}=0\)) \(v_{1f} = \frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}v_{1i}\), \(v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1+m_2}v_{1i}\) Equal masses exchange velocities; \(\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 90°\) in 2D.
