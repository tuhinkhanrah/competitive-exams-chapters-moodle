Gravitation

Planetary Motion, Universal Gravitation, Gravitational Potential Energy, Escape Speed, and Satellite Dynamics

Learning Objectives and Overview

Gravitation governs both everyday terrestrial phenomena—such as falling objects and planetary weight—and large-scale celestial mechanics, including the orbits of planets and artificial satellites. For NEET UG, questions from this chapter frequently test proportional reasoning, graphs of gravitational field and potential, energy conservation in radial fields, and satellite orbital parameters.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial
  • Kepler's Three Laws: Elliptical orbits, conservation of angular momentum (areal velocity), and the period–semi-major axis relation.
  • Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation: Vector formulation, principle of superposition, and the gravitational behavior of uniform spherical shells and solid spheres.
  • Acceleration due to Gravity: Surface value \(g\) and its exact and approximate variations with altitude \(h\) and depth \(d\).
  • Gravitational Potential and Potential Energy: Conservative nature of gravity, potential due to point masses and spheres, and multi-particle potential energy.
  • Escape Speed and Satellite Motion: Minimum escape speed, orbital velocity, time period, mechanical energy of bound orbits, and weightlessness.

Planetary Motion and Kepler's Laws

Early astronomical models evolved from Earth-centered (geocentric) schemes, which required complex combinations of circles to explain planetary paths, to Sun-centered (heliocentric) models proposed by Aryabhatta and later formalized by Copernicus. Using decades of naked-eye planetary observations compiled by Tycho Brahe, Johannes Kepler formulated three empirical laws describing planetary motion.

1. Law of Orbits

Every planet revolves around the Sun in an elliptical orbit, with the Sun located at one of the two foci of the ellipse. A circular orbit is simply the special case in which both foci merge at the centre.

  • Perihelion (\(P\)): The point on the orbit closest to the Sun (distance \(r_p\)).
  • Aphelion (\(A\)): The point on the orbit farthest from the Sun (distance \(r_a\)).
  • Semi-major axis (\(a\)): Half the length of the major axis, so \(2a = r_p + r_a\).

2. Law of Areas

The radius vector drawn from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time. In other words, the areal velocity \(\Delta A / \Delta t\) is constant throughout the orbit. Consequently, a planet moves fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion.

Areal Velocity and Angular Momentum Conservation \[ \frac{\Delta \vec{A}}{\Delta t} = \frac{1}{2}(\vec{r} \times \vec{v}) = \frac{\vec{L}}{2m} = \text{constant} \]

Because gravitational attraction is a central force (directed strictly along the line joining the planet and the Sun), it produces zero torque about the Sun (\(\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} = \vec{0}\)). Hence, the orbital angular momentum \(\vec{L}\) is conserved. At perihelion and aphelion, velocity is perpendicular to the position vector, giving:

\[ m v_p r_p = m v_a r_a \implies \frac{v_p}{v_a} = \frac{r_a}{r_p} \]

3. Law of Periods

The square of the orbital time period \(T\) of any planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis \(a\) of its elliptical orbit:

Kepler's Third Law \[ T^2 \propto a^3 \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{T^2}{a^3} = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM_S} = \text{constant} \]

Here \(M_S\) is the mass of the central body (the Sun for planets, or Earth for Earth satellites). Notice that the constant \(T^2/a^3\) depends only on the mass of the central body, not on the mass of the orbiting body.

Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation

Comparing the centripetal acceleration of the Moon with the acceleration of falling objects at Earth's surface led Isaac Newton to deduce that gravitational attraction follows an inverse-square law. Every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the separation between them.

Scalar and Vector Form \[ F = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}, \qquad \vec{F}_{21} = -G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{r}_{12} = -G\frac{m_1 m_2}{|\vec{r}_{12}|^3}\vec{r}_{12} \]

Here \(\vec{F}_{21}\) is the gravitational force on \(m_2\) due to \(m_1\), and \(\vec{r}_{12} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1\) points from \(m_1\) to \(m_2\). The negative sign indicates that gravity is always attractive. By Newton's third law, \(\vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}\).

  • Universal Gravitational Constant (\(G\)): \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,kg^{-2}}\), with dimensional formula \([\mathrm{M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}}]\). It is independent of the intervening medium, temperature, or chemical nature of the masses.
  • Experimental Determination: Henry Cavendish measured \(G\) using a sensitive torsion balance, where the gravitational torque between large stationary lead spheres and small suspended spheres twisted a fine wire through a measurable angle \(\theta\) until balanced by the wire's restoring torque \(\tau\theta\).
  • Principle of Superposition: The net gravitational force on a point mass due to multiple masses is the vector sum of the individual forces exerted by each mass independently: \(\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \sum_{i} \vec{F}_i\).

Gravitational Shell Theorems

For spherically symmetric mass distributions (uniform spherical shells or solid spheres), calculus yields two indispensable results:

  1. Outside a Spherical Shell or Solid Sphere: The body attracts an external point mass as if its entire mass were concentrated at its geometric centre.
  2. Inside a Uniform Spherical Shell: The net gravitational force on a point mass placed anywhere inside a hollow spherical shell of uniform density is strictly zero, because forces from opposite regions of the shell cancel completely.
Key Distinction: No Gravitational Shielding

Although a hollow spherical shell exerts zero net force on a particle inside it, it cannot shield the interior particle from the gravitational pull of bodies located outside the shell. Unlike electrostatic shielding by a conductor, gravitational shielding does not exist.

Acceleration due to Gravity and Its Variations

Treating Earth as a uniform sphere of mass \(M_E\) and radius \(R_E\), the gravitational force on a mass \(m\) at its surface is \(F = GM_E m / R_E^2\). Equating this to \(mg\) gives the surface acceleration due to gravity:

Surface Gravity in Terms of Mass and Mean Density \(\rho\) \[ g = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2} = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R_E \]

Notice that \(g\) is independent of the mass \(m\) of the falling body. Knowing \(g\), \(R_E\), and \(G\) allows us to determine the mass of Earth—the reason Cavendish is often said to have "weighed the Earth."

Variation with Altitude \(h\)

At height \(h\) above Earth's surface, the distance from Earth's centre is \(r = R_E + h\). Since the point lies outside Earth, all of Earth's mass acts from the centre:

\[ g(h) = \frac{GM_E}{(R_E + h)^2} = \frac{g}{\left(1 + \frac{h}{R_E}\right)^2} \]

For small altitudes (\(h \ll R_E\)), binomial expansion \((1 + h/R_E)^{-2} \approx 1 - 2h/R_E\) gives:

\[ g(h) \approx g\left(1 - \frac{2h}{R_E}\right) \qquad (h \ll R_E) \]

Variation with Depth \(d\)

At depth \(d\) below the surface (distance \(r = R_E - d\) from the centre), Earth can be split into an inner solid sphere of radius \(r = R_E - d\) and an outer spherical shell of thickness \(d\). By the shell theorem, the outer shell exerts zero net force. Only the inner sphere of mass \(M_s = M_E (r/R_E)^3\) contributes:

\[ g(d) = \frac{GM_s}{r^2} = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^3}(R_E - d) = g\left(1 - \frac{d}{R_E}\right) = g\frac{r}{R_E} \]

At the centre of Earth (\(d = R_E\), \(r = 0\)), \(g = 0\). Thus, assuming uniform density, \(g\) is maximum at Earth's surface and decreases whether you move upward or downward.

Gravitational Potential and Potential Energy

Because the gravitational force is conservative, the work done by or against gravity depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the path taken. Choosing the reference zero of potential energy at infinite separation (\(U(\infty) = 0\)), the gravitational potential energy of two point masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) separated by distance \(r\) is always negative (reflecting an attractive interaction):

Gravitational Potential Energy and Potential \[ U(r) = -\frac{Gm_1 m_2}{r}, \qquad V(r) = \frac{U(r)}{m} = -\frac{GM}{r} \]
  • Gravitational Potential (\(V\)): Defined as the gravitational potential energy per unit test mass, or the work done per unit mass in bringing a test mass slowly from infinity to that point. Its SI unit is \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) and its dimensions are \([\mathrm{L^2T^{-2}}]\).
  • System of Particles: By superposition, the total gravitational potential energy of an \(N\)-particle system is the scalar sum over all distinct pairs of particles: \[ U_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i < j} \left(-\frac{Gm_i m_j}{r_{ij}}\right) \] For example, a system of \(N\) particles has \(\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\) pairs.
  • Connection to \(mgh\): When a mass \(m\) is lifted from Earth's surface (\(r_1 = R_E\)) to a small height \(h \ll R_E\) (\(r_2 = R_E + h\)), the exact change in potential energy reduces to: \[ \Delta U = GM_E m \left(\frac{1}{R_E} - \frac{1}{R_E + h}\right) = \frac{GM_E m h}{R_E(R_E + h)} \approx \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2}mh = mgh \]

Escape Speed

The escape speed (\(v_e\)) is the minimum initial speed with which a body must be projected from a planet's surface so that it just reaches infinity with zero residual speed and never falls back. Applying conservation of mechanical energy between the surface (\(r = R_E\)) and infinity (\(E(\infty) = 0\)):

\[ \frac{1}{2}m v_e^2 - \frac{GM_E m}{R_E} = 0 \]
Escape Speed from a Spherical Planet of Mass \(M\) and Radius \(R\) \[ v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} = \sqrt{2gR} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}\pi G \rho}\,R \]
  • Numerical Value for Earth: With \(g \approx 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(R_E \approx 6.4 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m}\), \(v_e \approx 11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\).
  • Independence: Escape speed is completely independent of the mass of the projectile and the angle (direction) of projection (neglecting atmospheric drag and Earth's rotation).
  • Excess Speed at Infinity: If a body is launched with speed \(v > v_e\), its speed \(v_\infty\) far away from the planet is given by energy conservation: \[ \frac{1}{2}v^2 - \frac{1}{2}v_e^2 = \frac{1}{2}v_\infty^2 \implies v_\infty = \sqrt{v^2 - v_e^2} \]
  • Planetary Atmospheres: The Moon's escape speed is only about \(2.3\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). Gas molecules at lunar daytime temperatures easily exceed this speed, which is why the Moon has no atmosphere.

Satellite Motion, Orbital Energy, and Weightlessness

For a satellite of mass \(m\) revolving in a circular orbit of radius \(r = R_E + h\) around Earth, the required centripetal force is provided entirely by Earth's gravitational pull:

\[ \frac{mv_o^2}{r} = \frac{GM_E m}{r^2} \]
Orbital Speed and Time Period \[ v_o = \sqrt{\frac{GM_E}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{gR_E^2}{R_E + h}}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v_o} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM_E}} \]
  • Near-Earth Satellite (\(h \ll R_E\)): \(v_o \approx \sqrt{gR_E} \approx 7.9\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) and \(T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{R_E/g} \approx 85\,\mathrm{min}\).
  • Relation to Escape Speed: At the surface, \(v_e = \sqrt{2}\,v_o\). More generally, at any orbital radius \(r\), the local escape speed is \(\sqrt{2}\) times the circular orbital speed at that radius.

Energy of an Orbiting Satellite

Using \(v_o^2 = GM_E / r\), the kinetic, potential, and total mechanical energies of a satellite in a circular orbit of radius \(r\) are:

\[ K = \frac{1}{2}mv_o^2 = +\frac{GM_E m}{2r}, \qquad U = -\frac{GM_E m}{r}, \qquad E = K + U = -\frac{GM_E m}{2r} \]

Notice the exact algebraic link among the three energies for any circular orbit:

Energy Ratios in a Circular Orbit \[ E = -K = \frac{U}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad |E| : K : |U| = 1 : 1 : 2 \]

The negative total energy signifies a bound system. The positive quantity \(|E| = \frac{GM_E m}{2r}\) is the binding energy—the minimum energy that must be supplied to free the satellite from Earth's gravity. Note also that if a satellite moves to a higher orbit (\(r\) increases), its kinetic energy decreases (it slows down), while its potential energy and total energy increase (become less negative).

Weightlessness in Orbit

An astronaut inside an orbiting spacecraft feels weightless not because Earth's gravity is zero there, but because both the astronaut and the spacecraft are in continuous free fall around Earth with the exact same centripetal acceleration \(g(h)\). Consequently, the normal reaction force between the astronaut and the spacecraft floor is zero.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Applying Superposition to Find Net Gravitational Force

Problem: Three particles of masses \(m\), \(m\), and \(2m\) are placed at corners \(A(0, a)\), \(C(a, 0)\), and \(D(a, a)\) of a square of side \(a\), respectively. Find the magnitude and direction of the net gravitational force on a particle of mass \(m\) placed at the fourth corner \(B(0, 0)\). (Assume \(a\) and \(m\) are fixed positive constants.)

Solution:

The particle at \(B(0, 0)\) experiences three attractive forces:

  • Due to mass \(m\) at \(A(0, a)\): \(\vec{F}_A = \dfrac{Gm^2}{a^2}\hat{j}\) (along \(+y\)).
  • Due to mass \(m\) at \(C(a, 0)\): \(\vec{F}_C = \dfrac{Gm^2}{a^2}\hat{i}\) (along \(+x\)).
  • Due to mass \(2m\) at \(D(a, a)\), at distance \(BD = \sqrt{2}\,a\): \[ F_D = \frac{G(2m)(m)}{(\sqrt{2}\,a)^2} = \frac{2Gm^2}{2a^2} = \frac{Gm^2}{a^2} \] directed along the diagonal \(BD\) (at \(45°\) to the \(+x\)-axis).

First combine \(\vec{F}_A\) and \(\vec{F}_C\), which are perpendicular and equal in magnitude:

\[ F_{AC} = \sqrt{F_A^2 + F_C^2} = \sqrt{2}\,\frac{Gm^2}{a^2} \]

Because \(F_A = F_C\), \(\vec{F}_{AC}\) also points along the diagonal \(BD\) (\(45°\) to the \(+x\)-axis). Adding \(F_D\) in the same direction gives:

\[ F_{\text{net}} = F_{AC} + F_D = (\sqrt{2} + 1)\frac{Gm^2}{a^2} \quad \text{directed along } BD. \]

Example 2: Comparing Gravity at Altitude and Depth

Problem: At what depth \(d\) below Earth's surface is the acceleration due to gravity equal to its value at a height \(h = 32\,\mathrm{km}\) above the surface? (Take Earth's radius \(R_E = 6400\,\mathrm{km}\) as a standard assumption and treat Earth as uniform.)

Solution:

Since \(h = 32\,\mathrm{km} \ll R_E = 6400\,\mathrm{km}\), we can use the linear approximation for altitude:

\[ g(h) \approx g\left(1 - \frac{2h}{R_E}\right) \]

At depth \(d\) below the surface, the exact expression for a uniform Earth is:

\[ g(d) = g\left(1 - \frac{d}{R_E}\right) \]

Setting \(g(d) = g(h)\):

\[ 1 - \frac{d}{R_E} = 1 - \frac{2h}{R_E} \implies d = 2h = 2 \times 32\,\mathrm{km} = 64\,\mathrm{km}. \]

Example 3: Applying Energy Conservation to Sub-Escape Projection

Problem: A projectile is launched vertically upward from Earth's surface with an initial speed \(v = \frac{1}{2}v_e\), where \(v_e\) is the escape speed from Earth's surface. Neglecting air resistance, find the maximum height \(h\) reached above the surface in terms of Earth's radius \(R_E\).

Solution:

Using \(v_e = \sqrt{2GM_E / R_E}\), the initial speed squared is \(v^2 = \frac{1}{4}v_e^2 = \frac{GM_E}{2R_E}\). The initial mechanical energy at the surface is:

\[ E_i = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{GM_E m}{R_E} = \frac{GM_E m}{4R_E} - \frac{GM_E m}{R_E} = -\frac{3GM_E m}{4R_E} \]

At maximum height \(h\), the projectile's speed is zero and its distance from Earth's centre is \(R_E + h\):

\[ E_f = 0 - \frac{GM_E m}{R_E + h} = -\frac{GM_E m}{R_E + h} \]

By conservation of mechanical energy (\(E_i = E_f\)):

\[ \frac{3}{4R_E} = \frac{1}{R_E + h} \implies 3R_E + 3h = 4R_E \implies h = \frac{R_E}{3}. \]

Example 4: Calculating Energy Changes During an Orbital Transfer

Problem: A \(300\,\mathrm{kg}\) satellite is transferred from a circular orbit of radius \(3R_E\) to a circular orbit of radius \(6R_E\). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(R_E = 6.4 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m}\) as standard assumptions, find (a) the minimum energy required for the transfer, (b) the change in kinetic energy \(\Delta K\), and (c) the change in gravitational potential energy \(\Delta U\).

Solution:

For a circular orbit of radius \(r\), total energy is \(E = -\dfrac{GM_E m}{2r}\). Thus:

\[ E_i = -\frac{GM_E m}{6R_E}, \qquad E_f = -\frac{GM_E m}{12R_E} \]

(a) Minimum energy required:

\[ \Delta E = E_f - E_i = \frac{GM_E m}{12R_E} \]

Using \(GM_E = gR_E^2\):

\[ \Delta E = \frac{mgR_E}{12} = \frac{300 \times 10 \times 6.4 \times 10^6}{12} = 1.6 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{J}. \]

(b) Since \(K = -E\), the change in kinetic energy is:

\[ \Delta K = -\Delta E = -1.6 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{J} \quad (\text{kinetic energy decreases}). \]

(c) Since \(U = 2E\), the change in potential energy is:

\[ \Delta U = 2\Delta E = +3.2 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{J} \quad (\text{potential energy increases}). \]

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A newly discovered exoplanet revolves around its host star in a circular orbit of radius four times that of Earth's orbit around the Sun. Assuming the host star has the same mass as the Sun, the orbital period of the exoplanet is:

  • (A) 2 years
  • (B) 4 years
  • (C) 8 years
  • (D) 16 years
Answer: (C) 8 years

Solution: By Kepler's third law, \(T^2 \propto r^3\) when the central star's mass is the same. Therefore:

\[ \frac{T_{\text{exo}}}{T_E} = \left(\frac{r_{\text{exo}}}{r_E}\right)^{3/2} = (4)^{3/2} = 8 \]

Since \(T_E = 1\,\mathrm{year}\), \(T_{\text{exo}} = 8\,\mathrm{years}\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A comet moves around the Sun in an elliptical orbit with perihelion distance \(1.5 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}\) and aphelion distance \(4.5 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{m}\). The ratio of the comet's speed at perihelion to its speed at aphelion is:

  • (A) \(1 : 3\)
  • (B) \(3 : 1\)
  • (C) \(9 : 1\)
  • (D) \(1 : 9\)
Answer: (B) 3 : 1

Solution: By conservation of angular momentum for a central force, \(m v_p r_p = m v_a r_a\). Hence:

\[ \frac{v_p}{v_a} = \frac{r_a}{r_p} = \frac{4.5 \times 10^{11}}{1.5 \times 10^{11}} = \frac{3}{1}. \]
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two point masses \(M\) and \(9M\) are held fixed at a separation \(L\). At what distance from the lighter mass \(M\) along the line joining them is the net gravitational force on a small test mass \(m\) equal to zero?

  • (A) \(\dfrac{L}{10}\)
  • (B) \(\dfrac{L}{4}\)
  • (C) \(\dfrac{L}{3}\)
  • (D) \(\dfrac{3L}{4}\)
Answer: (B) L / 4

Solution: Let the null point be at distance \(x\) from \(M\) (so \(L - x\) from \(9M\)). Equating the magnitudes of the two opposing gravitational pulls:

\[ \frac{GMm}{x^2} = \frac{G(9M)m}{(L - x)^2} \implies \frac{L - x}{x} = 3 \implies L - x = 3x \implies x = \frac{L}{4}. \]
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A block weighs \(160\,\mathrm{N}\) on the surface of Earth. Assuming Earth to be a sphere of uniform density, what would be the weight of the block in a mine at a depth equal to one-fourth of Earth's radius?

  • (A) \(40\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(80\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(120\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(140\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (C) 120 N

Solution: At depth \(d = R_E / 4\), the acceleration due to gravity is:

\[ g(d) = g\left(1 - \frac{d}{R_E}\right) = g\left(1 - \frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{3}{4}g \]

Therefore, the weight at depth \(d\) is \(W(d) = \frac{3}{4}W_0 = \frac{3}{4} \times 160\,\mathrm{N} = 120\,\mathrm{N}\).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which statement correctly describes the variation of gravitational field intensity \(g(r)\) with radial distance \(r\) from the centre of a uniform solid sphere of radius \(R\)?

  • (A) Constant for \(r < R\) and proportional to \(1/r^2\) for \(r > R\)
  • (B) Zero for \(r < R\) and proportional to \(1/r^2\) for \(r > R\)
  • (C) Proportional to \(r\) for \(r < R\) and proportional to \(1/r^2\) for \(r > R\)
  • (D) Proportional to \(1/r\) for \(r < R\) and proportional to \(1/r^2\) for \(r > R\)
Answer: (C) Proportional to r for r < R and proportional to 1/r² for r > R

Solution: Inside a uniform solid sphere (\(r < R\)), only the mass within radius \(r\) contributes, giving \(g(r) = \frac{GM}{R^3}r \propto r\). Outside (\(r > R\)), the entire mass acts as if concentrated at the centre, giving \(g(r) = \frac{GM}{r^2} \propto 1/r^2\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The minimum work required to lift a body of mass \(m\) from Earth's surface to an altitude \(h = 2R_E\) (where \(R_E\) is Earth's radius and \(g\) is the surface acceleration due to gravity) is:

  • (A) \(\dfrac{1}{3}mgR_E\)
  • (B) \(\dfrac{2}{3}mgR_E\)
  • (C) \(2mgR_E\)
  • (D) \(\dfrac{3}{2}mgR_E\)
Answer: (B) (2/3) mgR_E

Solution: At altitude \(h = 2R_E\), the distance from Earth's centre is \(r_2 = R_E + 2R_E = 3R_E\). The work required equals the increase in gravitational potential energy:

\[ W = U_f - U_i = -\frac{GM_E m}{3R_E} - \left(-\frac{GM_E m}{R_E}\right) = \frac{2GM_E m}{3R_E} \]

Substituting \(GM_E = gR_E^2\) yields \(W = \frac{2}{3}mgR_E\).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The escape speed from Earth's surface is \(11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). A hypothetical planet has twice the mass of Earth and half the radius of Earth. The escape speed from the surface of this planet is:

  • (A) \(5.6\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(22.4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(44.8\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (C) 22.4 km s⁻¹

Solution: Escape speed is \(v_e = \sqrt{2GM / R}\). For the hypothetical planet (\(M' = 2M_E\), \(R' = R_E/2\)):

\[ v_e' = \sqrt{\frac{2G(2M_E)}{R_E / 2}} = \sqrt{4 \cdot \frac{2GM_E}{R_E}} = 2v_e = 2 \times 11.2 = 22.4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}. \]
Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A satellite revolves around Earth in a circular orbit at an altitude equal to Earth's radius \(R_E\). If \(v_e\) is the escape speed from Earth's surface, the orbital speed of the satellite is:

  • (A) \(\dfrac{v_e}{2}\)
  • (B) \(\dfrac{v_e}{\sqrt{2}}\)
  • (C) \(\dfrac{v_e}{4}\)
  • (D) \(\sqrt{2}\,v_e\)
Answer: (A) v_e / 2

Solution: At altitude \(h = R_E\), the orbital radius is \(r = 2R_E\). Thus:

\[ v_o = \sqrt{\frac{GM_E}{2R_E}} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2GM_E}{R_E}} = \frac{v_e}{2}. \]
Practice Question 9 (Assertion–Reason)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as Assertion (A) and the other is labelled as Reason (R).

Assertion (A): An astronaut inside an orbiting space station experiences weightlessness.

Reason (R): Both the astronaut and the space station are in continuous free fall toward Earth with the same centripetal acceleration.

In light of the above statements, choose the most appropriate answer:

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Earth's gravitational pull at typical orbital altitudes is still substantial (not zero). The astronaut feels weightless precisely because both the astronaut and the space station accelerate radially toward Earth at \(g(h)\), reducing the normal contact force between them to zero.

Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.

Solution: In a circular orbit of radius \(r\), \(K = +\frac{GMm}{2r}\), \(U = -\frac{GMm}{r}\), and \(E = -\frac{GMm}{2r} < 0\). Clearly, \(E\) is negative and \(K = \frac{1}{2}|U|\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Overusing \(g(h) \approx g(1 - 2h/R_E)\): This binomial approximation is valid only when \(h \ll R_E\). If \(h\) is comparable to \(R_E\) (e.g., \(h = R_E / 2\) or \(h = R_E\)), always use the exact formula \(g(h) = g \left(\frac{R_E}{R_E+h}\right)^2\).
  • Using \(\Delta U = mgh\) for Large Heights: Never use \(mgh\) when \(h\) is a significant fraction of \(R_E\); use \(\Delta U = GM_E m \left(\frac{1}{R_E} - \frac{1}{R_E+h}\right)\).
  • Sign Confusion in Satellite Transfers: Moving a satellite to a higher orbit (\(r\) increases) makes \(E\) and \(U\) less negative (so \(\Delta E > 0\) and \(\Delta U > 0\)), while \(K\) and orbital speed \(v_o\) decrease (\(\Delta K < 0\)).
  • Assuming \(g = 0\) in Orbit: Weightlessness in a satellite occurs due to free fall (zero normal reaction), not because gravity vanishes.

Concise Final Recap

Gravitation is a central, conservative, inverse-square attractive interaction. Angular momentum and total mechanical energy are conserved for orbiting bodies, explaining Kepler's laws and bound satellite motion. Acceleration due to gravity peaks at Earth's surface and drops both with altitude and depth, while potential energy and total orbital energy remain negative for all bound systems relative to zero at infinity.
