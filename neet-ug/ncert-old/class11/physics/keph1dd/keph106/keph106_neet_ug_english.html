Systems of Particles and Rotational Motion

From Centre of Mass and Linear Momentum to Torque, Angular Momentum, Moment of Inertia, and Rigid-Body Dynamics

Learning Objectives and Overview

Until now, mechanics focused on a single idealised particle—an object with mass but negligible size. Real objects such as wheels, rods, molecules, and planets have finite dimensions and internal structure. This tutorial builds the complete framework needed for NEET UG to analyse both the overall translational motion of multi-particle systems and the rotational motion of rigid bodies.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between pure translation, fixed-axis rotation, and combined translational-rotational motion.
  • Calculate the position, velocity, and acceleration of the centre of mass (CM) for discrete and symmetric continuous bodies.
  • Apply conservation of linear momentum to explosions, decays, and isolated multi-particle systems.
  • Use vector (cross) products to compute torque and angular momentum, and relate linear and angular kinematics.
  • Analyse static mechanical equilibrium of rigid bodies using force balance, torque balance, and the principle of moments.
  • Compute moments of inertia and radii of gyration for standard geometries and apply \(\tau = I\alpha\), rotational work-energy, and conservation of angular momentum.

1. Rigid Bodies and Types of Motion

An ideal rigid body is an extended object with a definite, unchanging shape such that the distance between any pair of its constituent particles remains strictly constant under applied forces. While real solids deform slightly, treating wheels, beams, tops, and planets as rigid is an accurate and powerful approximation.

Three Fundamental Modes of Rigid-Body Motion

  • Pure Translational Motion: At any instant, every particle of the body has the exact same velocity vector \(\mathbf{v}\). A block sliding straight down an inclined plane without tipping or spinning is in pure translation.
  • Rotational Motion about a Fixed Axis: If a rigid body is constrained along a straight line (the axis of rotation), it cannot translate. Every particle moves in a circle lying in a plane perpendicular to the axis, centred on the axis. Particles lying directly on the axis remain stationary. Examples include a ceiling fan and a potter's wheel.
  • Combined Translation and Rotation: An unpivoted rigid body can simultaneously translate and rotate. For instance, a cylinder rolling down an incline has different instantaneous velocities at different points—its top moves faster than its centre, while the point of contact with the incline is momentarily at rest if it rolls without slipping.

Note on general rotation: In systems where a single point (rather than a full line) is fixed—such as a spinning top or an oscillating table fan—the axis of rotation passes through the fixed point and sweeps out space (precession or oscillation). Most NEET UG problems focus on rotation about a fixed axis.

2. Centre of Mass and Its Motion

The centre of mass (CM) of a system of particles is a unique point that moves as though the entire mass of the system were concentrated there and all external forces acted directly at that point.

Position of the Centre of Mass

For two particles of masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) located along the \(x\)-axis at \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), the CM coordinate \(X\) is the mass-weighted mean:

\[X = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2}\]

In three dimensions, for \(n\) discrete particles with total mass \(M = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\) and position vectors \(\mathbf{r}_i = x_i\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y_i\hat{\mathbf{j}} + z_i\hat{\mathbf{k}}\), the CM position vector \(\mathbf{R}\) is:

\[\mathbf{R} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \mathbf{r}_i \quad \implies \quad X = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{M}, \quad Y = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{M}, \quad Z = \frac{\sum m_i z_i}{M}\]

For a continuous rigid body, the summation becomes an integration over mass elements \(\mathrm{d}m\):

\[\mathbf{R} = \frac{1}{M}\int \mathbf{r}\,\mathrm{d}m\]

If the origin of the coordinate system is chosen at the CM itself, then \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{0}\), which implies \(\sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{0}\) (or \(\int \mathbf{r}\,\mathrm{d}m = \mathbf{0}\)).

Symmetry Shortcuts for Uniform Bodies:
  • Uniform thin rod, ring, circular disc, sphere, cylinder, cube: Because of reflection symmetry about the geometric centre, for every mass element \(\mathrm{d}m\) at \(\mathbf{r}\) there is an equal element at \(-\mathbf{r}\). Thus, the CM coincides with the geometric centre.
  • Uniform triangular lamina: Subdividing the plate into thin strips parallel to any base shows each strip's CM lies at its midpoint. Joining these midpoints forms a median; hence, the CM lies at the intersection of the medians—the centroid.
  • Important property: The CM does not have to lie inside the material of the body (for example, the CM of a uniform ring or an L-shaped plate can lie in empty space).

Velocity, Acceleration, and Newton's Second Law for a System

Differentiating \(M\mathbf{R} = \sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i\) twice with respect to time (assuming constant particle masses) yields the CM velocity \(\mathbf{V}\) and acceleration \(\mathbf{A}\):

\[M\mathbf{V} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{P} \qquad \text{and} \qquad M\mathbf{A} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \mathbf{a}_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{F}_i\]

Every particle experiences both external forces (from outside the system) and internal forces (from other particles within the system). By Newton's third law, internal forces occur in equal and opposite action-reaction pairs whose vector sum is identically zero. Therefore, only external forces govern the motion of the CM:

\[M\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\]

Conservation of Linear Momentum

When the net external force on a system is zero (\(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}\)), the total linear momentum \(\mathbf{P} = M\mathbf{V}\) remains constant in both magnitude and direction:

\[\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{P} = \text{constant} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}} = \text{constant}\]

Even if internal forces cause dramatic rearrangements—such as a mid-air shell explosion, radioactive decay of a moving nucleus, or two stars orbiting each other in a binary system—the CM continues along the exact trajectory dictated by external forces alone. In the centre-of-mass frame (where \(\mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{0}\)), a decaying particle splits into two fragments moving strictly back-to-back.

3. Vector Product and Rotational Kinematics

Rotational quantities such as torque, angular momentum, and tangential velocity are defined using the vector (cross) product.

Properties of the Vector Product

The cross product of two vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) is a vector \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}\) with:

  • Magnitude: \(c = ab\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) (\(0° \le \theta \le 180°\)) is the smaller angle between \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\).
  • Direction: Perpendicular to the plane containing \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), given by the right-hand rule (curl the fingers of your right hand from \(\mathbf{a}\) to \(\mathbf{b}\) through \(\theta\); your extended thumb points along \(\mathbf{c}\)).
  • Anti-commutativity: \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = -\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}\).
  • Distributivity: \(\mathbf{a} \times (\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{c}\).
  • Parallel or anti-parallel vectors: \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\) (since \(\sin 0° = 0\)).

For orthogonal unit vectors \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}, \hat{\mathbf{j}}, \hat{\mathbf{k}}\), cyclic products are positive (\(\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{j}} \times \hat{\mathbf{k}} = \hat{\mathbf{i}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{i}} = \hat{\mathbf{j}}\)) and reverse products are negative. In determinant form:

\[\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ a_x & a_y & a_z \\ b_x & b_y & b_z \end{vmatrix} = (a_y b_z - a_z b_y)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (a_z b_x - a_x b_z)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (a_x b_y - a_y b_x)\hat{\mathbf{k}}\]

Angular Velocity, Angular Acceleration, and Linear Velocity

When a rigid body rotates about a fixed axis, every particle shares the same instantaneous angular velocity \(\omega = \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t}\) (SI unit: \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\), dimension \([\mathrm{T^{-1}}]\)). As a vector, \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) points along the axis of rotation according to the right-hand rule. The linear velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) of a particle at position \(\mathbf{r}\) relative to an origin on the axis is:

\[\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r} \qquad \text{with magnitude } v = \omega r_{\perp}\]

where \(r_{\perp}\) is the perpendicular distance of the particle from the axis of rotation. The angular acceleration is \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\mathrm{d}t}\) (for a fixed axis, \(\alpha = \frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}t}\)).

Kinematics of Fixed-Axis Rotation (Uniform \(\alpha\))

When \(\alpha\) is constant, rotational kinematics mirrors one-dimensional linear kinematics with \(\theta\), \(\omega\), and \(\alpha\) replacing \(x\), \(v\), and \(a\):

\[\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t \qquad \theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2 \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha(\theta - \theta_0)\]

Remember to convert revolutions per minute (\(\mathrm{rpm}\)) into \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\) using \(\omega = \frac{2\pi \times \mathrm{rpm}}{60}\).

4. Torque, Angular Momentum, and Rigid-Body Equilibrium

Moment of Force (Torque)

Force alone does not determine how effectively a body is made to rotate; where and at what angle the force is applied matters equally. The torque (or moment of force) \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) of a force \(\mathbf{F}\) acting at a point with position vector \(\mathbf{r}\) relative to an origin \(O\) is:

\[\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \qquad \text{with magnitude } \tau = rF\sin\theta = r_{\perp}F = rF_{\perp}\]

Here \(r_{\perp} = r\sin\theta\) is the perpendicular distance (lever arm) from \(O\) to the line of action of \(\mathbf{F}\). Torque has SI unit \(\mathrm{N\,m}\) and dimensions \([\mathrm{ML^2T^{-2}}]\)—the same dimensions as work, though torque is a vector while work is a scalar. Torque vanishes if \(F = 0\), \(r = 0\), or the line of action passes through \(O\) (\(\theta = 0°\) or \(180°\)).

Angular Momentum and Its Conservation

The rotational analogue of linear momentum \(\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}\) is angular momentum (moment of momentum). For a single particle:

\[\mathbf{l} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p} \qquad \text{with magnitude } l = rp\sin\theta = r_{\perp}p = rp_{\perp}\]

Differentiating \(\mathbf{l} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}\) with respect to time gives \(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathbf{v} \times m\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{r} \times \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \boldsymbol{\tau}\). For a system of \(n\) particles with total angular momentum \(\mathbf{L} = \sum \mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{p}_i\), internal central forces (acting along the line joining particle pairs) produce zero net internal torque (\(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{int}} = \mathbf{0}\)). Thus:

\[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}}\]

Conservation of Angular Momentum: If the net external torque about a point (or axis) is zero (\(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}\)), the total angular momentum \(\mathbf{L}\) about that point (or axis) remains constant.

Mechanical Equilibrium of a Rigid Body

A rigid body is in complete mechanical equilibrium when both its linear momentum and angular momentum are constant in time:

  1. Translational Equilibrium: \(\sum \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{0}\) (no linear acceleration: \(\sum F_x = 0, \sum F_y = 0, \sum F_z = 0\)).
  2. Rotational Equilibrium: \(\sum \boldsymbol{\tau}_i = \mathbf{0}\) (no angular acceleration: \(\sum \tau_x = 0, \sum \tau_y = 0, \sum \tau_z = 0\)).

For coplanar forces, these reduce to three scalar equations: \(\sum F_x = 0\), \(\sum F_y = 0\), and \(\sum \tau_z = 0\). Crucially, if a rigid body is in translational equilibrium (\(\sum \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{0}\)), then the total torque is independent of the choice of origin.

Partial Equilibrium and a Couple: A body can satisfy one condition without the other. A pair of equal and opposite forces with different lines of action forms a couple. For a couple, \(\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) (translational equilibrium holds), yet the net torque is non-zero and equals \(F \times d_{\perp}\) (where \(d_{\perp}\) is the perpendicular separation between the lines of action), independent of the reference point. A couple produces pure rotation without translation.

Principle of Moments and Centre of Gravity

For an ideal lever pivoted at a fulcrum, a load \(F_1\) at load arm \(d_1\) is balanced by an effort \(F_2\) at effort arm \(d_2\) when anticlockwise and clockwise moments cancel:

\[F_1 d_1 = F_2 d_2 \qquad \implies \qquad \text{Mechanical Advantage (M.A.)} = \frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{d_2}{d_1}\]

The centre of gravity (CG) of a body is the point where the total gravitational torque on the body is zero: \(\sum \mathbf{r}_i \times m_i \mathbf{g} = \mathbf{0}\). In a uniform gravitational field (\(\mathbf{g}\) constant across the body), the CG coincides with the centre of mass. If the body is large enough that \(\mathbf{g}\) varies across it, CG and CM do not coincide.

5. Moment of Inertia and Fixed-Axis Rotational Dynamics

Moment of Inertia and Radius of Gyration

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis with angular speed \(\omega\), each particle at perpendicular distance \(r_i\) has speed \(v_i = r_i\omega\). Summing the kinetic energies of all particles gives:

\[K = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{2}m_i v_i^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i r_i^2\right)\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2\]

Here \(I = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i r_i^2\) is the moment of inertia (SI unit: \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), dimensions: \([\mathrm{ML^2}]\)). It is the rotational analogue of mass: it measures how much a body resists changes in its rotational state. Unlike mass, \(I\) is not an intrinsic constant of the body alone—it depends on the total mass, the shape and mass distribution, and the position and orientation of the axis of rotation.

The radius of gyration \(k\) about a given axis is defined by \(I = Mk^2\), meaning \(k = \sqrt{I/M}\). Physically, \(k\) is the distance from the axis at which the entire mass \(M\) could be concentrated as a point mass to yield the same moment of inertia.

Dynamics of Fixed-Axis Rotation

When a rigid body rotates about a fixed \(z\)-axis, only the \(z\)-components of external torques cause rotation; perpendicular torque components are balanced by constraint forces at the bearings/hinges. The key dynamical laws are:

  • Work done by a torque: \(\mathrm{d}W = \tau\,\mathrm{d}\theta\)
  • Instantaneous rotational power: \(P = \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = \tau\omega\)
  • Newton's second law for fixed-axis rotation: Equating power \(\tau\omega\) to \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{2}I\omega^2\right) = I\omega\alpha\) (for constant \(I\)) gives \(\tau = I\alpha\).
  • Angular momentum along a fixed symmetry axis: In general, \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{L}_z + \mathbf{L}_{\perp}\). For bodies symmetric about the rotation axis, \(\mathbf{L}_{\perp} = \mathbf{0}\), so \(\mathbf{L} = L_z\hat{\mathbf{k}} = I\omega\hat{\mathbf{k}}\).
  • Conservation of angular momentum about a fixed axis: If \(\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0\) and \(I\) changes (for example, a skater or diver pulling in their arms), then: \[I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2\]

Worked Examples

Example 1: Locating the Centre of Mass of a Discrete System

Assumed values: Three point masses are placed on a coordinate plane at specified positions.

Problem: Three particles of masses \(2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(3.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), and \(5.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) are located at \((0, 0)\), \((4.0\,\mathrm{m}, 0)\), and \((2.0\,\mathrm{m}, 3.0\,\mathrm{m})\), respectively. Find the coordinates \((X, Y)\) of their centre of mass.

Solution:

Total mass: \(M = 2.0 + 3.0 + 5.0 = 10.0\,\mathrm{kg}\).

Using the CM coordinate formulas:

\[X = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2 + m_3 x_3}{M} = \frac{2.0(0) + 3.0(4.0) + 5.0(2.0)}{10.0} = \frac{0 + 12.0 + 10.0}{10.0} = 2.2\,\mathrm{m}\] \[Y = \frac{m_1 y_1 + m_2 y_2 + m_3 y_3}{M} = \frac{2.0(0) + 3.0(0) + 5.0(3.0)}{10.0} = \frac{15.0}{10.0} = 1.5\,\mathrm{m}\]

The centre of mass is at \((2.2\,\mathrm{m}, 1.5\,\mathrm{m})\).

Example 2: Centre of Mass Motion During a Mid-Air Explosion

Assumed values: A projectile of mass \(4m\) is launched on level ground and explodes at the highest point; air resistance is neglected.

Problem: A shell of mass \(4m\) is fired from a cannon and would land at a horizontal range \(R = 120\,\mathrm{m}\) if intact. At the highest point of its trajectory, an internal explosion splits it into two fragments of masses \(m\) and \(3m\). The lighter fragment \(m\) drops vertically downward with zero horizontal velocity immediately after the explosion and lands at \(x_1 = 60\,\mathrm{m}\). Where does the heavier fragment \(3m\) land?

Solution:

Because the explosion is caused purely by internal forces, the horizontal position of the system's CM continues along the original parabolic trajectory and must land at \(X_{\text{CM}} = R = 120\,\mathrm{m}\).

At the instant both fragments reach the ground:

\[X_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m x_1 + 3m x_2}{m + 3m} \implies 120 = \frac{m(60) + 3m x_2}{4m}\] \[480 = 60 + 3x_2 \implies 3x_2 = 420 \implies x_2 = 140\,\mathrm{m}\]

The heavier fragment lands at \(140\,\mathrm{m}\) from the launch point.

Example 3: Computing Torque Using the Vector Product

Assumed values: Hypothetical 3D position and force vectors in SI units.

Problem: A force \(\mathbf{F} = (4\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 2\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N}\) acts on a particle at position \(\mathbf{r} = (2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \hat{\mathbf{j}} - \hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{m}\) relative to the origin. Calculate the torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) about the origin.

Solution:

Using \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}\):

\[\boldsymbol{\tau} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 4 & -2 & 3 \end{vmatrix}\] \[\boldsymbol{\tau} = \big((1)(3) - (-1)(-2)\big)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - \big((2)(3) - (-1)(4)\big)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \big((2)(-2) - (1)(4)\big)\hat{\mathbf{k}}\] \[\boldsymbol{\tau} = (3 - 2)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - (6 + 4)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (-4 - 4)\hat{\mathbf{k}} = (\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 10\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 8\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N\,m}\]
Example 4: Static Equilibrium of a Loaded Uniform Beam

Assumed values: A uniform horizontal plank with \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

Problem: A uniform horizontal plank of length \(4.0\,\mathrm{m}\) and mass \(12\,\mathrm{kg}\) rests on two supports placed at its ends \(A\) and \(B\). A crate of mass \(20\,\mathrm{kg}\) is placed \(1.0\,\mathrm{m}\) from end \(A\). Find the normal reaction forces \(N_A\) and \(N_B\) at the two supports.

Solution:

The plank's weight \(W_p = 12 \times 10 = 120\,\mathrm{N}\) acts at its midpoint (\(2.0\,\mathrm{m}\) from \(A\)). The crate's weight \(W_c = 20 \times 10 = 200\,\mathrm{N}\) acts at \(1.0\,\mathrm{m}\) from \(A\).

1. Translational equilibrium (vertical):

\[N_A + N_B = 120 + 200 = 320\,\mathrm{N}\]

2. Rotational equilibrium (taking moments about \(A\)):

\[N_B(4.0) - 120(2.0) - 200(1.0) = 0 \implies 4.0 N_B = 240 + 200 = 440 \implies N_B = 110\,\mathrm{N}\]

Substituting back: \(N_A = 320 - 110 = 210\,\mathrm{N}\).

Example 5: Rotational Dynamics of a Pulled Solid Cylinder

Assumed values: A frictionless axle and a massless inextensible cord.

Problem: A light cord is wrapped around the rim of a uniform solid cylinder of mass \(M = 10\,\mathrm{kg}\) and radius \(R = 0.40\,\mathrm{m}\), mounted on a fixed horizontal frictionless axle. A constant tangential pull \(F = 20\,\mathrm{N}\) is applied to the cord starting from rest. Find (a) the angular acceleration \(\alpha\) of the cylinder and (b) its rotational kinetic energy after \(2.0\,\mathrm{s}\).

Solution:

(a) Moment of inertia of the solid cylinder about its axis:

\[I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2 = \frac{1}{2}(10)(0.40)^2 = 0.80\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\]

Applied torque: \(\tau = FR = 20 \times 0.40 = 8.0\,\mathrm{N\,m}\).

\[\alpha = \frac{\tau}{I} = \frac{8.0}{0.80} = 10\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\]

(b) Angular speed after \(t = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}\) from rest (\(\omega_0 = 0\)):

\[\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t = 0 + (10)(2.0) = 20\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\] \[K = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.80)(20)^2 = 160\,\mathrm{J}\]

Practice Question 1: Two particles of masses \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(6\,\mathrm{kg}\) are separated by \(1.6\,\mathrm{m}\) on a straight line. The distance of their centre of mass from the \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) mass is:
  • (A) \(0.4\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(0.8\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(1.2\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(1.0\,\mathrm{m}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Place the \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) mass at the origin (\(x_1 = 0\)) and the \(6\,\mathrm{kg}\) mass at \(x_2 = 1.6\,\mathrm{m}\). Then:

\[X = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{2(0) + 6(1.6)}{2 + 6} = \frac{9.6}{8} = 1.2\,\mathrm{m}\]
Practice Question 2: A circular hole of radius \(R/2\) is cut out from a uniform circular disc of radius \(R\) such that the centre of the hole is at a distance \(R/2\) from the centre of the original disc. The distance of the centre of mass of the remaining portion from the centre of the original disc is:
  • (A) \(R/2\)
  • (B) \(R/4\)
  • (C) \(R/6\)
  • (D) \(R/8\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Let the centre of the original disc be at \(x = 0\) and the centre of the removed hole be at \(x_2 = +R/2\). Since mass is proportional to area (\(\pi r^2\)), if the full disc has mass \(M\), the removed disc has mass \(m_2 = M\left(\frac{R/2}{R}\right)^2 = \frac{M}{4}\). Treating the removed mass as negative:

\[X_{\text{CM}} = \frac{M(0) - (M/4)(R/2)}{M - M/4} = \frac{-MR/8}{3M/4} = -\frac{R}{6}\]

Thus, the CM shifts by a distance \(R/6\) opposite to the hole.

Practice Question 3: A particle of mass \(m\) moves with a constant velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) along a straight line that does not pass through the origin \(O\). Which statement about its angular momentum \(\mathbf{l}\) about \(O\) is correct?
  • (A) Its magnitude increases continuously with time.
  • (B) Its magnitude remains constant, but its direction reverses as the particle passes the point closest to \(O\).
  • (C) Both its magnitude and direction remain constant throughout the motion.
  • (D) It is zero at the point of closest approach to \(O\).
Answer: (C)

Solution: The magnitude of angular momentum is \(l = mv r_{\perp}\), where \(r_{\perp} = r\sin\theta\) is the perpendicular distance from \(O\) to the straight line of motion—which is constant. Also, \(\mathbf{r}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) always lie in the same plane and the sense of rotation about \(O\) never reverses, so both the magnitude and direction of \(\mathbf{l} = \mathbf{r} \times m\mathbf{v}\) remain constant.

Practice Question 4: A flywheel accelerates uniformly from \(300\,\mathrm{rpm}\) to \(900\,\mathrm{rpm}\) in \(10\,\mathrm{s}\). The number of revolutions it completes during this interval is:
  • (A) \(50\)
  • (B) \(100\)
  • (C) \(150\)
  • (D) \(200\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Convert angular speeds to \(\mathrm{rev/s}\): \(n_0 = \frac{300}{60} = 5\,\mathrm{rev/s}\) and \(n = \frac{900}{60} = 15\,\mathrm{rev/s}\). For uniform angular acceleration, the number of revolutions is:

\[N = \left(\frac{n_0 + n}{2}\right)t = \left(\frac{5 + 15}{2}\right) \times 10 = 100\,\mathrm{revolutions}\]
Practice Question 5: A uniform metre stick is balanced on a knife-edge at the \(50\,\mathrm{cm}\) mark. When a coin of mass \(10\,\mathrm{g}\) is placed at the \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) mark, the new balance point shifts to the \(45\,\mathrm{cm}\) mark. The mass of the metre stick is:
  • (A) \(50\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (B) \(60\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (C) \(70\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (D) \(80\,\mathrm{g}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: The weight of the uniform stick acts at its midpoint (\(50\,\mathrm{cm}\) mark). Balancing torques about the new fulcrum at \(45\,\mathrm{cm}\):

\[10\,g \times (45 - 10) = M_{\text{stick}}\,g \times (50 - 45)\] \[10 \times 35 = M_{\text{stick}} \times 5 \implies M_{\text{stick}} = 70\,\mathrm{g}\]
Practice Question 6: Equal torques are applied to a hollow cylinder and a solid sphere of identical mass \(M\) and identical radius \(R\), both initially at rest and free to rotate about their standard symmetry axes. After a given time \(t\), the ratio of the angular speed of the solid sphere to that of the hollow cylinder (\(\omega_{\text{sphere}} / \omega_{\text{cylinder}}\)) is:
  • (A) \(2 : 5\)
  • (B) \(5 : 2\)
  • (C) \(1 : 2\)
  • (D) \(2 : 1\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Since \(\omega = \alpha t = \frac{\tau}{I}t\) and \(\tau, t\) are the same for both, \(\omega \propto \frac{1}{I}\). Here \(I_{\text{cylinder}} = MR^2\) and \(I_{\text{sphere}} = \frac{2}{5}MR^2\). Therefore:

\[\frac{\omega_{\text{sphere}}}{\omega_{\text{cylinder}}} = \frac{I_{\text{cylinder}}}{I_{\text{sphere}}} = \frac{MR^2}{\frac{2}{5}MR^2} = \frac{5}{2}\]
Practice Question 7: A student stands at the centre of a frictionless turntable rotating at \(30\,\mathrm{rpm}\) with arms outstretched. When the student folds their arms, the moment of inertia drops to \(3/5\) of its initial value. What is the ratio of the final rotational kinetic energy to the initial rotational kinetic energy (\(K_f / K_i\))?
  • (A) \(3/5\)
  • (B) \(5/3\)
  • (C) \(9/25\)
  • (D) \(1\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: With no external torque about the rotation axis, angular momentum \(L = I\omega\) is conserved. Since \(K = \frac{L^2}{2I}\), we have \(K \propto \frac{1}{I}\) when \(L\) is constant:

\[\frac{K_f}{K_i} = \frac{I_i}{I_f} = \frac{I_i}{(3/5)I_i} = \frac{5}{3}\]

(The extra kinetic energy comes from internal muscular work done by the student in pulling their arms inward.)

Practice Question 8: A solid cylinder of mass \(10\,\mathrm{kg}\) and radius \(0.20\,\mathrm{m}\) rotates about its symmetry axis at \(50\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). Its rotational kinetic energy and the magnitude of its angular momentum about the axis are, respectively:
  • (A) \(250\,\mathrm{J}\) and \(10\,\mathrm{J\,s}\)
  • (B) \(500\,\mathrm{J}\) and \(20\,\mathrm{J\,s}\)
  • (C) \(250\,\mathrm{J}\) and \(20\,\mathrm{J\,s}\)
  • (D) \(125\,\mathrm{J}\) and \(5\,\mathrm{J\,s}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: \(I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2 = \frac{1}{2}(10)(0.20)^2 = 0.20\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\).

\[K = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.20)(50)^2 = 250\,\mathrm{J}\] \[L = I\omega = (0.20)(50) = 10\,\mathrm{J\,s}\]
Practice Question 9: An engine delivers a constant torque of \(150\,\mathrm{N\,m}\) to keep a rotor spinning at a uniform angular speed of \(120\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\) against friction. The power required by the engine (assuming \(100\%\) efficiency) is:
  • (A) \(1.25\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (B) \(9.0\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (C) \(18.0\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (D) \(36.0\,\mathrm{kW}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Instantaneous rotational power is \(P = \tau\omega\):

\[P = 150\,\mathrm{N\,m} \times 120\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}} = 18000\,\mathrm{W} = 18.0\,\mathrm{kW}\]
Practice Question 10 (Assertion–Reason):
Assertion (A): A pair of equal and opposite forces acting along different parallel lines on a rigid body produces pure rotation without any translational acceleration of its centre of mass.
Reason (R): For a couple, the net external force is zero while the net external torque is non-zero and independent of the choice of reference point.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Because the two forces are equal and opposite, \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{F} + (-\mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{0}\), so \(M\mathbf{A}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{0}\) (no translational acceleration). Since their lines of action do not coincide, they produce a non-zero torque \(\mathbf{d} \times \mathbf{F}\) that is independent of the origin. Hence both statements are true and (R) correctly explains (A).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Mistakes to Avoid:
  • Assuming CM must lie inside the body: For hollow or asymmetric shapes (ring, hollow sphere, L-lamina), the CM often lies outside the physical material.
  • Equating Centre of Gravity and Centre of Mass blindly: They coincide only when \(\mathbf{g}\) is uniform across the body.
  • Forgetting \(\sin\theta\) in cross products: Torque is \(rF\sin\theta\), not \(rF\). A force whose line of action passes through the pivot produces zero torque regardless of its magnitude.
  • Assuming \(\mathbf{L}\) is always parallel to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\): \(\mathbf{L} = I\boldsymbol{\omega}\) holds strictly when the fixed axis is an axis of symmetry of the rigid body.
  • Assuming kinetic energy is conserved when \(L\) is conserved: When \(I\) decreases with \(\tau_{\text{ext}} = 0\), \(\omega\) increases so \(K = \frac{L^2}{2I}\) increases due to internal work.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • \(\mathbf{R}_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i}{M}\), \(\quad M\mathbf{A}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\), \(\quad \mathbf{P} = M\mathbf{V}_{\text{CM}}\).
  • \(\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}\), \(\quad \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}\), \(\quad \mathbf{l} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}\), \(\quad \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}}\).
  • Mechanical equilibrium: \(\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\sum \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{0}\).
  • Moment of inertia: \(I = \sum m_i r_i^2 = Mk^2\), \(\quad \tau = I\alpha\), \(\quad K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2\), \(\quad P = \tau\omega\).

Concise Final Recap

The motion of any system of particles separates cleanly into two parts: the translational motion of its centre of mass, governed solely by net external forces (\(M\mathbf{A}_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\)), and rotational motion, governed by net external torques (\(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ext}}\)). For fixed-axis rotation of symmetric rigid bodies, moment of inertia \(I\) replaces mass, leading directly to \(\tau = I\alpha\), \(L = I\omega\), \(K = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2\), and conservation of angular momentum whenever external torque vanishes.
