Mechanical Properties of Solids

Elasticity, Stress–Strain Analysis, Elastic Moduli, and Structural Applications

Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Real solid objects are never perfectly rigid; under external forces, they stretch, compress, shear, or change volume. For NEET UG, this chapter tests conceptual clarity on elasticity, graph interpretation, dimensional analysis, and direct numerical applications of elastic moduli.

  • Distinguish between elasticity and plasticity based on interatomic restoring forces.
  • Define and compute longitudinal, shearing, and hydraulic stress and strain.
  • Apply Hooke's law and interpret every region of a stress–strain curve for ductile metals, brittle solids, and elastomers.
  • Calculate Young's modulus, shear modulus, bulk modulus, compressibility, and Poisson's ratio.
  • Determine the elastic potential energy and energy density stored in a deformed wire.
  • Explain engineering applications such as crane cables, I-section beams, flared pillars, and the maximum height of mountains.

1. Elasticity, Plasticity, and Deforming Forces

In rigid-body mechanics, we assume that the distance between any two particles of a solid remains fixed. In reality, atoms in a solid are bound together by interatomic forces that behave much like microscopic springs. When an external force (called a deforming force) acts on a solid in static equilibrium, the atoms are displaced slightly from their equilibrium positions, producing a measurable or microscopic change in shape or size.

  • Elasticity: The property of a body by virtue of which it tends to regain its original size and shape after the deforming force is removed. Examples include steel, quartz, and helical metal springs.
  • Plasticity: The property of a body by virtue of which it undergoes permanent deformation and shows no significant tendency to regain its original dimensions when the deforming force is removed. Putty, wet clay, and mud behave closely like ideal plastics.
NEET Conceptual Trap — What Does "More Elastic" Mean?
In everyday speech, people call rubber "more elastic" because it stretches easily. In physics, elasticity measures resistance to deformation (the magnitude of the restoring force developed per unit strain). Because steel requires a much larger force than rubber to produce the same fractional elongation, steel is far more elastic than rubber.

2. Stress and Strain

When an external force deforms a body without accelerating it, internal restoring forces develop throughout the material to oppose the deformation. In static equilibrium, the magnitude of the internal restoring force equals the applied deforming force \(F\).

Stress

Stress (\(\sigma\)) is defined as the internal restoring force acting per unit cross-sectional area:

\[\sigma = \frac{F}{A}\]
  • SI Unit: \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\) or pascal (\(\mathrm{Pa}\)).
  • Dimensional Formula: \([\mathrm{M^1 L^{-1} T^{-2}}]\) (identical to pressure and elastic modulus).
  • Nature of Quantity: Stress is a tensor quantity (not a simple vector), because its value depends on both the direction of the force and the orientation of the surface area on which it acts.

Strain

Strain (\(\varepsilon\)) measures the fractional change in a dimension of the body:

\[\text{Strain} = \frac{\text{Change in dimension}}{\text{Original dimension}}\]

Because strain is a ratio of two like quantities, it is dimensionless and unitless (\([\mathrm{M^0 L^0 T^0}]\)).

Three Fundamental Modes of Deformation

  1. Longitudinal (Tensile or Compressive) Stress and Strain: Two equal and opposite forces act perpendicular (normal) to opposite faces of a rod or cylinder of length \(L\) and area \(A\). If the length increases by \(\Delta L\) (tensile) or decreases by \(\Delta L\) (compressive), then: \[ \text{Longitudinal stress} = \frac{F}{A}, \qquad \text{Longitudinal strain} = \frac{\Delta L}{L} \]
  2. Shearing (Tangential) Stress and Strain: Two equal and opposite forces act parallel (tangential) to opposite faces of a solid, causing relative sideways displacement \(\Delta x\) between faces separated by perpendicular distance \(L\): \[ \text{Shearing stress} = \frac{F_{\parallel}}{A}, \qquad \text{Shearing strain} = \frac{\Delta x}{L} = \tan\theta \approx \theta \] where \(\theta\) (in radians) is the small angular tilt of the vertical faces. (For \(\theta = 10^\circ\), \(\theta\) and \(\tan\theta\) differ by only about \(1\%\).)
  3. Hydraulic (Volumetric) Stress and Volume Strain: When a solid is immersed in a fluid under uniform pressure \(p\), normal forces act inward at every point on its surface, decreasing its volume from \(V\) to \(V - \Delta V\) without changing its shape: \[ \text{Hydraulic stress} = p, \qquad \text{Volume strain} = \frac{\Delta V}{V} \]

3. Hooke's Law and Stress–Strain Curves

For sufficiently small deformations, stress is directly proportional to strain. This empirical relation is Hooke's law:

\[\text{Stress} = k \times \text{Strain}\]

The proportionality constant \(k\) is called the modulus of elasticity, which depends on the nature of the material and the type of deformation, not on the dimensions of the sample.

Interpreting the Stress–Strain Curve of a Metal

When a metallic wire is gradually stretched until it breaks, plotting tensile stress against longitudinal strain reveals five key regions and points:

  • Proportional Region (O to A): The graph is a straight line; Hooke's law is strictly obeyed. The slope of OA equals Young's modulus \(Y\).
  • Elastic Region up to Yield Point (A to B): Stress and strain are no longer proportional, yet the body still returns to its original length upon unloading. Point B is the yield point (or elastic limit), and its stress \(\sigma_y\) is the yield strength.
  • Plastic Region (B to D): Beyond B, small increases in stress produce large strains. If unloaded at a point C, the wire follows a parallel dashed path and retains a residual strain called a permanent set.
  • Ultimate Tensile Strength (D): The peak stress \(\sigma_u\) the material can withstand. Beyond D, necking occurs and strain increases even as the applied load decreases.
  • Fracture Point (E): The point where the material breaks.
    • If D and E are close together, the material fractures with little plastic deformation and is brittle (e.g., glass, cast iron).
    • If D and E are far apart, the material undergoes large plastic deformation before breaking and is ductile (e.g., copper, mild steel, gold).

Elastomers

Materials such as vulcanized rubber and the elastic tissue of the human aorta can be stretched to very large strains (around \(1.0\) or \(100\%\)) and still snap back to their original shape. However, their stress–strain curve is non-linear over almost the entire range—they do not obey Hooke's law and have no well-defined plastic region. Such solids are called elastomers.

4. Elastic Moduli, Poisson's Ratio, and Elastic Energy

Young's Modulus (\(Y\))

Defined within the proportional limit as the ratio of tensile (or compressive) stress to longitudinal strain:

\[Y = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} = \frac{F / A}{\Delta L / L} = \frac{F L}{A \Delta L}\]

Young's modulus applies only to solids. For a given material, \(Y\) has the same magnitude under tension and compression.

Shear Modulus or Modulus of Rigidity (\(G\))

Defined as the ratio of shearing stress to shearing strain:

\[G = \frac{\sigma_s}{\theta} = \frac{F / A}{\Delta x / L} = \frac{F L}{A \Delta x}\]

Shear modulus applies only to solids, because fluids cannot sustain a static tangential stress. For most isotropic metals, \(G \approx \frac{Y}{3}\).

Bulk Modulus (\(B\)) and Compressibility (\(k\))

Defined as the ratio of hydraulic stress (pressure \(p\)) to the resulting volume strain:

\[B = -\frac{p}{\Delta V / V}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{B} = -\frac{1}{p}\left(\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right)\]

The negative sign ensures \(B > 0\), because an increase in pressure (\(p > 0\)) causes a decrease in volume (\(\Delta V < 0\)). Bulk modulus applies to solids, liquids, and gases. Because interatomic coupling is strongest in solids and weakest in gases, \(B_{\text{solid}} \gg B_{\text{liquid}} \gg B_{\text{gas}}\). Conversely, gases are roughly a million times more compressible than solids.

Poisson's Ratio (\(\nu\))

When a wire of length \(L\) and diameter \(d\) is stretched by \(\Delta L\), its diameter contracts by \(\Delta d\). Within the elastic limit, the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain is a material constant called Poisson's ratio:

\[\nu = \frac{-\Delta d / d}{\Delta L / L}\]

It is dimensionless and unitless. For steel, \(\nu \approx 0.28\text{–}0.30\); for aluminium alloys, \(\nu \approx 0.33\).

Elastic Potential Energy in a Stretched Wire

Stretching a wire by an elongation \(l\) requires work against interatomic restoring forces. Since the restoring force \(F(x) = \frac{Y A}{L} x\) grows linearly from \(0\) to \(F = \frac{Y A l}{L}\), the work stored as elastic potential energy \(U\) is:

\[U = \int_0^l \frac{Y A}{L} x \, dx = \frac{1}{2} \frac{Y A}{L} l^2 = \frac{1}{2} F l = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Stress} \times \text{Strain} \times \text{Volume}\]

The elastic potential energy per unit volume (energy density \(u\)) is:

\[u = \frac{U}{A L} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma \varepsilon = \frac{1}{2} Y \varepsilon^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{2Y}\]
Graph Insight: The area under the linear portion of a stress–strain graph equals the elastic potential energy stored per unit volume (\(\frac{1}{2} \times \text{stress} \times \text{strain}\)).

5. Engineering and Geological Applications

  • Crane Cables: To lift a maximum load \(W = M g\) without permanent deformation, the cable's cross-sectional area must satisfy \(A \ge \frac{W}{\sigma_y}\) (often multiplied by a safety factor of \(10\)). Rather than a single rigid rod, crane ropes are braided from many thin steel wires for flexibility, strength, and ease of handling.
  • Bending of Beams and I-Section Girders: A uniform rectangular bar of length \(l\), breadth \(b\), and depth \(d\) supported near its ends and loaded at the center by \(W\) sags by: \[ \delta = \frac{W l^3}{4 b d^3 Y} \] To minimize sag \(\delta\): (1) choose a material with high \(Y\) (steel), (2) keep span \(l\) small, and (3) increase depth \(d\) rather than breadth \(b\), because \(\delta \propto d^{-3}\) whereas \(\delta \propto b^{-1}\). To prevent lateral buckling in deep beams while saving weight and cost, structural engineers use an I-shaped cross-section, which places most material at the top and bottom flanges where bending stresses are largest.
  • Load-Bearing Pillars: Pillars with flared (distributed) ends distribute compressive forces over a larger area than pillars with rounded ends, allowing them to support heavier loads.
  • Maximum Height of Mountains: At the base of a mountain of height \(h\) and rock density \(\rho\), the vertical weight per unit area is \(h \rho g\). Because the sides of the mountain are free, this creates a shear stress of order \(h \rho g\). Setting \(h \rho g \approx \sigma_{\text{crit}}\) (with \(\sigma_{\text{crit}} \approx 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\), \(\rho \approx 3 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), \(g \approx 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)) gives \(h_{\max} \approx 10\,\mathrm{km}\), explaining why Earth's tallest peaks are under \(10\,\mathrm{km}\).

Worked Examples

Applying Young's Modulus and Elastic Energy Density

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A cylindrical brass rod of assumed length \(2.0\,\mathrm{m}\) and radius \(5.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) is clamped at one end and stretched by a tensile force of \(15.7\,\mathrm{kN}\) at the other end. Taking Young's modulus of brass as \(Y = 1.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\) and \(\pi \approx 3.14\), calculate: (a) the tensile stress, (b) the elongation, and (c) the elastic potential energy stored per unit volume.

Solution:

Cross-sectional area: \(A = \pi r^2 = 3.14 \times (5.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 7.85 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}\).

(a) Tensile stress: \[\sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{15.7 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{N}}{7.85 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}} = 2.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\]

(b) Elongation: \[\Delta L = \frac{\sigma L}{Y} = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}})(2.0\,\mathrm{m})}{1.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 4.0\,\mathrm{mm}\]

(c) Energy density: \[u = \frac{\sigma^2}{2Y} = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^8)^2}{2 \times 1.0 \times 10^{11}} = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\]

Series Combination of Two Wires Under a Common Load

Problem (Assumed Scenario): An aluminium wire of assumed length \(1.4\,\mathrm{m}\) and a steel wire of assumed length \(2.0\,\mathrm{m}\), both having the same cross-sectional area \(2.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\), are joined end-to-end and stretched by a load \(W\). If the total elongation is \(1.5\,\mathrm{mm}\), find the applied load \(W\). (Take \(Y_{\text{Al}} = 7.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\) and \(Y_{\text{steel}} = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\).)

Solution:

Because the wires are in series and have identical cross-sectional area \(A\), they carry the same tensile stress \(\sigma = W / A\):

\[ \frac{\Delta L_{\text{Al}}}{\Delta L_{\text{steel}}} = \frac{L_{\text{Al}} / Y_{\text{Al}}}{L_{\text{steel}} / Y_{\text{steel}}} = \frac{1.4}{2.0} \times \frac{2.0 \times 10^{11}}{7.0 \times 10^{10}} = 2.0 \]

Given \(\Delta L_{\text{Al}} + \Delta L_{\text{steel}} = 1.5\,\mathrm{mm}\), we get \(\Delta L_{\text{steel}} = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm} = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(\Delta L_{\text{Al}} = 1.0\,\mathrm{mm}\).

Using the steel wire to find \(W\):

\[ W = \frac{Y_{\text{steel}} A \Delta L_{\text{steel}}}{L_{\text{steel}}} = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^{11})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})(5.0 \times 10^{-4})}{2.0} = 100\,\mathrm{N} \]

Shear Deflection of a Fixed Metal Block

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A cubical copper block of assumed side \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) has its bottom face fixed to a horizontal table. A tangential force of \(8.4 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N}\) is applied to its top face. Given the shear modulus of copper \(G = 4.2 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\), find the horizontal displacement of the top face.

Solution:

Area of top face: \(A = (0.20\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 0.04\,\mathrm{m^2}\), and height \(L = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\).

\[ \Delta x = \frac{F L}{A G} = \frac{(8.4 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N})(0.20\,\mathrm{m})}{(0.04\,\mathrm{m^2})(4.2 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}})} = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m} = 10\,\mathrm{µm} \]

Bulk Modulus and Fractional Volume Change at Depth

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A solid metal sphere is lowered to an assumed ocean depth of \(2000\,\mathrm{m}\), where seawater density is \(1.0 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). If the bulk modulus of the metal is \(1.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\), calculate the percentage decrease in its volume.

Solution:

Hydrostatic pressure at depth \(h = 2000\,\mathrm{m}\): \(p = h \rho g = 2000 \times 10^3 \times 10 = 2.0 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{Pa}\).

\[ \left|\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right| = \frac{p}{B} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^7}{1.0 \times 10^{11}} = 2.0 \times 10^{-4} \]

Percentage decrease in volume \(= 2.0 \times 10^{-4} \times 100\% = 0.02\%\).

Comparing Beam Sag for Different Depths

Problem (Assumed Scenario): Two beams of the same material, span \(l\), and breadth \(b\) carry the same central load \(W\). Beam P has depth \(d\), while beam Q has depth \(2d\). Find the ratio of their central sags \(\delta_P / \delta_Q\).

Solution:

From \(\delta = \frac{W l^3}{4 b d^3 Y}\), for constant \(W, l, b, Y\), we have \(\delta \propto \frac{1}{d^3}\). Therefore:

\[ \frac{\delta_P}{\delta_Q} = \left(\frac{d_Q}{d_P}\right)^3 = \left(\frac{2d}{d}\right)^3 = \frac{8}{1} \]

NEET UG Practice Questions

The following practice questions are aligned to the NEET UG examination pattern.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A wire of length \(L\) and cross-sectional area \(A\) is made of a material of Young's modulus \(Y\). If the wire is stretched by an amount \(x\) within the elastic limit, the work done is:

  • (A) \(\dfrac{Y A x^2}{L}\)
  • (B) \(\dfrac{Y A x^2}{2L}\)
  • (C) \(\dfrac{Y A x}{2L}\)
  • (D) \(\dfrac{Y L x^2}{2A}\)
Answer: (B)
Solution: Elastic potential energy stored in a stretched wire is \(U = \frac{1}{2} F x\). Since \(F = \frac{Y A x}{L}\), substituting \(F\) gives \(W = U = \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{Y A x}{L}\right) x = \frac{Y A x^2}{2L}\).
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two wires of the same material have lengths in the ratio \(1 : 2\) and radii in the ratio \(2 : 1\). If they are stretched by the same force, the ratio of their elongations is:

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(1 : 4\)
  • (C) \(1 : 8\)
  • (D) \(8 : 1\)
Answer: (C)
Solution: Elongation \(\Delta L = \frac{F L}{\pi r^2 Y}\). For the same material (\(Y\)) and force (\(F\)), \(\Delta L \propto \frac{L}{r^2}\). Thus, \[\frac{\Delta L_1}{\Delta L_2} = \left(\frac{L_1}{L_2}\right)\left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{8}.\]
Practice Question 3 (Conceptual MCQ)

The stretching of a helical metal spring is primarily determined by the:

  • (A) Young's modulus of the wire
  • (B) Bulk modulus of the wire
  • (C) Shear modulus of the wire
  • (D) Compressibility of the wire
Answer: (C)
Solution: When a helical spring is stretched along its axis, the wire itself undergoes twisting (torsion), which is a shearing deformation. Hence, the spring constant depends primarily on the shear modulus \(G\) of the wire.
Practice Question 4 (Numerical MCQ)

A steel wire of cross-sectional area \(1.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) is stretched so that its length increases by \(0.2\%\). If Young's modulus of steel is \(2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\), the tension in the wire is:

  • (A) \(200\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(400\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(1000\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(2000\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (B)
Solution: Strain \(\varepsilon = \frac{0.2}{100} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\). Area \(A = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\). \[F = Y A \varepsilon = (2.0 \times 10^{11})(1.0 \times 10^{-6})(2.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 400\,\mathrm{N}.\]
Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): Steel is more elastic than rubber.
Reason (R): For a given tensile stress, the strain produced in steel is much smaller than that produced in rubber.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)
Solution: Young's modulus \(Y = \frac{\text{Stress}}{\text{Strain}}\). Because steel develops much smaller strain than rubber for the same applied stress, \(Y_{\text{steel}} \gg Y_{\text{rubber}}\), making steel more elastic.
Practice Question 6 (Numerical MCQ)

What pressure increase is required to compress \(1.0\,\mathrm{litre}\) of water by \(0.05\%\)? (Bulk modulus of water \(B = 2.2 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\).)

  • (A) \(1.1 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • (B) \(1.1 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • (C) \(2.2 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • (D) \(4.4 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
Answer: (B)
Solution: Fractional volume change \(\left|\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right| = \frac{0.05}{100} = 5 \times 10^{-4}\). \[p = B \left|\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right| = (2.2 \times 10^9)(5 \times 10^{-4}) = 1.1 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}.\]
Practice Question 7 (Numerical MCQ)

A wire breaks when subjected to a tensile stress of \(4.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\). If the density of the wire is \(8.0 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), the maximum length of the wire that can hang vertically without breaking under its own weight is:

  • (A) \(500\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(2000\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(5000\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(10000\,\mathrm{m}\)
Answer: (C)
Solution: The maximum stress at the top end due to the wire's own weight is \(\sigma = \frac{m g}{A} = \frac{(\rho A L) g}{A} = \rho g L\). \[L_{\max} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{break}}}{\rho g} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^8}{(8.0 \times 10^3)(10)} = 5000\,\mathrm{m}.\]
Practice Question 8 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the two statements below:
Statement I: Bulk modulus is defined for solids, liquids, and gases.
Statement II: Elastomers obey Hooke's law over their entire elastic region.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
Answer: (B)
Solution: All three states of matter resist volume compression, so bulk modulus applies to solids, liquids, and gases (Statement I is correct). Elastomers such as rubber and aorta tissue have non-linear stress–strain curves and do not obey Hooke's law (Statement II is incorrect).
Practice Question 9 (Numerical MCQ)

A rigid horizontal bar of negligible mass is supported symmetrically by two vertical wires of the same length: one of copper (\(Y_c = 1.1 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)) and one of steel (\(Y_s = 2.2 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)). If both wires carry equal tension and undergo the same elongation, the ratio of the diameter of the copper wire to that of the steel wire is:

  • (A) \(1 : \sqrt{2}\)
  • (B) \(\sqrt{2} : 1\)
  • (C) \(1 : 2\)
  • (D) \(2 : 1\)
Answer: (B)
Solution: Since \(F, L, \Delta L\) are equal for both wires, \(Y A = \text{constant} \implies Y d^2 = \text{constant}\). \[\frac{d_c}{d_s} = \sqrt{\frac{Y_s}{Y_c}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.2 \times 10^{11}}{1.1 \times 10^{11}}} = \sqrt{2} : 1.\]
Practice Question 10 (Numerical MCQ)

A wire is stretched by a stress \(\sigma\) within the proportional limit. If \(Y\) is the Young's modulus of the material, the elastic potential energy stored per unit volume of the wire is:

  • (A) \(\dfrac{\sigma^2}{Y}\)
  • (B) \(\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2Y}\)
  • (C) \(\dfrac{Y}{2\sigma^2}\)
  • (D) \(\dfrac{2\sigma^2}{Y}\)
Answer: (B)
Solution: Energy density \(u = \frac{1}{2} \sigma \varepsilon\). Substituting \(\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{Y}\) yields \(u = \frac{\sigma^2}{2Y}\).

Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Tension in a pulled wire: If a wire is pulled at both ends by equal forces \(F\) (or hangs from a ceiling supporting weight \(F\)), the tension inside any cross-section is \(F\), not \(2F\).
  • Diameter vs. Radius: Always convert diameter \(d\) to radius \(r = d/2\) before computing \(A = \pi r^2\).
  • Unit Conversions: Remember \(1\,\mathrm{mm^2} = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{cm^2} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\); \(1\,\mathrm{GPa} = 10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}\).
  • Own-Weight Elongation: Note that if a uniform wire of length \(L\) and mass \(M\) stretches solely under its own weight, its effective average tension is \(\frac{Mg}{2}\), giving \(\Delta L = \frac{M g L}{2 A Y}\).

Quick Formula Checklist

  • Young's modulus: \(Y = \dfrac{F L}{A \Delta L}\)
  • Shear modulus: \(G = \dfrac{F L}{A \Delta x} = \dfrac{F}{A \theta}\)
  • Bulk modulus: \(B = -\dfrac{p V}{\Delta V}\), Compressibility \(k = \dfrac{1}{B}\)
  • Poisson's ratio: \(\nu = \dfrac{-\Delta d / d}{\Delta L / L}\)
  • Energy density: \(u = \dfrac{1}{2} \sigma \varepsilon = \dfrac{1}{2} Y \varepsilon^2 = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{2Y}\)
  • Beam sag: \(\delta = \dfrac{W l^3}{4 b d^3 Y}\)

Concise Final Recap

Elasticity quantifies a solid's resistance to deformation via its elastic moduli (\(Y\), \(G\), and \(B\)), which share the units and dimensions of stress (\(\mathrm{Pa}\)) while strain and Poisson's ratio remain dimensionless. Hooke's law holds only up to the proportional limit, beyond which ductile metals show plastic flow and permanent set, brittle materials fracture near their ultimate strength, and elastomers deform non-linearly without plasticity. Finally, energy stored per unit volume equals \(\frac{1}{2} \sigma \varepsilon\), and structural designs—from braided crane cables to I-beams—apply these principles to maximize strength and minimize sag.
