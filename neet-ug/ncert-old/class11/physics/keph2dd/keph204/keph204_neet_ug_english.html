Thermodynamics: Laws, Processes, and Heat Engines

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide to thermal equilibrium, the First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics, ideal gas processes, and Carnot cycles.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Thermodynamics investigates how thermal energy (heat) and mechanical energy (work) are interconverted in bulk matter. Unlike mechanics, which tracks the motion of a body as a whole, thermodynamics focuses on the internal macroscopic state of a system.

  • Distinguish between thermodynamic equilibrium, mechanical equilibrium, and thermal equilibrium using the Zeroth Law.
  • Classify thermodynamic quantities into state variables versus path functions, and intensive versus extensive variables.
  • Apply the First Law of Thermodynamics with accurate sign conventions to isochoric, isobaric, isothermal, adiabatic, and cyclic processes.
  • Relate molar specific heat capacities of ideal gases via Mayer's relation (\(C_P - C_V = R\)) and analyze \(P\)–\(V\) indicator diagrams.
  • Understand the limitations imposed by the Second Law of Thermodynamics, reversibility, and the maximum efficiency of a Carnot engine.

Thermal Equilibrium and the Zeroth Law

A macroscopic system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when its observable properties—such as pressure (\(P\)), volume (\(V\)), temperature (\(T\)), mass, and chemical composition—remain constant over time. Whether two systems exchange energy depends on the boundary separating them:

  • Adiabatic wall: An insulating boundary that prevents any heat flow between the system and its surroundings.
  • Diathermic wall: A conducting boundary that permits heat transfer until both sides reach the same thermal state.
Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

If two systems \(A\) and \(B\) are each separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system \(C\), then \(A\) and \(B\) are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This law establishes temperature (\(T\)) as the universal state variable that has the same value for systems in thermal equilibrium.

State Variables and Classification

An equilibrium state of a fixed mass of an ideal gas is governed by its equation of state:

\[P V = \mu R T\]

where \(\mu\) is the number of moles and \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) is the universal gas constant. To test whether a state variable is intensive or extensive, mentally divide a uniform equilibrium system into two equal halves: variables that stay unchanged are intensive, while those that halve are extensive.

Classification Definition Examples
Intensive Variables Independent of system size or mass Pressure (\(P\)), Temperature (\(T\)), Density (\(\rho\)), Molar heat capacity (\(C\))
Extensive Variables Proportional to the size or mass of the system Volume (\(V\)), Mass (\(M\)), Internal energy (\(U\)), Heat capacity (\(S\))
Path Functions Depend on the process path, not just endpoints Heat transferred (\(\Delta Q\)), Work done (\(\Delta W\))

Internal Energy, Heat, Work, and the First Law

Internal energy (\(U\)) is the sum of the random kinetic energies (translational, rotational, vibrational) and intermolecular potential energies of all molecules in the frame where the system's centre of mass is at rest. Bulk kinetic energy of a moving container does not contribute to \(U\). For an ideal gas (negligible intermolecular forces), \(U\) depends solely on temperature \(T\).

Heat (\(Q\)) and Work (\(W\)) are not properties stored inside a system; they are modes of energy in transit across the system boundary. Heat flows due to a temperature difference, whereas work is energy transfer via macroscopic mechanical displacement (such as moving a piston).

First Law of Thermodynamics (Energy Conservation) \[\Delta Q = \Delta U + \Delta W\]

For a gas expanding quasi-statically against a pressure \(P\) by a small volume \(dV\), the work done by the gas is \(dW = P\,dV\), giving the differential form:

\[dQ = dU + P\,dV \qquad \text{and} \qquad W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\,dV\]
Standard Physics Sign Convention (NEET UG)
  • \(\Delta Q > 0\): Heat absorbed by the system;   \(\Delta Q < 0\): Heat released by the system.
  • \(\Delta W > 0\): Work done by the system (expansion, \(\Delta V > 0\));   \(\Delta W < 0\): Work done on the system (compression, \(\Delta V < 0\)).
  • \(\Delta U > 0\): Internal energy increases (temperature rises for an ideal gas).
  • Path independence: Although \(\Delta Q\) and \(\Delta W\) depend on the path, their difference \(\Delta Q - \Delta W = \Delta U\) depends only on the initial and final states.

Specific Heat Capacities and Mayer's Relation

The heat \(\Delta Q\) needed to raise the temperature of a substance by \(\Delta T\) defines its heat capacity \(S = \Delta Q / \Delta T\). Dividing by mass \(m\) gives specific heat capacity \(s\), while dividing by moles \(\mu\) gives molar specific heat capacity \(C\):

\[s = \frac{1}{m}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T} \quad (\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}) \qquad \text{and} \qquad C = \frac{1}{\mu}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T} \quad (\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}})\]
  • Water and the calorie: One calorie is the heat required to raise \(1\,\mathrm{g}\) of water from \(14.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(15.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). In SI units, \(1\,\mathrm{cal} = 4.186\,\mathrm{J}\), and the specific heat capacity of water is \(4186\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\).
  • Solids (Dulong-Petit prediction): Treating each atom in a solid as a 3D harmonic oscillator with average energy \(3k_B T\), one mole has \(U = 3RT\). Since \(\Delta V\) is negligible for solids, \(C \approx \frac{\Delta U}{\Delta T} = 3R \approx 24.9\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) at room temperature (carbon is a notable low-value exception at room temperature).

Molar Specific Heats of an Ideal Gas

Because heat capacity depends on the process path, two standard conditions are defined for gases: constant volume (\(C_V\)) and constant pressure (\(C_P\)).

  • At constant volume (\(\Delta V = 0\)): \(\Delta Q = \Delta U \implies C_V = \left(\frac{\Delta U}{\Delta T}\right)_V = \frac{dU}{dT}\) (for 1 mole of an ideal gas).
  • At constant pressure: \(\Delta Q = \Delta U + P\Delta V\). Using \(PV = RT\) for 1 mole gives \(P\Delta V = R\Delta T\), which yields Mayer's relation:
\[C_P - C_V = R \qquad \text{and} \qquad \gamma = \frac{C_P}{C_V}\]

For an ideal gas with \(f\) degrees of freedom: \(C_V = \frac{f}{2}R\), \(C_P = \left(\frac{f}{2}+1\right)R\), and \(\gamma = 1 + \frac{2}{f}\). (Monatomic: \(f=3, \gamma = \frac{5}{3}\); Diatomic rigid: \(f=5, \gamma = \frac{7}{5}\)).

Thermodynamic Processes and \(P\)–\(V\) Analysis

A quasi-static process proceeds infinitely slowly so that the system remains in mechanical and thermal equilibrium with its surroundings at every intermediate stage, allowing it to be traced as a continuous curve on a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram. Rapid processes—such as free expansion of a gas into vacuum or an explosive combustion—pass through non-equilibrium states where \(P\) and \(T\) are not uniformly defined.

Free Expansion of an Ideal Gas

When an ideal gas expands freely into an evacuated, thermally insulated chamber, there is no heat exchange (\(\Delta Q = 0\)) and no external pressure to push against (\(\Delta W = 0\)). By the First Law, \(\Delta U = 0\), so the initial and final equilibrium temperatures are equal (\(T_f = T_i\)).

Process Condition Gas Relation Work Done (\(W\)) First Law (\(\Delta Q\))
Isochoric \(V = \text{const}\) \(\frac{P}{T} = \text{const}\) \(0\) \(\Delta Q = \Delta U = \mu C_V \Delta T\)
Isobaric \(P = \text{const}\) \(\frac{V}{T} = \text{const}\) \(P(V_2 - V_1) = \mu R \Delta T\) \(\Delta Q = \mu C_P \Delta T\)
Isothermal \(T = \text{const}\) \(PV = \text{const}\) \(\mu R T \ln\!\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)\) \(\Delta U = 0 \implies \Delta Q = W\)
Adiabatic \(\Delta Q = 0\) \(PV^\gamma = \text{const}\) \(\frac{P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{\mu R (T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1}\) \(\Delta U = -W\)
Cyclic Returns to initial state \(\Delta U = 0\) Area enclosed by loop \(Q_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{net}}\)

Notice the slope relationship on a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram: for an isothermal curve, \(\left(\frac{dP}{dV}\right)_{\text{iso}} = -\frac{P}{V}\), whereas for an adiabatic curve, \(\left(\frac{dP}{dV}\right)_{\text{adi}} = -\gamma\frac{P}{V}\). Since \(\gamma > 1\), the adiabatic curve is always \(\gamma\) times steeper than the isothermal curve at the point of intersection.

The Second Law, Reversibility, and the Carnot Engine

The First Law permits any process that conserves energy, yet many energy-conserving processes never occur spontaneously—for instance, a book on a table never cools the table to launch itself upward. The Second Law of Thermodynamics specifies the allowed direction of natural processes through two equivalent statements:

  • Kelvin–Planck Statement: No cyclic process is possible whose sole result is the absorption of heat from a single reservoir and its complete conversion into work. (A heat engine cannot have \(100\%\) efficiency.)
  • Clausius Statement: No process is possible whose sole result is the transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter body without external work. (A refrigerator cannot have an infinite coefficient of performance.)

Reversible vs. Irreversible Processes

A process is reversible only if both the system and its surroundings can be restored to their exact initial states without leaving any change elsewhere in the universe. Reversibility requires two strict idealizations: (1) the process must be quasi-static, and (2) there must be no dissipative effects such as friction, viscosity, or inelasticity. All spontaneous natural processes (free expansion, heat conduction across a finite temperature gap, diffusion, combustion) are irreversible.

The Carnot Engine

Proposed by Sadi Carnot, the Carnot engine is an idealized reversible heat engine operating between a hot reservoir (source) at absolute temperature \(T_1\) and a cold reservoir (sink) at absolute temperature \(T_2\). Its cycle consists of four reversible steps:

  1. 1 → 2 (Isothermal expansion at \(T_1\)): Gas absorbs heat \(Q_1 = \mu R T_1 \ln(V_2 / V_1)\) from the source and performs work \(W_{1\to 2} = Q_1\).
  2. 2 → 3 (Adiabatic expansion from \(T_1\) to \(T_2\)): Insulated expansion; temperature drops from \(T_1\) to \(T_2\).
  3. 3 → 4 (Isothermal compression at \(T_2\)): Gas rejects heat \(Q_2 = \mu R T_2 \ln(V_3 / V_4)\) to the sink.
  4. 4 → 1 (Adiabatic compression from \(T_2\) to \(T_1\)): Insulated compression restores the gas to \((P_1, V_1, T_1)\).

Using the adiabatic relations \(T_1 V_2^{\gamma - 1} = T_2 V_3^{\gamma - 1}\) and \(T_1 V_1^{\gamma - 1} = T_2 V_4^{\gamma - 1}\), we obtain \(\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{V_3}{V_4}\), which simplifies the Carnot efficiency (\(\eta\)) to:

\[\eta = \frac{W}{Q_1} = 1 - \frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = 1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{T_1}{T_2}\]

Carnot's Theorem: Between two given reservoir temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), no engine can exceed the efficiency of a reversible Carnot engine, and this maximum efficiency is completely independent of the working substance.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Applying the First Law to Isobaric Heating

Problem: Suppose \(2.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a monatomic ideal gas (\(C_V = \frac{3}{2}R\), assumed) is heated at constant pressure from \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(400\,\mathrm{K}\). Taking \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), calculate the change in internal energy, the work done by the gas, and the total heat supplied.

Solution:

The temperature change is \(\Delta T = 400\,\mathrm{K} - 300\,\mathrm{K} = 100\,\mathrm{K}\).

1. Change in internal energy (depends only on \(\Delta T\) for an ideal gas):

\[\Delta U = \mu C_V \Delta T = 2.0 \times \frac{3}{2} \times 8.314 \times 100 = 2494.2\,\mathrm{J}\]

2. Work done by the gas at constant pressure:

\[\Delta W = P\Delta V = \mu R \Delta T = 2.0 \times 8.314 \times 100 = 1662.8\,\mathrm{J}\]

3. By the First Law of Thermodynamics, total heat supplied is:

\[\Delta Q = \Delta U + \Delta W = 2494.2 + 1662.8 = 4157.0\,\mathrm{J}\]

Example 2: Calculating Net Work from a Cyclic \(P\)–\(V\) Diagram

Problem: An ideal gas undergoes a hypothetical clockwise cyclic process \(A \to C \to B \to A\) as shown below, where pressures are in \(\mathrm{kPa}\) and volumes are in litres (\(1\,\mathrm{L} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\)). Find the net work done by the gas and the net heat absorbed in one complete cycle.

Solution:

Because the cycle is traversed clockwise on the \(P\)–\(V\) diagram, the net work done by the gas is positive and equals the area enclosed by the triangle:

\[\Delta V = 4.0\,\mathrm{L} - 1.0\,\mathrm{L} = 3.0\,\mathrm{L} = 3.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\] \[\Delta P = 600\,\mathrm{kPa} - 200\,\mathrm{kPa} = 400\,\mathrm{kPa} = 4.0 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}\] \[W_{\text{net}} = \frac{1}{2} \times \Delta V \times \Delta P = \frac{1}{2} \times (3.0 \times 10^{-3}) \times (4.0 \times 10^{5}) = 600\,\mathrm{J}\]

For any complete cyclic process, \(\Delta U = 0\). Therefore, net heat absorbed is \(Q_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{net}} = 600\,\mathrm{J}\).

Example 3: Adiabatic Compression and Temperature Rise

Problem: Assume \(1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a monatomic ideal gas (\(\gamma = \frac{5}{3}\)) initially at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) is compressed adiabatically to one-eighth of its initial volume (\(V_2 = V_1 / 8\)). Find its final temperature and the work done on the gas. (Take \(R = 8.3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\).)

Solution:

For a reversible adiabatic process, \(T_1 V_1^{\gamma - 1} = T_2 V_2^{\gamma - 1}\), where \(\gamma - 1 = \frac{5}{3} - 1 = \frac{2}{3}\):

\[T_2 = T_1 \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma - 1} = 300 \times (8)^{2/3} = 300 \times 4 = 1200\,\mathrm{K}\]

Work done by the gas in an adiabatic process is:

\[W = \frac{\mu R (T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{1.0 \times 8.3 \times (300 - 1200)}{2/3} = \frac{8.3 \times (-900) \times 3}{2} = -11205\,\mathrm{J}\]

The negative sign confirms that \(11205\,\mathrm{J}\) of work is done on the gas.

Example 4: Carnot Engine Efficiency and Heat Rejection

Problem: A hypothetical Carnot engine operates between a hot reservoir at \(527\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and a cold reservoir at \(127\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). If it absorbs \(2000\,\mathrm{J}\) of heat from the hot reservoir per cycle, determine its efficiency, work output per cycle, and heat rejected to the cold reservoir.

Solution:

Convert both temperatures to Kelvin first:

\[T_1 = 527 + 273 = 800\,\mathrm{K}, \qquad T_2 = 127 + 273 = 400\,\mathrm{K}\]

Efficiency of the Carnot engine:

\[\eta = 1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 1 - \frac{400}{800} = 0.50 \quad (50\%)\]

Work output per cycle: \(W = \eta Q_1 = 0.50 \times 2000\,\mathrm{J} = 1000\,\mathrm{J}\).

Heat rejected to the cold reservoir: \(Q_2 = Q_1 - W = 2000 - 1000 = 1000\,\mathrm{J}\).

Answer Key

1. (C)  |  2. (B)  |  3. (C)  |  4. (B)  |  5. (A)  |  6. (B)  |  7. (B)  |  8. (A)

Worked Solutions

Solution 1 (C): Pressure and density do not change when an equilibrium system is divided into two equal halves, so both are intensive. Internal energy, volume, and mass are extensive.

Solution 2 (B): Heat absorbed gives \(\Delta Q = +420\,\mathrm{J}\). Work done on the system gives \(\Delta W = -180\,\mathrm{J}\). By the First Law, \(\Delta U = \Delta Q - \Delta W = 420 - (-180) = +600\,\mathrm{J}\).

Solution 3 (C): Free expansion into vacuum against zero external pressure means \(\Delta W = 0\). Because the container is thermally insulated, \(\Delta Q = 0\). Thus \(\Delta U = 0\), and for an ideal gas \(T\) remains unchanged.

Solution 4 (B): At constant pressure, \(\Delta Q = \mu C_P \Delta T\) and \(\Delta U = \mu C_V \Delta T\). Therefore, the fraction is \(\frac{\Delta U}{\Delta Q} = \frac{C_V}{C_P} = \frac{1}{\gamma} = \frac{5}{7}\).

Solution 5 (A): During expansion (\(V_2 > V_1\)), the adiabatic curve drops more steeply than the isothermal curve because \(\gamma > 1\). Hence at \(2V_0\), \(P_{\text{iso}} > P_{\text{adi}}\), and the area under the isothermal curve is larger, so \(W_{\text{iso}} > W_{\text{adi}}\).

Solution 6 (B): Area of the rectangle is \(\Delta P \times \Delta V = (5.0 - 2.0) \times 10^{5} \times (3.0 - 1.0) \times 10^{-3} = 600\,\mathrm{J}\). Because the cycle is anticlockwise, \(W_{\text{net}} = -600\,\mathrm{J}\). Since \(\Delta U = 0\), \(Q_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{net}} = -600\,\mathrm{J}\), meaning \(600\,\mathrm{J}\) of heat is released.

Solution 7 (B): Initial sink temperature \(T_2 = 27 + 273 = 300\,\mathrm{K}\). With \(\eta_1 = 0.25 = 1 - \frac{300}{T_1}\), we get \(\frac{300}{T_1} = 0.75 \implies T_1 = 400\,\mathrm{K}\). For \(\eta_2 = 0.40 = 1 - \frac{T_2'}{400}\), we find \(\frac{T_2'}{400} = 0.60 \implies T_2' = 240\,\mathrm{K}\). The sink must be lowered by \(300 - 240 = 60\,\mathrm{K}\).

Solution 8 (A): Both statements are true, and the reversible nature of the four-step Carnot cycle between two fixed temperatures guarantees (by Carnot's theorem) that \(\eta = 1 - T_2/T_1\) depends only on \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), not on the working substance.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Celsius vs. Kelvin in Carnot Efficiency: Always convert temperatures to Kelvin before computing \(\eta = 1 - T_2/T_1\). Using \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is a classic error.
  • Confusing Heat with Internal Energy: A system possesses internal energy \(U\), never "heat". Heat \(Q\) and work \(W\) exist only during energy transfer.
  • Sign of Work in Compression: When work is done on the gas, \(\Delta W\) is negative in \(\Delta Q = \Delta U + \Delta W\).
  • Loop Direction on \(P\)–\(V\) Diagrams: Clockwise cycles yield positive net work; anticlockwise cycles yield negative net work.
  • Isothermal vs. Free Expansion: Both have \(\Delta U = 0\) for an ideal gas, but in isothermal expansion \(\Delta Q = \Delta W \neq 0\), whereas in free expansion \(\Delta Q = \Delta W = 0\).

Concise Final Recap

  • Zeroth Law: Defines temperature \(T\) as the common property of systems in thermal equilibrium.
  • First Law: \(\Delta Q = \Delta U + \Delta W\), with \(\Delta U = \mu C_V \Delta T\) for any process of an ideal gas.
  • Mayer's Relation: \(C_P - C_V = R\) and \(\gamma = C_P / C_V\).
  • Second Law & Carnot Engine: No engine can convert \(100\%\) of absorbed heat into work; maximum efficiency between \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) is \(\eta_{\max} = 1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1}\).
