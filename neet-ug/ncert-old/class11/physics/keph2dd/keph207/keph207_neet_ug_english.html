Mechanical Waves: Propagation, Superposition, and Standing Modes

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide covering progressive waves, wave speed, reflection, normal modes in strings and organ pipes, and beats.

Learning Objectives and Overview

In mechanics, we first study individual particles oscillating in isolation. When many such oscillating particles are coupled together by elastic forces inside a continuous medium, a local disturbance does not stay confined—it travels outward as a wave. Mastering wave mechanics is essential for NEET UG, as questions frequently test wave equations, speed dependencies, organ pipe harmonics, and beat frequencies.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish mechanical waves from electromagnetic and matter waves, and classify transverse versus longitudinal motion.
  • Interpret every parameter in the progressive harmonic wave equation \(y(x,t) = a\sin(kx \mp \omega t + \phi)\).
  • Calculate wave speeds in stretched strings, fluids, solid bars, and ideal gases using Laplace's adiabatic correction.
  • Apply the principle of superposition to analyze constructive/destructive interference and boundary reflections.
  • Determine normal-mode frequencies for stretched strings, open organ pipes, and closed-open organ pipes.
  • Solve tuning and frequency-shift problems using the beat frequency relation \(\nu_{\text{beat}} = |\nu_1 - \nu_2|\).

Nature and Classification of Waves

A wave is a self-propagating pattern of disturbance that transfers energy and information from one region to another without any net transport of matter as a whole. For instance, when a pebble drops into a still pond, ripples travel outward while a floating cork merely bobs up and down near its original spot. Similarly, sound waves travel through air via local pressure and density fluctuations rather than a bulk wind.

Three Broad Categories of Waves

  • Mechanical Waves: Require a material medium possessing both inertia (mass density) and elasticity (restoring forces). Examples include waves on a stretched string, sound waves in air, and seismic waves. They cannot travel through vacuum.
  • Electromagnetic Waves: Oscillating electric and magnetic fields that require no material medium and travel through vacuum at \(c = 299{,}792{,}458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (approximately \(3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)). Light, radio waves, and X-rays belong to this class.
  • Matter Waves: Quantum-mechanical waves associated with moving particles such as electrons, protons, and atoms, utilized in devices like electron microscopes.

Transverse vs. Longitudinal Mechanical Waves

Mechanical waves are classified according to the direction of particle oscillation relative to the direction of wave propagation:

  • Transverse Waves: Constituents of the medium oscillate perpendicular to the wave velocity. Each element experiences shearing strain, so transverse mechanical waves can propagate only in media that sustain shear stress (solids and stretched strings, not bulk fluids).
  • Longitudinal Waves: Constituents oscillate parallel (back and forth) to the wave velocity, creating alternating zones of compression (high density and pressure) and rarefaction (low density and pressure). Because solids, liquids, and gases all resist volume compression (possess a bulk modulus), longitudinal waves propagate in all elastic media.

Special case — Water Surface Waves: Ripples of very short wavelength (a few centimetres) are capillary waves restored by surface tension, whereas long ocean waves (metres to hundreds of metres) are gravity waves restored by gravity. Particles in ocean waves trace circular or elliptical paths, combining transverse and longitudinal motion.

Mathematical Description of a Progressive Harmonic Wave

A travelling (progressive) wave depends on both position \(x\) and time \(t\). Fixing \(t\) reveals the spatial shape of the wave, while fixing \(x\) reveals the simple harmonic oscillation of a single medium particle over time.

\[y(x,t) = a \sin(kx - \omega t + \phi) \quad \text{(travelling in }+x\text{ direction)}\] \[y(x,t) = a \sin(kx + \omega t + \phi) \quad \text{(travelling in }-x\text{ direction)}\]

For a longitudinal wave, the transverse displacement \(y(x,t)\) is replaced by the longitudinal displacement \(s(x,t)\) along the direction of propagation. If a wave is expressed as a linear combination \(y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t) + B\cos(kx - \omega t)\), its resultant amplitude is \(a = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}\) and its initial phase is \(\phi = \tan^{-1}(B/A)\).

Key Wave Parameters

  • Amplitude (\(a\)): Maximum magnitude of displacement from equilibrium (always taken as positive).
  • Phase and Initial Phase (\(\phi\)): The entire argument \((kx \mp \omega t + \phi)\) is the phase; \(\phi\) is the phase at \(x = 0, t = 0\).
  • Wavelength (\(\lambda\)) and Angular Wave Number (\(k\)): \(\lambda\) is the minimum distance between two points in the same state of vibration (e.g., two consecutive crests). Since sine repeats every \(2\pi\): \[k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \quad (\text{SI unit: }\mathrm{rad\,m^{-1}}\text{ or }\mathrm{m^{-1}})\]
  • Time Period (\(T\)), Frequency (\(\nu\)), and Angular Frequency (\(\omega\)): \(T\) is the time for one complete oscillation of a particle, and \(\nu = 1/T\) is measured in hertz (\(\mathrm{Hz}\)): \[\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi\nu \quad (\text{SI unit: }\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}})\]
  • Phase Difference vs. Path Difference: At a fixed instant, two points separated by \(\Delta x\) differ in phase by \(\Delta\phi = k\,\Delta x = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta x\). At a fixed location, two instants separated by \(\Delta t\) differ in phase by \(\Delta\phi = \omega\,\Delta t = \frac{2\pi}{T}\Delta t\).

Direction Rule (Quick Exam Check)

Look at the relative signs of the \(x\) and \(t\) terms inside the phase:

  • If \(kx\) and \(\omega t\) have opposite signs (e.g., \(kx - \omega t\) or \(\omega t - kx\)), the wave travels in the \(+x\) direction.
  • If \(kx\) and \(\omega t\) have the same sign (e.g., \(kx + \omega t\) or \(-kx - \omega t\)), the wave travels in the \(-x\) direction.

Speed of a Travelling Wave in Different Media

Tracking a point of constant phase \((kx - \omega t = \text{constant})\) gives \(k\,\Delta x - \omega\,\Delta t = 0\), so the wave speed is:

\[v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \lambda\nu = \frac{\lambda}{T}\]

Crucially, for mechanical waves in a non-dispersive medium, wave speed \(v\) depends solely on the elastic and inertial properties of the medium, not on the amplitude or frequency. The source fixes the frequency \(\nu\), the medium fixes the speed \(v\), and the wavelength adjusts as \(\lambda = v/\nu\).

1. Transverse Wave on a Stretched String

For a string under tension \(T\) with linear mass density (mass per unit length) \(\mu = m/L\):

\[v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}\]

2. Longitudinal Wave in Fluids and Solids

In a fluid of bulk modulus \(B = -\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta V / V}\) and volume mass density \(\rho\):

\[v = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}}\]

For longitudinal waves along a thin solid bar where lateral expansion is negligible, the bulk modulus is replaced by Young's modulus \(Y\):

\[v = \sqrt{\frac{Y}{\rho}}\]

Even though solids and liquids have much higher densities than gases, their elastic moduli (\(B\) or \(Y\)) are orders of magnitude larger, making sound travel significantly faster in liquids and solids than in gases.

3. Speed of Sound in an Ideal Gas: Newton vs. Laplace

Isaac Newton assumed sound propagation in air is isothermal (\(PV = \text{constant}\)), giving an isothermal bulk modulus \(B_{\text{iso}} = P\) and \(v = \sqrt{P/\rho}\). At standard temperature and pressure (STP), this yields about \(280\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), which is roughly \(15\%\) lower than the measured \(331\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Pierre-Simon Laplace corrected this by noting that compressions and rarefactions occur so rapidly that heat has no time to flow; hence the process is adiabatic (\(PV^\gamma = \text{constant}\), where \(\gamma = C_p/C_v\)). Differentiating gives \(B_{\text{ad}} = \gamma P\), leading to the Newton–Laplace formula:

\[v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T_{\text{K}}}{M}}\]

where \(R\) is the universal gas constant, \(T_{\text{K}}\) is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and \(M\) is the molar mass. For air (primarily diatomic gases), \(\gamma = 7/5 = 1.4\), yielding \(331.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Factors Affecting Speed of Sound in Air

  • Pressure: At constant temperature, \(P/\rho = RT_{\text{K}}/M\) is constant. Thus, speed of sound in an ideal gas is independent of pressure.
  • Temperature: \(v \propto \sqrt{T_{\text{K}}}\). Speed increases as temperature rises.
  • Humidity: Water vapour has a lower molar mass (\(18\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) than dry air (\(\approx 29\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)). Moist air is less dense than dry air at the same pressure and temperature, so sound travels faster in humid air.

Superposition, Interference, and Boundary Reflection

When two or more waves overlap in the same region of a medium, each wave propagates as if the others were absent, and the net displacement is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements:

\[y(x,t) = y_1(x,t) + y_2(x,t) + \dots + y_n(x,t)\]

Interference of Two Harmonic Waves

Suppose two waves of equal amplitude \(a\), identical \(\omega\) and \(k\), travel in the \(+x\) direction with a constant phase difference \(\phi\):

\(y_1 = a\sin(kx - \omega t)\) and \(y_2 = a\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)\). Adding them via \(\sin A + \sin B\) gives:

\[y(x,t) = \left(2a\cos\frac{\phi}{2}\right)\sin\left(kx - \omega t + \frac{\phi}{2}\right)\]
  • Constructive Interference: When \(\phi = 0, 2\pi, 4\pi, \dots\) (waves in phase), \(\cos(\phi/2) = \pm 1\), giving maximum amplitude \(A = 2a\).
  • Destructive Interference: When \(\phi = \pi, 3\pi, 5\pi, \dots\) (waves completely out of phase), \(\cos(\phi/2) = 0\), giving zero resultant displacement everywhere.

Reflection at Rigid vs. Open Boundaries

  • Rigid (Fixed) Boundary: The boundary point cannot move (\(y = 0\) at all times). By superposition, the reflected wave must cancel the incident wave at the boundary, meaning it undergoes a phase reversal of \(\pi\) radians (\(180^\circ\)). If \(y_i = a\sin(kx - \omega t)\), then \(y_r = -a\sin(kx + \omega t)\).
  • Free (Open) Boundary: Such as a string attached to a frictionless ring on a rod, or the open end of an organ pipe. The boundary moves freely, so the reflected wave experiences zero phase change (\(0\) radians): \(y_r = a\sin(kx + \omega t)\).

Standing Waves and Normal Modes

When two identical harmonic waves travel in opposite directions along the same medium—such as an incident wave and its reflection—they superpose to form a standing (stationary) wave. Adding \(y_1 = a\sin(kx - \omega t)\) and \(y_2 = a\sin(kx + \omega t)\) yields:

\[y(x,t) = \left[2a\sin(kx)\right]\cos(\omega t)\]

Unlike a progressive wave, the spatial term \(\sin(kx)\) and time term \(\cos(\omega t)\) are separated. Energy is not transmitted along the medium; instead, each particle oscillates with the same frequency \(\omega\) but with a position-dependent amplitude \(2a|\sin(kx)|\):

  • Nodes (N): Fixed points of zero displacement (\(\sin kx = 0\)), located at \(x = n\frac{\lambda}{2}\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, \dots\).
  • Antinodes (A): Fixed points of maximum amplitude \(2a\) (\(|\sin kx| = 1\)), located at \(x = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\lambda}{2}\).
  • Spacing: Distance between two consecutive nodes (or two consecutive antinodes) is \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\). Distance between an adjacent node and antinode is \(\frac{\lambda}{4}\).
  • Phase Behaviour: All particles between two successive nodes oscillate in phase with one another, whereas particles on opposite sides of a node are out of phase by \(\pi\).

Normal Modes of Bounded Systems

Boundary conditions restrict a finite medium of length \(L\) to vibrate only at discrete natural frequencies called normal modes (or harmonics).

1. Stretched String Fixed at Both Ends

Both \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) must be displacement nodes, requiring \(L = n\frac{\lambda}{2}\):

\[\nu_n = \frac{nv}{2L} = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\]

All harmonics (both even and odd) are present in the ratio \(1 : 2 : 3 : \dots\).

2. Organ Pipe Open at Both Ends

Both open ends are displacement antinodes (and pressure nodes). The length fits an integer number of half-wavelengths, \(L = n\frac{\lambda}{2}\):

\[\nu_n = \frac{nv}{2L}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\]

All harmonics are present, with fundamental frequency \(\nu_1 = \frac{v}{2L}\).

3. Organ Pipe Closed at One End and Open at the Other

The closed end is a displacement node (pressure antinode), while the open end is a displacement antinode (pressure node). Thus, \(L = (2m - 1)\frac{\lambda}{4}\) for \(m = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) (or equivalently \(L = (n + \frac{1}{2})\frac{\lambda}{2}\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)):

\[\nu = \frac{(2m - 1)v}{4L}, \quad m = 1, 2, 3, \dots\]

Here, the fundamental frequency is \(\nu_1 = \frac{v}{4L}\) (half that of an open pipe of the same length), and only odd harmonics (\(1 : 3 : 5 : \dots\)) are possible. Note that the third harmonic (\(3\nu_1\)) is the first overtone.

Beats

When two sound waves of nearly equal frequencies \(\nu_1\) and \(\nu_2\) and comparable amplitudes superpose at a point, the listener hears a tone at the average frequency \(\nu_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\nu_1 + \nu_2}{2}\) whose loudness periodically rises and falls (waxes and wanes). This phenomenon is called beats.

Taking \(s_1 = a\cos(\omega_1 t)\) and \(s_2 = a\cos(\omega_2 t)\) at \(x = 0\):

\[s = \left[2a\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{2}t\right)\right]\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}t\right)\]

Because the intensity depends on the square of the amplitude, maximum loudness occurs whenever \(\cos\left(\frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{2}t\right) = \pm 1\)—twice per cycle of the slowly varying envelope. Therefore, the beat frequency is:

\[\nu_{\text{beat}} = |\nu_1 - \nu_2|\]

Worked Examples

Analyzing a Progressive Wave Equation

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A transverse harmonic wave on a long taut cord is described in SI units by:

\[y(x,t) = 0.02 \sin(4.0\,x + 120\,t + \pi/6)\]

Determine: (a) the direction of propagation and wave speed, (b) the wavelength and frequency, and (c) the phase difference between two points on the cord separated by \(0.25\pi\,\mathrm{m}\) at the same instant.

Solution:

  1. Comparing with \(y(x,t) = a\sin(kx + \omega t + \phi)\), we identify \(a = 0.02\,\mathrm{m}\), \(k = 4.0\,\mathrm{rad\,m^{-1}}\), and \(\omega = 120\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\).
  2. Because \(kx\) and \(\omega t\) carry the same sign (\(+\)), the wave travels in the \(-x\) direction. Its speed is: \[v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{120}{4.0} = 30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]
  3. Wavelength and frequency are: \[\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{4.0} = \frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 1.57\,\mathrm{m}, \qquad \nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{120}{2\pi} = \frac{60}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Hz} \approx 19.1\,\mathrm{Hz}\]
  4. At a fixed instant, the phase difference for separation \(\Delta x = 0.25\pi\,\mathrm{m}\) is: \[\Delta\phi = k\,\Delta x = 4.0 \times 0.25\pi = \pi\,\mathrm{rad}\]

Wave Speed and Tension on a Stretched Wire

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A uniform metal wire of length \(2.5\,\mathrm{m}\) and mass \(20\,\mathrm{g}\) is stretched between two rigid clamps under a tension of \(72\,\mathrm{N}\). Calculate the speed of a transverse pulse on the wire and the time it takes to travel from one clamp to the other.

Solution:

  1. Convert mass to kilograms: \(m = 20 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg} = 0.020\,\mathrm{kg}\).
  2. Linear mass density: \[\mu = \frac{m}{L} = \frac{0.020\,\mathrm{kg}}{2.5\,\mathrm{m}} = 8.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}\]
  3. Transverse wave speed: \[v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{72}{8.0 \times 10^{-3}}} = \sqrt{9000} \approx 94.9\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]
  4. Time to traverse length \(L = 2.5\,\mathrm{m}\): \[t = \frac{L}{v} = \frac{2.5}{94.9} \approx 0.0263\,\mathrm{s} = 26.3\,\mathrm{ms}\]

Resonance in Open and Closed Organ Pipes

Problem (Assumed Scenario): An air column of length \(50\,\mathrm{cm}\) is open at both ends. Taking the speed of sound in air as \(340\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), find its fundamental frequency. If one end of the pipe is suddenly closed, will a \(680\,\mathrm{Hz}\) tuning fork resonate with the closed pipe?

Solution:

  1. For an open pipe of length \(L = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}\), the fundamental frequency is: \[\nu_1^{\text{open}} = \frac{v}{2L} = \frac{340}{2 \times 0.50} = 340\,\mathrm{Hz}\]
  2. When one end is closed, the new fundamental frequency becomes: \[\nu_1^{\text{closed}} = \frac{v}{4L} = \frac{340}{4 \times 0.50} = 170\,\mathrm{Hz}\]
  3. A closed-open pipe supports only odd harmonics: \(170\,\mathrm{Hz}\), \(510\,\mathrm{Hz}\), \(850\,\mathrm{Hz}\), etc. Since \(680\,\mathrm{Hz} = 4 \times 170\,\mathrm{Hz}\) is an even (fourth) harmonic, no resonance will occur.

Determining Unknown Frequency Using Beats

Problem (Assumed Scenario): Two violin strings \(P\) and \(Q\) produce \(4\,\mathrm{beats\,s^{-1}}\) when sounded together. String \(P\) has a known frequency of \(320\,\mathrm{Hz}\). When the tension in string \(Q\) is slightly increased, the beat frequency rises to \(6\,\mathrm{beats\,s^{-1}}\). What was the original frequency of string \(Q\)?

Solution:

  1. Initially, \(|\nu_P - \nu_Q| = 4\,\mathrm{Hz}\), so \(\nu_Q\) is either \(324\,\mathrm{Hz}\) or \(316\,\mathrm{Hz}\).
  2. Increasing tension in \(Q\) increases its frequency \(\nu_Q\) (since \(\nu \propto \sqrt{T}\)).
  3. If \(\nu_Q\) had been \(316\,\mathrm{Hz}\), increasing it slightly would move it closer to \(320\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and decrease the beat frequency. Instead, the beat frequency increased to \(6\,\mathrm{Hz}\), meaning \(\nu_Q\) moved further above \(320\,\mathrm{Hz}\).
  4. Hence, the original frequency of string \(Q\) was \(324\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

Answer Key and Worked Solutions

Q1: (A) Comparing \(y = 0.04\sin(100\pi t - 2\pi x)\) with standard form gives \(\omega = 100\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\) and \(k = 2\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,m^{-1}}\). Therefore, \(v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{100\pi}{2\pi} = 50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{2\pi} = 1.0\,\mathrm{m}\).

Q2: (B) Wave speed on a string is \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\). With \(T' = 4T\) and \(\mu' = \mu/2\), the new speed is \(v' = \sqrt{\frac{4T}{\mu/2}} = \sqrt{8}\,\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} = 2\sqrt{2}\,v\).

Q3: (D) From the Newton–Laplace relation \(v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T_{\text{K}}}{M}}\), at constant temperature \(P/\rho\) remains constant. Hence, the speed of sound is independent of pressure changes.

Q4: (C) Resultant amplitude is \(A = 2a\cos(\phi/2)\). For \(\phi = 120^\circ\), \(\phi/2 = 60^\circ\), so \(A = 2a\cos 60^\circ = 2a \times \frac{1}{2} = a\).

Q5: (B) Comparing with \(y = 2a\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)\), we have \(k = \frac{\pi}{3}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\). Since \(k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\), \(\lambda = 6.0\,\mathrm{m}\). The distance between two consecutive nodes is \(\frac{\lambda}{2} = \frac{6.0}{2} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m}\).

Q6: (B) For an open pipe, the third harmonic is \(\nu_3^{\text{open}} = 3\left(\frac{v}{2L}\right) = \frac{3v}{2L}\). For a closed-open pipe, the third harmonic is \(\nu_3^{\text{closed}} = 3\left(\frac{v}{4L}\right) = \frac{3v}{4L}\). Their ratio is \(\frac{3v/(2L)}{3v/(4L)} = \frac{2}{1}\).

Q7: (B) Initially \(\nu_B = 480 \pm 5\), i.e., \(485\,\mathrm{Hz}\) or \(475\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Loading fork \(B\) with wax increases its inertia and decreases its frequency. Since the beat frequency dropped from \(5\) to \(2\), \(\nu_B\) moved closer to \(480\,\mathrm{Hz}\) when lowered. Thus, \(\nu_B\) must have been \(485\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

Q8: (A) Propagation of transverse mechanical waves requires a medium capable of sustaining shear stress (having a non-zero shear modulus). Gases like air have zero shear modulus and only resist volume compression, so only longitudinal waves can propagate through air.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent NEET Traps
  • Overtone vs. Harmonic in Closed Pipes: In a closed-open pipe, even harmonics are absent. Therefore, the first overtone is the third harmonic (\(3\nu_1\)), and the second overtone is the fifth harmonic (\(5\nu_1\)).
  • Pressure Node vs. Displacement Node: At the closed end of an organ pipe, particle displacement is zero (displacement node), but pressure variation is maximum (pressure antinode). At the open end, displacement is maximum (displacement antinode), while pressure variation is zero (pressure node).
  • Waxing vs. Filing a Tuning Fork: Adding wax increases mass and decreases frequency; filing removes mass and increases frequency.
  • Unit Consistency: Always convert \(\mathrm{cm}\) to \(\mathrm{m}\) and \(\mathrm{g}\) to \(\mathrm{kg}\) before computing \(\mu = m/L\) or \(v = \omega/k\).

  • Can I solve beat-frequency problems involving tension changes or waxing/filing?

Concise Final Recap

Mechanical waves transfer energy through coupled elastic oscillations without bulk matter flow. Progressive waves carry constant amplitude across all particles and travel at a medium-determined speed \(v = \lambda\nu\), whereas standing waves arise from counter-propagating wave interference and trap energy between fixed nodes and antinodes. Boundary geometry dictates the allowed normal modes—all harmonics for strings and open pipes, and exclusively odd harmonics for closed-open pipes—while slight frequency mismatches between superposing waves produce beats at \(\nu_{\text{beat}} = |\nu_1 - \nu_2|\).
