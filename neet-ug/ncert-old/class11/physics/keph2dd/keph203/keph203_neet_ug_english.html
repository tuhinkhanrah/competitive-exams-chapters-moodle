1. Temperature, Heat, and Thermometry

Temperature is a macroscopic measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body. Two bodies placed in thermal contact exchange energy until they reach thermal equilibrium, at which point they share the same temperature. Heat is the form of energy transferred between a system and its surroundings (or between two systems) solely due to a temperature difference. Its SI unit is the joule (\(\mathrm{J}\)), while the SI unit of temperature is the kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\)).

Constructing a Temperature Scale

A thermometer exploits a measurable physical property—called a thermometric property, such as the volume of mercury, pressure of a constant-volume gas, or electrical resistance of a wire—that varies smoothly with temperature. Defining a linear temperature scale requires two reproducible fixed points: the ice point (normal freezing point of pure water at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\)) and the steam point (normal boiling point of pure water at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\)).

General Linear Scale Conversion
\[ \frac{t_{\mathrm{C}} - 0}{100} = \frac{t_{\mathrm{F}} - 32}{180} = \frac{T - 273.15}{100} = \frac{X - X_{\mathrm{ice}}}{X_{\mathrm{steam}} - X_{\mathrm{ice}}} \]

Here \(t_{\mathrm{C}}\) is temperature in °C, \(t_{\mathrm{F}}\) in °F, \(T\) in \(\mathrm{K}\), and \(X\) is the reading on any linear thermometer with fixed-point readings \(X_{\mathrm{ice}}\) and \(X_{\mathrm{steam}}\).

Ideal-Gas Equation and Absolute Zero

Liquid-in-glass thermometers depend on the expansion properties of the liquid, so they can disagree slightly between fixed points. By contrast, dilute gases at low pressure obey Boyle's law (\(PV = \text{constant}\) at constant \(T\)) and Charles' law (\(V/T = \text{constant}\) at constant \(P\)), combining into the ideal-gas equation:

\[ PV = \mu R T \]

where \(\mu\) is the number of moles and \(R = 8.31\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) is the universal gas constant. In a constant-volume gas thermometer, \(P \propto T\). Extrapolating the straight-line plot of \(P\) versus \(t_{\mathrm{C}}\) to zero pressure yields the universal intercept \(-273.15\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) for all low-density gases. This temperature is defined as absolute zero (\(0\,\mathrm{K}\)). Modern thermometry uses a single primary fixed point: the triple point of water, where solid, liquid, and vapour coexist in equilibrium at \(T_{\mathrm{tr}} = 273.16\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(0.01\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)) and \(P_{\mathrm{tr}} = 6.11 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa}\).

2. Thermal Expansion and Thermal Stress

Most materials expand when heated because increased thermal energy raises the average separation between oscillating atoms. For a small temperature change \(\Delta T\), fractional changes in length, area, and volume of an isotropic solid are proportional to \(\Delta T\):

Expansion Type Defining Relation Coefficient Relation Typical Units
Linear Expansion \(\dfrac{\Delta l}{l} = \alpha_l \Delta T\) \(\alpha_l\) (characteristic of material) \(\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) or \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}^{-1}\)
Area Expansion \(\dfrac{\Delta A}{A} = \alpha_A \Delta T\) \(\alpha_A = 2\alpha_l\) \(\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) or \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}^{-1}\)
Volume Expansion \(\dfrac{\Delta V}{V} = \alpha_v \Delta T\) \(\alpha_v = 3\alpha_l\) (isotropic solids) \(\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) or \(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}^{-1}\)
Ideal Gas (const. \(P\)) \(\dfrac{\Delta V}{V} = \dfrac{\Delta T}{T}\) \(\alpha_v = \dfrac{1}{T}\) (temperature-dependent) \(\mathrm{K^{-1}}\)
Key NEET Concept — Expansion of Cavities and Holes: When a solid containing a hole or hollow cavity is heated uniformly, the hole expands by the exact same fractional amount as a solid piece of the same material filling that hole. Think of uniform thermal expansion as photographic enlargement: every linear dimension (outer radius, inner hole radius, circumference) increases according to \(l' = l(1 + \alpha_l \Delta T)\).

Thermal Stress in Constrained Solids

If a rod of length \(l\), cross-sectional area \(A\), and Young's modulus \(Y\) is rigidly clamped at both ends so it cannot expand (or contract) when its temperature changes by \(\Delta T\), it develops a thermal strain \(\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta l}{l} = \alpha_l \Delta T\). The resulting thermal stress and thermal force are:

\[ \text{Thermal Stress} = Y \alpha_l \Delta T, \qquad F = Y A \alpha_l \Delta T \]

Notice that the thermal force \(F\) is independent of the original length \(l\) of the rod.

Anomalous Expansion of Water

Unlike ordinary liquids, water contracts when heated from \(0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) to \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and expands above \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). Consequently, water attains its maximum density (\(1.000 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) and minimum specific volume at \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). In winter, as surface water in a lake cools toward \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), it becomes denser and sinks, driving convection. Once the entire lake reaches \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), further cooling of the surface below \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) makes the top layer less dense, so it stays at the surface and freezes into ice. The deeper water remains liquid near \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), allowing aquatic life to survive.

3. Specific Heat Capacity, Calorimetry, and Change of State

The heat capacity \(S\) of a body is the heat required per unit temperature rise: \(S = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T}\). Dividing by mass \(m\) gives the specific heat capacity \(s\) (SI unit: \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)), and dividing by moles \(\mu\) gives the molar specific heat capacity \(C\) (SI unit: \(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)):

\[ \Delta Q = m s \Delta T = \mu C \Delta T \]

For gases, heat transfer depends on constraints: \(C_p\) (at constant pressure) is greater than \(C_v\) (at constant volume). Water has an exceptionally high specific heat capacity (\(s_{\mathrm{water}} = 4186\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \approx 1\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}\,^{\circ}C^{-1}}\)), making it ideal as an engine coolant and moderating coastal temperatures.

Principle of Calorimetry

In an isolated system (such as a well-insulated calorimeter) where no heat escapes to the surroundings, conservation of energy requires:

\[ \text{Heat lost by hotter bodies} = \text{Heat gained by colder bodies} \]

The water equivalent \(W = m_{\mathrm{cal}} s_{\mathrm{cal}} / s_{\mathrm{water}}\) of a calorimeter represents the mass of water that absorbs the same heat as the calorimeter for a given temperature rise.

Change of State and Latent Heat

During a phase transition (melting/freezing, vaporisation/condensation, or sublimation), heat supplied or removed breaks or forms intermolecular bonds without changing the temperature. The heat required per unit mass is the latent heat \(L\):

\[ Q = m L \]
  • For water at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\): latent heat of fusion \(L_{\mathrm{f}} = 3.33 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) (\(80\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}\)) at \(0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\); latent heat of vaporisation \(L_{\mathrm{v}} = 22.6 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) (\(540\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}\)) at \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).
  • Because \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) of steam at \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) carries \(22.6 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}\) more thermal energy than \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) of liquid water at \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), steam burns are far more severe than boiling-water burns.
  • Effect of Pressure: Increasing pressure raises the boiling point of water (basis of a pressure cooker) and lowers the melting point of ice (causing regelation—ice melts under high pressure and refreezes when pressure is removed).

4. Modes of Heat Transfer and Cooling Laws

Conduction and Thermal Resistance

Conduction transfers heat through molecular collisions and free-electron motion without bulk movement of matter. In steady state across a uniform bar of length \(L\), cross-sectional area \(A\), thermal conductivity \(K\), and end temperatures \(T_1 > T_2\), the rate of heat flow (heat current) \(H\) is:

Steady-State Heat Conduction & Electrical Analogy
\[ H = \frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = K A \frac{T_1 - T_2}{L} = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{R_{\mathrm{th}}}, \qquad \text{where } R_{\mathrm{th}} = \frac{L}{K A} \]
  • Series combination (same heat current \(H\)): \(R_{\mathrm{eq}} = R_1 + R_2\). For two rods of equal \(L\) and \(A\), \(K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \dfrac{2 K_1 K_2}{K_1 + K_2}\).
  • Parallel combination (same \(\Delta T\)): \(\dfrac{1}{R_{\mathrm{eq}}} = \dfrac{1}{R_1} + \dfrac{1}{R_2}\). For two rods of equal \(L\) and \(A\), \(K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \dfrac{K_1 + K_2}{2}\).
Tutorial Diagram 4: Series thermal conduction through two insulated rods joined at junction temperature \(T_0\).

Convection

Convection transfers heat in fluids (liquids and gases) by bulk motion of matter. In natural convection, gravity and buoyancy drive warm, less dense fluid upward while cooler, denser fluid sinks—explaining daytime sea breezes (land warms faster than water), nighttime land breezes, and trade winds. In forced convection, a pump or fan drives circulation, as in the human circulatory system or automobile radiators.

Radiation and Blackbody Laws

Thermal radiation transfers energy via electromagnetic waves at the speed of light (\(3 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)) and requires no material medium. Dark, matte surfaces are both good absorbers and good emitters, whereas shiny, light-colored surfaces are poor emitters and good reflectors.

Wien's Displacement Law & Stefan-Boltzmann Law
  • Wien's Displacement Law: The wavelength \(\lambda_m\) of maximum spectral intensity emitted by a blackbody varies inversely with absolute temperature \(T\): \[ \lambda_m T = b \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K} \]
  • Stefan-Boltzmann Law: Total radiant power emitted by a surface of area \(A\), emissivity \(e\) (\(0 \le e \le 1\); \(e = 1\) for an ideal blackbody), and absolute temperature \(T\) (in \(\mathrm{K}\)) is: \[ H = e \sigma A T^4, \qquad \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}} \] If the surroundings are at temperature \(T_s\), the net rate of radiant heat loss is \(H_{\mathrm{net}} = e \sigma A (T^4 - T_s^4)\).

Newton's Law of Cooling

For small temperature differences between a body at temperature \(T\) and its surroundings at \(T_s\), the rate of loss of heat is proportional to the excess temperature \((T - T_s)\):

\[ -\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = k (T - T_s) \implies -\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = K (T - T_s), \quad \text{where } K = \frac{k}{ms} \]

Integrating gives exponential cooling: \(T(t) = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)e^{-Kt}\), so \(\ln(T - T_s)\) versus \(t\) is a straight line with negative slope \(-K\). For quick NEET numericals over a small interval from \(T_i\) to \(T_f\) in time \(\Delta t\), use the average-temperature approximation:

\[ \frac{T_i - T_f}{\Delta t} \approx K \left( \frac{T_i + T_f}{2} - T_s \right) \]

Worked Examples

Calibrating a Faulty Thermometer

Assumed practice values

Problem: A faulty Celsius thermometer reads \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) in melting ice and \(96\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) in boiling water at standard atmospheric pressure. Find the true temperature when this thermometer reads \(50\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).

Solution: Using linear scale interpolation between the ice and steam points:

\[ \frac{T_{\mathrm{faulty}} - X_{\mathrm{ice}}}{X_{\mathrm{steam}} - X_{\mathrm{ice}}} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{true}} - 0}{100 - 0} \] \[ \frac{50 - 4}{96 - 4} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{true}}}{100} \implies \frac{46}{92} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{true}}}{100} \implies T_{\mathrm{true}} = 0.5 \times 100 = 50\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}. \]

Thermal Stress in a Constrained Steel Rod

Assumed practice values

Problem: A uniform steel rod of cross-sectional area \(2.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) is rigidly clamped between two immovable supports at \(20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). Calculate the compressive force developed in the rod when its temperature rises to \(70\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). (Take \(\alpha_{\mathrm{steel}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), \(Y_{\mathrm{steel}} = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)).

Solution:

Temperature rise: \(\Delta T = 70 - 20 = 50\,\mathrm{K}\). Area: \(A = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\).

\[ \text{Thermal Strain} = \alpha_{\mathrm{steel}} \Delta T = (1.2 \times 10^{-5})(50) = 6.0 \times 10^{-4} \] \[ F = Y_{\mathrm{steel}} A (\alpha_{\mathrm{steel}} \Delta T) = (2.0 \times 10^{11})(2.0 \times 10^{-4})(6.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 2.4 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N}. \]

Calorimetry with Phase Change

Assumed practice values

Problem: \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of ice at \(0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) is mixed with \(200\,\mathrm{g}\) of water at \(60\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) in an insulated vessel. Find the final equilibrium temperature. (Take \(s_{\mathrm{water}} = 1\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}\,^{\circ}C^{-1}}\), \(L_{\mathrm{f}} = 80\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

Heat needed to melt all \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of ice at \(0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\): \(Q_{\mathrm{melt}} = 100 \times 80 = 8000\,\mathrm{cal}\).

Maximum heat available from hot water cooling to \(0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\): \(Q_{\mathrm{avail}} = 200 \times 1 \times 60 = 12000\,\mathrm{cal}\).

Since \(Q_{\mathrm{avail}} > Q_{\mathrm{melt}}\), all ice melts and the final temperature \(T > 0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). Equating heat gained to heat lost:

\[ 8000 + 100(1)(T - 0) = 200(1)(60 - T) \implies 300T = 4000 \implies T = \frac{40}{3}\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C} \approx 13.3\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}. \]

Steady-State Junction Temperature in Composite Rods

Assumed practice values

Problem: Two rods of equal length \(L\) and equal cross-sectional area \(A\) are joined in series. Rod 1 (\(K_1 = 60\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)) has its free end at \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), and Rod 2 (\(K_2 = 180\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)) has its free end at \(20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). Find the junction temperature \(T_0\) and the equivalent thermal conductivity \(K_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

Solution:

In steady state, the heat current is the same through both rods:

\[ \frac{K_1 A (100 - T_0)}{L} = \frac{K_2 A (T_0 - 20)}{L} \implies 60(100 - T_0) = 180(T_0 - 20) \] \[ 100 - T_0 = 3T_0 - 60 \implies 4T_0 = 160 \implies T_0 = 40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}. \]

For two equal-length rods in series, the equivalent thermal conductivity is:

\[ K_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{2 K_1 K_2}{K_1 + K_2} = \frac{2 \times 60 \times 180}{60 + 180} = 90\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}. \]

Applying Newton's Law of Cooling

Assumed practice values

Problem: A metal sphere cools from \(72\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) to \(64\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) in \(4\,\mathrm{min}\) in a room at \(28\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). Estimate the time taken to cool from \(64\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) to \(56\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) in the same room.

Solution:

For the first interval, average temperature \(= \frac{72 + 64}{2} = 68\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\):

\[ \frac{72 - 64}{4} = K(68 - 28) \implies 2 = 40K \implies K = 0.05\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}. \]

For the second interval, average temperature \(= \frac{64 + 56}{2} = 60\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\):

\[ \frac{64 - 56}{t} = K(60 - 28) \implies \frac{8}{t} = 0.05 \times 32 = 1.6 \implies t = \frac{8}{1.6} = 5\,\mathrm{min}. \]

At what temperature do the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales record the exact same numerical value?

  • (A) \(+40\)
  • (B) \(-40\)
  • (C) \(-32\)
  • (D) \(+273.15\)
Answer: (B) \(-40\)

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

A flat circular copper disc has a concentric circular hole of radius \(r\) at \(25\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\). If the disc is heated uniformly to \(125\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), the radius of the hole:

  • (A) decreases because the surrounding metal expands inward
  • (B) remains unchanged
  • (C) increases by the same fractional amount as any linear dimension of the disc
  • (D) increases by twice the fractional amount of the outer radius
Answer: (C) increases by the same fractional amount as any linear dimension of the disc

Worked Solution: Uniform thermal expansion enlarges every linear dimension of a solid—including internal holes and cavities—according to \(\frac{\Delta r}{r} = \alpha_l \Delta T\).

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): In cold climates, lakes freeze from the top surface downward rather than from the bottom upward.
Reason (R): Water has its maximum density at \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), so water cooled below \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) becomes less dense and remains at the surface.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Worked Solution: Because water expands on cooling below \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), its density decreases. Thus, water between \(0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) stays afloat at the top and freezes at the surface first, insulating the deeper water.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Suppose \(50\,\mathrm{g}\) of steam at \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) is passed into \(250\,\mathrm{g}\) of water at \(20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) in a calorimeter of negligible heat capacity. What is the final equilibrium temperature of the mixture? (Take \(s_{\mathrm{water}} = 1\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}\,^{\circ}C^{-1}}\) and \(L_{\mathrm{v}} = 540\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}\)).

  • (A) \(80\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)
  • (B) \(90\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)
  • (C) \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) \(110\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)
Answer: (C) \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)

Worked Solution: Heat required to warm \(250\,\mathrm{g}\) of water from \(20\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) to \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) is \(Q_{\mathrm{need}} = 250 \times 1 \times 80 = 20000\,\mathrm{cal}\). Heat available if all \(50\,\mathrm{g}\) of steam condenses at \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) is \(Q_{\mathrm{avail}} = 50 \times 540 = 27000\,\mathrm{cal}\). Since \(Q_{\mathrm{avail}} > Q_{\mathrm{need}}\), only part of the steam condenses, and the system reaches thermal equilibrium at \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).

Practice Question 5 Statement I & Statement II

Statement I: Cooking food in an open pot takes longer at high-altitude mountain stations than at sea level.
Statement II: Lower atmospheric pressure at high altitudes decreases the boiling point of water.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.

Worked Solution: Boiling occurs when vapour pressure equals external atmospheric pressure. At high altitudes, lower atmospheric pressure causes water to boil below \(100\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), so food cooks at a lower temperature and takes longer.

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

Two identical metal rods, each of thermal resistance \(R_{\mathrm{th}}\), are first connected in series and then in parallel between two constant-temperature reservoirs at \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) (\(T_1 > T_2\)). What is the ratio of the steady-state heat current in the series combination to that in the parallel combination (\(H_{\mathrm{series}} : H_{\mathrm{parallel}}\))?

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(1 : 4\)
  • (C) \(2 : 1\)
  • (D) \(4 : 1\)
Answer: (B) \(1 : 4\)

Worked Solution: In series, \(R_{\mathrm{series}} = 2R_{\mathrm{th}}\). In parallel, \(R_{\mathrm{parallel}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{th}}}{2}\). Since \(H = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{R_{\mathrm{eq}}}\):

\[ \frac{H_{\mathrm{series}}}{H_{\mathrm{parallel}}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{parallel}}}{R_{\mathrm{series}}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{th}}/2}{2R_{\mathrm{th}}} = \frac{1}{4}. \]

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

A spherical blackbody radiates power \(P\) when its absolute temperature is \(T\). If its radius is halved and its absolute temperature is doubled, the new radiated power becomes:

  • (A) \(P\)
  • (B) \(2P\)
  • (C) \(4P\)
  • (D) \(8P\)
Answer: (C) \(4P\)

Worked Solution: Radiated power is \(P = \sigma (4\pi r^2) T^4\). When \(r' = r/2\) and \(T' = 2T\):

\[ P' = \sigma \left(4\pi \frac{r^2}{4}\right) (2T)^4 = \frac{16}{4} P = 4P. \]

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

  • Kelvin vs. Celsius in formulas: Always convert \(T\) to kelvin in \(PV = \mu RT\), \(\alpha_v = 1/T\), \(\lambda_m T = b\), and \(H = e\sigma A T^4\). For temperature differences \(\Delta T\), \(1\,\mathrm{K} = 1\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).
  • Check for incomplete phase change in calorimetry: Always verify whether the available heat is sufficient to melt all ice or condense all steam before assuming a single-phase final temperature.
  • Cavity expansion: A hole in a heated metal plate expands outward, never inward.
  • Newton's law of cooling limit: The linear cooling relation \(-\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = K(T - T_s)\) is valid only when \((T - T_s)\) is small compared to \(T_s\) (in kelvin).

Concise Final Recap

Heat flows spontaneously from higher to lower temperature via conduction (\(H = KA\Delta T/L\)), convection (fluid bulk motion), or radiation (\(H = e\sigma A T^4\)). Matter responds to heat by changing temperature (\(\Delta Q = ms\Delta T\)), expanding (\(\alpha_v = 3\alpha_l\)), or undergoing isothermal phase transitions (\(\Delta Q = mL\))—with water's high specific heat and \(4\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) density maximum playing vital physical and environmental roles.
