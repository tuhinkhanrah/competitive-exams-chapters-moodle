Oscillations and Simple Harmonic Motion

Conceptual Foundations, Analytical Equations, Energy Dynamics, and Exam-Aligned Problem Solving

Learning Objectives and Exam Scope

Oscillatory motion forms the bedrock of wave optics, acoustics, alternating current circuits, and atomic vibrations. For NEET UG, questions from this chapter consistently test graphical interpretation, phase relationships, spring-mass combinations, energy ratios, and simple pendulum variations. By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between periodic motion, oscillatory motion, and simple harmonic motion (SHM).
  • Formulate displacement, velocity, and acceleration equations using phase constants and reference-circle geometry.
  • Apply the linear restoring force law \(F = -kx\) to horizontal, vertical, and combined spring-mass systems.
  • Analyze kinetic, potential, and total mechanical energy as functions of both time and displacement.
  • Derive and adapt the time period of a simple pendulum under small-angle conditions and effective gravity changes.

1. Periodic Motion, Oscillations, and Mathematical Description

Periodic vs. Oscillatory Motion

Any motion that repeats its exact state (position, velocity, and acceleration) after a fixed, smallest interval of time is called periodic motion. This smallest repetition interval is the time period \(T\) (SI unit: \(\mathrm{s}\)). The number of repetitions completed per unit time is the frequency \(\nu\) (or \(f\)), measured in hertz (\(\mathrm{Hz} = \mathrm{s}^{-1}\)):

\[\nu = \frac{1}{T}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi\nu = \frac{2\pi}{T}\]

Here, \(\omega\) is the angular frequency in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\).

When a body in periodic motion moves back and forth along the same path about a stable mean (equilibrium) position—where the net force on it is zero—the motion is termed oscillatory (or vibratory when the frequency is high). Whenever the body is nudged away from equilibrium, a restoring force pulls it back toward the mean position.

Key Distinction for Conceptual MCQs

Every oscillatory motion is periodic (assuming no damping), but not every periodic motion is oscillatory. For example, uniform circular motion of a planet or a rotating fan blade repeats every \(T\) seconds, yet it does not move to and fro about an equilibrium point—so it is periodic, not oscillatory.

Generalized Displacement and Periodic Functions

In oscillations, the term displacement is not restricted to linear distance \(x\). It represents any physical quantity that varies with time about an equilibrium value—such as the angular deviation \(\theta\) of a pendulum, the alternating voltage across a capacitor, or pressure fluctuations in a sound wave.

A function \(f(t)\) represents periodic motion if \(f(t + T) = f(t)\) for some positive constant \(T\). By Fourier's theorem, any periodic function can be expressed as a superposition of sine and cosine functions of suitable frequencies. In particular, a linear combination of sine and cosine of the same angular frequency \(\omega\) is always simple harmonic:

\[A\sin\omega t + B\cos\omega t = D\sin(\omega t + \phi), \quad \text{where } D = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2} \text{ and } \tan\phi = \frac{B}{A}\]

If a function is a sum of harmonics with different angular frequencies \(\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3, \dots\), it is periodic only if the ratios of their periods are rational numbers. The net time period of the sum is the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of the individual periods \(T_1, T_2, T_3, \dots\). Functions such as \(e^{-\omega t}\), \(\ln(\omega t)\), or \(1 + \omega t + \omega^2 t^2\) change monotonically and never repeat; hence they are non-periodic.

2. Kinematics of Simple Harmonic Motion and the Reference Circle

Defining Equation of SHM

Simple harmonic motion is the simplest oscillatory motion in which the displacement \(x(t)\) from the mean position (\(x = 0\)) is a single-frequency sinusoidal function of time:

\[x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\]
  • Amplitude (\(A\)): The maximum magnitude of displacement from the equilibrium position (\(A > 0\)). The particle oscillates strictly between \(-A\) and \(+A\).
  • Phase (\(\omega t + \phi\)): The time-varying angle (in radians) that uniquely specifies both the position and direction of motion at instant \(t\).
  • Phase Constant (\(\phi\)): Also called the initial phase, it is the value of the phase at \(t = 0\). It depends on the initial position \(x(0)\) and initial velocity \(v(0)\).

SHM as a Projection of Uniform Circular Motion

Consider a reference particle \(P\) moving anticlockwise with constant angular speed \(\omega\) on a circle of radius \(A\) centered at the origin \(O\). If the radius vector \(\vec{OP}\) makes an angle \(\phi\) with the \(+x\)-axis at \(t = 0\), then at time \(t\) its angle is \((\omega t + \phi)\). The foot of the perpendicular \(P'\) dropped from \(P\) onto the \(x\)-axis has position:

\[x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\]

Similarly, the projection onto the \(y\)-axis gives \(y(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)\), which is an SHM of the same amplitude and period, lagging behind the \(x\)-projection by a phase of \(\pi/2\).

Velocity and Acceleration in SHM

Differentiating \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with respect to time—or projecting the tangential velocity \(\omega A\) and centripetal acceleration \(\omega^2 A\) of the reference particle onto the \(x\)-axis—yields:

\[v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = -\omega A\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \pm\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}\] \[a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = -\omega^2 A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 x(t)\]

From these relations, the key kinematic benchmarks are:

  • Mean position (\(x = 0\)): Speed is maximum (\(v_{\max} = \omega A\)), while acceleration is zero (\(a = 0\)).
  • Extreme positions (\(x = \pm A\)): Velocity is instantaneously zero (\(v = 0\)), while acceleration magnitude is maximum (\(a_{\max} = \omega^2 A\)), directed back toward the mean position.
  • Phase differences: Velocity leads displacement by \(\pi/2\) radians (\(90°\)); acceleration leads velocity by \(\pi/2\) radians (\(90°\)); acceleration and displacement are out of phase by \(\pi\) radians (\(180°\)).

3. Force Law and Spring-Mass Oscillators

Restoring Force Criterion

Applying Newton's second law \(F = ma\) to \(a = -\omega^2 x\) gives the characteristic force law for linear SHM:

\[F(x) = -kx, \qquad \text{where } k = m\omega^2 \implies \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \quad \text{and} \quad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}\]

Here, \(k\) is the force constant (or spring constant, unit: \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\)). The negative sign guarantees that \(F\) always opposes the displacement \(x\) and points toward the mean position. If the restoring force contains higher powers such as \(x^2\) or \(x^3\) (e.g., \(F = -kx^3\) or \(a = -200x^2\)), the motion is oscillatory but non-linear (not SHM). Furthermore, a positive coefficient like \(a = +0.7x\) drives the particle away from equilibrium and cannot produce oscillations at all.

Spring-Mass Combinations

  • Single spring clamped at one end: A block of mass \(m\) attached to a spring of constant \(k\) oscillates with \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\), whether placed horizontally on a frictionless surface or suspended vertically (gravity merely shifts the equilibrium position by \(mg/k\) without altering \(T\)).
  • Parallel springs (or block between two walls): When a block of mass \(m\) is connected between two springs \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) fixed to opposite walls and displaced by \(x\), one spring stretches by \(x\) and the other compresses by \(x\). Both exert restoring forces in the same direction: \(F_{\text{net}} = -(k_1 + k_2)x\), so \(k_{\text{eff}} = k_1 + k_2\) and \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k_1 + k_2}}\).
  • Series springs: Two springs \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) joined end-to-end share the same tension, giving \(\frac{1}{k_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2}\), or \(k_{\text{eff}} = \frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\).
  • Two-body spring system: If two equal masses \(m\) are attached to opposite ends of a single spring of constant \(k\) on a frictionless floor, the reduced mass is \(\mu_{\text{red}} = \frac{m \cdot m}{m + m} = \frac{m}{2}\), yielding \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{2k}}\).

4. Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion

Because the linear restoring force \(F = -kx\) is conservative, the mechanical energy of an undamped harmonic oscillator is conserved. At any displacement \(x\) and time \(t\):

\[K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}k(A^2 - x^2) = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi)\] \[U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\cos^2(\omega t + \phi)\] \[E = K + U = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 A^2\]

Important energy properties frequently tested in NEET UG:

  • Frequency doubling: Using \(\sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}\), both \(K(t)\) and \(U(t)\) oscillate with angular frequency \(2\omega\), i.e., frequency \(2\nu\) and time period \(T/2\). They peak twice during every oscillation of the particle.
  • Equal energy point: Kinetic and potential energies become equal (\(K = U = E/2\)) when \(\frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{4}kA^2\), which occurs at \(x = \pm \frac{A}{\sqrt{2}}\).
  • Time-averaged values: Over one complete cycle, \(\langle K \rangle = \langle U \rangle = \frac{1}{4}kA^2 = \frac{E}{2}\).

5. The Simple Pendulum and Fluid Oscillators

Simple Pendulum Under Small-Angle Approximation

An ideal simple pendulum consists of a point bob of mass \(m\) suspended by an inextensible, massless string of length \(L\) from a rigid support. When displaced by an angle \(\theta\) from the vertical, the weight \(mg\) resolves into a radial component \(mg\cos\theta\) (balancing tension \(T\) minus centripetal force) and a tangential restoring component \(mg\sin\theta\).

The restoring torque about the support is \(\tau = -mgL\sin\theta\). Using rotational dynamics \(\tau = I\alpha\) with moment of inertia \(I = mL^2\):

\[\alpha = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta\]

For small angular displacements (\(\theta \lesssim 10°\text{–}15°\) in radians, where \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\)), this reduces to \(\alpha = -\left(\frac{g}{L}\right)\theta\), which is angular SHM with:

\[\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\]
  • Independence of mass and amplitude: For small oscillations, \(T\) does not depend on the bob's mass \(m\) or the angular amplitude \(\theta_0\).
  • Seconds pendulum: A pendulum with \(T = 2\,\mathrm{s}\) (1 second per half-swing) has length \(L = \frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} \approx 1\,\mathrm{m}\) on Earth (\(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).
  • Effective gravity (\(g_{\text{eff}}\)): If the support accelerates (e.g., an elevator accelerating upward at \(a_0\), or a car moving on a circular track of radius \(R\) at speed \(v\)), replace \(g\) with \(g_{\text{eff}} = |\vec{g} - \vec{a}_{\text{frame}}|\). For the circular track, \(g_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{g^2 + (v^2/R)^2}\).

Floating Cylinders and U-Tube Liquid Columns

Two classic fluid-mechanics oscillators also reduce to \(F = -kx\):

  • Floating cylinder: A cylinder of height \(h\) and density \(\rho\) floating vertically in a liquid of density \(\rho_L\) experiences an extra buoyant restoring force \(F = -A\rho_L g x\) when depressed by \(x\). Since mass \(m = Ah\rho\), its period is \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{h\rho}{\rho_L g}}\).
  • U-tube liquid column: If a liquid column of total length \(L\) in a uniform U-tube is displaced by \(x\) in one limb, the level difference becomes \(2x\), giving restoring force \(F = -2A\rho_L g x\) and period \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{2g}}\).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classifying Time Functions and Finding Combined Periods

Problem: Suppose a particle's position varies with time according to two hypothetical expressions (where \(\omega_0\) is a positive constant): (i) \(x_1(t) = 3\sin(\omega_0 t) - 4\cos(\omega_0 t)\) and (ii) \(x_2(t) = \cos(\omega_0 t) + \cos(2\omega_0 t) + \cos(3\omega_0 t)\). Determine whether each represents SHM or periodic non-SHM, and find the time period of each.

Solution:

(i) Both terms share the same angular frequency \(\omega_0\). Writing \(3 = R\cos\alpha\) and \(4 = R\sin\alpha\), we get amplitude \(R = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\). Thus, \(x_1(t) = 5\sin(\omega_0 t - \alpha)\). Because it is a single sinusoidal term, it represents SHM with amplitude \(5\) units and time period \(T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}\).

(ii) The three terms have different angular frequencies \(\omega_0, 2\omega_0, 3\omega_0\), so their sum cannot be reduced to a single sine/cosine function; hence it is periodic but not SHM. Their individual periods are \(T_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}\), \(T_2 = \frac{\pi}{\omega_0} = \frac{T_1}{2}\), and \(T_3 = \frac{2\pi}{3\omega_0} = \frac{T_1}{3}\). Since \(T_1\) is an integer multiple of both \(T_2\) and \(T_3\), the net period is \(\mathrm{LCM}(T_1, T_2, T_3) = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}\).

Example 2: Determining Amplitude and Phase Constant from Initial Conditions

Problem: Assume a particle executes SHM along the \(x\)-axis according to \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). At \(t = 0\), the particle is at \(x = +2\,\mathrm{cm}\) and moves with velocity \(v = -4\pi\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\). Find the amplitude \(A\) and the initial phase \(\phi\).

Solution:

At \(t = 0\):

\[x(0) = A\cos\phi = 2\,\mathrm{cm}\] \[v(0) = -\omega A\sin\phi = -4\pi\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\]

Substituting \(\omega = 2\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\) into the velocity equation gives \(A\sin\phi = \frac{4\pi\sqrt{3}}{2\pi} = 2\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Squaring and adding both equations:

\[A^2(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi) = 2^2 + (2\sqrt{3})^2 = 4 + 12 = 16 \implies A = 4\,\mathrm{cm}\]

Dividing \(A\sin\phi\) by \(A\cos\phi\): \(\tan\phi = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{2} = \sqrt{3}\). Since both \(\cos\phi > 0\) and \(\sin\phi > 0\), \(\phi\) lies in the first quadrant, so \(\phi = \frac{\pi}{3}\,\mathrm{rad}\) (\(60°\)).

Example 3: Energy Partition and Speed at Intermediate Displacement

Problem: A \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) block attached to a spring of force constant \(k = 200\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) is pulled to \(x = 12\,\mathrm{cm}\) on a smooth horizontal table and released from rest. Calculate its total mechanical energy, potential energy, kinetic energy, and speed when it passes through \(x = 6\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Solution:

Because the block is released from rest at \(x = 12\,\mathrm{cm}\), the amplitude is \(A = 0.12\,\mathrm{m}\).

  • Total Energy: \(E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.12)^2 = 100 \times 0.0144 = 1.44\,\mathrm{J}\).
  • Potential Energy at \(x = 0.06\,\mathrm{m}\): \(U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.06)^2 = 0.36\,\mathrm{J}\) (which is \(E/4\) since \(x = A/2\)).
  • Kinetic Energy: \(K = E - U = 1.44 - 0.36 = 1.08\,\mathrm{J}\).
  • Speed: From \(\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 1.08\,\mathrm{J}\) with \(m = 2\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(v^2 = 1.08\), so \(v = \sqrt{1.08} \approx 1.04\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Example 4: Combined Spring-Mass Oscillator

Problem: Suppose a \(0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) block rests on a frictionless surface and is attached to two springs of spring constants \(k_1 = 30\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) and \(k_2 = 50\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) fixed to opposite rigid walls. Find the time period of small horizontal oscillations.

Solution:

When the block is displaced by \(x\), both springs exert restoring forces in the same direction, acting in parallel. The effective spring constant is:

\[k_{\text{eff}} = k_1 + k_2 = 30 + 50 = 80\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\]

The time period is:

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k_{\text{eff}}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.5}{80}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1}{160}} = \frac{2\pi}{4\sqrt{10}} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{10}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 0.497\,\mathrm{s}\]

Example 5: Simple Pendulum in an Accelerating Frame

Problem: A simple pendulum of length \(L = 0.5\,\mathrm{m}\) is suspended inside a vehicle moving at a constant speed \(v = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) along a flat circular track of radius \(R = 10\,\mathrm{m}\). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find the time period of small radial oscillations of the pendulum.

Solution:

The vehicle has a horizontal centripetal acceleration \(a_c = \frac{v^2}{R} = \frac{10^2}{10} = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). In the vehicle's non-inertial frame, the bob experiences both downward gravity \(g\) and an outward pseudo-acceleration \(a_c\). Since \(g\) and \(a_c\) are perpendicular, the effective gravitational acceleration is:

\[g_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{g^2 + a_c^2} = \sqrt{10^2 + 10^2} = 10\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\]

The time period of small oscillations is:

\[T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g_{\text{eff}}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.5}{10\sqrt{2}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1}{20\sqrt{2}}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 1.18\,\mathrm{s}\]

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle executes simple harmonic motion along the \(x\)-axis with amplitude \(A\) and time period \(T\). What is the shortest time taken by the particle to travel from \(x = +\frac{A}{2}\) to \(x = +A\)?

  1. (A) \(\dfrac{T}{4}\)
  2. (B) \(\dfrac{T}{6}\)
  3. (C) \(\dfrac{T}{8}\)
  4. (D) \(\dfrac{T}{12}\)
Answer: (B) \(\dfrac{T}{6}\)

Solution: Using \(x = A\cos(\omega t)\) starting from \(x = +A\) at \(t = 0\), the particle reaches \(x = A/2\) when \(\cos(\omega t) = \frac{1}{2}\), which gives \(\omega t = \frac{\pi}{3}\). Since \(\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}\), we get \(\frac{2\pi}{T}t = \frac{\pi}{3} \implies t = \frac{T}{6}\). By symmetry, moving from \(A/2\) to \(A\) takes the same time \(\frac{T}{6}\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following equations of motion represents simple harmonic motion?

  1. (A) \(a = +12x\)
  2. (B) \(a = -15x^2\)
  3. (C) \(a = -9(x - 2)\)
  4. (D) \(a = +4x^3\)
Answer: (C) \(a = -9(x - 2)\)

Solution: For linear SHM, acceleration must be linearly proportional to displacement from an equilibrium point and directed opposite to it: \(a = -\omega^2(x - x_0)\). In (C), \(a = -9(x - 2)\) represents SHM about \(x_0 = 2\) with \(\omega = 3\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). Options (A) and (D) have positive signs (unstable), while (B) is non-linear.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle performs SHM with amplitude \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\). At what distance from the mean position is its kinetic energy equal to three times its potential energy?

  1. (A) \(2.5\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  2. (B) \(5.0\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  3. (C) \(5\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  4. (D) \(5\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{cm}\)
Answer: (B) \(5.0\,\mathrm{cm}\)

Solution: Given \(K = 3U\). Since \(K + U = E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\), we have \(4U = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 \implies 4\left(\frac{1}{2}kx^2\right) = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 \implies x = \frac{A}{2} = \frac{10}{2} = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the two statements carefully and choose the correct option:

Statement I: If a particle executes SHM with frequency \(5\,\mathrm{Hz}\), its kinetic energy oscillates with a frequency of \(10\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

Statement II: The total mechanical energy of an ideal linear harmonic oscillator remains constant with time.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
  3. (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  4. (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.

Solution: Kinetic energy \(K = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi) = \frac{1}{4}kA^2[1 - \cos(2\omega t + 2\phi)]\), so its frequency is \(2\nu = 2 \times 5 = 10\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Since the restoring force \(F = -kx\) is conservative and there is no damping, total mechanical energy \(E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\) is constant. Thus both statements are true.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A spring of force constant \(k\) is cut into two equal halves. One of the halves is then attached to a block of mass \(m\). What is the new time period of oscillation compared to the original period \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\)?

  1. (A) \(\dfrac{T}{2}\)
  2. (B) \(\dfrac{T}{\sqrt{2}}\)
  3. (C) \(\sqrt{2}\,T\)
  4. (D) \(2T\)
Answer: (B) \(\dfrac{T}{\sqrt{2}}\)

Solution: The spring constant of a uniform spring is inversely proportional to its length (\(k \propto 1/L\)). Cutting it into two equal halves doubles the spring constant of each half to \(k' = 2k\). Therefore, \(T' = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{2k}} = \frac{T}{\sqrt{2}}\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two simple pendulums of lengths \(1.44\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(1.00\,\mathrm{m}\) start swinging in phase from the same extreme position. After how many oscillations of the shorter pendulum will they be in phase again?

  1. (A) \(5\)
  2. (B) \(6\)
  3. (C) \(10\)
  4. (D) \(12\)
Answer: (B) \(6\)

Solution: The ratio of time periods is \(\frac{T_1}{T_2} = \sqrt{\frac{L_1}{L_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.44}{1.00}} = \frac{1.2}{1.0} = \frac{6}{5}\). Thus \(5T_1 = 6T_2\). When the longer pendulum completes \(5\) oscillations, the shorter pendulum completes \(6\) oscillations and both return to the initial phase together.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle executes SHM with a maximum speed of \(30\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\) and a maximum acceleration of \(60\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}\). What is the time period of its oscillation?

  1. (A) \(\pi\,\mathrm{s}\)
  2. (B) \(2\pi\,\mathrm{s}\)
  3. (C) \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\,\mathrm{s}\)
  4. (D) \(4\pi\,\mathrm{s}\)
Answer: (A) \(\pi\,\mathrm{s}\)

Solution: We have \(v_{\max} = \omega A = 30\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\) and \(a_{\max} = \omega^2 A = 60\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}\). Dividing the two gives \(\omega = \frac{a_{\max}}{v_{\max}} = \frac{60}{30} = 2\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). Hence, \(T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{2} = \pi\,\mathrm{s}\).

Practice Question 8 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): The time period of a simple pendulum on the Moon is greater than its time period on the Earth.

Reason (R): Acceleration due to gravity on the Moon is smaller than that on the Earth, and \(T \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\).

  1. (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  2. (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  3. (C) A is true, but R is false.
  4. (D) Both A and R are false.
Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Solution: Since \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\) and \(g_{\text{Moon}} \approx g_{\text{Earth}}/6\), a smaller \(g\) yields a larger time period \(T\). Thus both A and R are true and R correctly explains A.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

For a particle executing SHM, the phase difference between its instantaneous velocity and instantaneous acceleration is:

  1. (A) \(0\)
  2. (B) \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\)
  3. (C) \(\pi\)
  4. (D) \(\dfrac{3\pi}{2}\)
Answer: (B) \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\)

Solution: With \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\), we have \(v = -\omega A\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \omega A\cos(\omega t + \phi + \frac{\pi}{2})\) and \(a = -\omega^2 A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \omega^2 A\cos(\omega t + \phi + \pi)\). The phase difference between \(a\) and \(v\) is \(\pi - \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A cylindrical wooden block of height \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) and density \(0.6\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) floats vertically in water (density \(1.0\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)). If it is depressed slightly and released, what is the time period of its vertical oscillations? (Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(\pi^2 \approx 10\).)

  1. (A) \(0.42\,\mathrm{s}\)
  2. (B) \(0.69\,\mathrm{s}\)
  3. (C) \(1.10\,\mathrm{s}\)
  4. (D) \(2.00\,\mathrm{s}\)
Answer: (B) \(0.69\,\mathrm{s}\)

Solution: Using \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{h\rho}{\rho_L g}}\) with \(h = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\), \(\rho = 0.6\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\), \(\rho_L = 1.0\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\), and \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\):
\(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.20 \times 0.6}{1.0 \times 10}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.012} \approx 2 \times 3.142 \times 0.1095 \approx 0.69\,\mathrm{s}\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Avoid These Frequent Exam Mistakes
  • Using degrees inside \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\): The small-angle approximation holds only when \(\theta\) is in radians.
  • Confusing particle frequency with energy frequency: If SHM has frequency \(\nu\), kinetic and potential energies oscillate at \(2\nu\) (period \(T/2\)), while total energy has zero frequency (constant).
  • Assuming constant speed between \(0\) to \(A/2\) and \(A/2\) to \(A\): Because speed is maximum near the mean position and zero at the extremes, \(0 \to A/2\) takes \(T/12\), whereas \(A/2 \to A\) takes \(T/6\) (twice as long).
  • Applying \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) to a cut spring without updating \(k\): Cutting a spring of length \(L\) into \(n\) equal parts increases each part's spring constant to \(nk\).
  • Applying gravity to a spring-mass period: Unlike a simple pendulum, a spring-mass oscillator's period \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) is completely independent of \(g\) (even on the Moon or in an elevator).

Concise Final Recap

Simple harmonic motion is governed by a linear restoring force \(F = -kx\), producing sinusoidal displacement \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with angular frequency \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\) independent of amplitude. Velocity leads displacement by \(\pi/2\), acceleration opposes displacement (\(a = -\omega^2 x\)), and total mechanical energy \(E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\) continuously trades between kinetic and potential forms at twice the oscillation frequency. For small angles, a simple pendulum behaves as an angular harmonic oscillator with \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g_{\text{eff}}}\).
