Kinetic Theory of Gases

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide connecting molecular motion to macroscopic gas laws, internal energy, specific heats, and mean free path.

Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

Kinetic theory bridges the microscopic world of rapidly moving atoms and molecules with macroscopic thermodynamic variables such as pressure, volume, temperature, and specific heat capacity. For NEET UG, this topic tests both direct formula manipulation and conceptual reasoning about molecular speeds, degrees of freedom, and gas mixtures.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial
  • How molecular spacing and intermolecular forces distinguish solids, liquids, and gases.
  • The ideal gas equation in molar, molecular, and density forms, along with Boyle's, Charles', and Dalton's laws.
  • Derivation and physical meaning of gas pressure \(P = \frac{1}{3}nm\overline{v^2}\) and the kinetic interpretation of absolute temperature.
  • Calculation of root-mean-square speed \(v_{\mathrm{rms}}\) and its dependence on temperature and molar mass.
  • The law of equipartition of energy and its application to monatomic, diatomic, polyatomic gases, and solids.
  • Mean free path \(l\) and collision frequency in terms of number density, pressure, temperature, and molecular diameter.

Molecular Picture of Matter and Gas Behaviour

All matter is composed of discrete atoms and molecules in continuous thermal motion. An individual atom has a characteristic size of roughly \(1\,\mathrm{Å}\) (\(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)). The physical state of a substance depends on the balance between thermal kinetic energy and short-range intermolecular forces (attractive at separations of a few ångströms, strongly repulsive when electron clouds overlap).

State of Matter Typical Intermolecular Spacing Molecular Motion & Interactions
Solid ~2 Å (tightly packed) Atoms vibrate about fixed equilibrium positions; strong intermolecular forces maintain rigidity.
Liquid ~2–3 Å Molecules remain closely packed but can slide past one another, enabling flow.
Gas Tens of Å (~30–40 Å at STP) Molecules are far apart; interactions are negligible except during brief elastic collisions.

Even when a gas appears macroscopically still, it is in dynamic equilibrium: billions of molecules collide every second, exchanging momentum and energy, while macroscopic averages such as pressure and temperature remain steady.

Equation of State of an Ideal Gas

An ideal gas is a theoretical model in which molecules are treated as point masses with zero volume and no intermolecular forces except during elastic collisions. Real gases approximate ideal behaviour closely at low pressures and high temperatures, where molecules are widely separated and their kinetic energy far exceeds intermolecular potential energy.

Equivalent Forms of the Ideal Gas Equation \[ PV = \mu R T = N k_{\mathrm{B}} T \qquad \text{and} \qquad P = n k_{\mathrm{B}} T = \frac{\rho R T}{M_0} \]

Here \(\mu = \frac{M}{M_0} = \frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{A}}}\) is the number of moles, \(N\) is the total number of molecules, \(n = \frac{N}{V}\) is the number density, \(\rho\) is the mass density, \(M_0\) is the molar mass, and \(T\) is the absolute temperature in kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\)).

Fundamental Constant / Reference Symbol Standard Value
Universal gas constant \(R\) \(8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)
Avogadro number \(N_{\mathrm{A}}\) \(6.02 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\)
Boltzmann constant (\(R / N_{\mathrm{A}}\)) \(k_{\mathrm{B}}\) \(1.38 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\)
Molar volume at STP (\(273\,\mathrm{K}\), \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\)) \(V_{\mathrm{m}}\) \(22.4\,\mathrm{L} = 22.4 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\)

Classical Gas Laws as Special Cases

  • Avogadro's Hypothesis: At identical \(P\), \(V\), and \(T\), equal volumes of all ideal gases contain the same number of molecules (\(N = \frac{PV}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\)).
  • Boyle's Law: For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature \(T\), \(PV = \text{constant}\), so pressure varies inversely with volume.
  • Charles' Law: For a fixed mass of gas at constant pressure \(P\), \(\frac{V}{T} = \text{constant}\), so volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature.
  • Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures: For a mixture of non-reacting ideal gases occupying volume \(V\) at temperature \(T\), the total pressure equals the sum of individual partial pressures: \[ P = P_1 + P_2 + \dots = (\mu_1 + \mu_2 + \dots)\frac{RT}{V} \]

Kinetic Theory of Pressure and Temperature

To derive macroscopic pressure from molecular motion, kinetic theory models an ideal gas as \(N\) identical molecules of mass \(m\) inside a container of volume \(V\), moving randomly with negligible intermolecular forces and undergoing purely elastic collisions with each other and the walls.

Derivation Summary of Gas Pressure

In a small interval \(\Delta t\), only molecules within a distance \(v_x \Delta t\) of the wall of area \(A\) and moving toward it can strike it. On average, half of the molecules in the volume \(A v_x \Delta t\) move toward the wall, giving \(\frac{1}{2} n A v_x \Delta t\) collisions. Multiplying by the momentum \(2mv_x\) imparted per collision and dividing by \(A \Delta t\) gives the pressure contribution \(n m v_x^2\). Summing over all velocity groups and using isotropy (\(\overline{v_x^2} = \overline{v_y^2} = \overline{v_z^2} = \frac{1}{3}\overline{v^2}\)) yields:

Kinetic Pressure Formula \[ P = \frac{1}{3} n m \overline{v^2} = \frac{1}{3} \rho v_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 \]

Kinetic Interpretation of Temperature

Multiplying both sides of \(P = \frac{1}{3} n m \overline{v^2}\) by volume \(V\) and noting that \(N = nV\) and total translational kinetic energy \(E = N \left(\frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2}\right)\):

\[ PV = \frac{2}{3} E \]

Comparing this with \(PV = N k_{\mathrm{B}} T\), we obtain two cornerstone results of kinetic theory:

Average Translational Kinetic Energy and RMS Speed \[ \frac{E}{N} = \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \frac{3}{2} k_{\mathrm{B}} T \qquad \text{and} \qquad v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 k_{\mathrm{B}} T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 R T}{M_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 P}{\rho}} \]
Key Physical Insights for NEET UG
  • Temperature measures average translational kinetic energy: \(\frac{1}{2}m\overline{v^2} = \frac{3}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\) depends only on absolute temperature \(T\), not on the mass, atomicity, pressure, or volume of the ideal gas.
  • Gas mixtures in thermal equilibrium: All gases in a mixture at temperature \(T\) have the same average translational kinetic energy per molecule, so lighter molecules move faster: \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{M_0}}\).
  • Rate of diffusion: Because lighter molecules have higher rms speeds, gases diffuse at rates inversely proportional to \(\sqrt{M_0}\) (Graham's law of diffusion).

Law of Equipartition of Energy and Specific Heats

The number of independent coordinates or velocity components required to specify the configuration and motion of a molecule is called its degrees of freedom. Each independent quadratic term in the total energy expression of a molecule represents one energy mode.

  • Translational modes (3): \(\varepsilon_t = \frac{1}{2}mv_x^2 + \frac{1}{2}mv_y^2 + \frac{1}{2}mv_z^2\) (present in all gases).
  • Rotational modes: A linear (diatomic) molecule has 2 independent rotational axes perpendicular to the internuclear axis, giving \(\varepsilon_r = \frac{1}{2}I_1\omega_1^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_2\omega_2^2\) (rotation about the bond axis has negligible moment of inertia and is quantum-mechanically frozen). A non-linear polyatomic molecule has 3 rotational degrees of freedom.
  • Vibrational modes: Each vibrational mode acts like a harmonic oscillator with both kinetic energy \(\frac{1}{2}m\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^2\) and potential energy \(\frac{1}{2}ky^2\), contributing two quadratic terms.
Law of Equipartition of Energy In thermal equilibrium at absolute temperature \(T\), each quadratic energy mode contributes an average energy of \(\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\) per molecule. Each vibrational mode contributes \(2 \times \frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T = k_{\mathrm{B}}T\).

Molar Specific Heats of Ideal Gases and Solids

For one mole of an ideal gas with internal energy \(U\), the molar specific heat at constant volume is \(C_v = \frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}T}\). By Mayer's relation, \(C_p - C_v = R\) holds for every ideal gas regardless of atomicity, and the adiabatic index is \(\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v}\).

Type of Substance Active Modes (per molecule) \(U\) (per mole) \(C_v\) \(C_p\) \(\gamma = C_p / C_v\)
Monatomic gas (e.g., He, Ne, Ar) 3 trans. \(\frac{3}{2}RT\) \(\frac{3}{2}R\) \(\frac{5}{2}R\) \(\frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67\)
Rigid diatomic gas (e.g., \(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2}\)) 3 trans. + 2 rot. \(\frac{5}{2}RT\) \(\frac{5}{2}R\) \(\frac{7}{2}R\) \(\frac{7}{5} = 1.40\)
Non-rigid diatomic gas (with 1 vib. mode) 3 trans. + 2 rot. + 1 vib. (2 terms) \(\frac{7}{2}RT\) \(\frac{7}{2}R\) \(\frac{9}{2}R\) \(\frac{9}{7} \approx 1.29\)
Polyatomic gas (\(f\) vib. modes) 3 trans. + 3 rot. + \(f\) vib. \((3+f)RT\) \((3+f)R\) \((4+f)R\) \(\frac{4+f}{3+f}\)
Crystalline solid (3D oscillator) 3 vib. (6 quadratic terms) \(3RT\) \(C \approx 3R\) \(C \approx 3R\) —

For solids, volume change \(\Delta V\) during heating is negligible, so \(C_p \approx C_v \approx 3R \approx 24.9\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) at room temperature (Dulong–Petit law). Light, tightly bound elements such as carbon (diamond) are exceptions at room temperature because their vibrational modes are only partially excited.

Mean Free Path and Collision Frequency

Although gas molecules travel at hundreds of metres per second, a scent or gas leak takes noticeable time to spread across a room because molecules undergo frequent collisions that deflect their paths. The average distance travelled by a molecule between two successive collisions is called the mean free path (\(l\)).

Accounting for the relative motion of all molecules introduces a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\), giving the mean free path \(l\) and average collision time \(\tau\):

Mean Free Path and Mean Collision Time \[ l = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi n d^2} = \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi d^2 P} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \tau = \frac{l}{\langle v \rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi n d^2 \langle v \rangle} \]

Notice that \(l\) is inversely proportional to number density \(n\) and molecular cross-section \(\pi d^2\). At constant temperature, reducing the pressure \(P\) increases the mean free path proportionally.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Determining Gas Density and Molecular Count from State Variables

Problem: Suppose a rigid laboratory vessel of volume \(16.62\,\mathrm{L}\) holds pure nitrogen gas (\(\mathrm{N_2}\), molar mass \(M_0 = 28\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) at a pressure of \(2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\) and temperature \(300\,\mathrm{K}\). Assuming ideal gas behaviour and taking \(R = 8.31\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), find (a) the number of moles, (b) the total number of molecules, and (c) the mass density of the gas.

Solution:

Convert volume to SI units: \(V = 16.62\,\mathrm{L} = 16.62 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\).

(a) Using \(PV = \mu RT\):

\[ \mu = \frac{PV}{RT} = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa})(16.62 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3})}{(8.31\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}})(300\,\mathrm{K})} = \frac{3324}{2493} = 1.33\,\mathrm{mol} \]

(b) Total number of molecules \(N = \mu N_{\mathrm{A}}\):

\[ N = 1.33 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 8.0 \times 10^{23}\text{ molecules} \]

(c) Mass density \(\rho = \frac{P M_0}{RT}\), with \(M_0 = 28 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}\):

\[ \rho = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^5)(28 \times 10^{-3})}{8.31 \times 300} = \frac{5600}{2493} \approx 2.25\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \]
Example 2: Comparing Root-Mean-Square Speeds in a Binary Gas Mixture

Problem: A thermally insulated cylinder contains a hypothetical mixture of helium (\(\mathrm{He}\), \(M_1 = 4\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) and oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(M_2 = 32\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) in thermal equilibrium at \(27\,\mathrm{°C}\). Calculate (a) the ratio of the average translational kinetic energy per molecule of \(\mathrm{He}\) to that of \(\mathrm{O_2}\), and (b) the ratio of their root-mean-square speeds, \(\frac{v_{\mathrm{rms}}(\mathrm{He})}{v_{\mathrm{rms}}(\mathrm{O_2})}\).

Solution:

(a) Both gases are at the same temperature \(T = 27 + 273 = 300\,\mathrm{K}\). Since the average translational kinetic energy per molecule is \(\frac{3}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\), independent of molecular mass or atomicity:

\[ \frac{\langle K_{\mathrm{He}} \rangle}{\langle K_{\mathrm{O_2}} \rangle} = 1 : 1 \]

(b) Using \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M_0}}\) at the same temperature \(T\):

\[ \frac{v_{\mathrm{rms}}(\mathrm{He})}{v_{\mathrm{rms}}(\mathrm{O_2})} = \sqrt{\frac{M_{\mathrm{O_2}}}{M_{\mathrm{He}}}} = \sqrt{\frac{32}{4}} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83 \]
Example 3: Applying Equipartition of Energy to a Gas Mixture

Problem: Suppose \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a monatomic ideal gas are mixed with \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a rigid diatomic ideal gas without chemical reaction. Determine the effective molar specific heat at constant volume \(C_{v,\text{mix}}\) and the adiabatic index \(\gamma_{\text{mix}}\) of the mixture.

Solution:

For the monatomic gas, \(C_{v1} = \frac{3}{2}R\); for the rigid diatomic gas, \(C_{v2} = \frac{5}{2}R\). The total internal energy of the mixture is \(U_{\text{mix}} = \mu_1 C_{v1} T + \mu_2 C_{v2} T\), so:

\[ C_{v,\text{mix}} = \frac{\mu_1 C_{v1} + \mu_2 C_{v2}}{\mu_1 + \mu_2} = \frac{2\left(\frac{3}{2}R\right) + 3\left(\frac{5}{2}R\right)}{2 + 3} = \frac{3R + 7.5R}{5} = \frac{10.5R}{5} = 2.1R = \frac{21}{10}R \]

Using Mayer's relation for the ideal gas mixture, \(C_{p,\text{mix}} = C_{v,\text{mix}} + R = \frac{31}{10}R\). Therefore:

\[ \gamma_{\text{mix}} = \frac{C_{p,\text{mix}}}{C_{v,\text{mix}}} = \frac{31/10}{21/10} = \frac{31}{21} \approx 1.48 \]
Example 4: Scaling of Mean Free Path with Pressure and Temperature

Problem: An ideal gas has a mean free path \(l_0 = 1.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}\) at temperature \(T_0 = 300\,\mathrm{K}\) and pressure \(P_0 = 1.0\,\mathrm{atm}\). Assuming the molecular diameter remains constant, what will be the new mean free path if the gas is heated to \(450\,\mathrm{K}\) while its pressure is raised to \(3.0\,\mathrm{atm}\)?

Solution:

From \(l = \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi d^2 P}\), for a given gas (\(d\) constant), \(l \propto \frac{T}{P}\). Thus:

\[ \frac{l}{l_0} = \left(\frac{T}{T_0}\right)\left(\frac{P_0}{P}\right) = \left(\frac{450}{300}\right)\left(\frac{1.0}{3.0}\right) = \frac{3}{2} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{2} \] \[ l = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}}{2} = 6.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m} \]

NEET UG Practice Questions

Attempt all questions independently before checking the solutions. Each question is an original practice item designed to match NEET UG difficulty and format.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ): A sealed container holds an ideal gas at \(27\,\mathrm{°C}\). To double the root-mean-square speed of its molecules, the gas must be heated at constant volume to:
  • (A) \(54\,\mathrm{°C}\)
  • (B) \(327\,\mathrm{°C}\)
  • (C) \(600\,\mathrm{°C}\)
  • (D) \(927\,\mathrm{°C}\)
Correct Answer: (D)

Solution: Initial temperature \(T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300\,\mathrm{K}\). Since \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} \propto \sqrt{T}\), doubling \(v_{\mathrm{rms}}\) requires quadrupling the absolute temperature: \(T_2 = 4 T_1 = 1200\,\mathrm{K}\). In Celsius, \(t_2 = 1200 - 273 = 927\,\mathrm{°C}\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ): Two vessels of equal volume contain hydrogen (\(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(M = 2\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) and helium (\(\mathrm{He}\), \(M = 4\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) at the same temperature and pressure. The ratio of the number of molecules of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) to \(\mathrm{He}\) and the ratio of their total internal energies are respectively:
  • (A) \(1:1\) and \(5:3\)
  • (B) \(1:2\) and \(5:3\)
  • (C) \(1:1\) and \(3:5\)
  • (D) \(2:1\) and \(1:1\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: By Avogadro's hypothesis (\(N = \frac{PV}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\)), equal volumes at identical \(P\) and \(T\) contain equal numbers of molecules, so \(N_{\mathrm{H_2}} : N_{\mathrm{He}} = 1 : 1\) (and \(\mu_1 = \mu_2\)). For internal energy, rigid diatomic \(\mathrm{H_2}\) has \(f_1 = 5\) (\(U_1 = \frac{5}{2}\mu RT\)) while monatomic \(\mathrm{He}\) has \(f_2 = 3\) (\(U_2 = \frac{3}{2}\mu RT\)). Hence \(U_1 : U_2 = 5 : 3\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ): The pressure of an ideal gas of density \(\rho = 1.5\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) is \(P = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\). What is the root-mean-square speed of the gas molecules?
  • (A) \(400\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(516\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(632\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(750\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Using \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3P}{\rho}}\):

\[ v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 \times 2.0 \times 10^5}{1.5}} = \sqrt{4.0 \times 10^5} = 200\sqrt{10} \approx 200 \times 3.162 \approx 632\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]
Practice Question 4 (Statement Type): Read the two statements carefully.
Statement I: At the same temperature, the average translational kinetic energy per molecule of nitrogen is equal to that of argon.
Statement II: Nitrogen is diatomic whereas argon is monatomic, so nitrogen has a larger total internal energy per mole than argon at the same temperature.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: Average translational kinetic energy per molecule is \(\frac{3}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\), which depends only on temperature \(T\); thus Statement I is correct. Total internal energy per mole includes rotational modes: \(\frac{5}{2}RT\) for rigid diatomic \(\mathrm{N_2}\) versus \(\frac{3}{2}RT\) for monatomic \(\mathrm{Ar}\); thus Statement II is also correct.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ): A diatomic gas molecule has 3 translational, 2 rotational, and 1 vibrational mode active at high temperature. What is the ratio \(\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v}\) for this gas?
  • (A) \(\frac{7}{5}\)
  • (B) \(\frac{9}{7}\)
  • (C) \(\frac{8}{6}\)
  • (D) \(\frac{11}{9}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Each vibrational mode contributes 2 quadratic terms (kinetic + potential). Total quadratic terms = \(3 + 2 + 2 = 7\). Thus \(C_v = \frac{7}{2}R\), \(C_p = \frac{7}{2}R + R = \frac{9}{2}R\), and \(\gamma = \frac{9}{7}\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ): A vessel contains a non-reacting mixture of \(16\,\mathrm{g}\) of oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(M_0 = 32\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) and \(14\,\mathrm{g}\) of nitrogen (\(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(M_0 = 28\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) at temperature \(T\). If the total pressure of the mixture is \(P\), the partial pressure of oxygen is:
  • (A) \(\frac{P}{4}\)
  • (B) \(\frac{P}{3}\)
  • (C) \(\frac{P}{2}\)
  • (D) \(\frac{2P}{3}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Moles of \(\mathrm{O_2}\): \(\mu_1 = \frac{16}{32} = 0.5\,\mathrm{mol}\). Moles of \(\mathrm{N_2}\): \(\mu_2 = \frac{14}{28} = 0.5\,\mathrm{mol}\). By Dalton's law, \(P_1 = \left(\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_1 + \mu_2}\right)P = \left(\frac{0.5}{1.0}\right)P = \frac{P}{2}\).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ): An air bubble of volume \(2.0\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) rises from the bottom of a freshwater lake of depth \(30\,\mathrm{m}\) (where temperature is \(7\,\mathrm{°C}\)) to the surface (where atmospheric pressure is \(1.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\) and temperature is \(27\,\mathrm{°C}\)). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and water density \(\rho_w = 1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), the volume of the bubble just as it reaches the surface is approximately:
  • (A) \(4.3\,\mathrm{cm^3}\)
  • (B) \(6.4\,\mathrm{cm^3}\)
  • (C) \(8.6\,\mathrm{cm^3}\)
  • (D) \(10.2\,\mathrm{cm^3}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Pressure at bottom: \(P_1 = P_{\mathrm{atm}} + \rho_w g h = 1.0 \times 10^5 + (1000)(10)(30) = 4.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\). Temperatures: \(T_1 = 7 + 273 = 280\,\mathrm{K}\), \(T_2 = 27 + 273 = 300\,\mathrm{K}\). Using \(\frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2}\):

\[ V_2 = V_1 \left(\frac{P_1}{P_2}\right)\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) = 2.0 \times \frac{4.0 \times 10^5}{1.0 \times 10^5} \times \frac{300}{280} = 8.0 \times \frac{15}{14} \approx 8.57\,\mathrm{cm^3} \approx 8.6\,\mathrm{cm^3} \]
Practice Question 8 (Assertion–Reason):
Assertion (A): In a gas confined in a closed rigid cylinder, the mean free path of the molecules remains unchanged even if the temperature is doubled.
Reason (R): Mean free path depends on the number density \(n\) and molecular diameter \(d\), both of which remain constant in a rigid closed container.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: From \(l = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi n d^2}\), \(l\) depends only on \(n = N/V\) and \(d\). In a closed rigid container, \(N\) and \(V\) are fixed, so \(n\) does not change when \(T\) is doubled (pressure doubles proportionally, keeping \(T/P\) constant). Thus both (A) and (R) are true and (R) correctly explains (A).

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ): According to the law of equipartition of energy, the molar specific heat of a crystalline solid at room temperature (ignoring electronic contributions) is approximately:
  • (A) \(\frac{3}{2}R\)
  • (B) \(\frac{5}{2}R\)
  • (C) \(3R\)
  • (D) \(6R\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Each atom in a solid vibrates in 3 dimensions, having 3 vibrational modes (6 quadratic terms). Average energy per atom is \(6 \times \frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T = 3k_{\mathrm{B}}T\). For 1 mole, \(U = 3RT\), giving \(C = \frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}T} = 3R\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ): If the diameter of gas molecules were hypothetically halved while keeping number density \(n\) and temperature \(T\) unchanged, the mean free path \(l\) would become:
  • (A) \(4l\)
  • (B) \(2l\)
  • (C) \(\frac{l}{2}\)
  • (D) \(\frac{l}{4}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: Since \(l = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi n d^2}\), we have \(l \propto \frac{1}{d^2}\) at constant \(n\). Replacing \(d\) by \(d/2\) increases \(l\) by a factor of \(\frac{1}{(1/2)^2} = 4\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent Mistakes to Avoid in NEET UG
  • Celsius vs. Kelvin: Always convert temperatures to kelvin (\(T(\mathrm{K}) = t(\mathrm{°C}) + 273\)) before using \(PV = \mu RT\), \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} \propto \sqrt{T}\), or Charles' law.
  • Counting vibrational degrees of freedom: Each vibrational mode contributes 2 quadratic terms (kinetic + potential), adding \(k_{\mathrm{B}}T\) per molecule (or \(R\) to \(C_v\)), not \(\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\).
  • Mean square vs. square of the mean: Remember that \(\overline{v^2} \neq (\langle v \rangle)^2\). Specifically, \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}}\) is always greater than the mean speed \(\langle v \rangle\).
  • Gauge pressure vs. absolute pressure: When a problem states gauge pressure (e.g., in a cylinder or at depth in a lake), add atmospheric pressure (\(1\,\mathrm{atm}\)) to obtain absolute pressure \(P\) before applying the ideal gas law.
  • Mean free path at constant volume vs. constant pressure: At constant pressure, \(l \propto T\). At constant volume (rigid container), \(n\) is fixed so \(l\) is independent of \(T\).
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Ideal gas law: \(PV = \mu RT = N k_{\mathrm{B}}T\); density form \(P = \frac{\rho RT}{M_0}\).
  • Pressure & temperature: \(P = \frac{1}{3}nm\overline{v^2} = \frac{1}{3}\rho v_{\mathrm{rms}}^2\); \(PV = \frac{2}{3}E\); \(\frac{E}{N} = \frac{3}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\).
  • RMS speed: \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3P}{\rho}}\).
  • Specific heats: Monatomic \(\gamma = \frac{5}{3}\); rigid diatomic \(\gamma = \frac{7}{5}\); non-rigid diatomic (1 vib.) \(\gamma = \frac{9}{7}\); solid \(C = 3R\).
  • Mean free path: \(l = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi n d^2} = \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi d^2 P}\).

Concise Final Recap

The kinetic theory of gases explains macroscopic gas behaviour from the statistical mechanics of randomly moving molecules undergoing elastic collisions. Pressure arises from molecular momentum transfer to container walls, while absolute temperature directly measures average translational kinetic energy per molecule. Equipartition of energy predicts specific heats across monatomic, diatomic, and polyatomic gases as well as crystalline solids, and the finite size of molecules governs the mean free path and collision frequency.
