NEET UG Physics Fluid Mechanics

Mechanical Properties of Fluids

A complete, exam-oriented study guide covering hydrostatics, fluid dynamics, viscosity, and surface phenomena for NEET UG preparation.

Learning Objectives & Chapter Scope

Fluids—encompassing both liquids and gases—lack a fixed shape and yield continuously to even tiny tangential (shear) forces. Mastering this unit requires connecting force balance, energy conservation, and intermolecular forces across four core pillars:

  • Fluid Statics: Defining pressure as a scalar, deriving depth-dependent hydrostatic pressure, and applying Pascal's law to hydraulic machines and manometers.
  • Fluid Dynamics: Using the equation of continuity (mass conservation) and Bernoulli's principle (energy conservation) to analyze flow speed, efflux, and dynamic lift.
  • Viscosity: Quantifying internal fluid friction, contrasting temperature effects in liquids and gases, and calculating terminal velocity via Stokes' law.
  • Surface Tension & Capillarity: Relating surface energy to excess pressure in drops and bubbles, contact angles, and capillary rise.

1. Fluid Statics: Pressure, Depth, and Pascal's Law

1.1 What Makes a Fluid?

Unlike solids, which resist shear stress with a restoring elastic force proportional to shear strain, a fluid at rest cannot sustain any tangential force. If a tangential force acts on a fluid layer, the fluid immediately begins to flow. Consequently, any force exerted by a static fluid on a submerged object or container wall is strictly normal (perpendicular) to the surface at every point.

Liquids are nearly incompressible and maintain a definite volume with a free surface, whereas gases are highly compressible and expand to fill their entire container.

1.2 Pressure and Density

If a fluid exerts a normal force of magnitude \(F\) uniformly over a flat area \(A\), the average pressure is \(P_{\mathrm{av}} = F/A\). At a specific point, pressure is defined in the limiting sense as:

\[ P = \lim_{\Delta A \to 0} \frac{\Delta F}{\Delta A} \]

Although force is a vector, pressure is a scalar quantity because at any point in a static fluid, the pressure is identical in all directions and depends only on the normal component of force per unit area. Its dimensional formula is \([\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-2}}]\), and its SI unit is the pascal (\(\mathrm{Pa} = \mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)).

  • Standard Atmospheric Pressure: \(1\,\mathrm{atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • Bar: \(1\,\mathrm{bar} = 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • Torr (mm of Hg): \(1\,\mathrm{torr} = 1\,\mathrm{mm\ of\ Hg} \approx 133\,\mathrm{Pa}\)

Density (\(\rho = m/V\), SI unit \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) measures mass per unit volume. Relative density (specific gravity) is the dimensionless ratio of a substance's density to the density of water at \(4\,°\mathrm{C}\) (\(1.00 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)).

Substance (at STP) Density \(\rho\) (\(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) Relative Density
Pure Water (\(4\,°\mathrm{C}\)) \(1.00 \times 10^3\) \(1.00\)
Sea Water \(1.03 \times 10^3\) \(1.03\)
Mercury \(13.6 \times 10^3\) \(13.6\)
Ethyl Alcohol \(0.806 \times 10^3\) \(0.806\)
Air \(1.29\) \(1.29 \times 10^{-3}\)

1.3 Variation of Pressure with Depth

Consider a vertical cylindrical column of an incompressible fluid of uniform density \(\rho\), cross-sectional area \(A\), and height \(h\) at rest. Balancing the downward force \(P_1 A\) on the top face, the weight \(mg = \rho A h g\) of the fluid column, and the upward force \(P_2 A\) on the bottom face gives:

\[ P_2 - P_1 = \rho g h \]

When the top face lies at the free surface open to the atmosphere (\(P_1 = P_a\)), the absolute pressure \(P\) at depth \(h\) is:

\[ P = P_a + \rho g h \]

The excess pressure above atmospheric pressure, \(P_g = P - P_a = \rho g h\), is called the gauge pressure. Notice that cross-sectional area \(A\) cancels out completely: pressure at a given depth depends only on \(\rho\), \(g\), and vertical depth \(h\), never on the shape or volume of the vessel. This explains the hydrostatic paradox—interconnected vessels of wildly different shapes fill to the exact same horizontal level.

1.4 Pascal's Law and Hydraulic Machines

Pascal's Law: Any change in external pressure applied to an enclosed, incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished and equally in all directions throughout the fluid and to the walls of the container.

In a hydraulic lift, a small force \(F_1\) applied to a piston of small area \(A_1\) creates a pressure \(P = F_1 / A_1\). This pressure acts on a large piston of area \(A_2\), generating a much larger upward force \(F_2\):

\[ \frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2} \implies F_2 = F_1 \left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right) \]

Because the liquid is incompressible, the volume displaced by both pistons is identical (\(A_1 d_1 = A_2 d_2\)). Thus, a force multiplication by factor \(A_2/A_1\) comes at the cost of a proportional reduction in displacement, conserving mechanical work (\(F_1 d_1 = F_2 d_2\)).

1.5 Pressure Measuring Devices

  • Mercury Barometer: An inverted glass tube filled with mercury stands in a mercury trough. With near-zero vapor pressure in the closed top space, the atmospheric pressure \(P_a\) on the open surface supports a mercury column of height \(h\): \(P_a = \rho_{\mathrm{Hg}} g h\). At sea level, \(h \approx 76\,\mathrm{cm}\).
  • Open-Tube Manometer: A U-tube containing a manometric liquid (mercury for large pressure differences, oil for small ones) has one limb connected to a gas vessel at pressure \(P\) and the other open to the atmosphere (\(P_a\)). Equating pressure at the same horizontal level gives the gauge pressure \(P - P_a = \rho g h\), where \(h\) is the level difference.

2. Fluid Dynamics: Continuity and Bernoulli's Principle

2.1 Streamline Flow and Equation of Continuity

In steady (streamline) flow, the velocity of every fluid particle passing a given point in space remains constant over time (though velocity may vary from point to point). A streamline is a curve whose tangent at any point gives the direction of fluid velocity at that point. Two streamlines can never intersect; if they did, a fluid particle at the intersection would have two different velocities simultaneously, violating steady flow. Above a certain critical speed, steady flow breaks down into irregular, eddying turbulent flow.

For an incompressible fluid (\(\rho = \text{constant}\)) flowing steadily through a tube of varying cross-section, conservation of mass requires that the volume flow rate (volume flux) remain constant everywhere:

\[ A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 = \text{constant} \]

Where the pipe narrows (\(A\) decreases), streamlines crowd closer together and fluid speed \(v\) increases.

2.2 Bernoulli's Principle

Applying the work-energy theorem to an ideal fluid (steady, incompressible, non-viscous, and irrotational) moving along a streamline yields Bernoulli's equation:

\[ P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant} \]

Here, \(P\) is the static pressure, \(\frac{1}{2}\rho v^2\) is the kinetic energy per unit volume, and \(\rho g h\) is the gravitational potential energy per unit volume. In a horizontal pipe (\(h_1 = h_2\)), faster flow (narrower cross-section) corresponds to lower static pressure.

2.3 Key Applications of Bernoulli's Principle

  • Speed of Efflux (Torricelli's Law): For a small orifice of area \(A_1\) at depth \(h\) below the free surface of a large tank (\(A_2 \gg A_1\), so \(v_2 \approx 0\)), if the space above the liquid is at pressure \(P\): \[ v_1 = \sqrt{2gh + \frac{2(P - P_a)}{\rho}} \] When the tank is open to the atmosphere (\(P = P_a\)), this reduces to \(v_1 = \sqrt{2gh}\), identical to the speed of a body falling freely through height \(h\).
  • Dynamic Lift & Magnus Effect: A spinning ball moving through air drags adjacent air layers. On the side where spin velocity aligns with oncoming airflow, relative air speed is higher and pressure is lower; on the opposite side, air speed is lower and pressure is higher. This pressure asymmetry produces a sideways or upward force (the Magnus effect), curving the ball's path. Similarly, an aircraft wing (aerofoil) is shaped so air travels faster over its curved upper surface than beneath its flatter lower surface, creating a net upward lift.

3. Viscosity and Terminal Velocity

3.1 Coefficient of Viscosity

Real fluids experience internal friction called viscosity whenever adjacent layers move at different speeds. In laminar flow between two parallel plates separated by distance \(l\) (bottom plate fixed, top plate moving at speed \(v\)), the fluid layer in contact with each plate has the same velocity as that plate (no-slip condition). The shear stress \(F/A\) is directly proportional to the velocity gradient (rate of shear strain) \(v/l\):

\[ \eta = \frac{F / A}{v / l} = \frac{F l}{v A} \]

The constant \(\eta\) is the coefficient of viscosity. Its SI unit is \(\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}\) (also called poiseuille, \(\mathrm{Pl}\), or \(\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-2}}\)), and its dimensional formula is \([\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-1}}]\). In CGS units, \(1\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s} = 10\,\mathrm{poise}\).

Crucial Temperature Contrast:

  • In liquids, viscosity decreases as temperature rises because increased thermal energy weakens intermolecular cohesive forces.
  • In gases, viscosity increases with temperature because faster random molecular motion enhances momentum transfer between adjacent gas layers.

3.2 Stokes' Law and Terminal Velocity

A small sphere of radius \(a\) moving slowly at speed \(v\) through a viscous medium of viscosity \(\eta\) experiences a retarding viscous drag force opposing motion:

\[ F_v = 6 \pi \eta a v \]

When a sphere of density \(\rho\) falls freely in a fluid of density \(\sigma\), it initially accelerates under gravity. As its speed rises, the upward viscous drag \(F_v\) grows until the upward forces (viscous drag + buoyant upthrust \(F_B\)) exactly balance the downward weight \(W\):

\[ 6 \pi \eta a v_t + \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 \sigma g = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 \rho g \implies v_t = \frac{2 a^2 (\rho - \sigma) g}{9 \eta} \]

From this point onward, net force and acceleration are zero, and the sphere descends at a constant terminal velocity \(v_t\). Notice that \(v_t \propto a^2\): doubling the radius quadruples the terminal speed.

4. Surface Tension, Contact Angle, and Capillarity

4.1 Surface Energy and Surface Tension

A molecule deep inside a liquid is pulled equally in all directions by neighboring molecules, giving it a low (negative) potential energy. A molecule at the free surface lacks neighbors above it, so it experiences a net inward pull and possesses excess potential energy. To minimize this energy, a liquid spontaneously contracts to the smallest surface area allowed by external constraints—which is why small free droplets are spherical.

Surface tension (\(S\)) is defined either as the surface energy per unit area (\(\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}\)) or as the tensile force per unit length (\(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\)) acting perpendicular to any imaginary line drawn on the liquid interface. Its dimensional formula is \([\mathrm{M\,T^{-2}}]\). Because a thin soap film on a wire frame has two liquid-air surfaces, stretching a movable slider of length \(l\) requires a force \(F = 2 S l\).

Like liquid viscosity, the surface tension of a liquid generally decreases with increasing temperature.

4.2 Angle of Contact and Wetting

The angle of contact (\(\theta\)) is the angle inside the liquid between the tangent to the liquid surface at the point of contact and the solid surface. Balancing the three interfacial tensions—solid-air (\(S_{\mathrm{sa}}\)), solid-liquid (\(S_{\mathrm{sl}}\)), and liquid-air (\(S_{\mathrm{la}}\))—along the solid surface gives:

\[ S_{\mathrm{la}} \cos\theta + S_{\mathrm{sl}} = S_{\mathrm{sa}} \implies \cos\theta = \frac{S_{\mathrm{sa}} - S_{\mathrm{sl}}}{S_{\mathrm{la}}} \]
  • Acute angle (\(\theta < 90°\)): Liquid molecules adhere strongly to the solid (e.g., water on clean glass). The liquid wets and spreads on the solid. Adding detergents lowers \(S_{\mathrm{la}}\) and reduces \(\theta\), improving wetting.
  • Obtuse angle (\(\theta > 90°\)): Liquid molecules cohere more strongly to each other than to the solid (e.g., mercury on glass, or water on a waxy leaf). The liquid forms rounded beads and does not wet the solid.

4.3 Excess Pressure in Drops and Bubbles

Across any curved liquid-gas interface, the pressure on the concave (inner) side exceeds the pressure on the convex (outer) side:

  • Liquid drop in air (or air bubble inside a liquid): Has one spherical interface of radius \(r\): \[ \Delta P = P_i - P_o = \frac{2S}{r} \]
  • Soap bubble in air: Has two spherical interfaces (inner and outer) of nearly equal radius \(r\): \[ \Delta P = P_i - P_o = \frac{4S}{r} \]

4.4 Capillary Rise

When a narrow glass tube of radius \(a\) is dipped into a wetting liquid (\(\theta < 90°\)), the meniscus inside is concave, and the radius of curvature of the meniscus is \(r = a / \cos\theta\). Because pressure just below the concave meniscus is lower than atmospheric pressure by \(2S/r = (2S\cos\theta)/a\), atmospheric pressure pushes the liquid up the tube to a height \(h\) until hydrostatic pressure restores equilibrium:

\[ h = \frac{2 S \cos\theta}{a \rho g} \]

For non-wetting liquids (\(\theta > 90°\), such as mercury in glass), \(\cos\theta < 0\), so \(h\) is negative—the liquid level is depressed inside the capillary.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Applying Pascal's Law to a Hydraulic Press

Problem: In a hypothetical hydraulic press, the input piston has a radius of \(2.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) and the output piston has a radius of \(12.0\,\mathrm{cm}\). (a) What input force is needed to support a \(1800\,\mathrm{kg}\) crate on the output piston? (b) If the input piston is pushed down by \(18\,\mathrm{cm}\), how high does the crate rise? (Assume \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).)

Solution:

(a) By Pascal's law, pressure is transmitted equally: \(\frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2}\). Since \(A = \pi r^2\):

\[ F_1 = F_2 \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2 = (1800 \times 10)\,\mathrm{N} \times \left(\frac{2.0}{12.0}\right)^2 = 18000 \times \frac{1}{36}\,\mathrm{N} = 500\,\mathrm{N} \]

(b) By volume conservation of the incompressible hydraulic oil, \(A_1 d_1 = A_2 d_2\):

\[ d_2 = d_1 \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^2 = 18\,\mathrm{cm} \times \frac{1}{36} = 0.50\,\mathrm{cm} \]
Example 2: Determining Gauge Pressure with a U-Tube Manometer

Problem: An open-tube manometer filled with mercury (\(\rho_{\mathrm{Hg}} = 13.6 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) is connected to a gas cylinder. The mercury level in the open limb stands \(15.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) higher than in the limb connected to the cylinder. Calculate the gauge pressure and absolute pressure of the gas. (Assume \(P_a = 1.01 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\) and \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).)

Solution:

The gauge pressure is given by \(P_g = P - P_a = \rho_{\mathrm{Hg}} g h\):

\[ P_g = (13.6 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}})(9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}})(0.15\,\mathrm{m}) = 19{,}992\,\mathrm{Pa} \approx 2.00 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{Pa} \]

The absolute pressure inside the cylinder is:

\[ P = P_a + P_g = 1.01 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} + 0.20 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} = 1.21 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} \]
Example 3: Calculating Bernoulli Pressure Drop in a Horizontal Pipe

Problem: Water (\(\rho = 1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) flows steadily through a horizontal pipe whose cross-sectional area narrows from \(20\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) to \(5.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}\). If the speed in the wider section is \(1.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and the static pressure there is \(1.40 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\), find the speed and pressure in the narrower section.

Solution:

First, apply the equation of continuity \(A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2\):

\[ v_2 = v_1 \left(\frac{A_1}{A_2}\right) = 1.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \times \left(\frac{20}{5.0}\right) = 6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Next, apply Bernoulli's equation for a horizontal streamline (\(h_1 = h_2\)):

\[ P_2 = P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho(v_1^2 - v_2^2) = 1.40 \times 10^5 + \frac{1}{2}(1000)(1.5^2 - 6.0^2) \] \[ P_2 = 1.40 \times 10^5 + 500(2.25 - 36.0) = 1.40 \times 10^5 - 1.6875 \times 10^4 \approx 1.23 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} \]
Example 4: Finding Terminal Velocity of a Falling Sphere

Problem: A small steel sphere of radius \(1.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) and density \(7.8 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) falls through glycerine of density \(1.3 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and viscosity \(0.85\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}\). Calculate its terminal velocity. (Assume \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).)

Solution:

Using the terminal velocity formula \(v_t = \frac{2 a^2 (\rho - \sigma) g}{9 \eta}\):

\[ v_t = \frac{2 \times (1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m})^2 \times (7.8 - 1.3)\times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \times 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}}{9 \times 0.85\,\mathrm{Pa\cdot s}} \] \[ v_t = \frac{2 \times 10^{-6} \times 6.5 \times 10^3 \times 9.8}{7.65} \approx 1.67 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 1.67\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}} \]
Example 5: Computing Capillary Rise for Water in Glass

Problem: A clean glass capillary tube of internal diameter \(0.80\,\mathrm{mm}\) is dipped vertically into pure water at \(20\,°\mathrm{C}\) (surface tension \(S = 0.073\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\), density \(\rho = 1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), contact angle \(\theta \approx 0°\)). Find the height to which water rises in the tube. (Take \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).)

Solution:

The tube radius is \(a = 0.40\,\mathrm{mm} = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}\), and \(\cos 0° = 1\). Using \(h = \frac{2 S \cos\theta}{a \rho g}\):

\[ h = \frac{2 \times 0.073\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}} \times 1}{(4.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m})(1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}})(9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}})} = \frac{0.146}{3.92} \approx 0.0372\,\mathrm{m} = 3.72\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

A diver descends from a depth of \(10\,\mathrm{m}\) to a depth of \(30\,\mathrm{m}\) in a freshwater lake (\(\rho = 1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)). Taking atmospheric pressure as \(1.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\), what is the percentage increase in absolute pressure experienced by the diver?

  1. (A) \(50\%\)
  2. (B) \(100\%\)
  3. (C) \(150\%\)
  4. (D) \(200\%\)
Answer: (B) \(100\%\)
Explanation: At \(h_1 = 10\,\mathrm{m}\), \(P_1 = 1.0 \times 10^5 + 1000 \times 10 \times 10 = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\). At \(h_2 = 30\,\mathrm{m}\), \(P_2 = 1.0 \times 10^5 + 1000 \times 10 \times 30 = 4.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\). The percentage increase is \(\frac{4.0 - 2.0}{2.0} \times 100\% = 100\%\).
Practice Question 2 Conceptual MCQ

Three open vessels—a narrow cylinder, a wide conical flask, and an inverted funnel—have identical base areas and are filled with mercury to the exact same vertical height \(h\). How do the hydrostatic forces exerted by the mercury on the base of the three vessels compare?

  1. (A) Largest in the inverted funnel, smallest in the narrow cylinder
  2. (B) Largest in the wide conical flask, smallest in the inverted funnel
  3. (C) Equal in all three vessels
  4. (D) Proportional to the total mass of mercury in each vessel
Answer: (C) Equal in all three vessels
Explanation: Gauge pressure at the bottom is \(P_g = \rho g h\), which depends only on vertical height \(h\) and density \(\rho\), not on vessel shape. Since the base areas \(A\) are equal, the downward force \(F = P A\) is identical in all three vessels (hydrostatic paradox).
Practice Question 3 Numerical MCQ

An open cylindrical tank contains water to a depth of \(3.2\,\mathrm{m}\). A tiny hole is punched in the side wall at a height of \(1.4\,\mathrm{m}\) above the bottom. Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), what is the speed of efflux of water from the hole?

  1. (A) \(4.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  2. (B) \(6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  3. (C) \(8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  4. (D) \(9.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B) \(6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Explanation: The depth of the hole below the free water surface is \(h = 3.2 - 1.4 = 1.8\,\mathrm{m}\). By Torricelli's law, \(v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 1.8} = \sqrt{36} = 6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
Practice Question 4 Numerical MCQ

Water flows through a horizontal nozzle whose inlet diameter is \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) and outlet diameter is \(1.0\,\mathrm{cm}\). If water enters at \(0.50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), what is the exit speed?

  1. (A) \(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  2. (B) \(4.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  3. (C) \(8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  4. (D) \(16.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (C) \(8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Explanation: By continuity, \(A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \implies d_1^2 v_1 = d_2^2 v_2\). Thus \(v_2 = 0.50 \times (4.0 / 1.0)^2 = 0.50 \times 16 = 8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
Practice Question 5 Statement Type

Statement I: As temperature increases, the viscosity of a liquid decreases while the viscosity of a gas increases.
Statement II: In gases, viscosity arises primarily from momentum transfer by randomly moving molecules between adjacent layers, which intensifies at higher temperatures.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
Explanation: Higher temperature weakens intermolecular cohesion in liquids (reducing \(\eta\)), whereas in gases it increases molecular thermal speeds and interlayer momentum exchange (increasing \(\eta\)).
Practice Question 6 Ratio / Numerical MCQ

Eight identical spherical raindrops, each falling through air at a terminal velocity of \(2.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\), coalesce to form a single large spherical drop. Neglecting air density compared to water density, what is the terminal velocity of the combined drop?

  1. (A) \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
  2. (B) \(8.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
  3. (C) \(16.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
  4. (D) \(32.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B) \(8.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\)
Explanation: Volume conservation gives \(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = 8 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \implies R = 2r\). Since terminal velocity \(v_t \propto r^2\), \(v_{\text{large}} = v_{\text{small}} \times (R/r)^2 = 2.0 \times 2^2 = 8.0\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\).
Practice Question 7 Numerical MCQ

A U-shaped wire frame with a movable light slider of length \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) holds a thin soap film. What total weight (including the slider) can the film support if the surface tension of the soap solution is \(0.030\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\)?

  1. (A) \(6.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N}\)
  2. (B) \(1.2 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N}\)
  3. (C) \(3.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N}\)
  4. (D) \(2.4 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N}\)
Answer: (B) \(1.2 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N}\)
Explanation: A soap film has two free surfaces, so the upward surface tension force on the slider is \(F = 2 S l = 2 \times 0.030\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}} \times 0.20\,\mathrm{m} = 1.2 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{N}\).
Practice Question 8 Numerical MCQ

The excess pressure inside a soap bubble of radius \(R_1\) is three times the excess pressure inside another soap bubble of radius \(R_2\) made of the same soap solution. What is the ratio of their volumes \(V_1 : V_2\)?

  1. (A) \(1 : 3\)
  2. (B) \(1 : 9\)
  3. (C) \(1 : 27\)
  4. (D) \(27 : 1\)
Answer: (C) \(1 : 27\)
Explanation: Excess pressure \(\Delta P = 4S/R\), so \(\Delta P_1 / \Delta P_2 = R_2 / R_1 = 3 \implies R_1 / R_2 = 1/3\). Volume \(V \propto R^3\), hence \(V_1 / V_2 = (1/3)^3 = 1/27\).
Practice Question 9 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Adding detergent to water helps remove greasy dirt from clothes.
Reason (R): Detergent decreases the surface tension of water and reduces its angle of contact with the fabric, allowing water to penetrate fine pores.

  1. (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  2. (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  3. (C) A is true, but R is false.
  4. (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
Explanation: Detergents act as wetting agents: they lower liquid-air surface tension and decrease the contact angle \(\theta\), enabling water to spread and wet oily/fabric surfaces effectively.

Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

  • Drop vs. Soap Bubble Factor: Always use \(\Delta P = 2S/r\) for a liquid drop in air or an air bubble submerged in a liquid (one interface), and \(\Delta P = 4S/r\) for a soap bubble in air (two interfaces).
  • Soap Film on a Wire Frame: A stretched liquid film has two sides, so the capillary/surface force on a slider of length \(l\) is \(2Sl\), not \(Sl\).
  • Bernoulli's Restrictions: Bernoulli's equation does not apply to turbulent, compressible, or strongly viscous flows (such as river rapids or narrow-tube viscous flow).
  • Gauge vs. Absolute Pressure: Read problems carefully to see whether \(P_a\) must be added. A submarine window with atmospheric pressure inside experiences only the gauge pressure \(\rho g h\) as net pressure.
  • Viscosity vs. Temperature: Never confuse liquids and gases—\(\eta_{\text{liquid}}\) falls with \(T\), whereas \(\eta_{\text{gas}}\) rises with \(T\).
