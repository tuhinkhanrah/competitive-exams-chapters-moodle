Evolution: Origin of Life, Mechanisms, and Human Ancestry

A standalone, exam-aligned tutorial covering cosmic and chemical origins, Darwinian and modern evolutionary theory, population genetics, geological timelines, and hominid evolution.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Evolutionary biology investigates how life originated on Earth and diversified over billions of years. For NEET UG, mastery of this topic requires connecting geological timescales, experimental evidence, comparative anatomy, mathematical population genetics, and fossil hominid milestones.

  • Trace the timeline from the origin of the universe and Earth to chemical evolution and the emergence of cellular life.
  • Evaluate early hypotheses of life's origin alongside the Oparin–Haldane theory and S.L. Miller's experimental verification.
  • Distinguish Lamarckism, Darwinian natural selection, and Hugo de Vries's mutation theory (saltation).
  • Classify evolutionary evidence—paleontological, embryological, anatomical (homology vs. analogy), biochemical, and anthropogenic (industrial melanism and drug resistance).
  • Apply the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium equation and analyze the five evolutionary forces and three modes of natural selection.
  • Sequence major plant, vertebrate, and human evolutionary stages along the geological timescale.

Cosmic Background and the Origin of Life

Observing distant stars is equivalent to looking into the past because stellar light travels across trillions of kilometres over millions of years before reaching Earth. Against this cosmic backdrop, the universe is estimated to be about 20 billion years old and consists of vast clusters of galaxies.

  • Big Bang Theory: Proposes that the universe began with a singular, unimaginable physical explosion. Subsequent expansion lowered temperatures, allowing hydrogen and helium to form and condense under gravity into galaxies.
  • Formation of Earth: Within the Milky Way solar system, Earth formed roughly 4.5 billion years ago. Early Earth lacked a free-oxygen atmosphere; instead, molten masses released water vapour, methane (\( \text{CH}_4 \)), carbon dioxide (\( \text{CO}_2 \)), and ammonia (\( \text{NH}_3 \)).
  • Cooling and Ocean Formation: Solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation split water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen. Light \( \text{H}_2 \) escaped into space, while oxygen reacted with \( \text{NH}_3 \) and \( \text{CH}_4 \) to yield water, \( \text{CO}_2 \), and other compounds, eventually contributing to the ozone layer. As Earth cooled, water vapour condensed as rain, filling surface depressions to form oceans. Life appeared around 4 billion years ago (roughly 500 million years after Earth formed).

Early Theories of Life's Origin

  • Theory of Special Creation: Traditional view asserting three claims: (1) all species were created exactly as they exist today, (2) biodiversity has remained unchanged since creation and will stay unchanged, and (3) Earth is only about 4,000 years old. Nineteenth-century scientific findings refuted all three claims.
  • Panspermia: Idea favoured by early Greek thinkers and some astronomers that units of life called spores were transferred to Earth from outer space.
  • Theory of Spontaneous Generation: Long-held belief that living organisms arose directly from decaying non-living matter such as straw or mud. Louis Pasteur decisively disproved this using pre-sterilised flasks containing killed yeast: no life appeared in sealed/protected flasks, whereas living organisms grew only when exposed to air-borne contamination, proving that life arises solely from pre-existing life.

Chemical Evolution and Miller's Experiment

While Pasteur showed that modern life comes from pre-existing life, Oparin (Russia) and Haldane (England) independently proposed that the very first life arose from pre-existing non-living organic molecules (such as RNA and proteins), preceded by chemical evolution—the synthesis of organic molecules from inorganic precursors under early Earth conditions (high temperature, volcanic storms, and a reducing atmosphere rich in \( \text{CH}_4 \) and \( \text{NH}_3 \)).

In 1953, American scientist S.L. Miller tested this hypothesis in a closed laboratory apparatus. He circulated \( \text{CH}_4 \), \( \text{H}_2 \), \( \text{NH}_3 \), and water vapour at 800 °C past electric sparks simulating lightning and observed the formation of amino acids. Subsequent experiments by other researchers yielded sugars, nitrogenous bases, pigments, and fats, and meteorite analyses revealed similar organic compounds elsewhere in space.

The first non-cellular forms of life (giant self-replicating macromolecular aggregates of RNA, proteins, and polysaccharides) likely originated around 3 billion years ago. The first cellular forms of life (single-celled, strictly aquatic) appeared around 2000 million years ago (mya). This gradual emergence of life from non-living molecules is termed abiogenesis (chemical evolution followed by biological evolution).

Theories of Biological Evolution

Theory / Proponent Core Mechanism Key Characteristics & Status
Lamarckism
(Jean-Baptiste Lamarck)		 Use and disuse of organs; inheritance of acquired characters (e.g., giraffes stretching necks to reach tall foliage and passing elongated necks to offspring). Disproved; somatic changes acquired during a lifetime are not inherited genetically.
Darwinism
(Charles Darwin & Alfred Wallace)		 Branching descent and natural selection. Influenced by Thomas Malthus's observation that populations grow exponentially while resources remain limited, creating competition. Variations are small, heritable, and directional. Fitness refers strictly to reproductive fitness. Rate of evolution depends on generation time/life span (rapid in bacteria, slow in vertebrates).
Mutation Theory
(Hugo de Vries)		 Sudden, large heritable changes (mutations) observed in evening primrose (Oenothera lamarckiana) drive speciation. Mutations are random and directionless. Speciation occurs via a single-step large mutation termed saltation.

Charles Darwin's observations during his voyage on H.M.S. Beagle and Alfred Wallace's parallel conclusions in the Malay Archipelago established that existing species share common ancestors that lived across different geological epochs, periods, and eras.

Lines of Evidence for Evolution

  • Paleontological Evidence: Fossils are preserved hard parts of organisms embedded in sedimentary rock strata. Deeper layers hold older fossils; radioactive dating determines fossil age, showing that distinct life forms were restricted to specific geological time spans.
  • Embryological Evidence: Ernst Haeckel noted shared vertebrate embryonic features (such as vestigial gill slits behind the head, functional only in adult fish) and proposed that ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny. However, Karl Ernst von Baer disproved Haeckel's claim, demonstrating that embryos never pass through the adult stages of other animals.
  • Biochemical Evidence: Similarities in proteins and genes performing identical functions across diverse taxa point to shared common ancestry.
  • Artificial Selection: Intensive human breeding of crops and domesticated animals (e.g., dog breeds) over centuries demonstrates that selection can rapidly generate diverse varieties from a common stock.

Comparative Anatomy: Homology vs. Analogy

Natural Selection in Action & Anthropogenic Evolution

  • Industrial Melanism in England: Before industrialisation (1850s), white-coloured lichens covered tree trunks, camouflaging white-winged moths while dark melanised moths were easily spotted by predators. Post-industrialisation (1920s), soot darkened tree trunks and eliminated pollution-sensitive lichens (which serve as industrial pollution indicators), reversing survival in favour of melanised moths. In unpolluted rural areas, melanic moth frequency remained low. Crucially, no variant was completely wiped out.
  • Evolution by Anthropogenic Action: Excessive use of herbicides, pesticides, and antibiotics selects for pre-existing resistant variants within months or years rather than centuries. This confirms that evolution is a stochastic (chance-based) process driven by random mutations and environmental events, not a directed or deterministic goal.

Adaptive Radiation and Convergent Evolution

Adaptive radiation is the evolution of diverse species from a single ancestral stock within a given geographical area, radiating into different ecological habitats.

  • Darwin's Finches (Galapagos Islands): From an original seed-eating ancestral finch, multiple species evolved with modified beak shapes suited for insectivorous, vegetarian, or cactus-feeding niches.
  • Australian Marsupials: A wide array of marsupials (kangaroo, wombat, koala, bandicoot, marsupial mole, sugar glider, numbat, tiger cat, Tasmanian wolf) evolved from a common ancestral marsupial stock on the isolated Australian continent.

When more than one adaptive radiation occurs in an isolated geographical region across similar habitats, it results in convergent evolution. For example, placental mammals and Australian marsupials evolved strikingly similar counterparts: placental wolf and Tasmanian wolf; flying squirrel and flying phalanger (sugar glider); anteater and numbat; mole and marsupial mole; lemur and spotted cuscus; bobcat and Tasmanian tiger cat; mouse and marsupial mouse.

Population Genetics: Hardy–Weinberg Principle & Selection Modes

The Hardy–Weinberg principle states that allele frequencies in a large, randomly mating population remain stable and constant from generation to generation—a state called genetic equilibrium (constant gene pool). For a diploid locus with two alleles \( A \) (frequency \( p \)) and \( a \) (frequency \( q \)):

\[ p + q = 1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1 \]

where \( p^2 \) = frequency of \( AA \), \( 2pq \) = frequency of \( Aa \), and \( q^2 \) = frequency of \( aa \).

Any deviation between observed and expected genotype frequencies indicates evolutionary change. Five factors disturb Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium:

  1. Gene Migration / Gene Flow: Movement of individuals adds alleles to the recipient population and removes them from the donor population; repeated migration constitutes gene flow.
  2. Genetic Drift: Random, chance-driven fluctuations in allele frequencies (prominent in small populations). When a small drifted sample colonises a new area and diverges into a new species, the original drifted individuals are founders—the Founder effect.
  3. Mutation: Introduces new alleles randomly.
  4. Genetic Recombination: Crossing over and independent assortment during gametogenesis reshuffle alleles into new combinations.
  5. Natural Selection: Differential reproductive success alters allele frequencies in favour of adaptive phenotypes.

Geological History of Life and Human Evolution

  • 2000 mya: First cellular life forms; some evolved photosynthetic pigments capable of splitting water and releasing \( \text{O}_2 \).
  • 500 mya: Invertebrates formed and became active.
  • 350 mya: Jawless fish evolved; stout-finned lobefins (e.g., Coelacanth, once thought extinct until a specimen was caught in South Africa in 1938) evolved into the first amphibians.
  • 320 mya: Seaweeds and early land plants existed; plants invaded land before animals. Giant ferns (pteridophytes) later formed coal deposits.
  • Reptilian & Mammalian Radiation: Amphibians evolved into reptiles with thick-shelled eggs resistant to desiccation. Early reptiles diverged into Sauropsids (giving rise to turtles, lizards, snakes, tuataras, crocodiles, dinosaurs such as the 20-feet-tall Tyrannosaurus rex, marine reptiles like Ichthyosaurus around 200 mya, and birds) and Synapsids (pelycosaurs → therapsids → viviparous mammals). Dinosaurs vanished abruptly around 65 mya.
  • Continental Drift: Joining of South and North America caused North American fauna to override native South American mammals, whereas geographic isolation allowed Australian pouched mammals (marsupials) to survive without placental competition.

Worked Examples

Applying the Hardy–Weinberg Equation to a Population

Problem (Assumed Scenario): In a hypothetical, randomly mating population of 1,000 wild beetles in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, 160 individuals display the homozygous recessive pale-shell phenotype (\( aa \)). Calculate: (a) the frequency of the recessive allele \( a \), (b) the frequency of the dominant allele \( A \), and (c) the number of heterozygous (\( Aa \)) beetles.

Solution:

  1. Frequency of homozygous recessive individuals: \[ q^2 = \frac{160}{1000} = 0.16 \]
  2. Recessive allele frequency: \[ q = \sqrt{0.16} = 0.4 \]
  3. Using \( p + q = 1 \), dominant allele frequency: \[ p = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6 \]
  4. Heterozygous genotype frequency: \[ 2pq = 2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.48 \]
  5. Number of heterozygous beetles = \( 0.48 \times 1000 = 480 \).

Distinguishing Homology from Analogy in Plant and Animal Organs

Problem: Classify the following pairs as homologous or analogous, and state the evolutionary pattern each represents: (i) Sweet potato and potato tubers; (ii) Thorns of Bougainvillea and tendrils of Cucurbita.

Solution:

  • (i) Sweet potato and potato: Both store starch (similar function), but sweet potato is a modified root whereas potato is a modified stem (different anatomical origins). Therefore, they are analogous organs resulting from convergent evolution.
  • (ii) Thorns of Bougainvillea and tendrils of Cucurbita: Both arise from axillary buds (shared anatomical origin), yet thorns serve for protection while tendrils aid in climbing (different functions). Therefore, they are homologous organs resulting from divergent evolution.

Comparing Darwinian Selection, Saltation, and Genetic Drift

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A small flock of 12 finches is blown by a storm to a newly formed volcanic island, where their descendants establish a population with an unusually high frequency of a rare allele. Identify the evolutionary mechanism and contrast it with Darwinian natural selection and de Vries's saltation.

Solution:

  • Mechanism: This is an example of the Founder effect, a form of genetic drift in which a small, randomly sampled group establishes a new population whose allele frequencies differ by chance from the parent population.
  • Contrast: Unlike Darwinian natural selection (which is directional and driven by differential reproductive fitness) or de Vries's saltation (single-step, large, random mutations causing speciation), genetic drift alters allele frequencies purely through sampling chance in small populations.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In S.L. Miller's 1953 experiment simulating early Earth conditions, which gas mixture and temperature were used to synthesise amino acids?

  • (A) \( \text{CH}_4 \), \( \text{NH}_3 \), \( \text{O}_2 \), and \( \text{H}_2\text{O} \) at 400 °C
  • (B) \( \text{CH}_4 \), \( \text{NH}_3 \), \( \text{H}_2 \), and \( \text{H}_2\text{O} \) vapour at 800 °C
  • (C) \( \text{CO}_2 \), \( \text{N}_2 \), \( \text{H}_2 \), and \( \text{H}_2\text{O} \) vapour at 800 °C
  • (D) \( \text{CH}_4 \), \( \text{CO}_2 \), \( \text{O}_2 \), and \( \text{NH}_3 \) at 200 °C
Answer: (B)
Solution: Early Earth had a reducing atmosphere lacking free \( \text{O}_2 \). Miller used \( \text{CH}_4 \), \( \text{NH}_3 \), \( \text{H}_2 \), and water vapour at 800 °C with electric spark discharge to obtain amino acids.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which pair of structures represents convergent evolution?

  • (A) Forelimbs of whale and bat
  • (B) Thorns of Bougainvillea and tendrils of Cucurbita
  • (C) Eye of octopus and eye of mammals
  • (D) Hearts of amphibians and mammals
Answer: (C)
Solution: The eyes of an octopus and a mammal perform the same visual function despite having different embryonic/anatomical origins; hence they are analogous organs arising via convergent evolution. Options (A), (B), and (D) are homologous structures showing divergent evolution.
Practice Question 3 (Numerical / Population Genetics)

In an assumed Hardy–Weinberg population, the frequency of the dominant allele \( A \) is \( 0.7 \). What percentage of the population is expected to be homozygous recessive (\( aa \))?

  • (A) 9%
  • (B) 21%
  • (C) 42%
  • (D) 49%
Answer: (A)
Solution: Given \( p = 0.7 \), then \( q = 1 - p = 0.3 \). The homozygous recessive genotype frequency is \( q^2 = (0.3)^2 = 0.09 \), which corresponds to 9%.
Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Consider the following two statements regarding evolutionary theory:

Statement I: According to Hugo de Vries, mutations are random, directionless, and cause speciation in a single large step called saltation.
Statement II: According to Charles Darwin, variations are small, directional, and drive gradual evolution through reproductive fitness.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
Answer: (A)
Solution: Both statements accurately contrast de Vries's mutation theory (random, directionless, single-step saltation) with Darwin's theory of natural selection (small, directional, gradual variations).
Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): During post-industrialisation in England, the proportion of dark-winged (melanised) moths increased relative to white-winged moths.
Reason (R): Industrial soot darkened tree trunks and killed white lichens, making white-winged moths more conspicuous to predators.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true
Answer: (A)
Solution: Industrial melanism is a classic example of directional natural selection: soot-darkened bark camouflaged melanic moths while exposing light moths to predation.

Who disproved Ernst Haeckel's proposal that vertebrate embryos pass through the adult stages of other animals?

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When natural selection favours individuals with extreme trait values at both ends of a phenotypic distribution while selecting against the mean, it is termed:

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Confusing Homology and Analogy: Always check anatomical origin first. Shared origin + different function = Homology (divergent). Different origin + same function = Analogy (convergent).
  • Misinterpreting Hardy–Weinberg Frequencies: Remember that \( q^2 \) is the frequency of homozygous recessive individuals, not the recessive allele frequency \( q \). Always take \( \sqrt{q^2} \) before calculating \( p = 1 - q \) and \( 2pq \).
  • Equating Darwinian Fitness with Physical Strength: For Darwin, fitness means strictly reproductive fitness—leaving more surviving progeny.
  • Mixing Up Brain Capacities & Diets: Homo habilis (650–800 cc) likely did not eat meat; Homo erectus (~900 cc) probably ate meat; Neanderthals (1400 cc) buried their dead.
  • Forgetting Evolution Is Non-Directed: Antibiotic resistance does not arise because bacteria "need" it; pre-existing random variants are selected by the antibiotic.

Concise Final Recap

The universe originated ~20 billion years ago, Earth formed ~4.5 billion years ago, and life appeared ~4 billion years ago after chemical evolution under reducing atmospheric conditions (verified by Miller's 1953 spark-discharge experiment). Darwin's natural selection (branching descent and reproductive fitness) and modern population genetics (Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium \( p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1 \) disturbed by gene flow, genetic drift, mutation, recombination, and selection) explain how biodiversity evolved—from aquatic single-celled organisms (2000 mya) through invertebrates, lobefins, amphibians, sauropsid and synapsid reptiles, and mammals, culminating in modern Homo sapiens.
