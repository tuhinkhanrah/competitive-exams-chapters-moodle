NEET UG Biology • Class XII Unit: Reproduction

Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

A complete concept-to-assessment tutorial covering floral gametogenesis, pollination ecology, double fertilisation, seed biology, and apomixis.

Learning Objectives and Exam Focus

Angiosperms (flowering plants) dominate terrestrial ecosystems largely because their sexual reproductive cycle is independent of external water for gamete fusion and encloses the embryo within a protective, nutrient-rich seed. For NEET UG, this chapter is high-yield across three themes: cellular ploidy tracking, structural sequences (anther wall layers, ovule anatomy, embryo sac nuclei), and ecological/applied mechanisms (outbreeding devices, artificial hybridisation, apomixis).

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Trace male gametophyte development from sporogenous tissue to 2-celled and 3-celled pollen grains.
  • Explain monosporic female gametophyte development culminating in a 7-celled, 8-nucleate embryo sac.
  • Distinguish autogamy, geitonogamy, and xenogamy genetically and functionally, and relate floral traits to abiotic/biotic pollination agents.
  • Account for double fertilisation (syngamy + triple fusion) and predict ploidy levels in zygote, endosperm, embryo, and seed tissues.
  • Differentiate albuminous vs. non-albuminous seeds, true vs. false vs. parthenocarpic fruits, and apomixis vs. polyembryony.

1. Floral Organisation and Pre-Fertilisation Commitment

Before a flower is externally visible, hormonal and structural signals commit the shoot apical meristem to form a floral primordium. Inflorescences bear floral buds that differentiate four whorls on the thalamus: non-essential accessory whorls (calyx of sepals, corolla of petals) and essential reproductive whorls—the androecium (whorl of stamens; male) and the gynoecium (whorl of carpels/pistils; female).

Because both male and female gametes in angiosperms are non-motile, plants have evolved specialised structures to protect developing gametophytes and reliable transport mechanisms to bring gametes together.

2. Male Reproductive Unit: Stamen, Anther, and Pollen Grain

2.1 Structure of the Stamen and Dithecous Anther

A typical stamen consists of a slender stalk called the filament (whose proximal end attaches to the thalamus or petal) and a terminal, bilobed anther. A standard angiosperm anther is:

  • Bilobed and Dithecous: Each of the two lobes contains two thecae separated by a longitudinal groove.
  • Tetragonal and Tetrasporangiate: In transverse section, four microsporangia sit at the four corners (two per lobe). As the anther matures, these microsporangia elongate longitudinally into pollen sacs packed with pollen grains.

2.2 Microsporangium Wall Layers and Tapetum

A circular microsporangium in cross-section is enclosed by four concentric wall layers surrounding a central mass of homogeneous, compactly arranged diploid cells called sporogenous tissue:

Wall Layer (Outer to Inner) Structural Features Primary Role
Epidermis Single-layered, flattened protective cells Protection of inner tissues
Endothecium Single layer with fibrous thickenings (alpha-cellulose bands) Protection and anther dehiscence (splits along stomium)
Middle Layers 1–3 layers of parenchymatous cells; short-lived Protection and temporary food storage
Tapetum Innermost layer; dense cytoplasm; often binucleate or multinucleate (polyploid via endomitosis) Nourishes developing pollen grains; secretes sporopollenin precursors and enzymes

2.3 Microsporogenesis and the Male Gametophyte (Pollen Grain)

Every cell of the diploid sporogenous tissue is a potential Pollen Mother Cell (PMC) or microspore mother cell. Each PMC (\(2n\)) undergoes meiosis (microsporogenesis) to produce a cluster of four haploid microspores (\(n\)) arranged as a microspore tetrad. As the anther matures and dehydrates, microspores dissociate and develop into pollen grains.

  • Size and Shape: Typically spherical, measuring \(25\text{–}50\,\mathrm{µm}\) in diameter.
  • Two-Layered Sporoderm:
    • Exine (Outer): Hard, sculptured layer composed of sporopollenin—one of the most chemically resistant organic polymers known (withstands extreme heat, strong acids, and alkalis; no known enzyme degrades it). This accounts for the excellent preservation of pollen as fossils. Thin areas lacking sporopollenin form germ pores, through which the pollen tube emerges.
    • Intine (Inner): Thin, continuous layer made of cellulose and pectin.
  • Cellular Maturation: An asymmetric mitotic division produces two unequal cells:
    • Vegetative Cell: Larger, with abundant food reserves and a large, irregularly shaped nucleus.
    • Generative Cell: Smaller, spindle-shaped, with dense cytoplasm and a nucleus, floating inside the cytoplasm of the vegetative cell.
  • Shedding Stage: In over 60% of angiosperms, pollen is shed at the 2-celled stage (vegetative + generative cell). In the remaining species, the generative cell divides mitotically before shedding, releasing pollen at the 3-celled stage (vegetative cell + 2 male gametes).
Applied Biology & Clinical Relevance:
  • Pollen Allergy: Airborne pollen of several plants triggers asthma and bronchitis; Parthenium (carrot grass), introduced into India as a contaminant with imported wheat, is a major ubiquitous allergen.
  • Pollen Nutrition & Viability: Pollen tablets and syrups are marketed as nutrient supplements. Viability varies widely—cereals like rice and wheat lose viability within 30 minutes, whereas members of Rosaceae, Leguminosae, and Solanaceae remain viable for months.
  • Pollen Banks: Pollen grains can be cryopreserved for years in liquid nitrogen at \(-196\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) for crop breeding programmes.

3. Female Reproductive Unit: Pistil, Ovule, and Embryo Sac

3.1 Pistil Diversity and the Anatropous Ovule

The gynoecium may consist of a single pistil (monocarpellary) or multiple pistils (multicarpellary). Multicarpellary pistils may be fused (syncarpous, e.g., Papaver) or free (apocarpous, e.g., Michelia). Each pistil has three regions: stigma (pollen landing platform), style (slender stalk), and basal ovary enclosing the ovarian cavity (locule). The placenta inside the locule bears megasporangia (ovules)—one per ovary in wheat, paddy, and mango, or many in papaya, watermelon, and orchids.

Key parts of a typical inverted (anatropous) ovule:

  • Funicle: Stalk attaching the ovule to the placenta.
  • Hilum: Junction where the ovule body fuses with the funicle.
  • Integuments: One or two protective envelopes encircling the nucellus, leaving a narrow apical pore called the micropyle.
  • Chalaza: Basal pole of the ovule, opposite the micropylar end.
  • Nucellus: Central mass of diploid parenchymatous cells rich in reserve food, enclosing the embryo sac.

3.2 Megasporogenesis and Monosporic Embryo Sac Development

A single large Megaspore Mother Cell (MMC, \(2n\)) differentiates in the micropylar region of the nucellus. It undergoes meiosis to form a linear tetrad of four haploid megaspores (\(n\)). In most angiosperms, three megaspores degenerate and only one (usually chalazal) remains functional—a pattern termed monosporic development.

The functional megaspore nucleus undergoes three sequential free-nuclear mitotic divisions (nuclear division without immediate cytokinesis), producing 2-nucleate, 4-nucleate, and 8-nucleate stages. Cell walls then form around six of the eight nuclei, organising the mature 7-celled, 8-nucleate female gametophyte (embryo sac):

  • Egg Apparatus (Micropylar end, 3 cells): One haploid egg cell and two synergids. Synergids bear finger-like cellular thickenings called the filiform apparatus at their micropylar tip, which chemically guides the pollen tube.
  • Antipodals (Chalazal end, 3 cells): Three haploid cells that generally degenerate after fertilisation.
  • Central Cell (1 large cell, 2 nuclei): Contains two haploid polar nuclei situated below the egg apparatus.

4. Pollination Ecology, Outbreeding Devices, and Pollen-Pistil Interaction

4.1 Types of Pollination

Type Source of Pollen Genetic & Functional Nature Key Examples / Notes
Autogamy Same flower (anther to stigma of same flower) Strict self-pollination; requires synchrony & proximity Viola, Oxalis, Commelina bear both open (chasmogamous) and closed (cleistogamous) flowers. Cleistogamous flowers ensure assured seed-set without pollinators.
Geitonogamy Different flower on the same plant Functionally cross-pollination (needs agent), but genetically autogamy Common in monoecious plants like castor and maize.
Xenogamy Flower on a genetically different plant True cross-pollination; brings genetically distinct pollen Essential for genetic variation; enforced in dioecious plants like papaya.

4.2 Abiotic and Biotic Pollinating Agents

  • Wind (Anemophily): Most common abiotic mode (e.g., grasses, corn cob tassels). Flowers are inconspicuous, lack nectar/fragrance, have well-exposed stamens, light non-sticky pollen produced in enormous quantities, large feathery stigmas, and usually a single ovule per ovary.
  • Water (Hydrophily): Rare (~30 genera, mostly monocots). Examples: freshwater Vallisneria (epihydrophily—female flower reaches water surface on a long stalk; pollen floats passively) and Hydrilla; marine seagrasses like Zostera (hypohydrophily—submerged female flowers; long, ribbon-like pollen). Water-pollinated pollen has a mucilaginous covering to prevent wetting. Note: Water hyacinth and water lily are aquatic plants, yet their flowers emerge above water and are pollinated by insects or wind.
  • Animals (Zoophily): Dominant mode; bees are the primary biotic pollinators, alongside butterflies, beetles, flies, birds (sunbirds, hummingbirds), bats, and even lemurs/rodents/lizards. Insect-pollinated flowers are large, colourful, fragrant, and nectar-rich (or clustered in inflorescences when small). Fly/beetle-pollinated flowers emit foul odours.
  • Specialised Floral Rewards: Besides nectar and sticky pollen, some plants offer safe oviposition (egg-laying) sites—such as Amorphophallus (tallest flower, ~6 feet) and the obligate mutualism between Yucca and the Pronuba moth (moth lays eggs in ovary locule and pollinates the flower; larvae feed on some developing seeds). Visitors that consume rewards without contacting anthers/stigma are pollen/nectar robbers.

4.3 Outbreeding Devices and Pollen-Pistil Interaction

Continued self-pollination causes inbreeding depression. Angiosperms discourage selfing via four major outbreeding devices:

  1. Non-synchronised pollen release and stigma receptivity (Dichogamy): Either pollen sheds early or stigma becomes receptive early.
  2. Spatial separation of anther and stigma (Herkogamy): Prevents self-pollen contact within the same flower.
  3. Self-incompatibility: A genetic mechanism that inhibits self-pollen germination or pollen tube growth in the pistil (prevents both autogamy and geitonogamy).
  4. Unisexual flowers: Monoecy (male and female flowers on same plant, e.g., castor, maize) prevents autogamy but not geitonogamy; Dioecy (male and female flowers on separate plants, e.g., papaya) prevents both autogamy and geitonogamy.

Pollen-Pistil Interaction is a dynamic chemical dialogue between pollen and pistil. Compatible pollen is recognised and accepted, germinating through a germ pore to form a pollen tube that grows through the style. If shed at the 2-celled stage, the generative cell divides mitotically inside the growing pollen tube to form two male gametes. The pollen tube enters the ovule via the micropyle and penetrates one synergid guided by the filiform apparatus.

Artificial Hybridisation (Crop Improvement):
  • Emasculation: Removal of anthers from bisexual flower buds before dehiscence using forceps. (Not required if the female parent bears unisexual flowers.)
  • Bagging: Covering emasculated or unisexual female buds with butter-paper bags to prevent contamination by unwanted pollen. Once receptive, desired pollen is dusted onto the stigma and the flower is rebagged.

5. Double Fertilisation and Post-Fertilisation Transformations

5.1 Syngamy and Triple Fusion

Upon entering a degenerating synergid, the pollen tube discharges both haploid male gametes (\(n\)). Two distinct fusion events occur inside the same embryo sac—a hallmark unique to angiosperms:

\[\text{Syngamy: } \text{Male gamete }(n) + \text{Egg cell }(n) \longrightarrow \text{Zygote }(2n) \longrightarrow \text{Embryo }(2n)\] \[\text{Triple Fusion: } \text{Male gamete }(n) + 2\text{ Polar nuclei }(n + n) \longrightarrow \text{PEN }(3n) \text{ inside PEC} \longrightarrow \text{Endosperm }(3n)\]

5.2 Endosperm and Embryo Development

Endosperm development always precedes embryo division; the zygote remains briefly dormant to ensure the young embryo receives assured nutrition from the newly formed endosperm.

  • Free-Nuclear vs. Cellular Endosperm: Most commonly, the PEN divides repeatedly without cell-wall formation (free-nuclear endosperm), followed later by cellularisation. In tender coconut, the coconut water is free-nuclear endosperm (thousands of nuclei) and the surrounding white kernel is cellular endosperm.
  • Embryogeny: Early stages are similar in monocots and dicots: Zygote → Proembryo → Globular embryo → Heart-shaped embryo → Mature embryo.
Tutorial Diagram 4: Structural comparison of a dicot embryo (two cotyledons) and a monocot grass embryo (single lateral scutellum, coleoptile, and coleorrhiza).

5.3 Seeds, Fruits, Apomixis, and Polyembryony

  • Non-albuminous (Ex-albuminous) Seeds: Endosperm is completely consumed during embryo development (e.g., pea, groundnut, beans).
  • Albuminous Seeds: Retain residual endosperm (e.g., wheat, maize, barley, castor, coconut).
  • Perisperm: Persistent, residual diploid nucellus found in seeds such as black pepper and beet.
  • Seed Dormancy & Longevity: Mature seeds dehydrate to 10–15% moisture by mass, entering metabolic dormancy; the micropyle remains as a pore for oxygen and water entry during germination. Record seed viability: Arctic lupine (Lupinus arcticus) germinated after ~10,000 years, and date palm (Phoenix dactylifera) near the Dead Sea after ~2,000 years.
  • Fruit Development: The ovary wall becomes the pericarp. True fruits develop solely from the ovary (mango, guava). False fruits incorporate the thalamus (apple, strawberry, cashew). Parthenocarpic fruits develop without fertilisation and are seedless (e.g., banana; can be induced by growth hormones).
  • Apomixis: Production of seeds without fertilisation (asexual reproduction mimicking sexual reproduction), seen in some Asteraceae and grasses. In some species, a diploid egg forms without meiosis and develops into an embryo directly.
  • Polyembryony: Occurrence of more than one embryo in a single seed, common in Citrus and Mango when diploid nucellar cells protrude into the embryo sac and form embryos.
  • Agricultural Value of Apomixis: Hybrid crop seeds must normally be purchased every year because progeny from hybrid seeds segregate and lose hybrid vigour. Converting hybrids into apomicts prevents segregation, enabling farmers to reuse hybrid seeds year after year.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Determining Ploidy Levels Across Floral Tissues

Problem: Assume a diploid angiosperm species has a somatic chromosome number of \(2n = 28\). Determine the chromosome number in its: (a) tapetal cell (prior to endomitosis), (b) microspore mother cell, (c) generative cell, (d) antipodal cell, (e) primary endosperm nucleus (PEN), and (f) perisperm cell.

Solution:

  • First, find the haploid number: \(n = \frac{28}{2} = 14\).
  • (a) Tapetal cell (initial): Sporophytic tissue, diploid (\(2n\)) = 28 (note: mature tapetal cells may become polyploid/binucleate via endomitosis).
  • (b) Microspore mother cell (PMC): Diploid (\(2n\)) = 28.
  • (c) Generative cell: Part of the male gametophyte formed after meiosis + mitosis, haploid (\(n\)) = 14.
  • (d) Antipodal cell: Part of the female gametophyte, haploid (\(n\)) = 14.
  • (e) Primary endosperm nucleus (PEN): Formed by triple fusion (\(n + n + n = 3n\)) = \(3 \times 14 =\) 42.
  • (f) Perisperm cell: Persistent diploid nucellus (\(2n\)) = 28.
Example 2: Calculating Meiotic and Mitotic Divisions for Gamete Formation

Problem: Suppose a plant breeder needs 120 viable seeds from an angiosperm species that sheds pollen at the 3-celled stage. Assuming 100% fertility and no wastage, calculate the minimum number of: (a) meiotic divisions required to produce the necessary pollen grains, and (b) total mitotic divisions inside those pollen grains prior to fertilisation.

Solution:

  • Each seed requires 1 fertilised ovule, which requires 1 pollen grain (delivering 2 male gametes for double fertilisation). Thus, 120 pollen grains are needed.
  • (a) Meiotic divisions for pollen: 1 PMC undergoes 1 meiosis to yield 4 microspores (4 pollen grains). Therefore, minimum meiotic divisions = \(\frac{120}{4} =\) 30.
  • (b) Mitotic divisions per 3-celled pollen grain: Each microspore undergoes 1 asymmetric mitosis (forming vegetative + generative cell), and the generative cell undergoes 1 mitosis (forming 2 male gametes) = 2 mitotic divisions per pollen grain. For 120 pollen grains: \(120 \times 2 =\) 240 mitotic divisions.
Example 3: Predicting Ploidy in an Interspecific Cross

Problem: Assume a tetraploid female plant (\(4n = 48\)) of an angiosperm is crossed with a diploid male plant (\(2n = 24\)) of a compatible species. What will be the chromosome number of the resulting embryo and endosperm?

Solution:

  • Female gametes: Produced by meiosis of \(4n = 48\), so the egg cell and each polar nucleus carry \(2n = 24\) chromosomes.
  • Male gametes: Produced from \(2n = 24\), so each male gamete carries \(n = 12\) chromosomes.
  • Embryo (Syngamy): \(\text{Egg }(24) + \text{Male gamete }(12) =\) 36 chromosomes (\(3n\)).
  • Endosperm (Triple Fusion): \(\text{2 Polar nuclei }(24 + 24) + \text{Male gamete }(12) =\) 60 chromosomes (\(5n\)).

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following wall layers of an angiosperm microsporangium is most commonly multinucleate and directly responsible for nourishing developing microspores?

  • (A) Epidermis
  • (B) Endothecium
  • (C) Middle layers
  • (D) Tapetum
Answer: (D)

Explanation: The tapetum is the innermost wall layer of the microsporangium. Its cells have dense cytoplasm, frequently possess more than one nucleus, and nourish developing pollen grains. The outer three layers primarily provide protection and assist in anther dehiscence.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In over 60% of angiosperms, pollen grains are shed at the 2-celled stage. When does the generative cell divide to form two male gametes in these species?

  • (A) Inside the microsporangium prior to anther dehiscence
  • (B) During growth of the pollen tube through the stigma and style
  • (C) After the pollen tube discharges its contents into the central cell
  • (D) Immediately after microspore tetrad dissociation
Answer: (B)

Explanation: In species shedding 2-celled pollen (vegetative cell + generative cell), the generative cell divides mitotically to form two male gametes while the pollen tube grows through the tissues of the stigma and style.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A typical mature angiosperm embryo sac is:

  • (A) 8-celled and 7-nucleate
  • (B) 7-celled and 8-nucleate
  • (C) 8-celled and 8-nucleate
  • (D) 7-celled and 7-nucleate
Answer: (B)

Explanation: After three free-nuclear mitotic divisions of the functional megaspore, 8 nuclei are formed. Six nuclei are enclosed into separate cells (3 antipodals, 1 egg, 2 synergids), while 2 polar nuclei remain in a single large central cell, giving a 7-celled, 8-nucleate organisation.

Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: Cleistogamous flowers produce assured seed-set even in the absence of pollinators.

Statement II: Geitonogamy is genetically equivalent to xenogamy because it requires a pollinating agent.

Choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Cleistogamous flowers never open, so anthers and stigma lie close together, guaranteeing autogamy and seed-set without pollinators (Statement I is correct). Geitonogamy transfers pollen between flowers of the same plant; functionally it is cross-pollination, but genetically it is similar to autogamy, not xenogamy (Statement II is incorrect).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which pair of aquatic plants is pollinated by insects or wind rather than water?

  • (B) Zostera and Vallisneria
  • (C) Water hyacinth and water lily
  • (D) Hydrilla and Zostera
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Although water hyacinth and water lily grow in water, their flowers emerge above the water surface and are pollinated by insects or wind. Vallisneria, Hydrilla, and marine seagrasses like Zostera are true water-pollinated angiosperms.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If a flowering plant is monoecious (bearing separate male and female flowers on the same individual, such as maize), which mode(s) of pollination are prevented?

  • (A) Both autogamy and geitonogamy
  • (B) Autogamy only, not geitonogamy
  • (C) Geitonogamy only, not autogamy
  • (D) Xenogamy only
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Unisexual flowers on the same plant (monoecy) prevent autogamy (selfing within one flower), but pollen from male flowers can still reach female flowers on the same plant (geitonogamy). Dioecy (separate male and female plants, as in papaya) prevents both autogamy and geitonogamy.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a plant breeding experiment using a bisexual female parent, which sequence of steps is correct for artificial hybridisation?

  • (A) Bagging → Emasculation → Dusting desired pollen → Rebagging
  • (B) Emasculation → Bagging → Dusting desired pollen → Rebagging
  • (C) Dusting desired pollen → Emasculation → Bagging → Fruit set

Explanation: Anthers are first removed from the bisexual bud before dehiscence (emasculation), then the bud is covered (bagging) until the stigma becomes receptive. Desired pollen is then dusted onto the stigma and the flower is rebagged to prevent contamination.

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Nucellar polyembryony, in which diploid nucellar cells protrude into the embryo sac and develop into multiple embryos, is commonly observed in:

  • (A) Pea and groundnut
  • (B) Citrus and mango
  • (C) Wheat and maize
  • (D) Papaya and watermelon
Answer: (B)

Explanation: In many Citrus and mango varieties, nucellar cells surrounding the embryo sac divide and form multiple embryos inside a single seed (polyembryony). Because nucellar cells are diploid sporophytic cells, these apomictic embryos are genetically identical clones of the maternal plant.

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Traps to Avoid:
  • Perisperm vs. Pericarp: Perisperm is persistent diploid nucellus inside the seed (black pepper, beet); pericarp is the fruit wall derived from the ovary wall.
  • Coleoptile vs. Coleorrhiza: Coleoptile encloses the shoot apex/plumule; coleorrhiza encloses the radicle/root cap.
  • Albuminous vs. Non-albuminous: Pea and groundnut are non-albuminous (endosperm fully consumed); castor, wheat, maize, and barley are albuminous.
  • Apomixis vs. Parthenocarpy: Apomixis produces seeds without fertilisation; parthenocarpy produces seedless fruits without fertilisation.
  • Emasculation in Unisexual Flowers: Do not select emasculation for female unisexual flowers—only bagging is needed.
Quick Revision Checklist:
  • Ploidy of sporogenous tissue, PMC, MMC, nucellus, integuments, perisperm, zygote = \(2n\).
  • Ploidy of microspore, pollen cells, megaspore, egg, synergids, antipodals, polar nuclei = \(n\).
  • Ploidy of PEN and endosperm in standard diploid angiosperms = \(3n\).
  • Pollen cryopreservation temperature = \(-196\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in liquid nitrogen.
  • Mature seed moisture content = 10–15% by mass.

Concise Final Recap

Sexual reproduction in angiosperms integrates haploid gametophyte formation (microsporogenesis and monosporic megasporogenesis) with specialised pollination strategies and unique double fertilisation. Syngamy establishes the diploid embryo while triple fusion builds the triploid endosperm, ensuring coordinated nourishment inside the seed. Finally, mechanisms such as outbreeding devices, apomixis, and polyembryony provide both genetic adaptability and powerful tools for modern hybrid agriculture.
