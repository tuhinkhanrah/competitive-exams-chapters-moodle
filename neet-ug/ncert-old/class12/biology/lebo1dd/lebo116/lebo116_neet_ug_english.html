NEET UG Biology Ecology & Environment

Environmental Issues: Pollution, Global Change, and Conservation

A complete, exam-oriented study guide covering air and water pollution, waste management, climate forcing, stratospheric ozone depletion, and sustainable resource stewardship.

Learning Objectives and Conceptual Map

Rapid human population growth over the past century has amplified demand for food, housing, energy, transport, and manufactured goods. Meeting these demands without degrading life-support systems requires understanding how pollutants enter air, water, and soil, how they disrupt ecosystems, and how engineering, ecological, and legal interventions restore balance.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between primary pollution control devices (electrostatic precipitators, scrubbers, catalytic converters) and their target pollutants.
  • Interpret downstream changes in Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) and Dissolved Oxygen (DO) after sewage discharge, and explain eutrophication and biomagnification.
  • Classify solid, biomedical, electronic, plastic, agricultural, and radioactive wastes along with their safe disposal or recycling strategies.
  • Explain the radiative mechanism of the greenhouse effect, global warming trends, and catalytic ozone destruction by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs).
  • Analyze land degradation, deforestation drivers, and community-based forest conservation movements in India.

Core Definitions and Indian Environmental Legislation

Pollution is any undesirable alteration in the physical, chemical, or biological characteristics of air, water, or land that harms living organisms or degrades resources. Agents that trigger such undesirable changes are termed pollutants. Three landmark Indian statutes frequently tested in NEET UG form the regulatory backbone:

Legislation Year Enacted / Amended Primary Scope
Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974 Safeguarding surface and groundwater bodies from domestic sewage and industrial effluents.
Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1981 (Amended 1987) Controlling atmospheric emissions; the 1987 amendment formally classified noise as an air pollutant.
Environment (Protection) Act 1986 Umbrella legislation empowering the Government of India to protect and improve air, water, and soil quality.

Atmospheric Pollution: Particulates, Gases, Vehicles, and Noise

Air pollutants injure plants by reducing crop growth and yield and causing premature death, while impairing human and animal respiratory systems. The severity of damage depends on pollutant concentration, exposure duration, and the target organism.

Industrial Exhaust Control: Electrostatic Precipitators and Scrubbers

Thermal power plants, smelters, and heavy industries emit harmless carrier gases (nitrogen, oxygen) mixed with suspended particulate matter and acidic gases. Two distinct engineering devices remove these pollutants before stack release:

  • Electrostatic Precipitator (ESP): Removes over 99% of particulate matter from thermal power plant exhaust. Electrode wires maintained at several thousand volts generate a corona that discharges electrons. These electrons attach to dust particles, imparting a net negative charge. Grounded collecting plates attract the negatively charged particles, provided the air velocity between plates is low enough for dust to settle.
  • Scrubber: Removes gaseous pollutants such as sulphur dioxide (\(\mathrm{SO_2}\)) by passing dirty exhaust through a fine spray of water or lime, neutralizing and washing out the gas.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Industrial exhaust treatment using a wet scrubber for acidic gases and an electrostatic precipitator for suspended particulates.

Fine Particulate Hazard (PM 2.5): Even high-efficiency precipitators fail to trap ultrafine particles. According to the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), particulate matter with diameter \(2.5\,\mathrm{µm}\) or less (\(\mathrm{PM}_{2.5}\)) poses the greatest health risk because it penetrates deep into the lungs, triggering respiratory irritation, inflammation, lung damage, and premature mortality.

Vehicular Emissions, Catalytic Converters, and Fuel Norms

Automobiles are a dominant source of urban air pollution. Fitting vehicles with catalytic converters—which use expensive metals such as platinum-palladium and rhodium as catalysts—detoxifies exhaust gases:

\[ \text{Unburnt Hydrocarbons} \xrightarrow{\text{Pt-Pd / Rh}} \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} \] \[ \text{Carbon Monoxide (CO)} \xrightarrow{\text{Pt-Pd / Rh}} \mathrm{CO_2} \qquad ; \qquad \text{Nitric Oxide (NO)} \xrightarrow{\text{Pt-Pd / Rh}} \mathrm{N_2} \]

Vehicles equipped with catalytic converters must strictly run on unleaded petrol because lead poisons and inactivates the noble-metal catalysts.

Delhi Vehicular Case Study & Auto Fuel Policy: Following a Public Interest Litigation (PIL) in the Supreme Court of India, Delhi's entire public bus fleet was converted from diesel to Compressed Natural Gas (CNG) by the end of 2002. CNG burns more completely than petrol or diesel, is cheaper, and cannot be siphoned off or adulterated; its main logistical hurdle is laying distribution pipelines and ensuring uninterrupted supply. Parallel measures—phasing out old vehicles, mandating unleaded low-sulphur fuels, and enforcing catalytic converters—led to a substantial drop in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) levels in Delhi between 1997 and 2005.

Under India's Auto Fuel Policy roadmap, Euro III norms capped sulphur at \(350\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in diesel and \(150\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in petrol, with aromatic hydrocarbons limited to \(42\%\) (targeting \(50\,\mathrm{ppm}\) sulphur and \(35\%\) aromatics alongside engine upgrades). Nationwide, Bharat Stage IV norms became mandatory for 4-wheelers, 3-wheelers, and 2-wheelers from April 2017.

Noise Pollution

Noise is an undesired high-intensity sound. Brief exposure to \(\ge 150\,\mathrm{dB}\) (such as a jet plane or rocket take-off) can rupture eardrums and permanently impair hearing, while chronic urban noise causes gradual hearing loss, sleeplessness, elevated heart rate, and altered breathing patterns. Control relies on sound-absorbent materials, industrial muffling, horn-free zones near hospitals and schools, and strict enforcement of loudspeaker and firecracker limits.

Aquatic Ecosystems Under Stress: Sewage, BOD, Eutrophication, and Biomagnification

Domestic sewage consists of \(99.9\%\) water and just \(0.1\%\) impurities—yet that \(0.1\%\) renders water unfit for human consumption. These impurities fall into three fractions: suspended solids (sand, silt, clay), colloidal matter (faecal matter, bacteria, cloth and paper fibres), and dissolved materials (nitrates, ammonia, phosphates, sodium, calcium). While solids are easily settled out, dissolved nutrients, toxic metal ions, and organic compounds are far harder to remove. Untreated sewage from homes and hospitals also spreads waterborne pathogens causing dysentery, typhoid, jaundice, and cholera.

Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) and River Self-Purification

Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) estimates the amount of biodegradable organic matter in water by measuring the oxygen consumed by aerobic microorganisms decomposing that organic load. When raw sewage enters a river:

  1. Microbial decomposers multiply rapidly using organic matter as substrate, causing a sharp spike in BOD and a steep drop in Dissolved Oxygen (DO) immediately downstream.
  2. Oxygen depletion causes fish kills and the disappearance of clean-water organisms.
  3. Further downstream, as biodegradable matter is exhausted, BOD declines, atmospheric oxygen redissolves, DO recovers, and clean-water organisms reappear.

Algal Blooms, Aquatic Weeds, and Cultural Eutrophication

  • Algal Bloom: Excess nutrients (nitrates and phosphates) stimulate explosive growth of planktonic (free-floating) algae, discolouring water, depleting oxygen, and sometimes releasing toxins lethal to fish, animals, and humans.
  • Water Hyacinth (Eichhornia crassipes): Introduced for its mauve flowers, this floating weed thrives in nutrient-rich (eutrophic) waters, choking waterways and disrupting aquatic ecosystems; it is infamously known as the 'Terror of Bengal'.
  • Natural vs. Cultural Eutrophication: Natural eutrophication is the slow, centuries-long aging of a lake as silt and nutrients accumulate, transforming a cold, clear lake into a warm, shallow marsh and eventually land. Human sewage, agricultural runoff, and industrial effluents radically speed this up—a process called Cultural (Accelerated) Eutrophication.
  • Thermal Wastewater: Heated discharges from thermal power plants kill temperature-sensitive organisms; in extremely cold regions, they may boost growth of select species only after damaging indigenous flora and fauna.

Biomagnification of Heavy Metals and DDT

Industrial effluents (petroleum, paper, metal extraction, chemical plants) carry heavy metals (elements with density \(> 5\,\mathrm{g/cm^3}\) such as mercury, cadmium, copper, lead) and persistent organics. When a toxicant cannot be metabolized or excreted, it accumulates in tissues and increases in concentration at each successive trophic level—a phenomenon called biological magnification (biomagnification).

Integrated Wastewater Treatment and Ecological Sanitation

Arcata Integrated Treatment (California): In collaboration with Humboldt State University, the town of Arcata combined artificial and natural purification in two stages: (1) conventional sedimentation, filtration, and chlorination, followed by (2) routing water through six connected marshes spanning \(60\,\mathrm{hectares}\) seeded with selected plants, algae, fungi, and bacteria that neutralize, absorb, and assimilate residual heavy metals and pollutants. The citizen group Friends of the Arcata Marsh (FOAM) maintains this biodiversity-rich sanctuary.

Ecological Sanitation (EcoSan): Dry composting toilets handle human excreta without water, converting waste into hygienic, natural fertilizer and reducing chemical fertilizer demand. Functional EcoSan systems operate in parts of Kerala and Sri Lanka.

Terrestrial and Technological Wastes: Solid, Plastic, Agro-chemical, E-Waste, and Radioactivity

Waste Category Key Characteristics & Hazards Management / Remedy
Municipal Solid Waste Paper, food, plastics, glass, metals, rubber, textile. Open dumps breed rats and flies; landfills fill rapidly and leach chemicals into groundwater. Segregate into biodegradable (deep pit composting), recyclable (recovered by rag-pickers/kabadiwallahs), and non-biodegradable; sanitary landfills (compacted and covered daily with dirt).
Hospital Waste Contains disinfectants, hazardous chemicals, and pathogenic microorganisms. Incineration is essential for safe disposal.
Plastic Waste (Polyblend) Non-biodegradable multi-layer packaging persists in soil and water. Ahmed Khan (Bangalore) developed Polyblend (fine powder of recycled modified plastic) mixed with bitumen, improving water repellency and tripling road life.
Electronic Waste (E-waste) Irreparable computers and electronics. Over half from developed nations is exported to India, China, and Pakistan, where manual recovery of Cu, Fe, Si, Ni, and Au exposes workers to toxins. Environment-friendly, mechanized recycling is the only sustainable solution.
Agro-chemicals Inorganic fertilizers, pesticides, herbicides, fungicides harm non-target soil organisms and drive aquatic eutrophication. Integrated Organic Farming (e.g., Ramesh Chandra Dagar, Sonipat, Haryana; Haryana Kisan Welfare Club): zero-waste cycle linking dairy, composting, water harvesting, bee-keeping, and biogas.
Radioactive Waste Accidental leakage (Three Mile Island, Chernobyl) and radiation cause high mutation rates; lethal at high doses, cancer and disorders at low doses. Pre-treat and seal in shielded containers buried ~\(500\,\mathrm{m}\) deep in rocks (faces strong public opposition).

Global Atmospheric Perturbations: Greenhouse Effect and Ozone Depletion

The Greenhouse Effect and Global Warming

The greenhouse effect is a natural phenomenon that warms Earth's surface and lower atmosphere. Without it, Earth's average surface temperature would be a frigid \(-18\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) instead of the present \(15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Clouds and atmospheric gases reflect roughly one-fourth of incoming solar radiation and absorb some, while almost half reaches and warms Earth's surface. The warmed surface re-emits energy as long-wave infrared (IR) radiation, which greenhouse gases absorb and re-radiate back toward Earth in a continuous cycle.

Relative Contribution of Greenhouse Gases to Total Global Warming (NEET High-Yield)

Carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)): \(60\%\)  |  Methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)): \(20\%\)  |  Chlorofluorocarbons (\(\mathrm{CFCs}\)): \(14\%\)  |  Nitrous oxide (\(\mathrm{N_2O}\)): \(6\%\)

Over the past century, Earth's mean temperature rose by \(0.6\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (mostly in the last three decades), triggering odd climatic patterns such as the El Niño effect, accelerating melting of polar ice caps and Himalayan snow caps, and threatening coastal submergence via sea-level rise. Mitigation requires cutting fossil fuel use, improving energy efficiency, curbing deforestation, planting trees, and slowing human population growth.

Stratospheric Ozone Depletion

While tropospheric ozone is a harmful pollutant ("bad" ozone), stratospheric ozone ("good" ozone) forms a protective shield absorbing damaging solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Column ozone thickness is measured in Dobson Units (DU). Normally, UV-driven ozone formation and degradation remain in dynamic equilibrium.

CFC-Catalysed Destruction: Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) used as refrigerants drift upward into the stratosphere, where UV rays cleave off chlorine (\(\mathrm{Cl}\)) atoms. Each \(\mathrm{Cl}\) atom acts strictly as a catalyst—converting ozone (\(\mathrm{O_3}\)) into molecular oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)) without being consumed—causing persistent, continuing depletion. Seasonal thinning is most severe over Antarctica between late August and early October, forming the ozone hole.

Biological Impacts of UV-B & International Action: DNA and proteins absorb UV-B radiation, breaking chemical bonds and causing mutations, premature skin aging, skin cell damage, and skin cancers. In the human eye, the cornea absorbs UV-B; high exposure causes corneal inflammation known as snow-blindness, cataracts, and permanent corneal injury. To phase out ozone-depleting substances, the Montreal Protocol was signed in 1987 (effective 1989).

Land Degradation, Deforestation, and Community Conservation

  • Soil Erosion and Desertification: Topsoil takes centuries to form but is stripped rapidly by over-cultivation, unrestricted grazing, deforestation, and poor irrigation, creating arid patches that merge into deserts.
  • Waterlogging and Soil Salinity: Irrigation without adequate drainage causes waterlogging, drawing dissolved salts to the soil surface and plant roots, severely damaging crop productivity (a major post-Green Revolution problem).
  • Deforestation Trends: Roughly \(40\%\) of tropical forests have been lost compared to only \(1\%\) in temperate regions. In India, forest cover fell from ~\(30\%\) at the start of the 20th century to \(21.54\%\) by its end, whereas the National Forest Policy (1988) recommends \(33\%\) forest cover for plains and \(67\%\) for hills.
  • Jhum Cultivation (Slash-and-Burn): Practised in north-eastern India, where forests are cut and burned for ash fertilizer. Shortened recovery intervals due to population pressure now drive permanent deforestation, elevated atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), biodiversity loss, disturbed hydrological cycles, and soil erosion.
  • Reforestation: Restoring previously cleared forests naturally or by planting native biodiversity.

People's Participation in Forest Conservation

  • Amrita Devi Bishnoi (1731, Jodhpur, Rajasthan): Sacrificed her life alongside her three daughters and hundreds of Bishnois hugging trees to stop royal woodcutters; honoured today by the Amrita Devi Bishnoi Wildlife Protection Award.
  • Chipko Movement (1974, Garhwal Himalayas): Local women hugged trees to protect them from commercial contractors.
  • Joint Forest Management (JFM, 1980s): Government-community partnership where local communities protect forests in exchange for sustainable forest products (fruits, gum, rubber, medicine).

Worked Examples

1. Calculating Biomagnification Across Trophic Levels

Problem: Suppose an aquatic ecosystem (assumed hypothetical scenario) has a persistent pesticide concentration of \(0.005\,\mathrm{ppb}\) in water. If the concentration increases by a factor of \(10^4\) from water to zooplankton, and then increases \(15\)-fold in small fish and another \(4\)-fold in fish-eating birds, calculate the final concentration in the birds in \(\mathrm{ppm}\).

Solution:

First convert the water concentration from \(\mathrm{ppb}\) to \(\mathrm{ppm}\) using \(1\,\mathrm{ppm} = 1000\,\mathrm{ppb}\):

\[ C_{\text{water}} = \frac{0.005}{1000}\,\mathrm{ppm} = 5 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{ppm} \]

Now apply the trophic magnification factors sequentially:

\[ C_{\text{zooplankton}} = (5 \times 10^{-6}) \times 10^4 = 0.05\,\mathrm{ppm} \] \[ C_{\text{small fish}} = 0.05 \times 15 = 0.75\,\mathrm{ppm} \] \[ C_{\text{birds}} = 0.75 \times 4 = 3.0\,\mathrm{ppm} \]

Answer: The pesticide reaches \(3.0\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in fish-eating birds, representing a \(6 \times 10^5\)-fold magnification over water.

2. Selecting the Correct Industrial Air Pollution Control Device

Problem: A coal-fired thermal power plant emits exhaust containing \(1200\,\mathrm{mg/m^3}\) of fly-ash particulates and \(400\,\mathrm{ppm}\) of \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) (assumed values). Which sequence of control units should be installed, and what operational condition is critical for particulate capture?

Solution:

  • Particulate removal: Install an electrostatic precipitator (ESP), which removes over \(99\%\) of particulate matter by charging particles via a high-voltage electron corona and attracting them to grounded plates.
  • Critical condition: The velocity of exhaust air between the collecting plates must be kept sufficiently low so that captured dust falls down rather than being swept back into the airstream.
  • Gaseous removal: Pass the exhaust through a wet scrubber using a water or lime spray to absorb and neutralize \(\mathrm{SO_2}\).

3. Interpreting Downstream River BOD and DO Profiles

Problem: Water samples are collected from a river at three points (assumed scenario): Point X (upstream of a municipal outfall), Point Y (\(200\,\mathrm{m}\) downstream of the outfall), and Point Z (\(15\,\mathrm{km}\) downstream). Rank the three points in increasing order of Dissolved Oxygen (DO) and explain the biological reason.

Solution:

  • At Point X (unpolluted upstream), organic load is minimal, so BOD is low and DO is high.
  • At Point Y (just downstream of discharge), aerobic microbes rapidly decompose biodegradable sewage, driving BOD to a peak and depleting DO to its lowest level.
  • At Point Z (far downstream), most biodegradable matter has been oxidized, BOD drops, and atmospheric re-aeration restores DO.

Answer: Increasing order of Dissolved Oxygen: \(\text{Point Y} < \text{Point Z} < \text{Point X}\).

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Why must motor vehicles fitted with catalytic converters strictly use unleaded petrol?

  • (A) Lead increases the viscosity of petrol and clogs fuel injectors
  • (B) Lead inactivates the platinum-palladium and rhodium catalysts
  • (C) Lead reacts with carbon dioxide to form explosive peroxides
  • (D) Unleaded petrol eliminates the formation of nitrogen gas in exhaust
Correct Answer: (B)
Catalytic converters contain expensive noble-metal catalysts (platinum-palladium and rhodium) that convert unburnt hydrocarbons to \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{CO}\) to \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), and \(\mathrm{NO}\) to \(\mathrm{N_2}\). Lead in leaded petrol poisons and inactivates these catalysts.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements regarding particulate matter size and health is correct according to the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB)?

  • (A) Particles larger than \(10\,\mathrm{µm}\) cause the greatest deep-lung inflammation
  • (B) Electrostatic precipitators remove \(100\%\) of \(\mathrm{PM}_{2.5}\) particles
  • (C) Particulate matter of size \(2.5\,\mathrm{µm}\) or less in diameter causes the greatest harm to human health

Correct Answer: (C)
Fine particulates of size \(2.5\,\mathrm{µm}\) or less (\(\mathrm{PM}_{2.5}\)) escape conventional precipitators, are inhaled deep into the lungs, and cause respiratory symptoms, inflammation, lung damage, and premature deaths.

Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: Without the natural greenhouse effect, the average surface temperature of Earth would be about \(-18\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) rather than \(15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
Statement II: Among greenhouse gases, methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)) contributes \(60\%\) and carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) contributes \(20\%\) to total global warming.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct
Correct Answer: (C)
Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because the relative contributions are reversed: \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) accounts for \(60\%\) of total global warming, whereas \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) accounts for \(20\%\) (with CFCs at \(14\%\) and \(\mathrm{N_2O}\) at \(6\%\)).
Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): High concentrations of DDT in aquatic food chains cause a decline in populations of fish-eating birds.
Reason (R): DDT undergoes biomagnification and disturbs calcium metabolism in birds, leading to thinning and premature breaking of eggshells.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true
Correct Answer: (A)
DDT cannot be metabolized or excreted, so its concentration magnifies from \(0.003\,\mathrm{ppb}\) in water up to \(25\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in fish-eating birds. This high burden disrupts calcium metabolism, thinning eggshells so they break prematurely and reducing bird populations.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the stratosphere, how do chlorine atoms released from CFCs cause continuous depletion of the ozone layer?

  • (A) Chlorine atoms permanently combine with ozone to form solid \(\mathrm{ClO_3}\)
  • (B) Chlorine atoms act as catalysts, degrading \(\mathrm{O_3}\) to \(\mathrm{O_2}\) without being consumed
  • (C) Chlorine atoms block solar UV rays from reaching molecular oxygen

Correct Answer: (B)

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Tropospheric vs. Stratospheric Ozone: Do not confuse "bad" ozone in the troposphere (a pollutant harmful to plants and animals) with "good" ozone in the stratosphere (which shields Earth from UV-B).
  • Greenhouse Gas Percentages: \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is \(60\%\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) is \(20\%\), followed by CFCs (\(14\%\)) and \(\mathrm{N_2O}\) (\(6\%\)). Students often swap CFCs and \(\mathrm{N_2O}\) or \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\).
  • BOD vs. DO Direction: High BOD means low Dissolved Oxygen, not high DO. Microbial decomposition consumes \(\mathrm{O_2}\) immediately downstream of sewage discharge.
  • Biomagnification Units: Pay attention to \(\mathrm{ppb}\) vs. \(\mathrm{ppm}\) in DDT chains (\(0.003\,\mathrm{ppb}\) in water \(\rightarrow 25\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in birds; \(1\,\mathrm{ppm} = 1000\,\mathrm{ppb}\)).
  • Hospital Waste vs. Municipal Waste: Hospital waste contains pathogens and hazardous chemicals and requires incineration, not open dumping or ordinary landfilling.
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Electrostatic precipitator (>\(99\%\) particulates) vs. Scrubber (\(\mathrm{SO_2}\)) vs. Catalytic converter (Pt-Pd, Rh; needs unleaded petrol).
  • \(\mathrm{PM}_{2.5}\): deepest lung penetration; \(\ge 150\,\mathrm{dB}\): permanent eardrum damage.
  • Sewage impurities: \(0.1\%\); Arcata marsh: 6 marshes, \(60\,\mathrm{hectares}\), FOAM; EcoSan: dry composting.
  • Nuclear waste: buried ~\(500\,\mathrm{m}\) deep in rocks; UV-B eye damage: snow-blindness & cataract; Montreal Protocol: 1987 (effective 1989).
  • Forest conservation: Amrita Devi Bishnoi (1731), Chipko (1974), JFM (1980s), National Forest Policy (1988: \(33\%\) plains, \(67\%\) hills).

Concise Final Recap

Environmental management links engineering controls, ecological restoration, and community stewardship. Air quality depends on trapping particulates (ESP), scrubbing acidic gases, and using clean fuels with catalytic converters. Water quality hinges on curbing biodegradable organic loads (BOD) and nutrient runoff (eutrophication) while preventing persistent toxicants (heavy metals, DDT) from biomagnifying. Globally, limiting greenhouse gas emissions (\(\mathrm{CO_2}, \mathrm{CH_4}, \mathrm{CFCs}, \mathrm{N_2O}\)) and phasing out ozone-depleting CFCs protect climate stability and DNA integrity, while sustainable agriculture, recycling, and participatory forestry safeguard terrestrial ecosystems.
