Biodiversity and Conservation

Comprehensive concept guide, quantitative relationships, conservation frameworks, worked examples, and NEET UG practice questions.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Biodiversity encompasses the vast variety of living organisms across every tier of biological organization—from cellular macromolecules to entire biomes. For NEET UG, this topic tests both conceptual understanding (ecological hypotheses, extinction drivers, conservation logic) and precise factual recall (global and Indian species counts, regression slopes, protected-area statistics, and international summits).

After completing this tutorial, you should be able to:
  • Distinguish genetic, species, and ecological diversity with standard biological examples.
  • Interpret global and Indian taxonomic proportions, including Robert May's global estimate and India's status as a megadiversity nation.
  • Explain latitudinal biodiversity gradients and apply Alexander von Humboldt's species-area equation on both linear and logarithmic scales.
  • Connect species richness to ecosystem stability using David Tilman's experiments and Paul Ehrlich's rivet popper hypothesis.
  • Analyze the four major drivers of biodiversity loss ("The Evil Quartet") and evaluate in situ versus ex situ conservation strategies.

Levels and Magnitude of Biodiversity

The term biodiversity was popularized by sociobiologist Edward Wilson to represent the combined heterogeneity across all levels of biological organization. Three hierarchical levels are central to ecology and conservation:

Level of Diversity Core Meaning Key Examples
Genetic Diversity Variation in genetic makeup within a single species across its geographic range. Rauwolfia vomitoria across Himalayan ranges shows variation in potency and concentration of the active alkaloid reserpine; India has >50,000 genetically distinct rice strains and ~1,000 mango varieties.
Species Diversity Variety and relative abundance of different species within a region. The Western Ghats harbour a much greater diversity of amphibian species than the Eastern Ghats.
Ecological Diversity Variety of ecosystems, habitats, and ecological processes within a geographical area. India (with deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries, and alpine meadows) exhibits far greater ecosystem diversity than a Scandinavian country such as Norway.

Global and Indian Species Richness

According to the IUCN (2004) inventory, slightly more than 1.5 million plant and animal species have been formally described. Because taxonomic surveys are far more complete in temperate zones than in tropical regions, biologists estimate total global diversity by comparing the temperate-to-tropical richness ratio of well-studied insect groups and extrapolating it to other taxa. While extreme estimates span 20 to 50 million, Robert May proposed a conservative and widely accepted estimate of approximately 7 million species worldwide.

  • Animals vs. Plants: Over 70% of all recorded species are animals, whereas plants (including algae, fungi, bryophytes, gymnosperms, and angiosperms in traditional inventory groupings) account for no more than 22%.
  • Dominance of Insects: Among animals, insects form the richest group, constituting over 70% of all animal species (roughly 7 out of every 10 known animals).
  • Fungi vs. Vertebrates: The global number of fungal species exceeds the combined total of fishes, amphibians, reptiles, and mammals.
  • Exclusion of Prokaryotes: Conventional species counts exclude prokaryotes because standard morphological taxonomy is unsuitable for microbes and many prokaryotes cannot be cultured in the laboratory. Using biochemical or molecular criteria, prokaryotic diversity alone could run into millions.
  • India's Megadiversity Profile: Occupying only 2.4% of the world's land area, India contributes 8.1% of global species diversity, making it one of the 12 megadiversity countries. Roughly 45,000 plant species and nearly twice as many (~90,000) animal species have been recorded in India. Applying May's proportion (only ~22% of total species recorded globally), India may still hold over 1,00,000 plant species and over 3,00,000 animal species awaiting discovery.

Spatial Patterns of Biodiversity

1. Latitudinal Gradients

Species diversity is unevenly distributed across Earth and generally decreases as we move away from the equator toward the poles. With rare exceptions, the tropics (23.5° N to 23.5° S) support far more species than temperate or polar regions.

Region / Latitude Observed Bird Species Comparative Significance
Colombia (near equator) ~1,400 Peak tropical avian richness
India (largely tropical) >1,200 High tropical-subtropical richness
New York (41° N) 105 Marked drop in temperate zone
Greenland (71° N) 56 Low polar species richness

Similarly, a tropical forest in Ecuador contains up to 10 times more vascular plant species than an equal-area temperate forest in the Midwest USA. The Amazonian rain forest in South America holds Earth's highest biodiversity: over 40,000 plant species, 3,000 fishes, 1,300 birds, 427 mammals, 427 amphibians, 378 reptiles, and more than 1,25,000 invertebrates, with at least 2 million insect species estimated still undiscovered.

Three Hypotheses Explaining Tropical Richness:
  1. Evolutionary Time: Speciation is a function of time. Unlike temperate regions disrupted by repeated glaciations, tropical latitudes remained relatively undisturbed for millions of years, allowing prolonged species diversification.
  2. Environmental Stability: Tropical climates are less seasonal, more constant, and more predictable, promoting niche specialization and coexistence of more species.
  3. Solar Energy Input: Higher solar radiation in the tropics drives greater primary productivity, which indirectly supports higher biodiversity.

2. Species–Area Relationship

During explorations in South American jungles, German naturalist and geographer Alexander von Humboldt observed that within a region, species richness increases with explored area, but only up to a limit. Across diverse taxa (angiosperms, birds, bats, freshwater fishes), the curve is a rectangular hyperbola:

\[ S = C A^Z \]

On a logarithmic (log–log) scale, this becomes a straight line:

\[ \log S = \log C + Z \log A \]

Here, \(S\) = species richness, \(A\) = area, \(Z\) = slope of the line (regression coefficient), and \(C\) = Y-intercept.

  • Regional scale: Ecologists find \(Z\) consistently lies between 0.1 and 0.2 across different taxonomic groups and regions (e.g., plants in Britain, birds in California, molluscs in New York).
  • Continental scale: Across very large areas such as entire continents, the slope becomes much steeper, with \(Z\) ranging from 0.6 to 1.2. For frugivorous (fruit-eating) birds and mammals in tropical forests across different continents, \(Z = 1.15\). A steeper slope indicates that species richness rises much more sharply with increasing area at continental scales due to habitat heterogeneity and biogeographic isolation.

Ecosystem Stability and Biodiversity Loss

Importance of Species Diversity to Ecosystems

A stable biological community exhibits three key traits: (1) minimal year-to-year variation in productivity, (2) resistance or resilience to occasional natural or anthropogenic disturbances, and (3) resistance to invasions by alien species. Through long-term outdoor plot experiments, David Tilman demonstrated that plots with higher species diversity showed lower year-to-year variation in total biomass and achieved higher overall productivity.

Paul Ehrlich's Rivet Popper Hypothesis:

Stanford ecologist Paul Ehrlich compared an ecosystem to an airplane and its species to the rivets holding it together. If passengers remove a few rivets (causing minor extinctions), flight safety (ecosystem functioning) may not fail immediately. However, continued rivet removal dangerously weakens the structure. Crucially, losing rivets on the wings (key species driving major ecosystem functions) poses a far more immediate threat than losing rivets on seats or windows.

Patterns and Drivers of Biodiversity Loss

Earth has experienced five mass extinctions over >3 billion years of life. The ongoing Sixth Extinction differs primarily in speed: current extinction rates are 100 to 1,000 times faster than pre-human background rates, driven by human activity. Key quantitative facts:

  • Historical Extinctions: Human colonization of tropical Pacific Islands led to the extinction of >2,000 native bird species. The IUCN Red List (2004) documents 784 extinctions in the last 500 years (338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates, 87 plants), with 27 species lost in the last 20 years alone.
  • Notable Recent Extinctions: Dodo (Mauritius), Quagga (Africa), Thylacine (Australia), Steller's Sea Cow (Russia), and three tiger subspecies (Bali, Javan, Caspian).
  • Current Extinction Threat: Over 15,500 species worldwide are threatened with extinction, including 12% of birds, 23% of mammals, 32% of amphibians (the most vulnerable vertebrate group), and 31% of gymnosperms.
  • Ecological Consequences: Biodiversity loss reduces plant production, lowers resistance to environmental perturbations such as drought, and increases variability in productivity, water use, and pest/disease cycles.

The Evil Quartet: Four Major Causes of Extinction

Cause Mechanism Standard Examples
1. Habitat Loss & Fragmentation Most important driver. Large habitats are cleared or broken into small fragments, harming migratory species and animals needing large territories. Tropical rain forests declined from >14% of Earth's land to ≤6%; Amazon rain forest ("lungs of the planet") cleared for soya bean cultivation and beef cattle pastures.
2. Over-exploitation Unsustainable harvesting when human need turns into greed. Extinction of Steller's sea cow and passenger pigeon; overharvesting of commercially valuable marine fishes.
3. Alien Species Invasions Introduced exotic species become invasive and outcompete or prey upon native species. Nile perch in Lake Victoria eliminated >200 native cichlid fish species; invasive weeds Parthenium (carrot grass), Lantana, and Eichhornia (water hyacinth); illegal introduction of African catfish Clarias gariepinus threatening native riverine catfishes.
4. Co-extinctions Obligatory ecological partners perish together when one disappears. Extinction of a host fish eliminates its unique parasite assemblage; loss of one partner in a coevolved plant–pollinator mutualism dooms the other.

Biodiversity Conservation: Rationale and Strategies

Why Conserve Biodiversity?

  • Narrowly Utilitarian: Direct economic benefits—food, firewood, fibre, construction materials, industrial products (tannins, lubricants, dyes, resins, perfumes), and medicines (>25% of global market drugs come from plants; 25,000 plant species used in traditional medicine). Exploring biodiversity for commercially valuable molecules and genes is termed bioprospecting.
  • Broadly Utilitarian: Essential ecosystem services—oxygen production (the Amazon forest generates ~20% of Earth's atmospheric O₂ via photosynthesis), pollination by bees, bumblebees, birds, and bats, flood/soil-erosion control, and intangible aesthetic pleasures.
  • Ethical: Every species possesses intrinsic value regardless of economic utility; humans bear a moral duty to preserve Earth's biological heritage for future generations.

In Situ vs. Ex Situ Conservation

Protecting an entire ecosystem to safeguard a species ("saving the entire forest to save the tiger") is in situ (on-site) conservation. When a species is critically endangered and requires urgent removal from the wild for survival, ex situ (off-site) conservation is used.

Feature In Situ Conservation Ex Situ Conservation
Core Approach Protects organisms within their natural habitat and ecosystem. Removes threatened organisms from the wild into specialized human-managed facilities.
Global Hotspots Regions with high species richness, high endemism (species confined to that region), and accelerated habitat loss. Total 34 hotspots globally (25 initial + 9 added); cover <2% of land area yet protecting them could reduce mass extinction by ~30%. Not applicable to whole-ecosystem hotspots; focuses on individual threatened taxa.
Indian Examples 3 hotspots in India: Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, and Himalaya. Protected network: 14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks, 448 wildlife sanctuaries, plus sacred groves (Khasi & Jaintia Hills in Meghalaya; Aravalli Hills in Rajasthan; Western Ghats of Karnataka & Maharashtra; Sarguja, Chanda & Bastar in Madhya Pradesh). Zoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks, cryopreservation of gametes, in vitro fertilization, plant tissue culture, and seed banks.

International Conservation Summits

  • The Earth Summit (Rio de Janeiro, 1992): The historic Convention on Biological Diversity urged all nations to adopt measures for biodiversity conservation and sustainable utilization of its benefits.
  • World Summit on Sustainable Development (Johannesburg, 2002): 190 countries pledged to achieve a significant reduction in the rate of biodiversity loss at global, regional, and local levels by 2010.

Worked Examples

Estimating Undiscovered Species Using Proportional Inventory

Problem: Suppose an ecological survey of a hypothetical tropical island records 11,000 beetle species. Assuming this represents the same 22% discovery proportion proposed by Robert May's global estimate, calculate the estimated total number of beetle species on the island and how many remain undiscovered.

Solution:

Given (assumed values): Recorded species = \(11{,}000\); discovery fraction = \(22\% = 0.22\).

\[ \text{Total estimated species} = \frac{11{,}000}{0.22} = 50{,}000 \] \[ \text{Undiscovered species} = 50{,}000 - 11{,}000 = 39{,}000 \]

Thus, approximately 39,000 beetle species remain to be discovered and described on the island.

Calculating Species Richness Using the Species–Area Equation

Problem: In a hypothetical continental survey of frugivorous birds across tropical forests, assume the Y-intercept parameter is \(C = 5\) and the regression slope is \(Z = 1.15\). Calculate the expected species richness \(S\) for a forest area of \(A = 100\,\mathrm{km^2}\).

Solution:

Using Alexander von Humboldt's equation \(S = C A^Z\) (or \(\log_{10} S = \log_{10} C + Z \log_{10} A\)):

\[ \log_{10} S = \log_{10}(5) + 1.15 \times \log_{10}(100) \]

Since \(\log_{10}(100) = 2\) and \(\log_{10}(5) \approx 0.699\):

\[ \log_{10} S = 0.699 + 1.15(2) = 0.699 + 2.30 = 2.999 \approx 3.0 \] \[ S = 10^{3.0} = 1{,}000\text{ species} \]

Notice how a continental slope (\(Z = 1.15\)) produces a rapid rise in species richness with area.

Diagnosing Extinction Drivers and Selecting Conservation Strategies

Problem: In a hypothetical freshwater lake, an introduced predatory fish eliminates 40 native snail species, and simultaneously a critically endangered endemic frog survives only as 18 individuals in a polluted marsh. Identify the extinction driver in the lake and recommend the appropriate conservation strategy for the frog.

Solution:

  • Lake extinction driver: Alien species invasion (analogous to the Nile perch eliminating native cichlids in Lake Victoria).
  • Conservation strategy for the frog: Because the wild population is critically small (18 individuals) and its habitat is polluted, ex situ conservation (captive breeding in a zoological park, in vitro fertilization, and gamete cryopreservation) is urgently required alongside habitat restoration.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Variations in the potency and concentration of the active chemical reserpine in Rauwolfia vomitoria growing across different Himalayan ranges represent:

  1. (A) Species diversity
  2. (B) Genetic diversity
  3. (C) Ecological diversity
  4. (D) Community diversity
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Rauwolfia vomitoria is a single species; variation in reserpine concentration and potency across populations in different Himalayan ranges reflects diversity at the genetic level.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements accurately reflects global taxonomic inventories?

  1. (A) Plants constitute more than 50% of all recorded species.
  2. (B) Insects account for roughly 70% of all recorded species, including plants and fungi.
  3. (C) The number of fungal species exceeds the combined total of fishes, amphibians, reptiles, and mammals.
  4. (D) Prokaryotes are fully catalogued in Robert May's 7 million estimate.
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Plants comprise ≤22% of total recorded species. Insects make up >70% of animals (not all species). Prokaryotes are excluded from conventional estimates. Fungi indeed outnumber all vertebrate classes combined.
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

With only 2.4% of the world's land area, India's share of global species diversity is approximately:

  1. (A) 2.4%
  2. (B) 5.2%
  3. (C) 8.1%
  4. (D) 12.0%
Answer: (C)
Explanation: India accounts for 2.4% of global land area but holds 8.1% of global species diversity, placing it among the 12 megadiversity nations.
Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: On a logarithmic scale, the species–area relationship is a straight line described by \(\log S = \log C + Z \log A\).
Statement II: When analyzing species–area relationships across entire continents, the regression slope \(Z\) typically falls between 0.1 and 0.2.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because \(Z = 0.1\text{–}0.2\) applies to small regional areas; across entire continents, the slope is much steeper (\(Z = 0.6\text{–}1.2\)).
Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In Paul Ehrlich's rivet popper hypothesis, the "rivets on the wings" of the airplane symbolize:

  1. (A) Invasive alien species
  2. (B) Key species that drive major ecosystem functions
  3. (C) Extinct species in zoological parks
  4. (D) Endemic plants in botanical gardens
Answer: (B)
Explanation: In the rivet popper analogy, the airplane represents the ecosystem and rivets represent species. Rivets on the wings correspond to critical/keystone species whose loss severely threatens ecosystem stability.

Among vertebrate groups, which taxon currently faces the highest percentage of species under threat of extinction worldwide?

  1. (A) Birds (12%)
  2. (B) Mammals (23%)
  3. (C) Amphibians (32%)
  4. (D) Fishes (15%)
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Extinction records show 32% of all amphibian species face extinction risk, higher than mammals (23%) and birds (12%).

Practice Question 9 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): Strict protection of the world's 34 biodiversity hotspots could reduce ongoing mass extinctions by nearly 30%.
Reason (R): Biodiversity hotspots cover less than 2% of Earth's land area yet harbour exceptionally high species richness and endemism.

  1. (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  2. (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  3. (C) A is true, but R is false.
  4. (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)
Explanation: Because hotspots concentrate extraordinarily high numbers of endemic and threatened species within <2% of Earth's land surface, prioritizing them for strict in situ protection yields an outsized reduction (~30%) in mass extinction.
Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following sets consists exclusively of ex situ conservation methods?

  1. (A) Biosphere reserves, sacred groves, and seed banks
  2. (B) National parks, botanical gardens, and wildlife sanctuaries
  3. (C) Cryopreservation of gametes, botanical gardens, and tissue culture
  4. (D) Sacred groves, zoological parks, and biodiversity hotspots
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Cryopreservation of gametes, botanical gardens, and tissue culture are all off-site (ex situ) methods. Biosphere reserves, national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, sacred groves, and hotspots are in situ strategies.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Pitfalls to Avoid:
  • 70% Insects Trap: Insects make up >70% of all animals, not >70% of all living species.
  • Slope \(Z\) Confusion: Always distinguish regional \(Z\) (0.1–0.2) from continental \(Z\) (0.6–1.2) and tropical frugivores (\(Z = 1.15\)).
  • Hotspot Numbers: Remember 25 initial + 9 added = 34 total hotspots worldwide, with 3 covering India (Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, Himalaya).
  • In Situ vs. Ex Situ Classification: Sacred groves and biosphere reserves are in situ; zoological parks, botanical gardens, seed banks, and cryopreservation are ex situ.
Rapid Revision Checklist:
  • Edward Wilson (term biodiversity); Robert May (~7 million global species); IUCN 2004 (>1.5 million described, 784 extinct in 500 years).
  • India: 2.4% land, 8.1% biodiversity, 12 megadiversity countries, 14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks, 448 wildlife sanctuaries.
  • David Tilman (diversity–stability plots); Paul Ehrlich (rivet popper); Alexander von Humboldt (\(S = CA^Z\)).
  • Earth Summit: Rio de Janeiro (1992); World Summit on Sustainable Development: Johannesburg (2002, 190 countries).

Concise Final Recap

Biodiversity spans genetic, species, and ecological levels and peaks in the stable, energy-rich tropics. Species richness scales predictably with area (\(S = CA^Z\)) and underpins ecosystem productivity and resilience. Because human-driven habitat loss, over-exploitation, alien invasions, and co-extinctions have accelerated extinction rates 100–1,000 times above background levels, conserving biodiversity through both in situ networks (hotspots, biosphere reserves, national parks, sanctuaries, sacred groves) and ex situ technologies (cryopreservation, seed banks, zoos, botanical gardens) is vital for ecological stability and human survival.
