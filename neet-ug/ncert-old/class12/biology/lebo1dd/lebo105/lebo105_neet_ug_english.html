Principles of Inheritance and Variation

Conceptual Foundations, Quantitative Genetics, Chromosomal Mechanics, and Human Genetic Disorders

Learning Objectives and Overview

Genetics examines two complementary phenomena: inheritance (the faithful transmission of characters from parents to progeny) and variation (the degree to which offspring differ from their parents and from one another). Long before the physical nature of genetic material was known, selective breeding—such as the domestication of wild cattle to produce breeds like Sahiwal cows in Punjab—demonstrated that sexual reproduction generates heritable diversity.

Mendelian Genetics and Single-Gene Inheritance

Between 1856 and 1863, Gregor Mendel conducted hybridisation experiments on the garden pea (Pisum sativum). His success rested on three methodological strengths: applying mathematical logic and statistical analysis to biology, using large sample sizes for credibility, and verifying inferences across successive generations using true-breeding lines (plants showing stable trait expression after continuous self-pollination).

Key Genetic Terminology

  • Gene (Factor): The discrete, stable unit of inheritance carrying information to express a trait.
  • Alleles: Slightly different forms of the same gene coding for contrasting traits (for example, T for tall stem and t for dwarf stem).
  • Genotype vs. Phenotype: Genotype is the allelic constitution of an individual (TT, Tt, or tt), whereas phenotype is its observable physical expression (tall or dwarf).
  • Homozygous vs. Heterozygous: Identical alleles at a locus form a homozygote (TT or tt); dissimilar alleles form a heterozygote (Tt).

Monohybrid Cross, Law of Dominance, and Law of Segregation

When a true-breeding tall pea plant (TT) is crossed with a true-breeding dwarf plant (tt) by emasculation (removing anthers before dehiscence) and controlled pollination, all first filial (F1) progeny are heterozygous (Tt) and phenotypically tall. Self-pollinating the F1 generation yields F2 progeny in a phenotypic ratio of 3 tall : 1 dwarf and a genotypic ratio of 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, with no intermediate heights (no blending).

\[ \left(\frac{1}{2}T + \frac{1}{2}t\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}TT + \frac{1}{2}Tt + \frac{1}{4}tt \]
  • Law of Dominance: Characters are controlled by discrete paired factors; in a dissimilar pair, the dominant allele masks the expression of the recessive allele in the F1 heterozygote.
  • Law of Segregation: Alleles do not blend. During meiosis (gamete formation), the two alleles of a pair separate randomly so each gamete receives only one allele with a 50% probability.

The Test Cross

Because TT and Tt plants look identical, Mendel devised the test cross: crossing an individual showing a dominant phenotype (unknown genotype) with a homozygous recessive parent (for example, tt). If all offspring show the dominant trait, the tested parent is homozygous dominant; if dominant and recessive phenotypes appear in a 1 : 1 ratio, the tested parent is heterozygous.

Deviations from Simple Dominance and Gene Interactions

Incomplete Dominance

In snapdragon (Antirrhinum sp.), crossing true-breeding red-flowered plants (RR) with white-flowered plants (rr) yields pink F1 hybrids (Rr). Self-pollinating F1 gives an F2 ratio of 1 Red (RR) : 2 Pink (Rr) : 1 White (rr). Both phenotypic and genotypic ratios are identical (1:2:1) because neither allele completely masks the other.

Molecular Basis of Dominance: A normal allele typically encodes a functional enzyme that converts substrate \(S\) into product. A modified allele may produce (i) an equally efficient enzyme (equivalent to normal), (ii) a non-functional enzyme, or (iii) no enzyme at all. In cases (ii) and (iii), the phenotype depends solely on the unmodified functioning allele, making the unmodified allele dominant and the modified allele recessive.

Co-dominance and Multiple Alleles

When both alleles express simultaneously in a heterozygote, the inheritance is co-dominant. Human ABO blood grouping is controlled by gene \(I\), which has three alleles in the population: \(I^A\), \(I^B\), and \(i\). Alleles \(I^A\) and \(I^B\) produce distinct surface sugar polymers on red blood cells (RBCs) and are completely dominant over \(i\) (which produces no sugar). When \(I^A\) and \(I^B\) occur together (\(I^A I^B\)), both sugars are expressed, yielding blood group AB. Because three alleles exist at the population level (though any diploid individual carries only two), ABO blood groups also illustrate multiple allelism, producing 6 genotypes and 4 phenotypes (A, B, AB, and O).

Pleiotropy and Phenotype-Dependent Dominance

A single gene that influences multiple phenotypic traits—usually by acting on a metabolic pathway—is called a pleiotropic gene. A classic human example is phenylketonuria (PKU), where mutation of the gene for phenylalanine hydroxylase causes mental retardation alongside reduced hair and skin pigmentation.

Similarly, in pea seeds, gene \(B\) controls starch synthesis. Homozygous \(BB\) seeds produce large starch grains and are round; \(bb\) seeds produce small starch grains and are wrinkled. Heterozygotes (\(Bb\)) are round (so \(B\) is dominant for seed shape) but have intermediate-sized starch grains (so \(B\) and \(b\) show incomplete dominance for grain size). Thus, dominance is not an autonomous property of a gene—it depends on the specific phenotype examined.

Polygenic Inheritance

Quantitative traits showing continuous variation across a gradient—such as human height and skin colour—are controlled by three or more genes (polygenic traits) and are also modulated by the environment. Each dominant allele contributes additively to the phenotype. For example, if skin colour is governed by three genes (\(A, B, C\)), \(AABBCC\) yields the darkest phenotype, \(aabbcc\) yields the lightest, and \(AaBbCc\) (three dominant and three recessive alleles) yields an intermediate shade.

Dihybrid Inheritance, Chromosomal Theory, and Linkage

Law of Independent Assortment

Crossing pea plants differing in two traits—round yellow seeds (RRYY) and wrinkled green seeds (rryy)—produces round yellow F1 hybrids (RrYy). Self-pollinating F1 produces four F2 phenotypes in a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio, which is mathematically the product of two independent 3 : 1 monohybrid ratios:

\[ (3\text{ Round} : 1\text{ Wrinkled}) \times (3\text{ Yellow} : 1\text{ Green}) = 9\text{ Round Yellow} : 3\text{ Round Green} : 3\text{ Wrinkled Yellow} : 1\text{ Wrinkled Green} \]

The F1 dihybrid produces four gamete types (RY, Ry, rY, ry) in equal proportions (25% each). Across the 16 Punnett squares, there are 4 phenotypes and 9 genotypes (genotypic ratio 1:2:1:2:4:2:1:2:1). This led to the Law of Independent Assortment: when two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, segregation of one pair is independent of the other.

Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance

Mendel's 1865 work remained unrecognised until 1900 (rediscovered independently by de Vries, Correns, and von Tschermak) due to poor communication, resistance to mathematical biology, and lack of physical proof for "factors." By 1902, Walter Sutton and Theodore Boveri observed that chromosome behaviour during meiosis mirrors gene behaviour: both occur in pairs, segregate at gamete formation so each gamete receives one member, and non-homologous pairs align independently at the metaphase plate during Anaphase I.

Linkage, Recombination, and Gene Mapping

Thomas Hunt Morgan experimentally verified the chromosomal theory using the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster (chosen for its two-week life cycle, large progeny numbers, simple synthetic medium, clear sexual dimorphism, and visible mutations). When Morgan studied dihybrid crosses of X-linked genes in Drosophila, the F2 ratio deviated sharply from 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 because genes on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together.

  • Linkage: Physical association of genes located on the same chromosome, resulting in a higher proportion of parental combinations than non-parental combinations.
  • Recombination: Generation of non-parental gene combinations via crossing over during meiosis. Tightly linked genes show very low recombination (for example, yellow body and white eye showed 1.3% recombination), whereas loosely linked genes show higher recombination (white eye and miniature wing showed 37.2% recombination).
  • Genetic Mapping: Morgan's student Alfred Sturtevant used recombination frequency between gene pairs as a measure of physical distance on a chromosome to construct genetic maps.

Chromosomal and Haplodiploid Sex Determination

Henking (1891) first noticed a specific nuclear structure ("X-body") in 50% of insect sperm, later identified as the X chromosome. Chromosomes are classified into autosomes (identical in both sexes) and sex chromosomes (determining sex). In humans, there are 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes (46 total).

Human Sex Determination: Because females produce only X-bearing ova while males produce 50% X-bearing and 50% Y-bearing sperm, the genetic makeup of the sperm determines the child's sex, with a 50% probability of a male or female child in each pregnancy.

Honey Bee Haplodiploidy: Fertilised eggs develop into diploid females (queen or worker, \(2n = 32\)), whereas unfertilised eggs develop into haploid males (drones, \(n = 16\)) by parthenogenesis. Females produce gametes by meiosis, but haploid males produce sperm by mitosis. Consequently, male honey bees have no father and cannot have sons, though they have a grandfather and can have grandsons.

Mutation, Pedigree Analysis, and Genetic Disorders

Mutation

Mutation is an alteration in DNA sequences that modifies genotype and phenotype. Large-scale deletions or insertions/duplications of DNA segments cause chromosomal aberrations (commonly seen in cancer cells). A change in a single DNA base pair is a point mutation (e.g., sickle-cell anaemia), while insertions or deletions of base pairs cause frameshift mutations. Physical or chemical agents that induce mutations (such as UV radiation) are called mutagens.

Pedigree Analysis

Because controlled experimental crosses are impossible in humans, inheritance of traits across generations is traced using a family tree called a pedigree. Standard symbols allow geneticists to distinguish autosomal from sex-linked and dominant from recessive traits.

Chromosomal Disorders

Chromosomal disorders arise from aneuploidy (gain or loss of one or more chromosomes due to failure of chromatid segregation during cell division) or polyploidy (gain of an entire chromosome set due to failure of cytokinesis after telophase, common in plants).

  • Down's Syndrome (Trisomy 21; 47 chromosomes): Extra copy of chromosome 21. Features include short stature, small round head, furrowed tongue, partially open mouth, broad palm with characteristic palm crease, congenital heart disease, and retarded physical, psychomotor, and mental development.
  • Klinefelter's Syndrome (47, XXY): Extra X chromosome in a male. Overall masculine development with feminine breast development (gynaecomastia); individuals are sterile.
  • Turner's Syndrome (45, XO): Absence of one X chromosome in a female. Short stature, rudimentary ovaries (sterile), and underdeveloped secondary sexual characters.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Gamete Diversity from Multiple Heterozygous Loci

Problem: Assume a diploid plant species has the genotype \(AaBbCcDdEe\) for five independently assorting genes. How many genetically distinct types of gametes can it produce?

Solution:

Each heterozygous locus produces \(2\) equally frequent allele options during meiosis. For \(n\) independently assorting heterozygous loci, the number of distinct gamete types is \(2^n\).

\[ \text{Number of gamete types} = 2^5 = 32 \]

Each of the \(32\) gamete types occurs with a probability of \(\frac{1}{32}\).

Example 2: Predicting Offspring Proportions in a Dihybrid Cross

Problem: In a hypothetical garden pea cross, a plant heterozygous for violet axial flowers (\(VvAa\)) is crossed with a plant having violet terminal flowers that is heterozygous for colour (\(Vvaa\)). Assuming independent assortment, what fraction of the progeny will have white terminal flowers?

Solution:

Treat each locus independently and multiply the probabilities:

  • Colour locus (\(Vv \times Vv\)): Offspring genotypes are \(\frac{1}{4}VV\), \(\frac{1}{2}Vv\), \(\frac{1}{4}vv\). Probability of white (\(vv\)) = \(\frac{1}{4}\).
  • Position locus (\(Aa \times aa\)): Offspring genotypes are \(\frac{1}{2}Aa\), \(\frac{1}{2}aa\). Probability of terminal (\(aa\)) = \(\frac{1}{2}\).
\[ P(\text{white, terminal}) = \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{8} \text{ (or } 12.5\%\text{)} \]
Example 3: Analysing ABO Blood Group Inheritance

Problem: A couple has a child with blood group B. If the mother has blood group AB and the father has blood group A, determine the father's genotype and the probability that their next child will have blood group A.

Solution:

The mother's genotype must be \(I^A I^B\). For a child to have blood group B (genotype \(I^B I^B\) or \(I^B i\)), the child must receive \(I^B\) from the mother and \(i\) from the father (since the father is blood group A and lacks \(I^B\)). Therefore, the father's genotype must be \(I^A i\).

Crossing \(I^A I^B \times I^A i\) yields four equally likely genotypes: \(I^A I^A\) (Group A), \(I^A i\) (Group A), \(I^A I^B\) (Group AB), and \(I^B i\) (Group B).

Thus, the probability of the next child having blood group A is \(\frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}\) (50%).

Example 4: Constructing a Linear Genetic Map from Recombination Frequencies

Problem: Suppose three linked genes \(P\), \(Q\), and \(R\) lie on the same chromosome. Hypothetical recombination frequencies are measured as: \(P\text{–}Q = 18\%\), \(Q\text{–}R = 6\%\), and \(P\text{–}R = 12\%\). Determine the linear order of the three genes.

Solution:

Recombination frequency is proportional to map distance (\(1\% = 1\text{ map unit}\)). The two genes with the largest distance (\(P\text{–}Q = 18\%\)) must occupy the outer positions. Since \(P\text{–}R + R\text{–}Q = 12\% + 6\% = 18\%\), gene \(R\) lies between \(P\) and \(Q\).

Therefore, the gene order is \(P - R - Q\) (or \(Q - R - P\)).

Example 5: Assessing Risk in an X-Linked Recessive Trait

Problem: A woman with normal colour vision whose father was red-green colour blind marries a man with normal colour vision. What is the probability that their first son will be colour blind?

Solution:

Because the woman's father was colour blind (\(X^c Y\)), he transmitted \(X^c\) to his daughter. Since she has normal vision, she is a carrier (\(X^C X^c\)). Her husband has normal vision (\(X^C Y\)).

A son receives his Y chromosome from his father and one X chromosome from his mother. Because the mother has a \(\frac{1}{2}\) chance of passing \(X^c\), the probability that their first son is colour blind is \(\frac{1}{2}\) (50%).

In a monohybrid cross between two heterozygous tall pea plants, what proportion of the tall F2 progeny are expected to be homozygous tall?

  1. (A) \(\frac{1}{4}\)
  2. (B) \(\frac{1}{3}\)
  3. (C) \(\frac{1}{2}\)
  4. (D) \(\frac{2}{3}\)
Answer: (B) \(\frac{1}{3}\)

Worked Solution: Selfing \(Tt \times Tt\) gives \(1\,TT : 2\,Tt : 1\,tt\). Out of the \(3\) tall F2 plants (\(1\,TT + 2\,Tt\)), only \(1\) is homozygous tall (\(TT\)). Thus, the proportion among tall progeny is \(\frac{1}{3}\).

Which of the following statements best explains why dominance is not an autonomous feature of a gene?

  2. (B) In pea seeds, the same \(Bb\) heterozygote appears dominant for seed shape but shows incomplete dominance for starch grain size.
  3. (C) Recessive alleles are always lethal in the homozygous state.
  4. (D) Co-dominant alleles cannot be expressed in diploid organisms.
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Dominance depends on the gene product and the specific phenotype examined. In pea, \(Bb\) seeds are round (complete dominance of \(B\) over \(b\) for shape) while having intermediate-sized starch grains (incomplete dominance for grain size).

In birds, which gamete determines the sex of the chick?

Single-Correct MCQPractice Question 10

Failure of cytokinesis after the telophase stage of cell division in plants most directly results in:

  1. (A) Aneuploidy
  2. (B) Point mutation
  3. (C) Polyploidy
  4. (D) Frameshift mutation

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Homozygous vs. Total F2 Tall: Always check whether a question asks for the fraction of TT among all F2 plants (\(\frac{1}{4}\)) or among tall F2 plants (\(\frac{1}{3}\)).
  • Qualitative vs. Quantitative Haemoglobinopathies: Sickle-cell anaemia is qualitative (altered structure); thalassemia is quantitative (reduced synthesis).
  • Polyploidy vs. Aneuploidy: Polyploidy = whole set gain (cytokinesis failure); aneuploidy = single chromosome gain/loss (nondisjunction/segregation failure).
  • Heterogametic Sex in Birds vs. Mammals: Males are heterogametic in humans/Drosophila (XY) and grasshoppers (XO), whereas females are heterogametic in birds (ZW).
  • Multiple Alleles in an Individual: A population can have three or more alleles (such as \(I^A, I^B, i\)), but a diploid individual still carries only two alleles.

Concise Final Recap

Mendelian inheritance establishes that traits are governed by discrete paired alleles that segregate during meiosis and assort independently when located on different chromosomes. Real-world expression expands these rules through incomplete dominance, co-dominance, multiple alleles, pleiotropy, polygenic additive effects, and chromosomal linkage, where recombination frequency maps gene order. Sex determination operates via chromosomal heterogamety (XY, XO, ZW) or haplodiploidy, while mutations and meiotic errors underlie Mendelian and chromosomal disorders traceable through pedigrees and karyotypes.
