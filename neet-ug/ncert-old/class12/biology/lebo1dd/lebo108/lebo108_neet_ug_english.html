NEET UG Biology • Unit: Biology in Human Welfare

Human Health and Disease

A comprehensive, exam-oriented study guide covering pathogens, human immunity, retroviral pathogenesis, oncology, and substance abuse.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Understanding how the human body maintains physiological equilibrium against environmental pathogens, cellular mutations, and chemical stressors is a high-weightage pillar of the NEET UG Biology syllabus. By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Define health in modern biological terms and distinguish infectious from non-infectious disorders.
  • Identify causative organisms, transmission routes, target organs, and diagnostic markers for major bacterial, viral, protozoan, helminthic, and fungal diseases.
  • Trace the complete digenetic life cycle of Plasmodium across human and mosquito hosts.
  • Differentiate innate barriers from acquired immune responses, including humoral (antibody-mediated) and cell-mediated immunity, active versus passive immunisation, allergies, and autoimmunity.
  • Explain the replication mechanism of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and the cellular basis of cancer transformation, diagnosis, and therapy.
  • Classify commonly abused plant-derived and synthetic drugs, and evaluate the physiological impacts of tobacco, alcohol, and anabolic steroids.

1. Foundations of Health and Disease Classification

Early medical traditions, including ancient Greek thought associated with Hippocrates and the Indian Ayurveda system, viewed health as an equilibrium of bodily fluids termed "humors." Under that model, an excess of "black bile" was presumed to produce a hot temperament and fever. This hypothesis was later overturned by experimental physiology: William Harvey demonstrated blood circulation, and clinical thermometry proved that individuals categorized as having "black bile" maintained normal body temperatures.

Modern biology recognises that the brain communicates with the immune system through neural and endocrine pathways, meaning psychological stress directly alters immune competence. Consequently, health is defined not merely as the absence of illness or physical fitness, but as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being. Good health enhances workforce productivity, increases longevity, and lowers infant and maternal mortality.

Primary Determinants of Health

  • Genetic factors: Inherited defects or congenital deficiencies present from birth.
  • Infections: Invasion and multiplication of pathogenic organisms.
  • Lifestyle choices: Quality of nutrition and drinking water, sleep and exercise routines, hygiene, and exposure to addictive substances.

When one or more organ systems malfunction—manifesting through observable signs and subjective symptoms—the state is termed a disease. Diseases fall into two broad categories:

  • Infectious diseases: Easily transmitted from an infected host to a healthy individual (for example, typhoid, common cold, malaria, and AIDS).
  • Non-infectious diseases: Not transmissible between individuals; they arise from genetic, metabolic, environmental, or lifestyle factors (for example, cancer, diabetes, and autoimmune disorders).

2. Pathogens and Major Human Infectious Diseases

Any organism—bacterial, viral, fungal, protozoan, or helminthic—that enters a host, multiplies, and disrupts normal physiological or morphological function is called a pathogen. Most parasites are pathogens because they derive nourishment at the host's expense. To succeed, gut pathogens must evolve adaptations to survive gastric acidity (low pH) and resist digestive enzymes.

Life Cycle of the Malarial Parasite

Plasmodium is a digenetic parasite requiring two hosts to complete its life cycle: humans (where asexual multiplication occurs) and the female Anopheles mosquito (which serves as both vector and definitive host for sexual reproduction).

Prevention and Public Health Control

Effective control of infectious diseases relies on interrupting transmission pathways:

  • Food- and water-borne diseases (typhoid, amoebiasis, ascariasis): Personal cleanliness, safe drinking water, washed produce, sanitary disposal of excreta, and periodic disinfection of water reservoirs.
  • Air-borne diseases (pneumonia, common cold): Avoiding close contact with infected individuals and their personal belongings.
  • Vector-borne diseases (malaria, filariasis via Anopheles/Culex; dengue and chikungunya via Aedes): Eliminating stagnant water, cleaning household coolers regularly, using wire-mesh screens and mosquito nets, spraying insecticides in drains/swamps, and introducing larvivorous fish such as Gambusia into ponds.

3. The Immune System: Innate and Acquired Defences

The host's overall capacity to resist and eliminate disease-causing agents is termed immunity. It operates through two coordinated arms: non-specific innate immunity present from birth, and pathogen-specific acquired immunity developed over a lifetime.

Innate Immunity: Four Barrier Systems

  • Physical barriers: Intact skin acts as the primary mechanical shield; mucus lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital epithelia traps invading microbes.
  • Physiological barriers: Gastric hydrochloric acid (low pH), lysozyme/enzymes in saliva, and tears inhibit microbial proliferation.
  • Cellular barriers: Specialized leukocytes—polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNL-neutrophils), monocytes, and natural killer (NK) lymphocytes in blood—alongside tissue macrophages phagocytose and destroy pathogens.
  • Cytokine barriers: Virus-infected cells secrete protective proteins called interferons, which shield neighbouring uninfected cells from viral invasion.

Acquired Immunity: Specificity and Immunological Memory

Acquired immunity is pathogen-specific and retains immunological memory. The first encounter with a pathogen triggers a low-intensity primary response. A subsequent encounter with the same antigen elicits a rapid, high-intensity secondary (anamnestic) response because memory cells already recognise the target.

Two specialised lymphocyte populations execute acquired immunity:

  • B-lymphocytes (Humoral Immune Response): Secrete soluble glycoproteins called antibodies (immunoglobulins) into the blood to neutralise pathogens. Major classes include IgA, IgM, IgE, and IgG.
  • T-lymphocytes (Cell-Mediated Immunity, CMI): Do not secrete antibodies themselves, but helper T-cells assist B-cells in antibody production, while cytotoxic T-cells directly mediate CMI. CMI is capable of distinguishing "self" from "non-self" and is the primary mechanism responsible for organ graft rejection. Therefore, tissue matching and blood group compatibility are mandatory before transplantation, and recipients require lifelong immunosuppressant therapy.

Active vs. Passive Immunity and Vaccination

Feature Active Immunity Passive Immunity
Mechanism Host's own immune system produces antibodies upon exposure to living, dead, or attenuated antigens. Ready-made (preformed) antibodies are directly transferred into the recipient.
Speed & Duration Slow onset; provides long-lasting protection and immunological memory. Immediate protection; short-lived with no memory cell generation.
Examples Natural infection; routine vaccination (e.g., recombinant Hepatitis B vaccine produced in yeast). Maternal IgA in colostrum; maternal antibodies crossing placenta; tetanus antitoxin; snakebite antivenom.

Allergies and Autoimmunity

  • Allergy: An exaggerated immune response to harmless environmental antigens (allergens such as dust mites, pollen, or animal dander). It is mediated by IgE antibodies, which trigger mast cells to release histamine and serotonin, causing sneezing, watery eyes, runny nose, and bronchoconstriction. Symptoms are rapidly relieved by anti-histamines, adrenaline, and steroids.
  • Autoimmunity: A breakdown in self-tolerance (due to genetic or unknown triggers) where the immune system attacks the body's own cells, as seen in rheumatoid arthritis.

Lymphoid Organs

  • Primary lymphoid organs (Bone marrow and Thymus): Sites where immature lymphocytes originate and differentiate into antigen-sensitive lymphocytes. Bone marrow generates all blood cells, while the thymus (a lobed organ near the heart, beneath the breastbone) provides the microenvironment for T-lymphocyte maturation. Notably, the thymus is large at birth and progressively shrinks to a very small size by puberty.
  • Secondary lymphoid organs (Spleen, Lymph nodes, Tonsils, Peyer's patches of small intestine, Appendix): Sites where mature lymphocytes interact with trapped antigens and proliferate into effector cells. The spleen (bean-shaped) filters blood-borne microbes and stores erythrocytes, whereas lymph nodes filter antigens from lymph and tissue fluid.
  • MALT (Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue): Lymphoid tissue lining the respiratory, digestive, and urogenital tracts; it accounts for roughly 50% of total human lymphoid tissue.

4. HIV/AIDS and Retroviral Pathogenesis

Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) is a non-congenital, fatal deficiency of the immune system caused by the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), an enveloped RNA retrovirus. First reported in 1981, HIV spreads strictly through body fluids—never by casual touch or physical contact. Major transmission routes are: (a) sexual contact with an infected person, (b) transfusion of contaminated blood or blood products, (c) sharing infected needles among intravenous drug users, and (d) vertical transmission from an infected mother to her foetus across the placenta.

Following infection, there is an asymptomatic lag phase ranging from a few months to 5–10 years. HIV simultaneously enters helper T-lymphocytes (\(\mathrm{T_H}\)), replicates, and destroys them, causing a progressive decline in \(\mathrm{T_H}\) count. The patient develops bouts of fever, diarrhoea, and weight loss, and becomes vulnerable to opportunistic infections by Mycobacterium, viruses, fungi, and parasites such as Toxoplasma.

  • Diagnosis & Treatment: Screened via ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay). Anti-retroviral drugs prolong survival but cannot cure the infection.
  • Prevention: Public awareness led by NACO and WHO, screening blood banks, enforcing disposable needles/syringes, promoting safe sex, and avoiding social stigma against HIV-positive individuals.

5. Cancer Biology: Transformation, Diagnosis, and Therapy

Normal cells regulate division through contact inhibition, halting proliferation upon touching neighbouring cells. Cancerous (neoplastic) cells lose contact inhibition and divide uncontrollably to form cell masses called tumors:

  • Benign tumors: Remain localized at their site of origin, do not invade surrounding tissues, and cause limited damage.
  • Malignant tumors: Rapidly proliferating neoplastic cells that invade surrounding normal tissues and starve them of nutrients. Cells sloughed off into the bloodstream lodge at distant sites to initiate secondary tumors—a lethal property termed metastasis.

Carcinogens and Genetic Basis

  • Physical agents: Ionising radiations (X-rays, gamma rays) and non-ionising radiation (UV rays) induce DNA damage.
  • Chemical agents: Carcinogens in tobacco smoke (major cause of lung cancer).
  • Biological agents: Oncogenic viruses carrying viral oncogenes. Additionally, normal cells harbour cellular oncogenes (c-onc) or proto-oncogenes, which can trigger neoplastic transformation when aberrantly activated.

Detection and Treatment

  • Diagnosis: Biopsy and histopathological staining; blood/bone marrow cell counts (for leukemias); radiography (X-rays); Computed Tomography (CT) using X-rays for 3D internal imaging; Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) using strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiations for high-precision soft-tissue pathology; and molecular detection of inherited susceptibility genes or cancer-specific antibodies.
  • Treatment: Usually combines surgery, radiotherapy (lethal irradiation of tumor while sparing normal tissue), chemotherapy (side effects include hair loss and anaemia), and immunotherapy using biological response modifiers like \(\alpha\)-interferon to activate the host immune system against tumor cells.

6. Drugs, Tobacco, and Alcohol Abuse

When psychoactive compounds are consumed for non-medicinal purposes or in doses/frequencies that impair physical, physiological, or psychological functioning, it constitutes drug abuse.

(Morphine, Heroin/Smack)		 Latex of poppy plant (Papaver somniferum); heroin is diacetylmorphine Opioid receptors in CNS and gastrointestinal tract Depressant; slows body functions. Morphine is a potent medical sedative/painkiller; heroin is snorted or injected.

(Cocaine / Coke / Crack)		 Erythroxylum coca (native to South America) Interferes with dopamine neurotransmitter transport Potent CNS stimulant producing euphoria and increased energy; excessive doses cause hallucinations. Usually snorted.

Clinical medications such as barbiturates, amphetamines, and benzodiazepines (prescribed for depression and insomnia) are also frequently abused. Tobacco contains the alkaloid nicotine, which stimulates the adrenal gland to release adrenaline and noradrenaline, raising blood pressure and heart rate. Smoking elevates blood carbon monoxide (CO), reducing haem-bound oxygen (causing hypoxia), and strongly increases the risk of lung, urinary bladder, and throat cancers, bronchitis, emphysema, coronary heart disease, and gastric ulcers; tobacco chewing increases oral cancer risk.

Adolescence, Addiction, Dependence, and Prevention

  • Adolescence (12–18 years): A vulnerable transitional phase driven by curiosity, peer pressure, academic stress, and media portrayal.
  • Addiction vs. Dependence: Addiction is psychological attachment to euphoria; repeated use raises receptor tolerance, demanding higher doses. Dependence is the body's tendency to manifest an unpleasant withdrawal syndrome (anxiety, shakiness, nausea, sweating) upon abrupt discontinuation.
  • Long-term Hazards: Chronic alcohol/drug abuse causes nervous system damage and liver cirrhosis. Intravenous sharing of needles transmits fatal HIV and Hepatitis B.
  • Anabolic Steroid Misuse in Sports: Causes masculinisation, abnormal menstrual cycles, facial hair, clitoral enlargement, and voice deepening in females; testicular shrinkage, reduced sperm count, gynaecomastia, premature baldness, prostate enlargement, and liver/kidney dysfunction in males; plus premature epiphyseal closure (stunted growth) in adolescents.
  • Prevention: Avoiding undue peer pressure, education and counselling, seeking help from parents/peers, monitoring warning signs (drop in academics, isolation, hygiene neglect), and professional de-addiction support.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Interpreting Primary vs. Secondary Immune Kinetics

Scenario (Assumed Values): A volunteer receives a first dose of an inactivated bacterial toxoid vaccine on Day 0, followed by a booster dose on Day 28. Hypothetical serum antibody concentrations are measured as \(12\,\mathrm{µg/mL}\) on Day 14 and \(240\,\mathrm{µg/mL}\) on Day 35.

Question: Explain the physiological basis for the 20-fold jump in antibody concentration within 7 days of the booster dose.

Solution: The Day 14 measurement (\(12\,\mathrm{µg/mL}\)) reflects the primary immune response, which is slow and of low intensity because naive B- and T-lymphocytes must first recognise the antigen, clonally expand, and differentiate. During this primary exposure, long-lived memory B- and T-cells are generated. When the booster is administered on Day 28, these memory cells immediately recognise the antigen and mount a rapid, amplified secondary (anamnestic) response, producing \(240\,\mathrm{µg/mL}\) of antibodies within a week.

Example 2: Distinguishing Active and Passive Immunisation in Clinical Emergencies

Scenario (Assumed Case): A farmer steps on a rusty nail and develops a deep puncture wound suspected of contamination with Clostridium tetani. Simultaneously, a newborn infant is breastfed during the first 48 hours after birth.

Question: Identify the type of immunity provided to the farmer by tetanus antitoxin injection and to the infant by colostrum, and justify why active vaccination alone is insufficient for the farmer.

Solution: Both scenarios represent passive immunity. The farmer receives preformed antibodies (antitoxin) because tetanus toxin acts rapidly, and waiting for the slow onset of active immunity would be life-threatening. Similarly, the newborn receives maternal IgA antibodies via yellowish colostrum, providing immediate mucosal protection while the infant's own immune system matures.

Example 3: Analysing Retroviral Depletion of Helper T-Cells

Scenario (Assumed Clinical Data): An untreated HIV-positive patient's helper T-lymphocyte (\(\mathrm{T_H}\)) count drops from \(900\,\mathrm{cells/mm^3}\) at seroconversion to \(150\,\mathrm{cells/mm^3}\) eight years later, accompanied by recurrent Mycobacterium pneumonia.

Question: Why does a drop in \(\mathrm{T_H}\) cells cripple both humoral and cell-mediated immunity, and why did the patient remain relatively asymptomatic during the early years?

Solution: HIV replicates inside macrophages (which act as viral factories without immediate lysis) and progressively destroys \(\mathrm{T_H}\) lymphocytes. Because \(\mathrm{T_H}\) cells are required to stimulate B-cells for antibody production and coordinate CMI, their depletion leaves the host unable to clear intracellular pathogens like Mycobacterium or Toxoplasma. The long asymptomatic period corresponds to the viral incubation/latency phase (often 5–10 years) before \(\mathrm{T_H}\) counts fall below the critical threshold.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which combination of causative organism, vector/carrier, and diagnostic or clinical feature is matched correctly?
  • (A) Salmonella typhi — Female Anopheles — Sustained high fever and Widal test
  • (B) Entamoeba histolytica — Housefly (mechanical carrier) — Stools with excess mucus and blood clots
  • (C) Wuchereria bancrofti — Aedes mosquito — Rupture of RBCs releasing haemozoin
  • (D) Rhino virus — Aerosol droplets — Fluid-filled alveoli and bluish fingernails
Correct Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: Entamoeba histolytica causes amoebiasis in the large intestine, transmitted via faecal contamination with houseflies acting as mechanical carriers, producing stools with excess mucus and blood clots. (A) is incorrect because typhoid is food/water-borne, not mosquito-borne. (C) mixes Wuchereria (lymphatic filariasis) with Plasmodium (haemozoin). (D) attributes pneumonia symptoms (fluid-filled alveoli) to Rhino viruses, which infect only the nose and upper respiratory tract.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ): During the life cycle of Plasmodium, fertilisation of male and female gametocytes and subsequent sporozoite formation occur in which location?
  • (A) Human liver hepatocytes
  • (B) Human red blood cells
  • (C) Mosquito gut
  • (D) Mosquito salivary glands
Correct Answer: (C)
Worked Solution: Gametocytes develop inside human RBCs and are ingested by a female Anopheles mosquito during a blood meal. Fertilisation and development into sporozoites take place in the mosquito's gut; mature sporozoites then migrate to the salivary glands for storage.
Practice Question 3 (Statement-Based MCQ): Read Statement I and Statement II carefully and choose the correct option.
Statement I: Virus-infected cells secrete interferons to protect neighbouring non-infected cells from further viral infection.
Statement II: Interferons belong to the cellular barrier of innate immunity.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (C)
Worked Solution: Statement I is accurate: interferons are antiviral proteins secreted by virus-infected cells. Statement II is incorrect because interferons constitute the cytokine barrier of innate immunity, whereas PMNL-neutrophils, monocytes, NK cells, and macrophages form the cellular barrier.
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ): A kidney transplant recipient experiences acute graft rejection despite blood group compatibility. Which immune mechanism is primarily responsible for rejecting the donor organ?
  • (A) IgE-mediated degranulation of mast cells
  • (B) Cell-mediated immunity driven by T-lymphocytes
  • (C) Neutralisation by maternal IgA antibodies
  • (D) Phagocytosis by alveolar macrophages
Correct Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: The immune system distinguishes "self" from "non-self" tissues primarily through T-lymphocytes executing cell-mediated immunity (CMI), which is responsible for graft rejection.
Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ):
Assertion (A): The thymus gland is quite large at birth but reduces to a very small size by puberty.
Reason (R): Both bone marrow and thymus provide microenvironments for the development and maturation of T-lymphocytes.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) Both (A) and (R) are false.
Correct Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: Both statements are scientifically true facts about primary lymphoid organs. However, the fact that bone marrow and thymus support T-cell maturation does not explain why the thymus undergoes age-related involution by puberty.
Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following statements about allergic reactions is INCORRECT?
  • (A) Allergies involve an exaggerated immune response to environmental antigens.
  • (B) Antibodies produced in response to allergens belong to the IgE class.

Worked Solution: Allergic symptoms (sneezing, wheezing, watery eyes) are rapidly alleviated by anti-histamines, adrenaline, and steroids—not interferons or antiretroviral drugs.
Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ): In HIV pathogenesis, which cell type functions as an "HIV factory" by continuously producing progeny virions while surviving infection?
  • (A) Neutrophils
  • (B) Macrophages
  • (C) Erythrocytes
  • (D) B-lymphocytes
Correct Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: After entering the body, HIV infects macrophages, where reverse transcriptase converts viral RNA into viral DNA that integrates into the host genome. The macrophages survive and continuously produce new virus particles, acting as an HIV factory.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET Traps to Avoid

  • Common cold vs. Pneumonia: Rhino viruses infect only the nose and upper respiratory passage; they do not infect the lung alveoli (unlike Streptococcus pneumoniae).
  • Primary vs. Secondary Lymphoid Organs: Bone marrow and thymus are primary; spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, Peyer's patches, and appendix are secondary. Do not misclassify the spleen as primary.
  • CT vs. MRI: Computed Tomography (CT) uses ionising X-rays; MRI uses strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiation.
  • Antibody Classes: Remember IgA (colostrum/passive), IgE (allergy/mast cells), and the \(\mathrm{H_2L_2}\) tetrameric polypeptide formula.
  • Morphine vs. Heroin: Morphine is extracted directly from poppy latex; heroin (smack) is produced by acetylation of morphine (diacetylmorphine).

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Health = physical + mental + social well-being (verified by Harvey's circulation & thermometry disproving "good humor").
  • Plasmodium: Sporozoites (infective) → Liver → RBCs (haemozoin → 3–4 day fever) → Gametocytes → Mosquito gut (fertilisation) → Salivary glands.
  • Innate barriers: Physical, Physiological, Cellular (PMNL, monocytes, NK, macrophages), Cytokine (interferons).
  • MALT = ~50% of total lymphoid tissue in the human body.
  • HIV targets macrophages (HIV factory) and \(\mathrm{T_H}\) cells via reverse transcriptase; diagnosed by ELISA.
  • Cancer = loss of contact inhibition; metastasis = spread of malignant tumors; \(\alpha\)-interferon = biological response modifier.

Concise Final Recap

Human health depends on genetic integrity, lifestyle, and defence against pathogens. Infectious diseases are countered first by non-specific innate barriers and then by memory-driven acquired immunity (B-cell humoral antibodies and T-cell CMI). Breakdowns of this regulatory balance manifest as allergies (IgE), autoimmunity, immunodeficiency (HIV depleting \(\mathrm{T_H}\) cells), or neoplasia (loss of contact inhibition and metastasis). Finally, preventing substance abuse—particularly during adolescence—protects neuro-endocrine, hepatic, and cardiovascular health.
