Microbes in Human Welfare

Comprehensive conceptual guide covering household fermentation, industrial bio-products, sewage treatment, biogas generation, biocontrol, and biofertilisers.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Microscopic organisms—including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, viruses, viroids, and proteinaceous infectious agents called prions—inhabit virtually every environment on Earth. Beyond soil, water, air, and living hosts, extremophilic microbes thrive inside thermal vents reaching 100°C, beneath thick snow layers, deep underground, and in highly acidic habitats.

While pathogenic microbes receive significant attention for causing disease, the vast majority of microbial species perform indispensable ecological and economic roles. When grown on solid nutritive media in the laboratory, bacteria and fungi multiply to form visible colonies, enabling researchers to isolate and harness their metabolic pathways.

Key Competencies for NEET UG
  • Match specific bacterial and fungal species to their household, industrial, and clinical products.
  • Trace the physical and biological stages of municipal sewage treatment and interpret Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD).
  • Explain the anaerobic breakdown of cellulose by methanogens in cattle rumen and biogas digesters.
  • Differentiate narrow-spectrum microbial biocontrol agents from chemical pesticides within Integrated Pest Management (IPM).
  • Classify symbiotic and free-living biofertilisers by their nutrient-enrichment mechanisms.

Microbes in Household Food Processing

Everyday food preparation relies heavily on controlled microbial fermentation, where enzymes secreted by bacteria or yeasts alter texture, flavour, gas content, and nutritional value.

Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB) and Dairy Conversion

Lactobacillus and related lactic acid bacteria (LAB) convert fresh milk into curd. Adding a small quantity of existing curd acts as an inoculum (starter) containing millions of LAB. At suitable temperatures, these bacteria multiply and secrete lactic acid, which coagulates and partially digests milk proteins (casein). This transformation also enhances nutritional quality by increasing Vitamin B12 content. Inside the human stomach, LAB play a protective role by checking the growth of disease-causing microbes.

Dough Fermentation and Traditional Foods

  • Idli and Dosa Batter: Fermented by naturally occurring bacteria. The characteristic puffed-up appearance results from carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) gas released during anaerobic respiration.
  • Bread Making: Wheat dough is leavened using baker's yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, which ferments sugars to produce \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) bubbles that make bread porous and soft.
  • Traditional Fermented Items: Toddy, a traditional beverage in parts of southern India, is produced by fermenting palm sap. Microbes are similarly used to ferment fish, soyabean, and bamboo shoots.

Cheese Ripening and Specificity

Distinct cheese varieties owe their texture, aroma, and taste to the specific microbe used during ripening:

  • Swiss Cheese: Features large holes caused by abundant \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) production by the bacterium Propionibacterium shermanii.
  • Roquefort Cheese: Ripened by growing a specific fungus on the cheese curds, imparting a sharp, characteristic flavour.

Industrial Fermentation: Beverages, Antibiotics, and Bioactive Molecules

Industrial microbiology scales up microbial metabolism in large stainless-steel vessels called fermentors, where temperature, pH, aeration, and nutrient supply are tightly regulated.

Fermented Beverages

Brewer's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae—the same species used in baking) ferments malted cereals and fruit juices to produce ethanol (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}\)). The final alcoholic beverage depends on both the raw substrate and whether distillation follows fermentation:

  • Produced Without Distillation: Wine and beer.
  • Produced With Distillation of Fermented Broth: Whisky, brandy, and rum.

Discovery and Impact of Antibiotics

The term antibiotic combines Greek roots meaning "against life" (anti = against, bio = life)—specifically against disease-causing microbes, while being "pro-life" for human patients. Antibiotics are chemical substances secreted by certain microbes that kill or inhibit the growth of other pathogenic microbes.

The first antibiotic, Penicillin, emerged from a chance observation by Alexander Fleming while culturing Staphylococci bacteria. He noticed a contaminating mould, Penicillium notatum, on an unwashed culture plate; bacteria failed to grow in a zone surrounding the mould because of a secreted chemical. Later, Ernest Chain and Howard Florey established Penicillin's full therapeutic potential, enabling large-scale treatment of wounded soldiers during World War II. Fleming, Chain, and Florey shared the 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Subsequent antibiotic discoveries transformed medicine by enabling effective treatment of previously devastating bacterial infections such as plague, whooping cough (kali khansi), diphtheria (gal ghotu), and leprosy (kusht rog).

Sewage Treatment and Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD)

Municipal waste-water, known as sewage, contains massive amounts of organic matter (largely human excreta) alongside pathogenic microbes. Discharging untreated sewage directly into rivers causes severe water pollution and water-borne epidemics. Sewage treatment plants (STPs) rely on naturally occurring heterotrophic microbes to purify waste-water in two distinct stages.

Stage 1: Primary Treatment (Physical Separation)

Physical processes remove suspended solids without microbial action:

  1. Sequential Filtration: Removes large floating debris.
  2. Sedimentation: Removes grit (soil and small pebbles) by gravity settling.

All settled solids constitute the primary sludge, while the liquid supernatant forms the primary effluent, which moves to secondary treatment.

Stage 2: Secondary Treatment (Biological Oxidation)

The primary effluent enters large aeration tanks where it is continuously agitated mechanically and supplied with pumped air. This oxygen-rich environment promotes vigorous growth of useful aerobic microbes into flocs—mesh-like masses formed by bacteria associated with fungal filaments.

As flocs grow, they consume and oxidise the major fraction of dissolved organic matter, sharply lowering the Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD).

Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD): The amount of dissolved oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)) consumed by bacteria to oxidise all organic matter present in \(1\,\mathrm{L}\) of water.
\[ \text{Higher Organic Matter} \implies \text{Higher BOD} \implies \text{Greater Polluting Potential} \]

Once BOD falls to an acceptable level, the effluent flows into a settling tank where bacterial flocs sediment out as activated sludge. A small portion of activated sludge is recycled back into the aeration tank as an inoculum. The remaining major fraction is pumped into large anaerobic sludge digesters, where anaerobic bacteria digest the bacteria and fungi in the sludge, generating an inflammable gas mixture called biogas (methane \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), hydrogen sulphide \(\mathrm{H_2S}\), and carbon dioxide \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)). The clarified effluent is safely released into natural rivers and streams.

To curb river pollution from rapid urbanisation, the Ministry of Environment and Forests launched the Ganga Action Plan and Yamuna Action Plan to expand STP capacity nationwide.

Microbial Biogas Production

Unlike yeast or LAB fermentations that primarily evolve \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), anaerobic bacteria known as methanogens (such as Methanobacterium) metabolise cellulosic material to produce large volumes of methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)) alongside \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2}\). This flammable gas mixture is called biogas.

Methanogens inhabit two major environments: anaerobic sludge digesters in STPs and the rumen (a stomach compartment) of cattle. Because cattle consume cellulose-rich fodder that humans cannot digest, rumen methanogens help break down cellulose for cattle nutrition. Consequently, cattle dung (gobar) is densely populated with methanogens and serves as the ideal feedstock for biogas (gobar gas) plants in rural areas.

Structure and Operation of a Biogas Plant

Developed in India largely through the efforts of the Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI) and the Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC), a typical biogas plant operates as follows:

  • Digester Tank: A concrete underground tank (\(10\text{–}15\,\mathrm{ft}\) deep) that receives bio-wastes and a slurry of cattle dung mixed with water.
  • Floating Gas-Holder: Placed directly over the slurry; it rises upward as microbial activity generates gas inside the tank.
  • Gas Outlet: A pipe connected to the top of the holder supplies biogas to nearby homes for cooking and lighting.
  • Spent Slurry Outlet: Exhausted slurry is removed through a separate outlet and applied to fields as an organic fertiliser.

Biocontrol Agents and Integrated Pest Management (IPM)

Conventional agriculture relies heavily on synthetic insecticides, pesticides, and weedicides, which poison soil, groundwater, crops, and non-target organisms indiscriminately. Biocontrol replaces toxic chemicals with biological methods—natural predation and targeted microbial pathogens—to regulate agricultural pests.

A core principle of organic farming is that biodiversity furthers health. Organic farmers do not aim to eradicate pests completely; instead, pests are maintained at manageable levels through ecological checks and balances. Complete eradication would starve the beneficial predatory and parasitic insects that depend on pests as food or hosts.

Key Predators and Microbial Biocontrol Agents

  • Insect Predators: The red-and-black Ladybird beetle controls aphids, while Dragonflies help eliminate mosquitoes.
  • Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt): Available as dried spores in sachets, mixed with water and sprayed on vulnerable crops (such as brassicas and fruit trees). When butterfly caterpillars ingest the spores, the bacterial toxin is released in the larval gut, killing the larvae while leaving other insects unharmed. Genetic engineering has also enabled the insertion of Bt toxin genes directly into crop plants, creating pest-resistant varieties such as Bt-cotton.
  • Trichoderma Species: Free-living fungi abundant in root ecosystems that act as effective biocontrol agents against several plant pathogens.
  • Baculoviruses (Genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus): Pathogens that attack insects and other arthropods. They are ideal for species-specific, narrow-spectrum insecticidal applications because they have no negative impact on plants, mammals, birds, fish, or non-target beneficial insects—making them invaluable in ecologically sensitive areas and Integrated Pest Management (IPM) programmes.

Biofertilisers in Sustainable Agriculture

Excessive use of chemical fertilisers degrades soil health and pollutes water bodies. Biofertilisers are living organisms that enrich soil nutrient quality sustainably. The three principal sources are bacteria, fungi, and cyanobacteria.

Worked Examples

1. Interpreting Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) Across Water Samples

Scenario (Hypothetical Values): Three unlabelled water samples—X, Y, and Z—are collected from (i) a relatively clean mountain river, (ii) secondary effluent leaving an STP settling tank, and (iii) raw untreated municipal sewage. A laboratory measures their BOD values as \(10\,\mathrm{mg/L}\), \(38\,\mathrm{mg/L}\), and \(480\,\mathrm{mg/L}\) for samples X, Y, and Z, respectively. Identify each sample and justify the ranking.

Reasoning: BOD measures the oxygen consumed by aerobic bacteria to oxidise organic matter in \(1\,\mathrm{L}\) of water; thus, BOD is directly proportional to organic pollution.

  • Sample Z (\(480\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)): Highest BOD indicates maximum organic matter and polluting potential, corresponding to raw untreated municipal sewage.
  • Sample Y (\(38\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)): Intermediate BOD reflects substantial reduction of organic load after aeration, corresponding to secondary STP effluent.
  • Sample X (\(10\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)): Lowest BOD indicates minimal oxidisable organic matter, corresponding to the clean mountain river.

2. Matching Bioactive Molecules to Microbial Sources and Clinical Roles

Scenario: A cardiology patient who recently survived a myocardial infarction requires a clot-dissolving agent, while a kidney-transplant recipient requires an immunosuppressant, and a hypercholesterolemia patient needs a cholesterol-lowering drug. Identify the three microbial agents and their exact source organisms.

Reasoning:

  1. Clot Buster: Streptokinase, produced by the bacterium Streptococcus (modified via genetic engineering), dissolves fibrin clots in blood vessels after myocardial infarction.
  2. Immunosuppressant: Cyclosporin A, produced by the fungus Trichoderma polysporum, prevents graft rejection in organ-transplant patients.
  3. Blood-Cholesterol Lowering Agent: Statins, produced by the yeast Monascus purpureus, act by competitively inhibiting the enzyme responsible for cholesterol synthesis.

3. Selecting a Biocontrol Agent for an Ecologically Sensitive Orchard

Scenario: An orchard adjacent to a honeybee sanctuary and a freshwater fish pond suffers from an outbreak of lepidopteran pests. Why should a farm manager choose a Nucleopolyhedrovirus formulation over a broad-spectrum synthetic insecticide?

Reasoning: Synthetic insecticides kill both target pests and beneficial pollinators (honeybees), while runoff threatens aquatic life (fish). Baculoviruses belonging to the genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus are species-specific and narrow-spectrum: they infect target insect/arthropod pests without harming plants, mammals, birds, fish, or non-target insects such as honeybees. This preserves biodiversity within an Integrated Pest Management (IPM) framework.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following pairs is incorrectly matched with respect to the microbe and its commercial product?

  1. (A) Aspergillus niger — Citric acid
  2. (B) Acetobacter aceti — Acetic acid
  3. (C) Clostridium butylicum — Lactic acid
  4. (D) Monascus purpureus — Statins

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Clostridium butylicum is a bacterium used for the commercial production of butyric acid, whereas lactic acid is produced by Lactobacillus. All other pairs—Aspergillus niger (citric acid), Acetobacter aceti (acetic acid), and Monascus purpureus (statins)—are correctly matched.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Which set of alcoholic beverages is produced without distillation of the fermented broth?

  1. (A) Wine and whisky
  2. (B) Beer and brandy
  3. (C) Wine and beer
  4. (D) Rum and whisky

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Brewer's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments malted cereals and fruit juices into ethanol. Wine and beer are produced without distillation, whereas whisky, brandy, and rum require distillation of the fermented broth.

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Large holes in Swiss cheese are formed due to the metabolic activity of Propionibacterium shermanii.
Reason (R): This bacterium consumes oxygen rapidly, creating vacuum pockets inside the cheese curd.

  1. (A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
  2. (B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  3. (C) A is true but R is false.
  4. (D) Both A and R are false.

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Assertion (A) is true: Propionibacterium shermanii is responsible for the large holes in Swiss cheese. However, Reason (R) is false because the holes are caused by the production of a large amount of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) gas, not oxygen consumption.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

During secondary sewage treatment, what are "flocs"?

  1. (A) Anaerobic methanogens suspended in primary sludge
  2. (B) Masses of aerobic bacteria associated with fungal filaments forming mesh-like structures

Answer: (B)

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

In a sewage treatment plant, what happens to the major part of the activated sludge after sedimentation in the settling tank?

  1. (A) It is discharged directly into rivers and streams.
  2. (B) It is pumped into anaerobic sludge digesters to produce biogas.
  3. (C) It is completely recycled back to the primary settling tank.
  4. (D) It is treated with pectinases and proteases to clarify the water.

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Only a small fraction of activated sludge is pumped back into the aeration tank to serve as an inoculum. Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

Why is cattle dung (gobar) especially suitable for generating biogas?

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

Which benefit is not associated with mycorrhizal (Glomus) symbiosis in crop plants?

  2. (B) Resistance to root-borne pathogens

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET Traps to Avoid

  • Fungus vs. Bacterium vs. Yeast: Remember that Aspergillus niger (citric acid) and Trichoderma polysporum (Cyclosporin A) are fungi; Monascus purpureus (statins) and Saccharomyces cerevisiae (ethanol) are yeasts (unicellular fungi); whereas Acetobacter aceti, Clostridium butylicum, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus are bacteria.
  • Phosphorus vs. Nitrogen in Biofertilisers: Glomus (mycorrhiza) absorbs phosphorus, whereas Rhizobium, Azospirillum, Azotobacter, and cyanobacteria fix nitrogen.
  • Primary vs. Secondary Sewage Treatment: Primary treatment is purely physical (filtration + sedimentation); secondary treatment is biological (aerobic flocs reducing BOD, followed by anaerobic sludge digestion).
  • Biogas Composition: Biogas from methanogens is predominantly methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)) along with \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2}\) (plus \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) in sludge digesters)—do not confuse this with \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-dominated bread or Swiss cheese fermentation.
  • Antibiotic Credit: Alexander Fleming discovered Penicillin (from Penicillium notatum), while Ernest Chain and Howard Florey established its full therapeutic potential; all three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize.

Concise Final Recap

Microbes underpin human welfare across five major domains: (1) household foods via LAB, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Propionibacterium shermanii; (2) industrial products including distilled/undistilled beverages, antibiotics, organic acids, enzymes (lipases, pectinases, proteases, streptokinase), and bioactive drugs (Cyclosporin A, statins); (3) sewage treatment using aerobic flocs to slash BOD and anaerobic digesters to recover biogas; (4) rural energy via methanogens in IARI/KVIC-designed biogas plants; and (5) sustainable agriculture through targeted biocontrol agents (Bt, Trichoderma, Nucleopolyhedrovirus) and nutrient-enriching biofertilisers (Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Glomus, cyanobacteria).
