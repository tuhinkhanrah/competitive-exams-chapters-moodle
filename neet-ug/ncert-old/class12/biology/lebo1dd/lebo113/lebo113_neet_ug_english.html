NEET UG Biology • Ecology

Organisms and Populations

Physiological Ecology, Population Dynamics, Growth Models, and Interspecific Interactions

Learning Objectives and Overview

Ecology examines how living organisms interact with one another and with their physical environment across four core levels of biological organisation: organisms, populations, communities, and biomes. At the organismic level, ecology focuses on physiological, morphological, and behavioural adaptations for survival and reproduction. At the population level, it connects environmental pressures to population genetics and natural selection.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between habitat and ecological niche, and explain how temperature, water, light, and soil shape biome distribution.
  • Classify organismic responses to abiotic stress into regulation, conformation, partial regulation, migration, and suspension.
  • Identify key morphological, physiological, and biochemical adaptations in extreme environments.
  • Calculate population attributes (per capita birth/death rates, density changes) and interpret age pyramids.
  • Apply exponential and Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth equations, including the significance of carrying capacity (\(K\)) and intrinsic rate of natural increase (\(r\)).
  • Analyse the six major interspecific population interactions and their classic biological examples.
Foundational Context & Scientific Inquiry

Ramdeo Misra (1908–1998) is revered as the Father of Ecology in India. After earning his Ph.D. in Ecology in 1937 under Prof. W. H. Pearsall, he established ecology teaching and research at Banaras Hindu University, Varanasi, laying the groundwork for tropical community ecology and environmental policy in India (leading to the National Committee for Environmental Planning and Coordination in 1972 and the Ministry of Environment and Forests in 1984).

In biological observation, ecologists distinguish between "how-type" questions (seeking the physiological or mechanical mechanism, such as how a bird's syrinx produces sound) and "why-type" questions (seeking the evolutionary or adaptive significance, such as why a bird sings to attract a mate during the breeding season).

Organism and Its Abiotic Environment

Earth's rotation around the Sun and the tilt of its axis generate seasonal variations in temperature intensity and duration. Together with annual variations in precipitation (both rain and snow), these climatic patterns produce distinct major biomes—including desert, grassland, tropical forest, temperate forest, coniferous forest, and arctic/alpine tundra. Within India, major biomes include tropical rain forests, deciduous forests, deserts, and sea coasts.

Habitat and Ecological Niche

A habitat is the physical and biological space where an organism lives; it includes both physico-chemical (abiotic) conditions and interacting biotic components such as pathogens, parasites, predators, and competitors. By contrast, an organism's ecological niche encompasses three specific aspects: (1) the defined range of environmental conditions it can tolerate, (2) the diversity of resources it utilises, and (3) its distinct functional role in the ecological system.

Major Abiotic Factors

Abiotic Factor Key Ecological Significance Important Classifications & Examples
Temperature Most ecologically relevant factor. Governs enzyme kinetics, metabolic rate, and physiological functions. Decreases from equator to poles and plains to mountain tops (subzero in polar/alpine zones to >50°C in tropical deserts; >100°C in thermal springs and deep-sea vents). Eurythermal: Tolerate a wide range of temperatures.
Stenothermal: Restricted to a narrow temperature range (e.g., mango trees cannot grow in Canada/Germany; snow leopards absent in Kerala; tuna fish rarely beyond tropical latitudes).
Water Essential for life and plant productivity. For aquatic organisms, chemical composition, pH, and salinity (parts per thousand, ppt) are critical due to osmotic balance. Salinity: <5 ppt (inland waters), 30–35 ppt (sea), >100 ppt (hypersaline lagoons).
Euryhaline: Wide salinity tolerance.
Stenohaline: Narrow salinity tolerance (freshwater fish cannot survive long in seawater and vice versa).
Light Drives photosynthesis in autotrophs. Photoperiod (diurnal/seasonal light duration) cues flowering in plants and foraging, reproduction, and migration in animals. Spectral quality matters; UV is harmful, and oceans >500 m deep are perpetually dark. Forest herbs/shrubs photosynthesise optimally under low light beneath tall canopies. Among marine algae (green, brown, red), red algae inhabit the deepest waters because their pigments absorb blue-green wavelengths that penetrate deepest.
Soil Soil composition, grain size, and aggregation determine percolation and water-holding capacity. Together with pH, mineral content, and topography, they dictate vegetation and thus animal distribution. In aquatic habitats, sediment characteristics determine the types of benthic (bottom-dwelling) animals that can thrive.

Responses to Abiotic Stress and Adaptations

Organisms perform biochemical reactions most efficiently when their internal environment (temperature, osmotic concentration of body fluids) remains relatively constant—a state called homeostasis. Faced with fluctuating external conditions, organisms adopt one of four major strategies:

Tutorial Diagram 2: Diagrammatic representation of organismic homeostatic responses to changing external conditions.

  • Regulate: Maintain constant internal temperature (thermoregulation) and osmotic concentration (osmoregulation) via physiological or behavioural means. Includes all birds and mammals, plus a very few lower vertebrates and invertebrates. Humans maintain 37°C by sweating (evaporative cooling) in summer and shivering (metabolic heat generation) in winter.
  • Conform: Internal temperature or osmotic concentration changes with the ambient environment. Roughly 99% of animals and nearly all plants are conformers. Why? Thermoregulation is energetically expensive, especially for small animals (e.g., shrews, hummingbirds) that have a large surface-area-to-volume ratio, causing rapid heat loss in cold climates. Hence, very small animals are rarely found in polar regions.
  • Partial Regulate: Regulate internal conditions up to a limited external range, beyond which they conform.
  • Migrate: Temporarily move from a stressful habitat to a more hospitable area and return when conditions improve (e.g., migratory birds from Siberia visiting Keoladeo National Park, Bharatpur, Rajasthan every winter).
  • Suspend: Escape stress in time by lowering metabolic activity. Examples include thick-walled spores in bacteria, fungi, and lower plants; seed dormancy in higher plants; hibernation (winter sleep in bears); aestivation (summer sleep in snails and fish to avoid heat and desiccation); and diapause (suspended development in lake/pond zooplankton).

Key Ecological Adaptations

An adaptation is any genetically fixed morphological, physiological, or behavioural attribute that enables survival and reproduction in a habitat:

  • Desert Animals: The North American kangaroo rat meets all its water requirements through internal fat oxidation (producing metabolic water) and excretes highly concentrated urine.
  • Desert Plants: Thick leaf cuticle, sunken stomata, CAM (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism) pathway allowing stomata to stay closed during the day, and leaves reduced to spines with flattened photosynthetic stems (e.g., Opuntia).
  • Cold Climates (Allen's Rule): Mammals from colder climates generally possess shorter ears and limbs to minimise heat loss. Polar aquatic mammals like seals have a thick layer of subcutaneous fat (blubber) acting as thermal insulation.
  • High-Altitude Acclimatisation: Above 3,500 m (e.g., Rohtang Pass near Manali or Leh), low atmospheric pressure reduces oxygen availability, causing altitude sickness (nausea, fatigue, heart palpitations). The body acclimatises by increasing RBC production, decreasing hemoglobin's oxygen-binding affinity, and increasing the breathing rate.
  • Extreme Biochemical & Behavioural Adaptations: Archaebacteria thrive in hot springs/hydrothermal vents (>100°C) due to heat-stable enzymes; Antarctic fish prevent body fluids from freezing below 0°C; deep-sea organisms withstand >100 atm pressure. Desert lizards regulate body temperature behaviourally by basking in the sun when cool and moving into shade or burrowing when hot.

Population Attributes and Growth Dynamics

A population is a group of individuals of the same species inhabiting a well-defined geographical area, sharing or competing for similar resources, and potentially interbreeding (ecological studies also treat asexually reproducing groups, such as bacteria in a culture plate or lotus plants in a pond, as populations). Natural selection operates at the population level.

Population Attributes (Absent in Individuals)

  1. Per Capita Birth Rate and Death Rate: Expressed as change in numbers per individual per unit time (e.g., births per individual per year).
  2. Sex Ratio: The proportion of males to females in a population (e.g., 60% females, 40% males).
  3. Age Distribution and Age Pyramids: Plotting the percentage of individuals across pre-reproductive, reproductive, and post-reproductive age groups yields an age pyramid whose shape reflects population growth status.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Age pyramids illustrating expanding (broad base), stable (bell-shaped), and declining (urn-shaped, narrow base) populations.

  1. Population Density (\(N\)): Usually measured as total number of individuals, but when counting is impractical or misleading, ecologists use percent cover or biomass (e.g., comparing 200 Parthenium plants to a single huge banyan tree) or relative/indirect measures (e.g., fish caught per trap in a lake, or tiger census via pug marks and fecal pellets).

Processes Governing Population Density

Four basic processes cause population density (\(N\)) to fluctuate over time: Natality (\(B\)) (births) and Immigration (\(I\)) (individuals entering from elsewhere) increase \(N\), whereas Mortality (\(D\)) (deaths) and Emigration (\(E\)) (individuals leaving) decrease \(N\). Under normal conditions, \(B\) and \(D\) dominate; during colonisation of a new habitat, \(I\) can be more significant.

Tutorial Diagram 4: The four primary demographic processes determining changes in population density (\(N\)).

Population Density Change Equation \[ N_{t+1} = N_t + \big[(B + I) - (D + E)\big] \]

Population Growth Models

1. Exponential (Geometric) Growth: When food and space are unlimited, a population realises its full innate potential to grow. If per capita birth rate is \(b\) and per capita death rate is \(d\), then \(r = (b - d)\) is the intrinsic rate of natural increase (a key parameter for assessing biotic/abiotic impacts; for example, \(r = 0.015\) for Norway rat, \(0.12\) for flour beetle, and \(0.0205\) for India's human population in 1981). Plotting \(N\) against time yields a J-shaped curve.

Exponential Growth (Differential & Integral Forms) \[ \frac{dN}{dt} = rN \qquad \text{and} \qquad N_t = N_0 e^{rt} \]

where \(N_0\) = initial density, \(N_t\) = density after time \(t\), and \(e \approx 2.71828\) (base of natural logarithms).

2. Verhulst–Pearl Logistic Growth: In nature, resources are finite and eventually become limiting. Every habitat has a maximum sustainable population size for a species called its carrying capacity (\(K\)). A population growing under limited resources exhibits an initial lag phase, followed by phases of acceleration and deceleration, and finally an asymptote at \(N = K\). Plotting \(N\) against time produces a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve. Because resources are finite in nature, the logistic model is considered more realistic.

Verhulst–Pearl Logistic Growth Equation \[ \frac{dN}{dt} = rN \left( \frac{K - N}{K} \right) \]

Tutorial Diagram 5: Comparison of exponential growth (\(\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\), Curve a) and Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth (\(\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(\frac{K - N}{K}\right)\), Curve b).

Life History Variation

Populations evolve to maximise their Darwinian (reproductive) fitness (high \(r\) value) under habitat constraints. Some organisms breed only once in their lifetime (e.g., Pacific salmon fish, bamboo), whereas others breed repeatedly (most birds and mammals). Some produce numerous small offspring (oysters, pelagic fishes), while others produce a few large offspring (birds, mammals).

Interspecific Population Interactions

No species exists in complete isolation. Even autotrophs depend on soil microbes for nutrient recycling and animals for pollination. Interactions between populations of two different species are classified by whether each species benefits (\(+\)), is harmed (\(-\)), or remains unaffected (\(0\)).

Interaction Species A Species B Core Mechanism & High-Yield NEET Examples
Mutualism \(+\) \(+\) Both species benefit, often showing tight co-evolution. Examples: Lichens (fungus + algae/cyanobacteria); Mycorrhizae (fungi + plant roots); Fig tree and pollinator wasp (female wasp pollinates inflorescence while laying eggs and larvae feed on some developing seeds); Mediterranean orchid Ophrys employing sexual deceit (petal mimics female bee, triggering pseudocopulation by male bee).
Competition \(-\) \(-\) Fitness (\(r\)) of one species is significantly lowered by another. Can occur between unrelated species (flamingoes & resident fishes competing for zooplankton in South American lakes) and even when resources are abundant (interference competition). Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle: closely related species competing for limiting resources cannot coexist indefinitely (e.g., Abingdon tortoise excluded by goats in Galapagos). Competitive release: Connell's experiment on Scottish rocky coasts where removing larger barnacle Balanus allowed smaller Chthamalus to expand. Co-existence via resource partitioning (MacArthur's 5 warbler species foraging differently on the same tree).
Predation \(+\) \(-\) Transfers fixed energy to higher trophic levels, regulates prey density, and maintains species diversity (e.g., removing starfish Pisaster from Pacific rocky intertidal zones caused >10 invertebrate species to go extinct via competition). Basis of biological pest control (prickly pear cactus in Australia controlled by cactus-feeding moth). Prey defences: camouflage; Monarch butterfly acquiring distasteful chemical from poisonous weed as caterpillar; plant thorns (Acacia, Cactus) and toxic chemicals (Calotropis cardiac glycosides; nicotine, caffeine, quinine, strychnine, opium).
Parasitism \(+\) \(-\) Host-specific co-evolution; adaptations include loss of sense organs/digestive system, adhesive organs/suckers, and high reproductive capacity. Complex life cycles: human liver fluke uses two intermediate hosts (snail and fish); malarial parasite uses mosquito vector. Ectoparasites: lice, ticks, marine copepods, Cuscuta (lacks chlorophyll/leaves). Endoparasites: live inside liver, kidney, lungs, RBCs. Brood parasitism: cuckoo (koel) lays eggs mimicking crow's eggs in crow's nest. Note: Female mosquito is not a parasite because it needs blood only for reproduction, not continuous lodging.
Commensalism \(+\) \(0\) One benefits, the other is neither harmed nor benefited. Examples: Orchid epiphyte on mango branch; barnacles on whale's back; cattle egret foraging near grazing cattle (flushed-out insects); clown fish living among sea anemone's stinging tentacles.
Amensalism \(-\) \(0\) One species is harmed while the other remains unaffected (e.g., antibiotics/inhibitory secretions from one microbe harming another).

Worked Examples

Calculating Per Capita Birth and Death Rates

Scenario (Assumed Values): A freshwater pond initially contains \(50\) water-lily plants. Over one year, vegetative propagation and seed germination add \(15\) new plants, while \(5\) plants die. Separately, in a laboratory culture of \(80\) fruit flies, \(12\) individuals die over a period of one week. Calculate the per capita birth rate of the water-lily population and the per capita death rate of the fruit-fly population.

Solution:

  1. Per capita birth rate (water lilies): \[ b = \frac{\text{Number of new individuals added}}{\text{Initial population size}} = \frac{15}{50} = 0.30\,\text{offspring per plant per year} \]
  2. Per capita death rate (fruit flies): \[ d = \frac{\text{Number of deaths}}{\text{Initial population size}} = \frac{12}{80} = 0.15\,\text{individuals per fly per week} \]

Applying the Population Density Change Equation

Scenario (Assumed Values): At the start of a study year (\(t\)), a wildlife sanctuary has \(N_t = 420\) spotted deer. During the year, \(65\) fawns are born (\(B\)), \(18\) deer immigrate into the sanctuary (\(I\)), \(40\) deer die (\(D\)), and \(23\) deer emigrate (\(E\)). Determine the deer population at time \(t + 1\).

Solution:

\[ N_{t+1} = N_t + \big[(B + I) - (D + E)\big] \] \[ N_{t+1} = 420 + \big[(65 + 18) - (40 + 23)\big] = 420 + (83 - 63) = 420 + 20 = 440\text{ deer} \]

Since \((B + I) > (D + E)\), the population density increases by \(20\) individuals.

Applying the Exponential Growth Equation

Scenario (Assumed Values): A bacterial culture in a nutrient-rich medium (unlimited resources) starts with \(N_0 = 500\) cells and grows exponentially with an intrinsic rate of natural increase \(r = 0.2\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}\). Estimate the population size after \(t = 5\,\mathrm{h}\) (take \(e^1 \approx 2.718\)).

Solution:

\[ N_t = N_0 e^{rt} \] \[ N_5 = 500 \times e^{(0.2 \times 5)} = 500 \times e^1 \approx 500 \times 2.718 = 1359\text{ cells} \]

Evaluating Growth Rate Under Carrying Capacity

Scenario (Assumed Values): A lake has a carrying capacity \(K = 1000\) fish for a particular species with \(r = 0.4\,\mathrm{year}^{-1}\). Calculate the population growth rate \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) when (a) \(N = 200\) and (b) \(N = 500\), assuming Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth.

Solution:

  1. At \(N = 200\): \[ \frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(\frac{K - N}{K}\right) = 0.4 \times 200 \times \left(\frac{1000 - 200}{1000}\right) = 80 \times 0.8 = 64\,\mathrm{fish\,year}^{-1} \]
  2. At \(N = 500\) (note \(N = K/2\)): \[ \frac{dN}{dt} = 0.4 \times 500 \times \left(\frac{1000 - 500}{1000}\right) = 200 \times 0.5 = 100\,\mathrm{fish\,year}^{-1} \]

Notice that the absolute growth rate \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) peaks at \(N = K/2\) and drops to \(0\) when \(N = K\).

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following correctly explains why very small mammals such as shrews are rarely found in polar regions?

  • (A) They are stenohaline and cannot tolerate low salinity.
  • (B) They have a large surface-area-to-volume ratio, leading to rapid heat loss and high metabolic cost for thermoregulation.
  • (C) They lack hemoglobin and cannot acclimatise to low oxygen levels.
  • (D) They undergo obligate diapause during winter months.
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Heat loss or gain is a function of surface area. Small animals have a larger surface area relative to their volume, so they lose body heat rapidly in cold environments and must expend enormous metabolic energy to maintain a constant internal temperature.

Practice Question 2 Statement-Based

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: A person travelling rapidly to a high-altitude region above \(3,500\text{ m}\) may experience altitude sickness due to low atmospheric pressure and reduced oxygen availability.

Statement II: During acclimatisation at high altitude, the body increases red blood cell production, increases the oxygen-binding affinity of hemoglobin, and decreases the breathing rate.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because the body compensates for low oxygen availability by decreasing (not increasing) the binding affinity of hemoglobin (facilitating oxygen unloading at tissues) and increasing (not decreasing) the breathing rate, alongside increasing RBC production.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

In an ecological survey of a forest plot, researchers record \(180\) herbaceous Parthenium plants and \(1\) massive banyan tree with an extensive canopy. Which measure of population density best reflects the ecological role of the banyan tree in this community?

  • (A) Absolute numerical count
  • (B) Per capita natality rate
  • (C) Percent cover or biomass
  • (D) Sex ratio
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: When comparing organisms of vastly different sizes (such as one huge banyan tree versus hundreds of small herbs), numerical count underestimates the ecological significance of the large tree; therefore, percent cover or biomass is the most meaningful measure.

Practice Question 5 Numerical / Calculation

A hypothetical population of \(500\) individuals is growing logistically in a habitat with a carrying capacity \(K = 2000\) and an intrinsic rate of natural increase \(r = 0.2\,\mathrm{year}^{-1}\). What is the growth rate \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) of this population at this instant?

  • (A) \(50\,\mathrm{individuals\,year}^{-1}\)
  • (B) \(75\,\mathrm{individuals\,year}^{-1}\)
  • (C) \(100\,\mathrm{individuals\,year}^{-1}\)
  • (D) \(150\,\mathrm{individuals\,year}^{-1}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Using the Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth equation: \[ \frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(\frac{K - N}{K}\right) = 0.2 \times 500 \times \left(\frac{2000 - 500}{2000}\right) = 100 \times \frac{1500}{2000} = 100 \times 0.75 = 75\,\mathrm{individuals\,year}^{-1}. \]

Practice Question 6 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): When the starfish Pisaster was experimentally removed from an enclosed rocky intertidal area of the American Pacific coast, more than \(10\) species of invertebrates became extinct within a year.

Reason (R): Predators help maintain species diversity in a community by reducing the intensity of competition among competing prey species.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Pisaster is a keystone predator that keeps dominant competitors in check. Removing it allowed interspecific competition among prey invertebrates to intensify, driving over \(10\) species to local extinction.

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following pairs of interactions and examples is incorrectly matched?

  • (A) Commensalism — Cattle egret and grazing cattle
  • (B) Mutualism — Mediterranean orchid Ophrys and its bee pollinator
  • (C) Parasitism — Female mosquito feeding on human blood
  • (D) Resource partitioning — Five closely related warbler species on the same tree
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: The female mosquito is not considered a true parasite because it requires human blood only as a nutritional supplement for reproduction (egg development), rather than living in/on the host for shelter and continuous nourishment. All other pairs are correctly matched.

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

On the rocky sea coasts of Scotland, the larger barnacle Balanus dominates the intertidal zone and excludes the smaller barnacle Chthamalus. When Balanus is experimentally removed, Chthamalus rapidly expands its distributional range. This phenomenon is termed:

  • (A) Competitive exclusion
  • (B) Competitive release
  • (C) Resource partitioning
  • (D) Interference competition
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Connell's field experiment demonstrated competitive release: a species whose distribution is restricted to a small area by a competitively superior species expands dramatically when the superior competitor is removed.

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

In an age pyramid representing a human population, if the number of individuals in the pre-reproductive age group is distinctly smaller than the number of individuals in the reproductive age group, the population is:

  • (A) Rapidly expanding
  • (B) Stable

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Female Mosquito Misconception: Do not classify the female mosquito as a parasite; it uses blood only for reproduction.
  • Logistic Growth Rate Maximum: While population size \(N\) reaches its maximum at \(K\), the growth rate \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) is zero at \(N = K\) and maximum at \(N = \frac{K}{2}\).
  • Competition Without Limiting Resources: Competition can still occur when resources are abundant via interference competition (inhibitory presence of another species).
  • Acclimatisation vs. Evolution: High-altitude RBC increase is a reversible physiological acclimatisation, whereas Allen's Rule (short ears/limbs) is a genetically fixed evolutionary adaptation.
  • Ophrys Orchid: Pollination occurs by sexual deceit (pseudocopulation), not by offering nectar or pollen rewards.
High-Yield Revision Checklist
  • Can you state the salinity thresholds for inland waters (<5 ppt), sea (30–35 ppt), and hypersaline lagoons (>100 ppt)?
  • Can you distinguish hibernation (winter), aestivation (summer), and diapause (zooplankton suspended development)?
  • Do you remember both forms of the exponential equation (\(\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\), \(N_t = N_0 e^{rt}\)) and the logistic equation (\(\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\frac{K-N}{K}\))?
  • Can you list at least two NCERT-aligned examples for each of the six interspecific interactions?

Concise Final Recap

Ecology links organismic physiology to population dynamics and community interactions. Temperature, water, light, and soil define habitat conditions, while organisms respond via regulation, conformation, migration, or suspension. Population density (\(N\)) fluctuates through natality, immigration, mortality, and emigration, growing exponentially under unlimited resources and logistically toward carrying capacity (\(K\)) under resource limitation. Finally, species co-exist and evolve within communities through mutualism, competition, predation, parasitism, commensalism, and amensalism.
