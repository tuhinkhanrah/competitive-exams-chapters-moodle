2. Foundations and Core Principles / जैव प्रौद्योगिकी की आधारशिला एवं मुख्य सिद्धांत

English

Meaning and EFB Definition

Biotechnology harnesses live organisms or their enzymes to create products and processes beneficial to humans. While traditional practices like curdling milk, baking bread, or fermenting wine rely on natural microbes, modern biotechnology uses genetically modified organisms (GMOs) alongside techniques such as in vitro fertilisation, gene synthesis, DNA vaccine development, and gene therapy.

The European Federation of Biotechnology (EFB) defines biotechnology as: "The integration of natural science and organisms, cells, parts thereof, and molecular analogues for products and services."

The Two Core Pillars

  1. Genetic Engineering: Techniques that chemically alter genetic material (DNA or RNA), introduce it into a host organism, and thereby modify the host's phenotype.
  2. Bioprocess Engineering: Maintaining a strictly sterile (microbial contamination-free) environment in chemical engineering vessels so that only the desired microbe or eukaryotic cell multiplies in large quantities to yield antibiotics, vaccines, or enzymes.

Why Genetic Engineering Outperforms Traditional Hybridisation

Asexual reproduction preserves existing genetic information, whereas sexual reproduction generates variation. Traditional plant and animal hybridisation mixes entire genomes, frequently introducing undesirable traits alongside desirable ones. Recombinant DNA technology overcomes this limitation by isolating and transferring only specific desirable genes into the target organism.

Origin of Replication (ori) and the First Recombinant DNA (1972)

An isolated piece of alien DNA transferred into a host cell cannot replicate on its own unless it integrates into a host chromosome or links to a vector carrying an origin of replication (ori)—the specific DNA sequence where replication initiates. Linking alien DNA to an ori enables gene cloning (producing multiple identical copies).

In 1972, Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer constructed the first artificial recombinant DNA molecule. They isolated an antibiotic resistance gene from a native plasmid of Salmonella typhimurium using restriction enzymes ("molecular scissors"), joined it to another plasmid vector using DNA ligase, and introduced the recombinant plasmid into Escherichia coli, where it replicated using the host's DNA polymerase.

Three Basic Steps in Genetically Modifying an Organism

1. Identification of DNA carrying desirable genes → 2. Introduction of the identified DNA into the host → 3. Maintenance of the introduced DNA in the host and its transfer to progeny.

3. Tools of Recombinant DNA Technology / पुनर्योगज डीएनए तकनीक के साधन

English

3.1 Restriction Enzymes (Molecular Scissors)

In 1963, two enzymes that restrict bacteriophage growth in E. coli were isolated: one added methyl groups to DNA (methylase), and the other cut DNA (restriction endonuclease). The first characterised restriction endonuclease, Hind II (isolated five years later), always cleaves DNA at a specific 6-base-pair (6 bp) recognition sequence. Today, over 900 restriction enzymes have been isolated from more than 230 bacterial strains.

Naming Convention

  • First letter: Genus of the source bacterium (e.g., E from Escherichia).
  • Second and third letters: Species of the source bacterium (e.g., co from coli).
  • Fourth letter: Strain designation (e.g., R from strain RY 13).
  • Roman numeral: Order of enzyme isolation from that strain (e.g., I = first isolated).

Exonucleases vs. Endonucleases & Palindromic Sequences

Restriction enzymes belong to the class nucleases. Exonucleases remove nucleotides from the ends of DNA strands, whereas endonucleases make internal cuts at specific positions.

Each restriction endonuclease inspects DNA until it locates its specific palindromic nucleotide sequence—a base-pair sequence that reads the exact same on both complementary strands when read in the same polarity (e.g., 5′ → 3′ on both strands):

5′-G A A T T C-3′
3′-C T T A A G-5′

Enzymes such as EcoRI cut both strands slightly away from the centre of the palindrome (between G and A on each strand), leaving single-stranded overhanging stretches called sticky ends. Complementary sticky ends form hydrogen bonds, facilitating permanent joining of the sugar-phosphate backbone by DNA ligase. Crucially, both vector DNA and foreign DNA must be cut with the same restriction enzyme to generate compatible sticky ends.

3.2 Separation and Isolation of DNA Fragments (Gel Electrophoresis)

Restriction digestion produces DNA fragments of varying lengths. Because DNA carries a net negative charge (due to phosphate groups), fragments migrate toward the anode (positive electrode) when placed in an electric field across an agarose gel matrix (a natural polymer extracted from seaweeds).

  • Sieving Effect: Agarose acts as a molecular sieve; smaller fragments travel farther toward the anode, while larger fragments remain closer to the loading wells.
  • Visualisation: Pure DNA is invisible in visible light. The gel is stained with ethidium bromide and exposed to UV radiation, revealing bright orange bands.
  • Elution: Desired DNA bands are excised from the agarose gel and extracted/purified for ligation into cloning vectors—this extraction step is called elution.

3.3 Cloning Vectors and Selection of Recombinants

Plasmids and bacteriophages replicate autonomously inside bacterial cells independent of chromosomal DNA. High-copy-number vectors (15–100+ copies per cell) allow proportional multiplication of any linked foreign DNA. Essential engineered features of a cloning vector include:

  1. Origin of Replication (ori): Initiates replication and controls the copy number of the linked DNA.
  2. Selectable Marker: Eliminates non-transformants and permits selective growth of transformants. Common markers for E. coli are genes conferring resistance to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline, or kanamycin (normal E. coli lacks resistance to these antibiotics).
  3. Cloning Sites: Ideally a single recognition site for each commonly used restriction enzyme. Multiple sites for the same enzyme fragment the vector and complicate cloning.

Two Strategies to Distinguish Recombinants from Non-Recombinants

Method Mechanism Outcome for Recombinants vs. Non-Recombinants
Antibiotic Resistance (e.g., pBR322) Foreign DNA is ligated at a site inside one antibiotic resistance gene (e.g., BamHI site in tetR), inactivating it while keeping ampR intact. Transformants grow on ampicillin. Recombinants die on tetracycline (ampR, tetS); Non-recombinants grow on both (ampR, tetR). Requires cumbersome replica plating on two media.
Insertional Inactivation (Blue-White Screening) Foreign DNA is inserted within the coding sequence of the enzyme β-galactosidase, inactivating the gene in the presence of a chromogenic substrate. Non-recombinants (intact gene) produce active enzyme → blue colonies. Recombinants (inactivated gene) produce no enzyme → white/colourless colonies.

Vectors for Plants and Animals

  • Agrobacterium tumifaciens (Dicot Plants): Naturally transfers T-DNA via its Ti (tumor-inducing) plasmid to induce crown gall tumors. Disarmed Ti plasmids retain gene-delivery ability without causing pathogenicity.
  • Retroviruses (Animals): Naturally transform normal animal cells into cancerous cells; disarmed retroviruses safely deliver desirable genes into animal cells.

3.4 Making the Host Competent and DNA Delivery Methods

DNA is a hydrophilic molecule and cannot cross the hydrophobic lipid bilayer of cell membranes unaided.

  • Chemical + Heat Shock (Bacteria): Bacterial cells are treated with a specific concentration of a divalent cation such as calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)) to increase cell-wall pore permeability. Cells are incubated with recombinant DNA on ice, briefly given a heat shock at \(42^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and immediately returned to ice to force DNA uptake.
  • Micro-injection (Animal Cells): Recombinant DNA is injected directly into the nucleus of an animal cell using a fine micropipette.
  • Biolistics / Gene Gun (Plant Cells): Plant cells are bombarded with high-velocity microparticles of gold or tungsten coated with DNA.
  • Disarmed Pathogen Vectors: Modified Ti plasmid or disarmed retroviruses infect host cells and transfer recombinant DNA.

4. Processes of Recombinant DNA Technology / पुनर्योगज डीएनए तकनीक के प्रक्रम

English

Step 1: Isolation of Genetic Material (DNA)

To cut DNA cleanly with restriction enzymes, it must be freed from membranes and other macromolecules (RNA, proteins, polysaccharides, lipids):

  • Cell Lysis Enzymes: Lysozyme (bacteria), Cellulase (plant cells), Chitinase (fungal cells).
  • Macromolecule Removal: Ribonuclease (RNase) digests RNA; Protease digests proteins (such as histones).
  • Precipitation & Spooling: Adding chilled ethanol precipitates purified DNA as fine threads, which are collected by spooling.

Step 2: Cutting at Specific Locations & Ligation

Purified source DNA and vector DNA are digested separately with the same restriction enzyme under optimal conditions. Agarose gel electrophoresis verifies digestion progression. The gene of interest and cut vector are mixed with DNA ligase to form recombinant DNA.

Step 3: Amplification of Gene of Interest using PCR

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) synthesises multiple copies of a target DNA segment in vitro using two sets of chemically synthesised primers (oligonucleotides complementary to flanking regions), deoxyribonucleotides (dNTPs), and a thermostable DNA polymerase—Taq polymerase, isolated from the thermophilic bacterium Thermus aquaticus.

  1. Denaturation (approx. \(94^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): Heat separates double-stranded DNA into two single-stranded templates.
  2. Primer Annealing (approx. \(50\text{–}60^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): Forward and reverse primers bind to complementary 3′ ends of the template strands.
  3. Extension (approx. \(72^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): Taq polymerase extends primers in the 5′ → 3′ direction using dNTPs, remaining active despite prior high-temperature denaturation.
Theoretical amplification after \(n\) cycles: \(\quad N = N_0 \times 2^n \quad\) (For \(n = 30\) cycles, \(2^{30} \approx 1.07 \times 10^9 \approx 1\text{ billion copies}\))

Step 4 & 5: Obtaining the Foreign Gene Product & Bioreactors

When a foreign gene is expressed in a heterologous host, the resulting protein is termed a recombinant protein. To maximise yield:

  • Continuous Culture System: Used medium is drained from one side while fresh medium is added from the other, keeping cells in their physiologically most active log (exponential) phase, generating larger biomass and higher protein yields.
  • Bioreactors (100–1000 litres): Large vessels that biologically convert raw materials into specific products under tightly controlled temperature, pH, substrate, salts, vitamins, and oxygen levels.
  • Stirred-Tank Bioreactors: Cylindrical with a curved base to facilitate even mixing. Components include an agitator (flat-bladed impeller), oxygen delivery system, foam breaker, temperature control, pH control, and sampling ports. In a sparged stirred-tank bioreactor, sterile air bubbles dramatically increase the surface area for oxygen transfer.

Step 6: Downstream Processing

Once biosynthesis is complete, the crude product undergoes separation and purification—collectively called downstream processing. For therapeutics, the purified product is formulated with suitable preservatives, subjected to strict quality control testing, and evaluated through clinical trials before marketing.

Worked Examples

English

Predicting Fragment Sizes in Restriction Digestion

Scenario (Assumed): A linear DNA molecule of length \(12\,\mathrm{kb}\) carries two recognition sites for EcoRI located at positions \(3\,\mathrm{kb}\) and \(8\,\mathrm{kb}\) from the left end. Meanwhile, a circular plasmid of \(12\,\mathrm{kb}\) also carries two EcoRI sites separated by \(4\,\mathrm{kb}\) along the shorter arc. How many fragments and of what sizes are obtained upon complete digestion of each with EcoRI, and how will they arrange on an agarose gel?

Reasoning:

  • For a linear DNA with \(k\) internal restriction sites, complete digestion yields \(k + 1\) fragments. With \(k = 2\) sites at \(3\,\mathrm{kb}\) and \(8\,\mathrm{kb}\), the fragment sizes are: \(3\,\mathrm{kb}\), \((8 - 3) = 5\,\mathrm{kb}\), and \((12 - 8) = 4\,\mathrm{kb}\) (total 3 fragments).
  • For a circular plasmid with \(k\) sites (\(k \ge 1\)), complete digestion yields \(k\) fragments. With \(k = 2\) sites, the two fragments are \(4\,\mathrm{kb}\) and \((12 - 4) = 8\,\mathrm{kb}\) (total 2 fragments).
  • Agarose Gel Migration: Smaller fragments migrate farther toward the anode. For the linear digest, the migration order from farthest to closest to the well is \(3\,\mathrm{kb} > 4\,\mathrm{kb} > 5\,\mathrm{kb}\).

7. Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist / सामान्य भ्रांतियाँ एवं पुनरीक्षण सूची

English

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Electrode Confusion: DNA moves toward the anode (+), never the cathode (−).
  • Enzyme Specificity: Vector and foreign DNA must be cut with the same restriction enzyme to produce compatible sticky ends; cutting with different enzymes usually prevents ligation.
  • Lysis Enzymes Mix-up: Lysozyme is for bacteria, cellulase for plants, and chitinase for fungi.
  • PCR Step Order: Always Denaturation → Annealing → Extension (never annealing before denaturation).

High-Yield Revision Checklist

  • 1972: Cohen & Boyer — first rDNA (S. typhimurium antibiotic resistance gene + plasmid in E. coli).
  • Hind II: First restriction endonuclease (6 bp recognition sequence).
  • EcoRI: Escherichia coli RY 13, 1st isolated; cuts 5′-G|AATTC-3′.
  • pBR322 markers: ampR (PstI, PvuI) and tetR (BamHI, SalI); rop codes for replication proteins.
  • Chilled ethanol precipitates purified DNA → removed by spooling.
  • Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus survives \(94^\circ\mathrm{C}\) denaturation.

8. Concise Final Recap / संक्षिप्त अंतिम सारांश

English

Modern biotechnology combines genetic engineering (creating recombinant DNA using restriction endonucleases, DNA ligase, and cloning vectors) with bioprocess engineering (sterile large-scale cultivation in bioreactors). Once purified DNA is cut, amplified via PCR using Taq polymerase, and introduced into a competent host, transformants and recombinants are screened using selectable markers or blue-white insertional inactivation. Finally, large-scale recombinant protein production in 100–1000 L stirred-tank bioreactors is followed by rigorous downstream processing (separation, purification, formulation, and quality control).
