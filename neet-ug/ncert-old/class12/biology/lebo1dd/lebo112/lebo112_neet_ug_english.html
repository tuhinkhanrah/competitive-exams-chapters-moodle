Biotechnology and Its Applications

From genetically engineered crops and recombinant therapeutics to transgenic animal models and bioethics

Learning Objectives and Scope

Biotechnology harnesses genetically modified microbes, fungi, plants, and animals for the industrial-scale manufacture of biologicals and biopharmaceuticals. Its reach spans therapeutics, molecular diagnostics, genetically modified (GM) crops, processed foods, bioremediation, waste treatment, and energy generation.

What You Will Master for NEET UG
  • The three core research pillars required to turn a biological discovery into a commercial product.
  • How recombinant DNA technology and RNA interference (RNAi) protect crops against insects and nematodes.
  • The molecular maturation of human insulin, the rationale behind ex vivo gene therapy for ADA deficiency, and early disease detection via PCR, radioactive probes, and ELISA.
  • The five major biomedical and industrial roles of transgenic animals, including specific landmark examples.
  • Regulatory oversight in India (GEAC) and the legal/ethical dimensions of biopiracy and patenting.

Core Pillars of Applied Biotechnology

Translating genetic engineering into reliable real-world products depends on three interconnected research areas:

  1. Providing the best catalyst: Developing an improved organism (typically a microbe) or isolating a pure enzyme with high catalytic efficiency.
  2. Creating optimal operating conditions: Using biochemical engineering (such as bioreactors) to maintain ideal pH, temperature, aeration, and nutrient levels for the catalyst to act.
  3. Downstream processing technologies: Separating, purifying, and formulating the desired protein or organic compound for safe commercial or clinical use.

Biotechnological Applications in Agriculture

Why Genetically Modified Crops?

Three broad strategies can raise global food production: agro-chemical-based agriculture, organic agriculture, and genetically engineered crop-based agriculture. Although the Green Revolution tripled food supply through improved varieties, better management, and agrochemicals (fertilisers and pesticides), conventional breeding has reached a yield ceiling and agrochemicals remain expensive and environmentally harmful.

Organisms—plants, bacteria, fungi, or animals—whose genomes have been altered by artificial gene manipulation are termed Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs). Genetic modification confers five major agricultural advantages:

  • Abiotic stress tolerance: Greater resilience against cold, drought, salinity, and heat.
  • Reduced pesticide dependence: Built-in pest resistance lowers chemical insecticide use.
  • Lower post-harvest losses: Improved shelf life and reduced spoilage after harvesting.
  • Efficient mineral usage: Better nutrient uptake prevents premature exhaustion of soil fertility.
  • Enhanced nutritional value: Biofortification, exemplified by Golden Rice enriched with Vitamin A (beta-carotene precursor).

Additionally, tailor-made GM plants supply alternative industrial raw materials such as starches, biofuels, and pharmaceuticals.

Bt Toxin and Insect-Resistant Crops

Certain strains of the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) synthesise crystal proteins during a specific growth phase. These crystals contain a toxic insecticidal protein encoded by cry genes. Because different cry proteins target specific insect orders, the choice of gene depends on both the crop and the pest.

Insect Order Targeted Representative Pests cry Gene / Crop Application
Lepidopterans Tobacco budworm, armyworm, cotton bollworms, corn borer cryIAc and cryIIAb control cotton bollworms; cryIAb controls corn borer
Coleopterans Beetles Specific cry variants cloned into target crops
Dipterans Flies, mosquitoes Specific cry variants targeting dipteran larvae

Mechanism of Action: Inside Bacillus thuringiensis, the toxin exists as an inactive protoxin, which is why it does not harm the bacterium itself. When a susceptible insect ingests the plant tissue expressing the cloned Bt gene, the alkaline pH of the insect's midgut solubilises the protein crystals and converts the protoxin into its active form. The activated toxin binds to receptors on the surface of midgut epithelial cells, creating pores that trigger cell swelling, lysis, and eventual death of the insect.

Nematode Resistance via RNA Interference (RNAi)

The root-knot nematode Meloidogyne incognita infects the roots of tobacco plants and severely reduces crop yield. To counter this parasite, biotechnologists exploit RNA interference (RNAi)—a natural cellular defense mechanism present in all eukaryotic organisms.

  • Core principle of RNAi: A specific messenger RNA (mRNA) is silenced when a complementary double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) molecule binds to it and prevents its translation.
  • Natural sources of complementary RNA: Infections by RNA-genome viruses or mobile genetic elements (transposons) that replicate through an RNA intermediate.
  • Transgenic strategy in tobacco: Using Agrobacterium vectors, nematode-specific genes are introduced into the host tobacco plant in an orientation that transcribes both sense and anti-sense RNA inside the host cells.
  • Outcome: Because the sense and anti-sense strands are complementary, they pair to form dsRNA, initiating RNAi and silencing the essential nematode mRNA. Unable to express vital proteins, the parasite cannot survive in the transgenic host.

Biotechnological Applications in Medicine

Recombinant DNA technology enables the mass production of safe, highly effective human therapeutics. Unlike products extracted from non-human animals, recombinant human proteins do not provoke unwanted immune reactions or carry animal-borne infections. Worldwide, about 30 recombinant therapeutics have been approved for human use, with 12 currently marketed in India.

Genetically Engineered Insulin

Historically, insulin for managing adult-onset diabetes was extracted from the pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs. Because animal insulin differs slightly from human insulin, it caused allergies and adverse immune responses in some patients. Moreover, insulin is a protein hormone and cannot be administered orally because digestive proteases in the human alimentary canal would hydrolyse it before absorption.

Mature human insulin consists of two short polypeptide chains—Chain A and Chain B—linked together by disulphide bridges. In mammals, insulin is initially synthesised as a pro-hormone (pro-insulin) containing an extra stretch called the C-peptide, which is cleaved away during maturation into functional insulin.

In 1983, the American company Eli Lilly overcame the challenge of assembling mature insulin in bacteria (which lack eukaryotic post-translational processing machinery) by preparing two separate DNA sequences corresponding to human Chain A and Chain B, inserting them into Escherichia coli plasmids, producing the two chains independently, extracting them, and chemically joining them via disulphide bonds to yield functional human insulin (Humulin).

Gene Therapy

Gene therapy comprises methods designed to correct a hereditary genetic defect diagnosed in a child or embryo by delivering a functional normal gene into the patient's cells and tissues to compensate for the non-functional allele.

The first clinical gene therapy was performed in 1990 on a 4-year-old girl suffering from adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency, an immune-system disorder caused by deletion of the ADA gene. While bone marrow transplantation or enzyme replacement therapy (periodic injections of functional ADA) can manage symptoms in some patients, neither approach is permanently curative.

  • Ex vivo lymphocyte therapy: Lymphocytes are isolated from the patient's blood and grown in culture outside the body. A functional ADA cDNA is introduced into these cells using a retroviral vector, and the genetically engineered lymphocytes are infused back into the patient.
  • Limitation: Because mature lymphocytes are not immortal, the patient requires periodic infusions of engineered cells.
  • Permanent cure strategy: Introducing the functional ADA gene isolated from marrow cells into cells at early embryonic stages could provide a permanent cure.

Molecular Diagnosis

Conventional diagnostic methods (serum and urine analysis) typically detect a pathogen only after symptoms appear—when pathogen concentration is already high. Modern molecular techniques allow early diagnosis even at ultra-low pathogen loads:

Diagnostic Method Core Working Principle Key Clinical Applications
Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) Amplifies trace amounts of pathogen or host nucleic acid into millions of detectable copies Early HIV detection in suspected AIDS; detecting gene mutations in suspected cancer patients and genetic disorders
Radioactive Probe & Autoradiography A single-stranded DNA or RNA tagged with a radioactive molecule (probe) hybridises to complementary DNA in a cell clone; detected on photographic film Mutation screening: a clone carrying a mutated gene fails to bind the probe and does not appear on the photographic film
ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay) Based on specific antigen–antibody interaction Detects pathogen antigens (proteins, glycoproteins) or antibodies synthesised by the host against the pathogen

Transgenic Animals

Transgenic animals are animals whose DNA has been manipulated to possess and express an extra (foreign) gene. Transgenic rats, rabbits, pigs, sheep, cows, and fish have been developed, though over 95% of all existing transgenic animals are mice. They serve five primary purposes:

  1. Normal physiology and development: Studying gene regulation and how specific factors—such as insulin-like growth factor—govern normal body function and development by introducing genes from other species that alter factor formation.
  2. Study of disease: Serving as living models for human diseases—including cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and Alzheimer's—to investigate disease mechanisms and test new therapies.
  3. Biological products: Producing costly human therapeutic proteins. Examples include human protein α-1-antitrypsin used to treat emphysema (with ongoing efforts for phenylketonuria and cystic fibrosis). In 1997, the first transgenic cow, Rosie, produced human protein-enriched milk (2.4 g/L) containing human α-lactalbumin, offering a nutritionally more balanced product for human infants than natural cow milk.
  4. Vaccine safety: Transgenic mice are being developed to test vaccine safety before human trials (for example, testing polio vaccine batches, potentially replacing monkeys).
  5. Chemical safety (toxicity) testing: Animals engineered to carry genes that heighten sensitivity to toxic substances allow faster toxicity screening of drugs and chemicals.

Ethical Issues, Regulation, and Biopiracy

Unregulated genetic manipulation raises moral concerns and ecological risks because introducing GMOs into ecosystems can have unpredictable consequences. To oversee this, the Indian Government established the GEAC (Genetic Engineering Approval Committee), which evaluates the validity of GM research and the safety of introducing GMOs for public services.

Biopiracy is the unauthorised use of bio-resources by multinational companies and other organisations without proper authorisation from the countries and indigenous people concerned and without compensatory payment.

Case Highlight: Basmati Rice and Traditional Knowledge
  • India holds immense agricultural biodiversity, with an estimated 200,000 varieties of rice, including 27 documented varieties of Basmati celebrated for centuries in ancient texts and folklore.
  • In 1997, an American company obtained patent rights on Basmati rice through the US Patent and Trademark Office after crossing Indian Basmati with semi-dwarf varieties and claiming it as a novel invention—extending restrictions to functional equivalents.
  • Similar patent attempts have targeted Indian traditional herbal medicines such as turmeric and neem.
  • To counter unauthorised exploitation of biodiversity and traditional knowledge—where industrialised nations are financially rich but biodiversity-poor, while developing nations are biodiversity-rich—the Indian Parliament cleared the second amendment of the Indian Patents Bill, addressing patent terms, emergency provisions, and R&D initiatives.

Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Selecting cry Genes for Targeted Pest Control
Hypothetical Scenario & Assumed Field Trial Data

An agricultural biotechnology team is evaluating three transgenic crop lines—Line X expressing cryIAb, Line Y expressing cryIAc, and Line Z expressing cryIIAb—against two pests: cotton bollworm and corn borer. Predict which lines will protect cotton against bollworms, which will protect maize against corn borer, and explain why the source bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis is not killed while synthesising these toxins.

Solution:

1. Pest-specific protection: Bt toxins are insect-group specific. The proteins encoded by cryIAc (Line Y) and cryIIAb (Line Z) specifically control cotton bollworms, whereas the protein encoded by cryIAb (Line X) controls corn borer.

2. Why the bacterium survives: Inside Bacillus thuringiensis, the insecticidal crystal protein is produced as an inactive protoxin. It becomes active only after ingestion by the insect, when the alkaline pH of the insect midgut solubilises the crystals and converts the protoxin into an active pore-forming toxin.

Worked Example 2: Interpreting Autoradiography for Mutation Screening
Hypothetical Screening Setup (4 Cell Clones)

A diagnostic lab screens four cell clones (Clone 1, Clone 2, Clone 3, and Clone 4) for a suspected single-gene mutation using a radioactive single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) probe complementary to the normal allele. After hybridisation and autoradiography on photographic film, dark bands appear for Clones 1, 3, and 4, while Clone 2 shows no signal. Identify the mutated clone and explain the molecular basis.

Solution:

Mutated clone: Clone 2 carries the mutated gene.

Molecular basis: The radioactive ssDNA probe is complementary to the normal (unmutated) DNA sequence. In Clones 1, 3, and 4, the probe hybridises stably and exposes the photographic film. In Clone 2, the mutation alters the base sequence so the probe lacks complementarity, fails to hybridise, and is washed away—leaving no band on the photographic film.

Worked Example 3: Estimating Therapeutic Protein Yield from Transgenic Milk
Assumed Daily Milk Yield: 15 L/day (Based on Rosie's 2.4 g/L Concentration)

The first transgenic cow, Rosie, produced milk enriched with human α-lactalbumin at a concentration of \(2.4\,\mathrm{g/L}\). Assuming a hypothetical daily milk output of \(15\,\mathrm{L/day}\), calculate the total mass of human α-lactalbumin produced in \(10\,\mathrm{days}\), and state why this milk is superior for human infants compared to natural cow milk.

Solution:

Step 1 — Daily protein yield:

\[ \text{Daily yield} = 2.4\,\mathrm{g/L} \times 15\,\mathrm{L/day} = 36\,\mathrm{g/day} \]

Step 2 — Total yield over \(10\,\mathrm{days}\):

\[ \text{Total yield} = 36\,\mathrm{g/day} \times 10\,\mathrm{days} = 360\,\mathrm{g} \]

Nutritional significance: Because this milk contains human α-lactalbumin, it is nutritionally a more balanced product for human babies than natural cow milk.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Why do crystals of Bt toxin produced by Bacillus thuringiensis fail to kill the bacterium itself?

  • (A) The bacterial cell wall completely blocks toxin entry into the cytoplasm.
  • (B) The toxin is synthesised in an inactive protoxin form that requires alkaline insect gut pH for activation.
  • (C) The bacterium possesses specialised lysosomes that rapidly degrade the toxin.
  • (D) The toxin binds only to eukaryotic nuclear membranes.
Answer: (B)

Explanation: B. thuringiensis produces the insecticidal protein as an inactive protoxin. Once ingested by a susceptible insect, the alkaline pH of the midgut solubilises the crystals and converts the protoxin into the active pore-forming toxin.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In transgenic tobacco plants engineered to resist the nematode Meloidogyne incognita, silencing of the parasite's essential mRNA is triggered by:

  • (A) Double-stranded DNA synthesised by reverse transcriptase in the root nodules.
  • (B) Single-stranded sense RNA that directly degrades the nematode genome.
  • (C) Complementary double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) formed from both sense and anti-sense transcripts.
  • (D) Alkaline proteases secreted into the soil by Agrobacterium vectors.
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Nematode-specific DNA is introduced using Agrobacterium vectors such that it produces both sense and anti-sense RNA in host plant cells. These complementary strands form dsRNA, initiating RNA interference (RNAi) and silencing the specific nematode mRNA.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A 4-year-old child with adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency receives ex vivo gene therapy using retrovirally transduced autologous lymphocytes. Why must this patient return for periodic infusions?

Explanation: Because mature lymphocytes are not immortal, periodic infusions of genetically engineered lymphocytes are required. A permanent cure is only possible if the ADA gene from marrow cells is introduced into cells at early embryonic stages.

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Pro-insulin vs. Mature Insulin: Mature insulin has only Chain A and Chain B joined by disulphide bonds; the C-peptide is absent in mature insulin.
  • Gene Specificity: Do not mix up cryIAb (corn borer) with cryIAc and cryIIAb (cotton bollworms).
  • Autoradiography Readout: A mutated gene clone shows no band on the photographic film because the radioactive probe fails to hybridise.
  • Oral Insulin: Insulin cannot be taken orally because digestive proteases break down the polypeptide chains.
  • Permanent Cure for ADA Deficiency: Ex vivo lymphocyte therapy is temporary (periodic infusions); only early embryonic marrow-gene introduction offers a permanent cure.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • 3 research pillars: best catalyst, optimal engineering conditions, downstream processing.
  • 5 GMO crop benefits: abiotic stress tolerance, fewer chemical pesticides, lower post-harvest loss, better mineral efficiency, enhanced nutrition (Golden Rice = Vitamin A).
  • Bt toxin: inactive protoxin in B. thuringiensis → activated by alkaline insect midgut pH → midgut epithelial pores → swelling, lysis, death.
  • RNAi in tobacco: Agrobacterium vector delivers nematode gene → sense + anti-sense RNA → dsRNA → silences Meloidogyne incognita mRNA.
  • Medical stats: ~30 recombinant therapeutics approved globally, 12 marketed in India; Eli Lilly synthesised A & B chains in E. coli in 1983.
  • Transgenic animals: >95% are mice; α-1-antitrypsin for emphysema; Rosie (1997) milk = 2.4 g/L human α-lactalbumin; transgenic mice for polio vaccine safety.
  • Regulation & Bioethics: GEAC oversees GM safety in India; 200,000 Indian rice varieties (27 Basmati); 1997 US Basmati patent; second amendment of Indian Patents Bill.

Concise Final Recap

Biotechnology bridges molecular genetics and large-scale bioprocessing to solve critical challenges in food security and healthcare. In agriculture, Bt crops and RNAi-protected plants reduce chemical pesticide reliance, while biofortified varieties enhance nutrition. In medicine, recombinant human insulin, ex vivo gene therapy for ADA deficiency, and ultra-sensitive diagnostics (PCR, radioactive probes, ELISA) deliver safer, earlier, and more targeted care. Transgenic animals—predominantly mice—accelerate disease modelling, drug toxicity testing, and biopharmaceutical production, while regulatory bodies such as GEAC and strengthened patent laws safeguard ecological integrity and indigenous bio-resources against biopiracy.
