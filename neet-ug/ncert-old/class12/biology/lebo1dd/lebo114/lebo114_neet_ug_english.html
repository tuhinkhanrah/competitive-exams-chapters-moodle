Ecosystems: Structure, Function, and Dynamics

A complete, exam-focused guide to productivity, decomposition, energy flow, ecological pyramids, succession, nutrient cycling, and ecosystem services.

Learning Objectives and Overview

An ecosystem is a self-regulating functional unit of nature in which living organisms (biotic community) interact continuously with one another and with their surrounding physical environment (abiotic factors). Ecosystems span vast scales—from a small freshwater pond to a tropical rainforest or an ocean—and together form the global biosphere.

What You Will Master
  • How biotic and abiotic components organize into characteristic physical structures such as stratification.
  • Quantitative relationships governing Gross Primary Productivity (GPP), Net Primary Productivity (NPP), and Secondary Productivity.
  • The five simultaneous and sequential processes of decomposition and the chemical and climatic factors controlling their rates.
  • Unidirectional energy flow, trophic levels, food webs, and the 10% energy transfer law.
  • Construction, shapes, exceptions, and limitations of ecological pyramids.
  • Primary and secondary ecological succession along hydrarch and xerarch pathways toward a mesic climax.
  • Gaseous (carbon) versus sedimentary (phosphorus) biogeochemical cycles and the economic valuation of ecosystem services.

Ecosystem Architecture and Functional Pillars

For convenience of study, ecologists classify ecosystems into two broad natural categories—terrestrial (forests, grasslands, deserts) and aquatic (ponds, lakes, wetlands, rivers, estuaries)—alongside man-made (anthropogenic) ecosystems such as crop fields and aquaria.

Structural Features

  • Species Composition: Identifying and enumerating all plant, animal, and microbial species present in a given area defines its species composition.
  • Stratification: The vertical distribution of different species occupying distinct height or depth layers is called stratification. In a mature forest, tall canopy trees occupy the uppermost stratum, shrubs occupy the middle layer, and herbs and grasses form the ground layer.

Four Core Functional Aspects

An ecosystem operates as an integrated unit through four interconnected processes: (1) Productivity, (2) Decomposition, (3) Energy Flow, and (4) Nutrient Cycling.

Model System: A Freshwater Pond

A shallow pond clearly demonstrates all four functional pillars within a compact boundary:

  • Abiotic Component: Water containing dissolved inorganic and organic nutrients, bottom soil sediments, and climatic regulators such as solar radiation, temperature cycles, and day-length.
  • Autotrophs (Producers): Phytoplankton, algae, and floating, submerged, or marginal plants along the pond edges that convert inorganic matter into organic biomass using sunlight.
  • Heterotrophs (Consumers): Zooplankton, free-swimming nekton, and bottom-dwelling (benthic) organisms feeding on producers or other consumers.
  • Decomposers: Fungi, bacteria, and flagellates, especially abundant in the bottom mud, which mineralise dead organic matter back into inorganic nutrients for reuse by autotrophs.

Biological Productivity and Biomass Generation

Sustained ecosystem function depends on a continuous input of solar energy. Primary production is the total amount of biomass or organic matter synthesized per unit area over a given time period by photosynthetic producers. It is measured in terms of dry weight (\(\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\)) or energy (\(\mathrm{kcal\,m^{-2}}\)).

Productivity refers specifically to the rate of biomass production, expressed as \(\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{kcal\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}\), allowing direct comparison across ecosystems.

\[ \mathrm{NPP} = \mathrm{GPP} - R \]
  • Gross Primary Productivity (GPP): The total rate of organic matter production during photosynthesis, including energy subsequently used by plants for their own metabolism.
  • Respiratory Loss (\(R\)): The fraction of GPP consumed by producers in cellular respiration.
  • Net Primary Productivity (NPP): The remaining biomass stored in plant tissues that is available for consumption by heterotrophs (herbivores and decomposers).
  • Secondary Productivity: The rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers through assimilation of ingested food. (Note: Ecologists do not use the term "secondary producers" for herbivores; herbivores are primary consumers.)

Global Productivity Patterns and Limiting Factors

Primary productivity varies sharply across biomes because it depends on the resident plant species, nutrient availability, photosynthetic capacity, and environmental conditions (light, temperature, moisture).

  • The annual NPP of the entire biosphere is approximately 170 billion tons (dry weight) of organic matter.
  • Although oceans cover about 70% of Earth's surface, their annual NPP is only 55 billion tons, whereas terrestrial ecosystems contribute roughly 115 billion tons.
  • Low oceanic productivity results primarily from limited sunlight penetration with depth and scarcity of essential mineral nutrients (such as nitrogen and phosphorus) in open surface waters.

Decomposition: Breakdown and Mineralisation

Detritus—the raw material for decomposition—consists of dead plant remains (fallen leaves, bark, flowers) and dead animal remains, including fecal matter. Decomposers (mainly bacteria and fungi) break down complex organic compounds in detritus into simple inorganic substances such as \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), water, and mineral nutrients.

Step Key Agents / Site Mechanism and Outcome
1. Fragmentation Detritivores (e.g., earthworms) Physical breakdown of large detritus into smaller particles, increasing surface area for microbial action.
2. Leaching Percolating water in soil Water-soluble inorganic nutrients move downward into soil horizons and precipitate as unavailable salts.
3. Catabolism Bacterial and fungal enzymes Enzymatic degradation of detritus into simpler inorganic and low-molecular-weight compounds.
4. Humification Soil microbes Formation of humus—a dark-colored, amorphous, colloidal substance highly resistant to microbial attack that acts as a slow-release nutrient reservoir.
5. Mineralisation Specialized soil microbes Slow degradation of humus to release inorganic nutrients back into the soil solution for plant uptake.
Key Exam Nuance: Timing and Controlling Factors

Fragmentation, leaching, and catabolism operate simultaneously on detritus, whereas humification and mineralisation occur within the soil matrix.

Decomposition is largely an oxygen-requiring (aerobic) process regulated by two main factors:

  • Chemical Composition of Detritus: Decomposition is slower when detritus is rich in lignin and chitin, and faster when it is rich in nitrogen and water-soluble sugars.
  • Climatic Factors: Warm and moist environments accelerate microbial activity and decomposition, whereas low temperature and anaerobiosis (lack of oxygen) inhibit decomposition, leading to organic matter accumulation.

Energy Flow, Food Webs, and the 10% Law

Except for deep-sea hydrothermal vent ecosystems (which rely on chemosynthesis), the Sun is the sole energy source for all ecosystems on Earth. Less than 50% of incident solar radiation is Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR), and plants capture only 2–10% of PAR (equivalent to roughly 1–5% of total incident sunlight). This small fraction sustains the entire living world.

Energy flow obeys the laws of thermodynamics: energy is neither created nor destroyed (First Law), and ecosystems require a constant energy input to synthesize ordered biomolecules and counteract increasing entropy (Second Law). Consequently, energy flow is strictly unidirectional—from Sun to producers to consumers—and dissipates as heat at every transfer.

Unidirectional flow of energy through grazing trophic levels (T1 to T4), respiratory heat dissipation, and dead biomass transfer to the detritus food chain.

Grazing vs. Detritus Food Chains and Food Webs

  • Grazing Food Chain (GFC): Begins with living green plants (producers) → herbivores (primary consumers) → primary carnivores (secondary consumers) → secondary carnivores (tertiary consumers). In aquatic ecosystems, the GFC is the major conduit for energy flow.
  • Detritus Food Chain (DFC): Begins with dead organic matter and is driven by decomposers (saprotrophs—mainly fungi and bacteria) that secrete digestive enzymes externally and absorb simple inorganic nutrients. In terrestrial ecosystems, a much larger fraction of energy flows through the DFC than through the GFC.
  • Food Web: Natural ecosystems consist of interconnected food chains. Organisms of the DFC may serve as prey for GFC animals, and omnivores (such as cockroaches, crows, and humans) feed across multiple chains.

Trophic Levels, Standing Crop, and the 10% Law

  • Trophic Level: A specific functional position an organism occupies in a food chain based on its nutrition source (T1 = Producers, T2 = Herbivores, T3 = Primary Carnivores, T4 = Top Carnivores). A single species can occupy more than one trophic level simultaneously (for example, a sparrow is a primary consumer when eating seeds and a secondary consumer when eating insects).
  • Standing Crop: The mass of living material (biomass) or number of individuals present at a trophic level at a particular time. Measuring biomass in terms of dry weight is more accurate than fresh weight because water content fluctuates widely with environment and physiology.
  • 10% Law: Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next higher level; the remaining ~90% is lost as metabolic heat or passes into detritus. This sharp attenuation restricts the number of trophic levels in a GFC (typically to 3–5 levels).

Ecological Pyramids: Shapes, Exceptions, and Limitations

Ecological pyramids graphically represent the trophic structure of an ecosystem in terms of number, biomass, or energy, with producers forming the base (T1) and top carnivores forming the apex.

Comparison of ecological pyramid geometries: upright energy pyramid, inverted aquatic biomass pyramid, and spindle-shaped tree number pyramid.

  • Pyramid of Number: Upright in grassland and pond ecosystems (millions of producers support fewer herbivores and very few top carnivores). On a single large tree, however, it is inverted (if insects are top-counted) or spindle-shaped (1 tree → many insects → fewer small birds → very few large predatory birds).
  • Pyramid of Biomass: Upright in terrestrial ecosystems (sharp decline in dry weight from producers to top carnivores). In a marine/aquatic ecosystem, it is generally inverted because the standing crop of phytoplankton at any instant is smaller than that of zooplankton and fishes, even though phytoplankton reproduce rapidly enough to sustain them.
  • Pyramid of Energy: Always upright and never inverted, because some energy is invariably lost as heat at every trophic step.
Three Classic Limitations of Ecological Pyramids
  1. They do not account for the same species belonging to two or more trophic levels simultaneously.
  2. They assume a simple linear food chain and fail to accommodate complex food webs.
  3. Saprophytes (decomposers) are not given any place in ecological pyramids despite their vital role.

Ecological Succession: From Bare Substrate to Climax

Ecological succession is the gradual, orderly, and fairly predictable change in the species composition of a given area over time, occurring in parallel with changes in the physical environment. The entire sequence of communities that successively replace one another is called a sere, each transitional stage is a seral community, and the final stable community in near equilibrium with the environment is the climax community. Across successive seral stages, species diversity, number of organisms, and total biomass all increase.

Feature Primary Succession Secondary Succession
Starting Substrate Lifeless area where no living organisms ever existed (bare rock, newly cooled lava, newly formed pond). Area that previously supported a community but lost it (abandoned farmland, burned or cut forest, flooded land).
Soil Availability No soil initially; weathering and soil formation must occur first. Soil or sediment, along with seeds or propagules, is already present.
Rate of Progress Extremely slow (takes hundreds to thousands of years to reach climax). Much faster than primary succession; climax is reached more quickly.

Hydrarch and Xerarch Plant Succession

The first species to invade a bare area are pioneer species. Regardless of whether succession begins in very wet habitats (hydrarch succession) or very dry habitats (xerarch succession), both pathways converge toward medium water conditions—the mesic climax forest.

Convergence of hydrarch (aquatic) and xerarch (bare rock) primary succession to a stable mesic forest climax.

Biogeochemical Nutrient Cycling

Unlike energy, which flows unidirectionally and is dissipated, mineral nutrients are never lost from ecosystems; they are recycled indefinitely between biotic and abiotic reservoirs. The amount of nutrients (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, etc.) present in the soil at any given time is called the standing state, which varies with ecosystem type and season.

A cycle's reservoir compensates for deficits caused by imbalances between influx and efflux, while environmental factors (soil moisture, pH, temperature) regulate nutrient release rates.

Feature Carbon Cycle (Gaseous) Phosphorus Cycle (Sedimentary)
Primary Reservoir Atmosphere and hydrosphere (oceans hold 71% of global carbon; atmosphere holds ~1%). Earth's crust (rocks containing phosphate minerals).
Biological Role Constitutes 49% of the dry weight of organisms (next only to water). Essential for biological membranes, nucleic acids, cellular energy systems (ATP), shells, bones, and teeth.
Major Fluxes Photosynthesis fixes ~\(4 \times 10^{13}\,\mathrm{kg}\) of carbon annually; returned as \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) via respiration, decomposition, forest fires, volcanic activity, and fossil fuel combustion. Rock weathering releases phosphates into soil solution → absorbed by plant roots → transferred to consumers → returned by phosphate-solubilising bacteria.
Atmospheric Exchange Major gaseous exchange of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) between organisms, oceans, and atmosphere. No respiratory release into atmosphere; rainfall inputs are minimal; gaseous exchange is negligible.

Ecosystem Services and Global Valuation

The products of healthy ecosystem processes are termed ecosystem services. Forests and natural biomes purify air and water, mitigate droughts and floods, cycle nutrients, generate fertile soils, provide wildlife habitat, maintain biodiversity, pollinate crops, store carbon, and offer aesthetic, cultural, and spiritual value.

Robert Costanza and colleagues estimated the average annual value of these fundamental life-support services at USD 33 trillion—nearly twice the global Gross National Product (GNP) of USD 18 trillion at the time of their assessment. Within this total:

  • Soil formation accounts for approximately 50% of the total value.
  • Recreation and nutrient cycling contribute less than 10% each.
  • Climate regulation and wildlife habitat account for about 6% each.

Worked Examples

Calculating Net Primary Productivity and Heterotroph Availability

Problem: In a hypothetical grassland plot (assumed values for illustration), producers fix solar energy at a Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) of \(1800\,\mathrm{kcal\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}\). If plant respiration consumes \(42\%\) of GPP, calculate the Net Primary Productivity (NPP) available to herbivores and decomposers.

Solution:

First, determine the respiratory loss \(R\):

\[ R = 0.42 \times 1800\,\mathrm{kcal\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}} = 756\,\mathrm{kcal\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}} \]

Applying \(\mathrm{NPP} = \mathrm{GPP} - R\):

\[ \mathrm{NPP} = 1800 - 756 = 1044\,\mathrm{kcal\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}} \]

Thus, \(1044\,\mathrm{kcal\,m^{-2}\,yr^{-1}}\) of biomass energy is available to heterotrophs.

Applying PAR Capture and the 10% Energy Transfer Law

Problem: Suppose \(2{,}000{,}000\,\mathrm{J}\) of total incident solar radiation falls on a hypothetical terrestrial meadow (assumed value). Assuming exactly \(50\%\) is Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR) and producers capture \(4\%\) of PAR into NPP, how much energy reaches a tertiary consumer (fourth trophic level) in a grazing food chain?

Solution:

  1. Calculate PAR: \(\mathrm{PAR} = 0.50 \times 2{,}000{,}000\,\mathrm{J} = 1{,}000{,}000\,\mathrm{J}\).
  2. Calculate energy fixed by producers (T1): \(E_{1} = 0.04 \times 1{,}000{,}000\,\mathrm{J} = 40{,}000\,\mathrm{J}\).
  3. Apply the 10% law at each successive trophic transfer:
    • Primary consumers (T2): \(E_{2} = 0.10 \times 40{,}000 = 4{,}000\,\mathrm{J}\).
    • Secondary consumers (T3): \(E_{3} = 0.10 \times 4{,}000 = 400\,\mathrm{J}\).
    • Tertiary consumers (T4): \(E_{4} = 0.10 \times 400 = 40\,\mathrm{J}\).

Only \(40\,\mathrm{J}\) reaches the tertiary consumer level.

Distinguishing Standing Crop from Standing State

Problem: In a pond survey (assumed values), the dry biomass of phytoplankton is \(12\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\), zooplankton is \(28\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\), and dissolved inorganic phosphate in the water column is \(0.4\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\). Classify each measurement and explain the pyramid shape.

Solution:

  • The dry biomass values of phytoplankton (\(12\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\)) and zooplankton (\(28\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\)) represent the standing crop of T1 and T2, respectively.
  • The dissolved inorganic phosphate (\(0.4\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\)) represents the standing state of phosphorus in the abiotic reservoir.
  • Because the standing crop of primary consumers (\(28\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\)) exceeds that of producers (\(12\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}\)), the pyramid of biomass is inverted—a classic feature of aquatic ecosystems driven by rapid phytoplankton turnover.

NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are designed in the NEET UG format. Attempt all questions before checking the worked solutions.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements accurately distinguishes Net Primary Productivity (NPP) from Secondary Productivity?

  • (A) NPP is the total solar energy captured by plants, whereas secondary productivity is the respiratory loss of herbivores.
  • (B) NPP is the biomass available for heterotrophic consumption, whereas secondary productivity is the rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers.
  • (C) NPP is always lower than secondary productivity in terrestrial ecosystems.
  • (D) Secondary productivity is calculated by subtracting plant respiration from NPP.

Answer: (B)

Solution: \(\mathrm{NPP} = \mathrm{GPP} - R\) represents the net organic matter stored by producers that is available to herbivores and decomposers. Secondary productivity is defined specifically as the rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers. Option (A) confuses GPP with NPP, (C) contradicts the 10% energy loss across trophic levels, and (D) is mathematically incorrect.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Under identical warm and moist climatic conditions, which type of detritus will undergo the fastest rate of decomposition?

  • (A) Wood bark rich in lignin and tannins
  • (B) Arthropod exoskeletons rich in chitin
  • (C) Young herbaceous leaves rich in nitrogen and water-soluble sugars
  • (D) Waterlogged peat under anaerobic conditions

Answer: (C)

Solution: Decomposition rate is accelerated when detritus is rich in nitrogen and water-soluble substances like sugars, and slowed when detritus contains recalcitrant polymers like lignin or chitin. Anaerobiosis (D) strongly inhibits aerobic decomposers.

Practice Question 3 (Statement-Based)

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: In a terrestrial forest ecosystem, a larger fraction of energy flows through the detritus food chain than through the grazing food chain.
Statement II: In an aquatic ecosystem, the grazing food chain is the major conduit for energy flow.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (A)

Solution: In terrestrial ecosystems, most primary production is not grazed directly by herbivores and instead enters the detritus pool as litter, making the detritus food chain (DFC) the dominant energy conduit. Conversely, in aquatic ecosystems, phytoplankton are heavily grazed by zooplankton, making the grazing food chain (GFC) the major conduit.

Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): The pyramid of energy in any natural ecosystem is always upright and can never be inverted.
Reason (R): When energy flows from a particular trophic level to the next trophic level, some energy is always lost as heat at each step.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Answer: (A)

Solution: Because energy transfer follows the Second Law of Thermodynamics, metabolic respiration dissipates a large fraction of energy as heat at every trophic step. Therefore, the energy content of a lower trophic level is invariably greater than that of the next higher level, making the pyramid of energy strictly upright.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A student observes that a single banyan tree supports 400 leaf-eating insects, which are preyed upon by 25 small insectivorous birds, which in turn are eaten by 2 hawks. What will be the shape of the pyramid of numbers for this food chain?

  • (A) Strictly upright
  • (B) Strictly inverted
  • (C) Spindle-shaped
  • (D) Rectangular

Answer: (C)

Solution: Plotting individuals from bottom to top gives 1 (producer) → 400 (primary consumers) → 25 (secondary consumers) → 2 (tertiary consumers). Narrow at the base, widest in the middle, and narrow at the apex produces a spindle-shaped pyramid.

Practice Question 6 (Match the Following)

Match the ecological terms in Column I with their correct descriptions in Column II:

Column I Column II
1. Humification (p) Vertical distribution of species in layers
2. Stratification (q) Total inorganic nutrients in soil at a given time
3. Standing state (r) Accumulation of dark, colloidal, resistant organic matter
4. Standing crop (s) Living biomass at a trophic level at a particular time
  • (A) 1-(r), 2-(p), 3-(q), 4-(s)
  • (B) 1-(r), 2-(q), 3-(p), 4-(s)
  • (C) 1-(s), 2-(p), 3-(q), 4-(r)
  • (D) 1-(q), 2-(p), 3-(s), 4-(r)

Answer: (A)

Solution: Humification produces dark, amorphous, colloidal humus (r); stratification is vertical layering of species (p); standing state refers to soil inorganic nutrients (q); and standing crop refers to living biomass at a trophic level (s).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Arrange the following seral stages of primary hydrarch succession in the correct chronological order:

I. Reed-swamp stage  |  II. Phytoplankton stage  |  III. Marsh-meadow stage  |  IV. Rooted-submerged plant stage  |  V. Scrub stage

  • (A) II → IV → I → III → V
  • (B) II → I → IV → III → V
  • (C) IV → II → I → V → III
  • (D) II → IV → III → I → V

Answer: (A)

Solution: In primary succession in water, pioneer phytoplankton (II) are followed by rooted-submerged plants (IV), then rooted/free-floating plants, reed-swamp (I), marsh-meadow (III), scrub (V), and finally a mesic forest climax.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding the phosphorus cycle compared to the carbon cycle?

  • (A) The natural reservoir of phosphorus is rock in the form of phosphates.
  • (B) Atmospheric inputs of phosphorus through rainfall are much smaller than carbon inputs.
  • (C) Gaseous exchanges of phosphorus between organisms and the environment are negligible.
  • (D) Producers release significant amounts of phosphorus back into the atmosphere via respiration.

Answer: (D)

Solution: Unlike the carbon cycle, there is no respiratory release of phosphorus into the atmosphere. Phosphorus is a sedimentary cycle whose reservoir lies in Earth's crust, and gaseous exchanges are negligible.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a hypothetical grazing food chain, if \(50{,}000\,\mathrm{J}\) of energy is trapped by producers (T1), how much energy will be available to secondary carnivores (T4)?

  • (A) \(5{,}000\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(500\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(50\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(5\,\mathrm{J}\)

Answer: (C)

Solution: Using the 10% law: T1 = \(50{,}000\,\mathrm{J}\) → T2 (herbivores) = \(5{,}000\,\mathrm{J}\) → T3 (primary carnivores) = \(500\,\mathrm{J}\) → T4 (secondary carnivores) = \(50\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

According to Robert Costanza and colleagues' valuation of global ecosystem services, which single service accounts for roughly \(50\%\) of the total estimated price tag of USD 33 trillion per year?

  • (A) Climate regulation
  • (B) Nutrient cycling
  • (C) Soil formation
  • (D) Wildlife habitat

Answer: (C)

Solution: Out of the USD 33 trillion annual valuation, soil formation accounts for about 50%, recreation and nutrient cycling account for less than 10% each, and climate regulation and wildlife habitat account for about 6% each.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield Traps to Avoid in NEET UG
  • PAR vs. Total Sunlight: PAR is less than 50% of incident solar radiation, and plants capture 2–10% of PAR (not 2–10% of total incident sunlight).
  • Oceans vs. Land Productivity: Oceans cover ~70% of Earth's surface but contribute only ~55 billion tons out of 170 billion tons of global annual NPP (~32%).
  • Trophic Level vs. Species: A trophic level is a functional role, not a species; omnivores and birds like sparrows can occupy multiple trophic levels at once.
  • Dry Weight vs. Fresh Weight: Always prefer dry weight for measuring standing crop biomass because water content varies.
  • Climax Convergence: Both hydrarch (wet) and xerarch (dry) successions lead to mesic conditions—neither too wet nor too dry.
  • Ecological Pyramid Omissions: Saprophytes (decomposers) are completely excluded from classical ecological pyramids.

Concise Final Recap

  • Structure & Function: Species composition and vertical stratification define structure; productivity, decomposition, energy flow, and nutrient cycling drive function.
  • Productivity: \(\mathrm{NPP} = \mathrm{GPP} - R\). Secondary productivity is new organic matter formed by consumers.
  • Decomposition: Fragmentation, leaching, and catabolism act simultaneously on detritus; humification and mineralisation occur in soil. Favoured by warm, moist, aerobic conditions and nitrogen/sugar-rich detritus.
  • Energy & Pyramids: Unidirectional flow with 10% transfer per trophic level. Pyramid of energy is always upright; biomass in sea is inverted; numbers on a single tree are inverted/spindle-shaped.
  • Succession & Cycles: Primary succession is slow (no soil); secondary succession is faster (soil present). Carbon is a gaseous cycle (ocean = 71% reservoir); phosphorus is a sedimentary cycle (rock reservoir, no respiratory release).
