1. Learning Objectives and Exam Overview

Understanding how genetic information is chemically stored, duplicated, transcribed into RNA, and translated into functional proteins forms the core of molecular biology. For NEET UG, this chapter carries high weightage and tests both factual precision (enzyme roles, organism genome sizes, experimental conclusions) and analytical problem solving (Chargaff calculations, strand polarity conversions, Meselson–Stahl generation ratios, and *lac* operon logic).

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Describe the chemical architecture of polynucleotide chains, DNA double-helix geometry, and chromatin packaging in prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
  • Evaluate the classic experiments that proved DNA is the hereditary material and established its semiconservative replication mechanism.
  • Distinguish between DNA replication, prokaryotic and eukaryotic transcription, post-transcriptional processing, and ribosomal translation.
  • Apply properties of the triplet genetic code to predict polypeptide sequences and analyse frameshift versus point mutations.
  • Explain transcriptional regulation using the *lac* operon, summarize Human Genome Project milestones, and interpret VNTR-based DNA profiles.

2. Chemical Architecture and Packaging of Genetic Material

2.1 Polynucleotide Structure and Polarity

Nucleic acids—deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA)—are linear polymers of nucleotide monomers. Each nucleotide consists of three parts: a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon (pentose) sugar, and a phosphate group.

  • Nitrogenous Bases: Divided into double-ring purines (Adenine [A] and Guanine [G]) and single-ring pyrimidines (Cytosine [C], Thymine [T], and Uracil [U]). Cytosine occurs in both DNA and RNA; Thymine (chemically 5-methyl uracil) is characteristic of DNA, whereas Uracil replaces Thymine in RNA.
  • Nucleoside vs. Nucleotide: A nitrogenous base attaches to the 1′ carbon (1′C) hydroxyl of a pentose sugar (2′-deoxyribose in DNA; ribose in RNA) via an N-glycosidic linkage to form a nucleoside (e.g., adenosine, deoxyadenosine, cytidine, deoxythymidine, uridine). Adding a phosphate group to the 5′C hydroxyl via a phosphoester linkage yields a nucleotide.
  • Chain Elongation and Polarity: Adjacent nucleotides join through 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkages, creating a repeating sugar–phosphate backbone with bases projecting laterally. One terminus retains a free phosphate at 5′C (the 5′-end), while the opposite terminus retains a free –OH at 3′C (the 3′-end).

2.2 The DNA Double Helix and Chargaff's Rules

Nucleic acid was first isolated as an acidic nuclear substance ("nuclein") by Friedrich Meischer in 1869. In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick integrated X-ray diffraction data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins with Erwin Chargaff's base-ratio observations to propose the right-handed double-helix model of DNA.

  • Antiparallel Strands: One polynucleotide strand runs 5′→3′ while its partner runs 3′→5′.
  • Complementary Hydrogen Bonding: Adenine pairs specifically with Thymine via two hydrogen bonds (\( \mathrm{A=T} \)), and Guanine pairs with Cytosine via three hydrogen bonds (\( \mathrm{G \equiv C} \)). Because a two-ring purine always pairs with a one-ring pyrimidine, the helix maintains an almost uniform diameter throughout its length.
  • Helical Dimensions: One complete right-handed turn (pitch) spans \(3.4\,\mathrm{nm}\) and accommodates roughly \(10\,\mathrm{bp}\), giving an axial rise of \(0.34\,\mathrm{nm}\) per base pair. Base-pair stacking interactions, together with hydrogen bonds, stabilize the duplex.
Chargaff's Equivalence Relations (for double-stranded DNA): \[ [\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{T}], \qquad [\mathrm{G}] = [\mathrm{C}], \qquad \frac{[\mathrm{A}]+[\mathrm{G}]}{[\mathrm{T}]+[\mathrm{C}]} = 1 \] \[ \%\mathrm{A} + \%\mathrm{T} + \%\mathrm{G} + \%\mathrm{C} = 100\% \]

2.4 DNA Packaging: Nucleoid to Chromatin

A diploid human cell holds roughly \(2.2\,\mathrm{m}\) of DNA inside a nucleus of diameter \(\approx 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}\). Packaging resolves this spatial challenge differently in prokaryotes and eukaryotes:

  • Prokaryotes (E. coli): Lacking a nuclear envelope, negatively charged DNA is organized into large loops anchored by positively charged proteins within a defined region called the nucleoid.
  • Eukaryotes: Negatively charged DNA wraps around a positively charged core of eight basic histone proteins (a histone octamer, rich in basic amino acids lysine and arginine) to form a nucleosome. A typical nucleosome encloses \(200\,\mathrm{bp}\) of DNA and is stabilized by an H1 linker histone.
  • Higher-Order Condensation: Repeating nucleosomes appear as a "beads-on-string" chromatin fiber under an electron microscope. Further coiling into metaphase chromosomes requires Non-Histone Chromosomal (NHC) proteins.
  • Euchromatin vs. Heterochromatin: Loosely packed, lightly staining, transcriptionally active chromatin is euchromatin; densely packed, darkly staining, transcriptionally inactive chromatin is heterochromatin.

3. Establishing DNA as the Hereditary Molecule

3.1 Criteria for a Genetic Material and DNA vs. RNA

A molecule serving as genetic material must satisfy four criteria: (1) generate its own replica, (2) remain chemically and structurally stable, (3) permit slow heritable changes (mutations) for evolution, and (4) express itself as Mendelian characters. Both DNA and RNA replicate via base complementarity, whereas proteins fail the very first criterion.

DNA is preferred for long-term genetic storage because it lacks the reactive 2′–OH group found in ribose (which makes RNA labile and catalytic) and possesses Thymine instead of Uracil, enhancing chemical stability and repair fidelity. Conversely, RNA is better suited for rapid transmission and expression of genetic information, and RNA viruses mutate and evolve faster due to RNA's higher reactivity. According to the RNA World hypothesis, RNA was the primordial genetic material around which early metabolism, translation, and splicing evolved before DNA emerged as a more stable archive.

4. Information Flow: Replication and Transcription

Francis Crick's Central Dogma states that genetic information flows unidirectionally in most cells: \(\mathrm{DNA \rightarrow RNA \rightarrow Protein}\). In retroviruses, information also flows in reverse (\(\mathrm{RNA \rightarrow DNA}\)) via reverse transcription.

4.1 Semiconservative DNA Replication

In 1958, Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl proved Watson and Crick's semiconservative model (each daughter duplex retains one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand). They grew E. coli for many generations in heavy nitrogen (\(^{15}\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\)—note that \(^{15}\mathrm{N}\) is a non-radioactive heavy isotope separated by density, not radioactivity) and shifted the cells to normal \(^{14}\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\) medium. Using cesium chloride (\(\mathrm{CsCl}\)) density-gradient centrifugation:

  • Generation 0 (\(0\,\mathrm{min}\)): 100% heavy DNA (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{15}\mathrm{N}\)).
  • Generation I (\(20\,\mathrm{min}\)): 100% hybrid/intermediate density DNA (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)).
  • Generation II (\(40\,\mathrm{min}\)): 50% hybrid DNA (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)) and 50% light DNA (\(^{14}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)).

Taylor and colleagues (1958) independently confirmed semiconservative chromosome replication in Vicia faba (faba beans) using radioactive thymidine.

4.2 Replication Machinery and the Fork

DNA replication begins at a specific site called the origin of replication (which is why recombinant DNA cloning requires a vector carrying an origin). In E. coli, \(4.6 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{bp}\) replicate in \(18\,\mathrm{min}\) at an average rate of \(\approx 2000\,\mathrm{bp/s}\). In eukaryotes, replication occurs during the S-phase of the cell cycle; failure of cell division after replication causes polyploidy.

  • Dual Role of dNTPs: Deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs) serve both as substrates and as energy donors (via cleavage of their two terminal high-energy phosphates).
  • Unidirectional Polymerization: DNA-dependent DNA polymerase adds nucleotides strictly in the 5′→3′ direction and cannot initiate synthesis de novo.
  • Leading vs. Lagging Strands: At the Y-shaped replication fork, the 3′→5′ template supports continuous synthesis (leading strand), whereas the 5′→3′ template undergoes discontinuous synthesis as short fragments (Okazaki fragments) that are subsequently sealed by DNA ligase.

4.3 Transcription Unit, Polarity, and Split Genes

Transcription copies only one strand of a specific DNA segment into RNA using DNA-dependent RNA polymerase (which polymerizes in the 5′→3′ direction, pairing Adenine on DNA with Uracil in RNA). Only one strand is transcribed because copying both strands would yield two different proteins from one locus and produce complementary RNAs that would hybridize into untranslatable double-stranded RNA.

  • Template Strand (3′→5′): Read by RNA polymerase to synthesize complementary RNA.
  • Coding Strand (5′→3′): Displaced during transcription; its sequence matches the newly formed RNA (with T in place of U). All positional references (upstream/downstream) are defined relative to the coding strand.
  • Three Regions of a Transcription Unit: The Promoter (located at the 5′-end/upstream of the coding strand; binds RNA polymerase and defines which strand is the template), the Structural Gene, and the Terminator (located at the 3′-end/downstream of the coding strand; signals termination).
  • Cistrons and Split Genes: A cistron is a DNA segment coding for a polypeptide. Prokaryotic structural genes are typically polycistronic, whereas eukaryotic structural genes are monocistronic and split into expressed sequences (exons) interrupted by non-coding intervening sequences (introns).

5. Decoding the Message: Genetic Code, tRNA, and Translation

5.1 Deciphering and Properties of the Genetic Code

George Gamow deduced that with 4 RNA bases coding for 20 amino acids, a triplet code (\(4^3 = 64\) codons) is the minimum required. Key experimental breakthroughs came from Har Gobind Khorana (chemical synthesis of defined RNA homopolymers/copolymers), Marshall Nirenberg (cell-free protein synthesis), and Severo Ochoa (polynucleotide phosphorylase for template-independent enzymatic RNA synthesis).

  • Triplet and Unambiguous: 61 codons specify amino acids; 3 codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) act as stop (terminator) codons with no cognate tRNA.
  • Degenerate: Most amino acids are encoded by more than one codon (e.g., Phenylalanine by UUU and UUC), whereas Methionine (AUG) and Tryptophan (UGG) have single codons.
  • Commaless (Contiguous): Read continuously without punctuation.
  • Nearly Universal: Identical across bacteria to humans (e.g., UUU = Phe), with minor exceptions in mitochondrial codons and certain protozoans.
  • Dual Role of AUG: Codes for Methionine and serves as the initiator codon.

5.2 Point Mutations vs. Frameshift Mutations

A point mutation alters a single base pair—for instance, replacing glutamate with valine in the β-globin chain causes sickle-cell anemia. By contrast, insertion or deletion of one or two bases shifts the entire triplet reading frame downstream of the mutation (frameshift mutation), altering every subsequent amino acid. Inserting or deleting three bases or multiples of three adds or removes whole amino acids while preserving the downstream reading frame.

5.3 tRNA (The Adapter Molecule) and Ribosomal Translation

Francis Crick predicted an adapter molecule that reads mRNA codons while carrying specific amino acids. tRNA (originally soluble RNA, sRNA) has a clover-leaf secondary structure and an inverted-L compact 3D structure. It features an anticodon loop complementary to the mRNA codon and an amino-acid acceptor end (3′-end).

  1. Charging (Aminoacylation) of tRNA: Amino acids are activated using ATP and covalently linked to their cognate tRNAs.
  2. Initiation: The small ribosomal subunit binds mRNA at the start codon (AUG), recognized by the initiator tRNA. Flanking untranslated regions (UTRs) at both 5′ and 3′ ends enhance translational efficiency.
  3. Elongation: Charged tRNAs enter the large ribosomal subunit (which provides two adjacent sites for tRNAs). Peptide bond formation is catalyzed by the ribosome itself—specifically, 23S rRNA in bacteria acts as a ribozyme (peptidyl transferase).
  4. Termination: When the ribosome reaches a stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA), a release factor binds instead of a tRNA, releasing the completed polypeptide.

6. Gene Regulation, Genomics, and DNA Profiling

6.1 Transcriptional Control: The lac Operon

In eukaryotes, gene expression is regulated at four levels: transcriptional, RNA processing (splicing), mRNA nuclear export, and translational. In prokaryotes, transcriptional initiation is the primary control point. François Jacob and Jacques Monod elucidated the lac operon in E. coli, a polycistronic unit regulated by a common promoter and operator.

  • Regulatory Gene (i): Constitutively expresses the lac repressor protein (i stands for inhibitor, not inducer).
  • Structural Genes (z, y, a): z encodes β-galactosidase (hydrolyzes lactose into glucose and galactose); y encodes permease (facilitates lactose entry; a basal level of expression is always required for initial entry); a encodes transacetylase.
  • Negative Regulation by Inducer: In the absence of lactose (or allolactose), the repressor binds the operator (o) and blocks RNA polymerase. When lactose is present (in the absence of glucose), it binds and inactivates the repressor, allowing transcription. Glucose and galactose cannot act as inducers.

6.2 Human Genome Project (HGP) Highlights

Launched in 1990 and completed in 2003 (with Chromosome 1 finished in May 2006), HGP was a 13-year international mega-project that catalyzed the field of Bioinformatics. Two sequencing strategies were used: Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs) (identifying only RNA-expressed genes) and Sequence Annotation (sequencing the entire genome—coding and non-coding—and assigning functions later). DNA fragments were cloned in bacterial or yeast hosts using BAC (Bacterial Artificial Chromosomes) and YAC (Yeast Artificial Chromosomes) vectors and sequenced via Frederick Sanger's automated method.

  • Total size: \(3164.7\,\mathrm{million\ bp}\); estimated genes: \(\approx 30,000\) (functions unknown for >50%).
  • Average gene size: \(3000\,\mathrm{bases}\); largest human gene: dystrophin (\(2.4\,\mathrm{million\ bases}\)).
  • Less than \(2\%\) of the genome codes for proteins; repetitive sequences constitute a large fraction.
  • Chromosome 1 carries the most genes (\(2968\)); the Y chromosome carries the fewest (\(231\)).
  • \(99.9\%\) of nucleotide bases are identical across all humans; roughly \(1.4\,\mathrm{million}\) Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) have been mapped.

6.3 DNA Fingerprinting (Alec Jeffreys)

Although \(99.9\%\) of human DNA is identical, the remaining \(0.1\%\) (\(\approx 3 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{bp}\)) accounts for individual uniqueness. Non-coding repetitive DNA separates from bulk DNA as satellite DNA peaks during density-gradient centrifugation. When a heritable mutation occurs at a locus in a population at a frequency greater than \(0.01\), it is termed DNA polymorphism.

Alec Jeffreys used a mini-satellite probe called VNTR (Variable Number of Tandem Repeats), whose repeat copy number varies between individuals (and between paternal and maternal chromosomes), spanning \(0.1\text{–}20\,\mathrm{kb}\). The classic Southern blot workflow comprises: (1) DNA isolation, (2) restriction endonuclease digestion, (3) agarose gel electrophoresis, (4) blotting onto a synthetic nitrocellulose or nylon membrane, (5) hybridization with a radiolabelled VNTR probe, and (6) detection by autoradiography. Coupled with PCR, even DNA from a single cell suffices for forensic matching, paternity disputes, and population diversity studies (only monozygotic twins share identical patterns).

Worked Examples

Applying Chargaff's Rule to Base Composition

Problem: Assume a hypothetical double-stranded DNA molecule extracted from a newly discovered bacterium contains \(18\%\) Guanine. Calculate the percentage of Adenine, Thymine, and Cytosine, as well as the ratio \((\mathrm{A+T})/(\mathrm{G+C})\).

Solution:

By Chargaff's equivalence rule for double-stranded DNA, \([\mathrm{G}] = [\mathrm{C}]\) and \([\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{T}]\).

\[ \%\mathrm{C} = \%\mathrm{G} = 18\% \] \[ \%\mathrm{G} + \%\mathrm{C} = 18\% + 18\% = 36\% \]

Since total bases equal \(100\%\):

\[ \%\mathrm{A} + \%\mathrm{T} = 100\% - 36\% = 64\% \] \[ \%\mathrm{A} = \%\mathrm{T} = \frac{64\%}{2} = 32\% \] \[ \frac{\mathrm{A+T}}{\mathrm{G+C}} = \frac{64\%}{36\%} = \frac{16}{9} \approx 1.78 \]

Answer: Cytosine = \(18\%\), Adenine = \(32\%\), Thymine = \(32\%\), and \((\mathrm{A+T})/(\mathrm{G+C}) \approx 1.78\).

Determining Meselson–Stahl Band Proportions Across Generations

Problem: Assume an E. coli culture grown for many generations in \(^{15}\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\) is transferred to \(^{14}\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\) medium and allowed to divide for \(80\,\mathrm{min}\) (assuming a generation time of \(20\,\mathrm{min}\)). What percentage of DNA molecules will have hybrid density (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)) and what percentage will have light density (\(^{14}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\))?

Solution:

Number of generations \(n = \frac{80\,\mathrm{min}}{20\,\mathrm{min/generation}} = 4\) generations. Total DNA duplexes produced per initial parental duplex = \(2^4 = 16\).

Because replication is semiconservative, the two original \(^{15}\mathrm{N}\) strands remain intact and each forms one hybrid duplex (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)). All remaining duplexes are purely light (\(^{14}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)).

\[ \text{Hybrid duplexes} = 2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{2}{16} \times 100\% = 12.5\% \] \[ \text{Light duplexes} = 16 - 2 = 14 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{14}{16} \times 100\% = 87.5\% \]

Answer: \(12.5\%\) hybrid DNA and \(87.5\%\) light DNA (a \(1:7\) hybrid-to-light ratio).

Predicting mRNA Sequence from a DNA Transcription Unit

Problem: A hypothetical DNA segment has the following template strand sequence: 3′-TACGGCTAAGTCACT-5′. Determine (a) the coding strand sequence with polarity and (b) the transcribed mRNA sequence with polarity.

Solution:

(a) The coding strand is complementary and antiparallel to the template strand (using T, A, C, G):

\[ \text{Coding strand: } 5'\text{-ATGCCGATTCAGTGA-3'} \]

(b) The mRNA is synthesized 5′→3′ complementary to the 3′→5′ template strand, with Uracil (U) replacing Thymine (T) (thus matching the coding strand with U instead of T):

\[ \text{mRNA: } 5'\text{-AUGCCGAUUCAGUGA-3'} \]

Notice that AUG is the start codon and UGA is a stop codon.

Calculating Nucleosomes and Physical DNA Length

Problem: Assume a hypothetical eukaryotic chromosome contains \(1.2 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{bp}\) of DNA. Estimate (a) its extended physical length in \(\mathrm{mm}\) and (b) the approximate number of nucleosomes required to package it.

Solution:

(a) Distance between consecutive base pairs = \(0.34\,\mathrm{nm} = 0.34 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}\).

\[ \text{Length} = 1.2 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{bp} \times 0.34 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m/bp} = 4.08 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 4.08\,\mathrm{mm} \]

(b) A typical nucleosome packages \(200\,\mathrm{bp}\) of DNA:

\[ \text{Number of nucleosomes} = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{bp}}{200\,\mathrm{bp/nucleosome}} = 6 \times 10^{4}\text{ nucleosomes} \]

Answer: Physical length = \(4.08\,\mathrm{mm}\); nucleosomes \(\approx 60,000\).

  • (B) Bacteriophage ϕ×174
  • (C) Escherichia coli
  • (D) Human haploid genome

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

During the Hershey and Chase experiment, if T2 bacteriophages were labelled separately with \(^{32}\mathrm{P}\) and \(^{35}\mathrm{S}\) and allowed to infect E. coli, where would radioactivity be detected after blending and centrifugation?

  • (A) Both \(^{32}\mathrm{P}\) and \(^{35}\mathrm{S}\) in the bacterial pellet
  • (B) \(^{35}\mathrm{S}\) in the bacterial pellet and \(^{32}\mathrm{P}\) in the supernatant
  • (C) \(^{32}\mathrm{P}\) in the bacterial pellet and \(^{35}\mathrm{S}\) in the supernatant
  • (D) Both \(^{32}\mathrm{P}\) and \(^{35}\mathrm{S}\) in the supernatant

Answer: (C)

Solution: DNA contains phosphorus (\(^{32}\mathrm{P}\)) and enters the bacterial cell during infection, so radioactivity is recovered in the heavier bacterial pellet. Viral protein coats contain sulfur (\(^{35}\mathrm{S}\)), remain outside, are sheared off by blending, and stay in the supernatant.

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Both strands of a DNA segment are not transcribed simultaneously into RNA during transcription.

Reason (R): Complementary RNA molecules produced from both strands would base-pair to form double-stranded RNA, preventing translation into protein.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) Both A and R are false.

Answer: (A)

Solution: Transcribing both strands would (1) complicate genetic coding by producing two different polypeptides from one segment and (2) generate complementary RNAs that hybridize into double-stranded RNA, halting ribosomal translation.

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

If an mRNA coding region has the sequence 5′-AUG UUU GGC UAA GCU-3′, how many amino acids will be present in the resulting polypeptide?

  • (A) 5
  • (B) 4
  • (C) 3
  • (D) 2

Answer: (C)

Solution: Reading in triplets from the start codon: AUG (Met, 1) – UUU (Phe, 2) – GGC (Gly, 3) – UAA (Stop codon). Translation terminates at UAA, so GCU is not translated. Thus, 3 amino acids are incorporated.

8. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Coding vs. Template Strand Polarity: The coding strand (5′→3′) does not act as the template; it simply shares the same sequence as mRNA (with T instead of U). The template strand is 3′→5′.
  • \(^{15}\mathrm{N}\) in Meselson–Stahl: \(^{15}\mathrm{N}\) is a heavy isotope, not radioactive. Separation relies on \(\mathrm{CsCl}\) density-gradient centrifugation, not radioactivity counting.
  • 5S rRNA Polymerase: While RNA Pol I transcribes 28S, 18S, and 5.8S rRNAs, 5S rRNA is transcribed by RNA Pol III.
  • DNA Fingerprinting Twins: VNTR banding patterns are unique to every individual except monozygotic (identical) twins.

9. Concise Final Recap

  • DNA Structure: Antiparallel right-handed helix; pitch \(3.4\,\mathrm{nm}\), \(10\,\mathrm{bp/turn}\), step \(0.34\,\mathrm{nm}\); \(\mathrm{A=T}\) (2 H-bonds), \(\mathrm{G \equiv C}\) (3 H-bonds).
  • Packaging: Histone octamer (lysine/arginine-rich) + \(200\,\mathrm{bp}\) DNA = nucleosome; euchromatin (active) vs. heterochromatin (inactive).
  • Replication: Semiconservative; DNA Pol synthesizes 5′→3′ using dNTPs; continuous leading strand, discontinuous lagging strand sealed by DNA ligase.
  • Transcription & Translation: Bacterial RNA Pol uses \(\sigma\) (initiation) and \(\rho\) (termination); eukaryotes use Pol I, II, III and process hnRNA via capping, splicing, and poly-A tailing. Triplet code (61 sense + 3 stop) is read by tRNA on ribosomes (23S rRNA ribozyme).
  • Regulation & Applications: lac operon is negatively regulated by repressor + lactose inducer; HGP mapped \(3164.7\,\mathrm{million\ bp}\); DNA fingerprinting exploits VNTR polymorphism.
