Reproductive Health: Principles, Contraception, STIs, and Assisted Technologies

Comprehensive conceptual guide, high-yield clinical criteria, and exam-aligned practice assessment

Learning Objectives and Overview

Reproductive health extends far beyond the mere absence of disease in the reproductive organs. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), it encompasses total well-being across physical, emotional, behavioural, and social dimensions of reproduction. For NEET UG aspirants, this topic tests precise recall of national programmes, contraceptive mechanisms, statutory medical regulations, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and assisted reproductive technologies (ART).

Core Competencies Covered
  • Goals and strategies of India's Reproductive and Child Health Care (RCH) programmes, including the statutory ban on sex-determination amniocentesis.
  • Demographic trends, causes of population explosion, and classification of contraceptive methods by mechanism, reversibility, and failure rate.
  • Legal, clinical, and temporal provisions of Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP) under the 1971 Act and 2017 Amendment.
  • Transmission, curability, complications, and prevention of major STIs.
  • Selection criteria and anatomical transfer sites for ART procedures (IVF-ET, ZIFT, IUT, GIFT, ICSI, AI/IUI).

Public Health Strategies and Demographic Stabilisation

India initiated national-level action plans for family planning in 1951, becoming one of the earliest nations to treat reproductive health as a social goal. These initiatives evolved into the broader Reproductive and Child Health Care (RCH) programmes, which integrate awareness creation, clinical infrastructure, and maternal-child support.

Key Pillars of RCH Implementation

  • Education and Counselling: Using audio-visual media, print campaigns, and school-level sex education to dispel myths among adolescents about puberty, hygienic sexual practices, and STIs.
  • Maternal and Neonatal Care: Promoting medically assisted deliveries, post-natal care, breastfeeding, and massive child immunisation to lower both Maternal Mortality Rate (MMR) and Infant Mortality Rate (IMR).
  • Amniocentesis Regulation: Amniocentesis samples amniotic fluid around the developing foetus to analyse fetal cells and dissolved substances for chromosomal or genetic disorders (such as Down syndrome, haemophilia, and sickle-cell anaemia) and fetal survivability. Because it was misused for prenatal sex determination leading to female foeticide, India imposed a statutory ban on using amniocentesis for sex determination.
  • Indigenous Research: Scientists at the Central Drug Research Institute (CDRI), Lucknow, developed Saheli—a non-steroidal, once-a-week oral contraceptive pill with minimal side effects and high contraceptive efficacy.

Population Growth Metrics

Improved healthcare and living conditions sharply lowered death rates, MMR, and IMR while increasing the proportion of individuals in the reproductive age group. Consequently, world population rose from roughly 2 billion in 1900 to 6 billion in 2000 and 7.2 billion in 2011. India's population grew from about 350 million at independence to nearly 1 billion by 2000, surpassing 1.2 billion by May 2011.

\[ \text{2011 Census Growth Rate} < 2\% \quad \left(\text{i.e., } < 20 \text{ per } 1000 \text{ individuals per year}\right) \]

Even a growth rate below 2% can double a population rapidly and strain food, shelter, and clothing. To stabilise population, India promoted the small-family norm (Hum Do Hamare Do), statutory minimum marriageable ages (18 years for females and 21 years for males), and widespread contraceptive access.

Contraceptive Methods: Mechanisms and Clinical Classification

An ideal contraceptive should be user-friendly, easily available, effective, reversible, have minimal or no side effects, and never interfere with sexual drive, desire, or the sexual act. Contraceptives are not required to maintain baseline reproductive health; rather, they are used voluntarily to prevent, delay, or space pregnancies. Possible side effects of hormonal or invasive methods include nausea, abdominal pain, breakthrough bleeding, irregular menstrual bleeding, or, rarely, breast cancer.

Category Examples Primary Mechanism of Action Key Clinical Notes
Natural / Traditional Periodic abstinence, Withdrawal (coitus interruptus), Lactational amenorrhea Avoids meeting of ovum and sperm without devices or drugs. Almost nil side effects, but high failure rate. Periodic abstinence avoids coitus on days 10–17 of the menstrual cycle. Lactational amenorrhea works only up to a maximum of 6 months post-parturition during intense full breastfeeding.

Condoms are disposable, offer privacy, and protect against STIs/AIDS. Diaphragms, cervical caps, and vaults are reusable rubber barriers covering the cervix, typically paired with spermicidal creams, jellies, or foams.

Copper-releasing IUDs CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375 Increases sperm phagocytosis; released \(\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}\) ions suppress sperm motility and fertilising capacity. Widely accepted in India for reversible, long-term pregnancy spacing.
Hormone-releasing IUDs Progestasert, LNG-20 Makes the uterus unsuitable for implantation and renders cervical mucus hostile to sperms. Combines intrauterine presence with local progestogen action.
Oral Pills Progestogen or Progestogen-estrogen pills; Saheli Inhibits ovulation and implantation; thickens cervical mucus to retard sperm entry. Conventional pills: taken daily for 21 days (starting within first 5 days of cycle), followed by a 7-day gap for menstruation. Saheli: non-steroidal, once-a-week pill.
Injectables & Implants Subdermal progestogen/estrogen implants and injections Same hormonal mechanism as oral pills (inhibits ovulation, implantation, and sperm entry). Provides a much longer effective duration than daily oral tablets.
Emergency Contraceptives Progestogens, Progestogen-estrogen combinations, or IUDs Prevents ovulation, fertilisation, or implantation after unprotected coitus. Must be administered within 72 hours of unprotected intercourse or rape.
Surgical (Sterilisation) Vasectomy (male), Tubectomy (female) Blocks gamete transport physically; does not stop gametogenesis. Terminal method: vas deferens (scrotal incision) or fallopian tube (abdominal/vaginal incision) is cut and tied. Highly effective, but very poor reversibility.

Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP)

Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP), or induced abortion, is the intentional or voluntary termination of pregnancy before full term. Globally, approximately 45 to 50 million MTPs are performed annually, representing about one-fifth (\(1/5\)) of all conceived pregnancies.

India legalised MTP in 1971 under strict safeguards to prevent misuse—particularly illegal female foeticide following unauthorised sex determination. MTP is clinically indicated for unwanted pregnancies resulting from contraceptive failure, casual unprotected intercourse, or rape, as well as cases where continuing pregnancy threatens the life or health of the mother, the foetus, or both.

Legal Framework: MTP (Amendment) Act, 2017
  • First Trimester (up to 12 weeks): Considered relatively safe; may be performed on the opinion of one registered medical practitioner.
  • Second Trimester (> 12 weeks to < 24 weeks): Clinically much riskier; requires the good-faith opinion of two registered medical practitioners.
  • Permitted Grounds: (i) Continuation poses a risk to the pregnant woman's life or grave injury to her physical or mental health; or (ii) Substantial risk that the child, if born, would suffer serious physical or mental abnormalities.

Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs)

Infections transmitted through sexual intercourse are termed Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs), venereal diseases (VD), or reproductive tract infections (RTI). Common examples include gonorrhoea, syphilis, genital herpes, chlamydiasis, genital warts, trichomoniasis, hepatitis-B, and HIV (leading to AIDS). Incidence is particularly high among individuals aged 15–24 years.

Transmission, Curability, and Complications

  • Non-Sexual Transmission Routes: Hepatitis-B and HIV can also spread via shared injection needles or surgical instruments, infected blood transfusions, and vertically from an infected mother to her foetus.
  • Curability Distinction (High-Yield): Except for hepatitis-B, genital herpes, and HIV, all other common STIs are completely curable if detected early and treated properly.
  • Delayed Detection & Complications: Early symptoms (genital itching, fluid discharge, mild pain, or swellings) are often minor, and infected females frequently remain asymptomatic. Social stigma further delays care, leading to severe complications: Pelvic Inflammatory Diseases (PID), abortions, still births, ectopic pregnancies, infertility, or reproductive tract cancers.
  • Three Preventive Principles: (1) Avoid sex with unknown or multiple partners; (2) Consistently use condoms during coitus; (3) Consult a qualified physician promptly upon any doubt for early diagnosis and complete treatment.

Infertility and Assisted Reproductive Technologies (ART)

Infertility is defined as the inability to conceive or produce children despite 2 years of unprotected sexual cohabitation. Underlying causes may be physical, congenital, disease-related, drug-induced, immunological, or psychological. Although social misconceptions often blame the female partner, the cause frequently lies with the male partner. When corrective clinical treatments fail, couples can be assisted through specialised Assisted Reproductive Technologies (ART).

ART Procedure Full Name Site of Fertilisation What Is Transferred & Where? Primary Indication
ZIFT Zygote Intra Fallopian Transfer In vitro (laboratory) Zygote or early embryo (up to 8 blastomeres) → Fallopian tube IVF-ET when early cleavage-stage transfer is selected.
IUT Intra Uterine Transfer In vitro (laboratory) Embryo (more than 8 blastomeres) → Uterus IVF-ET for advanced cleavage/morula-stage embryos.
GIFT Gamete Intra Fallopian Transfer In vivo (inside female) Donor ovum → Fallopian tube of recipient Female cannot produce an ovum, but provides a suitable environment for fertilisation and development.
ICSI Intra Cytoplasmic Sperm Injection In vitro (microinjection) Single sperm injected directly into ovum in lab; resulting embryo transferred via ZIFT/IUT Severe male-factor infertility or fertilisation failure.
AI / IUI Artificial Insemination / Intra-Uterine Insemination In vivo (inside female) Semen (husband/donor) → Vagina (AI) or Uterus (IUI) Male partner unable to inseminate or has very low sperm counts in ejaculate.

Because ART procedures require specialised expertise and expensive instrumentation, they are concentrated in limited centres and remain unaffordable for many couples. Furthermore, emotional, religious, and social factors can restrict adoption of ART. In India, legal adoption of orphaned and destitute children remains one of the best, legally supported paths to parenthood.

Worked Examples

Selecting an Optimal Contraceptive Strategy for Spacing vs. Terminal Care

Scenario (Hypothetical): Consider two couples consulting a family welfare clinic: Couple X has one 18-month-old child and wishes to delay their second pregnancy by 3 years without daily medication; Couple Y has completed their family and seeks a permanent method.

Reasoning & Solution:

  • Couple X (Spacing, no daily pills): Because lactational amenorrhea is reliable only up to 6 months post-parturition, it is unsuitable at 18 months. An Intra Uterine Device (IUD)—such as CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375, or LNG-20—is ideal for spacing pregnancies. It is reversible, requires no daily adherence, and acts locally (increasing sperm phagocytosis; Cu ions suppress sperm motility/fertilising capacity, while hormone-releasing IUDs make the uterus unsuitable for implantation and the cervix hostile to sperms).
  • Couple Y (Permanent): A surgical sterilisation method—vasectomy in the male or tubectomy in the female—is indicated. Both block gamete transport permanently with very high efficacy and very poor reversibility.

Evaluating Legal and Clinical Criteria for MTP

Scenario (Hypothetical): A pregnant woman at 16 weeks of gestation undergoes diagnostic evaluation showing a substantial risk that the child, if born, would suffer severe physical and mental abnormalities. Determine the legal requirement under the MTP (Amendment) Act, 2017.

Reasoning & Solution:

  • Gestational Window: 16 weeks falls in the second trimester (more than 12 weeks, fewer than 24 weeks).
  • Medical Opinion Required: Unlike pregnancies up to 12 weeks (which need 1 registered medical practitioner), pregnancies between 12 and 24 weeks require the good-faith opinion of two registered medical practitioners.
  • Statutory Ground: Substantial risk of serious physical or mental abnormalities in the unborn child is an explicitly valid ground under the Act.

Matching Infertility Causes to Assisted Reproductive Technologies

Scenario (Hypothetical): Three patients present at an infertility clinic: Patient P cannot produce ova but has a healthy uterus and fallopian tubes; Patient Q produces healthy ova and undergoes IVF, yielding an embryo at the 16-blastomere stage; Patient R has a partner with severe oligospermia (very low sperm count).

Reasoning & Solution:

  • Patient P: GIFT (Gamete Intra Fallopian Transfer) is appropriate—a donor ovum is transferred into Patient P's fallopian tube, where in vivo fertilisation and subsequent uterine development can occur.
  • Patient Q: Because the in vitro embryo has more than 8 blastomeres (16 blastomeres), it must be transferred into the uterus via IUT (Intra Uterine Transfer) rather than into the fallopian tube (ZIFT is for ≤ 8 blastomeres).
  • Patient R: Very low sperm count can be addressed by AI (Artificial Insemination) or IUI (Intra-Uterine Insemination) using concentrated semen, or by ICSI (direct microinjection of a single sperm into an ovum) if fertilisation fails.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements regarding copper-releasing and hormone-releasing Intra Uterine Devices (IUDs) is incorrect?

  • (A) Both types of IUDs increase phagocytosis of sperms within the uterus.
  • (B) Copper ions released by CuT and Multiload 375 suppress sperm motility and fertilising capacity.
  • (C) Hormone-releasing IUDs such as LNG-20 make the uterus unsuitable for implantation and the cervix hostile to sperms.
  • (D) IUDs inhibit ovulation in the ovary by suppressing pituitary gonadotropins.
Answer: (D)
Explanation: IUDs act locally within the uterus and cervix (phagocytosis of sperms, Cu2+ suppression of sperm motility/fertilising capacity, and—for hormone-releasing IUDs like Progestasert and LNG-20—anti-implantation uterine changes and hostile cervical mucus). Inhibiting ovulation via systemic hormone action is the mechanism of oral contraceptive pills, injections, and implants, not IUDs.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Identify the correct pair of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) that are not completely curable even if detected early:

  • (A) Gonorrhoea and Syphilis
  • (B) Chlamydiasis and Trichomoniasis
  • (C) Genital herpes and Hepatitis-B
  • (D) Genital warts and Gonorrhoea
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Among common STIs, three viral infections—hepatitis-B, genital herpes, and HIV—are not completely curable. Bacterial and protozoan STIs (such as gonorrhoea, syphilis, chlamydiasis, trichomoniasis) as well as genital warts are completely curable when detected early and treated properly.
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

An embryo formed by in vitro fertilisation is at the 6-blastomere stage. To complete its further development, where should it be transferred and what is the procedure called?

  • (A) Uterus; Intra Uterine Transfer (IUT)
  • (B) Fallopian tube; Zygote Intra Fallopian Transfer (ZIFT)
  • (C) Fallopian tube; Gamete Intra Fallopian Transfer (GIFT)
  • (D) Uterus; Intra-Uterine Insemination (IUI)
Answer: (B)
Explanation: A zygote or early embryo with up to 8 blastomeres (here, 6 blastomeres) is transferred into the fallopian tube via ZIFT. Embryos with more than 8 blastomeres are transferred into the uterus via IUT. GIFT transfers an unfertilised donor ovum, and IUI transfers semen.
Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: Surgical sterilisation methods such as vasectomy and tubectomy prevent conception by inhibiting gametogenesis in the gonads.
Statement II: Both vasectomy and tubectomy are highly effective terminal contraceptive methods with very poor reversibility.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (D)
Explanation: Statement I is incorrect because surgical sterilisation blocks gamete transport (by cutting and tying the vas deferens or fallopian tubes), not gamete formation. Statement II is correct: sterilisation is a highly effective terminal method with very poor reversibility.
Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): Lactational amenorrhea is considered a reliable natural contraceptive method only up to a maximum period of six months following parturition.
Reason (R): During intense full breastfeeding, ovulation and the menstrual cycle are suppressed, keeping the chances of conception almost nil during this period.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)
Explanation: Lactational amenorrhea relies on the physiological suppression of ovulation and menstruation during intense lactation after childbirth. Because this suppression reliably persists only up to about 6 months post-parturition while the mother breastfeeds fully, the method is limited to that 6-month window.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Under the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Act, 2017, what is the minimum number of registered medical practitioners required to form a good-faith opinion for terminating a 19-week pregnancy?

  • (A) One registered medical practitioner
  • (B) Two registered medical practitioners
  • (C) Three registered medical practitioners
  • (D) Termination is completely prohibited after 12 weeks
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Under the MTP (Amendment) Act, 2017, a pregnancy up to 12 weeks requires the opinion of 1 registered medical practitioner, whereas a pregnancy lasting more than 12 weeks and fewer than 24 weeks (such as 19 weeks) requires the good-faith opinion of two registered medical practitioners on specified statutory grounds.
Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following correctly describes the legitimate medical purpose of amniocentesis?

  • (A) Determining fetal sex to assist family planning
  • (B) Analysing amniotic fluid cells and dissolved substances for genetic disorders like Down syndrome and haemophilia
  • (C) Inducing labour during the third trimester
  • (D) Transferring an early embryo into the uterine cavity
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Amniocentesis analyses fetal cells and dissolved substances in amniotic fluid to detect genetic disorders (such as Down syndrome, haemophilia, and sickle-cell anaemia) and assess fetal survivability. Using it for sex determination is statutorily banned in India to prevent female foeticide.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Sterilisation vs. Gametogenesis: Vasectomy and tubectomy block gamete transport, never gamete formation or hormone secretion. Removal of gonads (castration) is not a contraceptive option.
  • ZIFT vs. IUT Blastomere Cutoff: ≤ 8 blastomeres go to the fallopian tube (ZIFT); > 8 blastomeres go to the uterus (IUT).
  • Curability of STIs: Only Hepatitis-B, Genital herpes, and HIV are incurable; bacterial/protozoan STIs (gonorrhoea, syphilis, chlamydiasis, trichomoniasis) and genital warts are curable if treated early.
  • Oral Pill Regimen: Conventional pills are taken for 21 days (starting within the first 5 days of the cycle), followed by a 7-day gap. Saheli is non-steroidal and taken once a week.
  • Emergency Contraception Window: Progestogens, progestogen-estrogen combinations, or IUDs must be used within 72 hours of coitus.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • 1951: Family planning launched in India → later expanded into RCH programmes.
  • 1971 & 2017: MTP legalised (1971); Amendment Act (2017) sets 1-doctor rule (≤ 12 weeks) and 2-doctor rule (> 12 to < 24 weeks).
  • 18 / 21 years: Statutory minimum marriageable age for females (18) and males (21).
  • Day 10–17: Fertile period avoided in periodic abstinence.
  • 6 months: Maximum effective duration of lactational amenorrhea post-parturition.
  • 2 years: Duration of unprotected cohabitation used to define infertility.

Concise Final Recap

Achieving reproductive health requires combining public awareness, safe maternal-child care, and statutory safeguards against practices such as sex-selective abortion. Contraceptive selection balances efficacy, reversibility, STI protection (unique to condoms), and user preference across natural, barrier, intrauterine, hormonal, emergency, and surgical methods. Early recognition of STIs prevents severe sequelae such as PID and infertility, while ART (ZIFT, IUT, GIFT, ICSI, AI/IUI) and legal adoption offer viable pathways to parenthood for infertile couples.
