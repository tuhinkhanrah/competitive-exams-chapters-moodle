NEET UG Biology Class XII · Human Physiology & Reproduction

Human Reproduction

A complete, exam-oriented guide to reproductive anatomy, gametogenesis, hormonal regulation, fertilisation, embryonic development, parturition, and lactation.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Humans are sexually reproducing, viviparous mammals with internal fertilisation. Reproductive competence begins at puberty and follows a coordinated sequence of events: gametogenesis (formation of haploid gametes), insemination (transfer of spermatozoa into the female reproductive tract), fertilisation (fusion of male and female gametes to form a diploid zygote), implantation (attachment and embedding of the blastocyst into the uterine endometrium), gestation (embryonic and foetal development), and parturition (delivery of the newborn), followed by lactation.

Key Learning Goals for NEET UG
  • Trace the anatomical sequence of male and female accessory ducts, glands, and uterine tissue layers.
  • Distinguish spermatogenesis from oogenesis in terms of timing, meiotic arrest, unequal cytokinesis, and hormonal control.
  • Correlate pituitary gonadotropins (FSH, LH) and ovarian steroids (estrogen, progesterone) with ovarian and uterine changes across a 28-day menstrual cycle.
  • Identify the exact site and mechanism of fertilisation, cleavage stages, placental hormones, and trimester-wise foetal milestones.
  • Explain the positive-feedback neuroendocrine reflex driving parturition and the immunological role of colostrum.

The Male Reproductive System

Located in the pelvic region, the male reproductive system comprises a pair of primary sex organs (testes), a network of accessory ducts, three types of accessory glands, and the external genitalia (penis).

Testes and Scrotal Thermoregulation

The paired oval testes (each approximately 4–5 cm long and 2–3 cm wide) are suspended outside the abdominal cavity within a pouch called the scrotum. The scrotum maintains testicular temperature at 2–2.5 °C lower than normal internal body temperature (approximately 37 °C), which is essential for normal spermatogenesis.

Internally, each testis is enclosed by a dense fibrous covering and partitioned into roughly 250 compartments called testicular lobules. Each lobule houses 1 to 3 highly coiled seminiferous tubules, the sites of sperm production. Two distinct cell populations line the inside of every seminiferous tubule:

  • Male germ cells (Spermatogonia): Diploid stem cells that undergo mitotic proliferation followed by meiotic division to produce haploid spermatozoa.
  • Sertoli cells: Tall somatic nurse cells that provide nutrition to developing germ cells and secrete factors required for spermiogenesis under FSH stimulation.

The regions outside the seminiferous tubules, known as interstitial spaces, contain small blood vessels, immunologically competent cells, and interstitial cells (Leydig cells). Leydig cells synthesise and secrete testicular steroid hormones called androgens (principally testosterone).

Male Accessory Glands and External Genitalia

The male accessory glands include paired seminal vesicles, a single prostate gland, and paired bulbourethral glands. Their combined secretions form the seminal plasma, which is rich in fructose (energy source for sperm), calcium ions (\( \mathrm{Ca^{2+}} \)), and specific enzymes. Secretions from the epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and prostate are essential for sperm maturation and motility. Seminal plasma together with spermatozoa constitutes semen. Additionally, the secretion of the bulbourethral glands helps in the lubrication of the penis.

The penis is the male external genitalia, composed of specialised erectile tissue that facilitates insemination. Its enlarged distal tip, the glans penis, is covered by a loose fold of skin called the foreskin.

The Female Reproductive System

Located in the pelvic region, the female reproductive system consists of a pair of ovaries, a pair of oviducts (fallopian tubes), a single uterus, the cervix, the vagina, and the external genitalia, integrated structurally and functionally with a pair of mammary glands.

Ovaries

Ovaries are the primary female sex organs, producing female gametes (ova) and steroid ovarian hormones (estrogen and progesterone). Each ovary is 2–4 cm in length, situated on either side of the lower abdomen, and anchored to the pelvic wall and uterus by ligaments. A thin epithelium encloses the ovarian stroma, which is divided into a peripheral cortex and an inner medulla.

Female Accessory Ducts and Uterine Layers

  • Oviducts (Fallopian Tubes): Each tube is 10–12 cm long and extends from the periphery of an ovary to the uterus. It has three regions:
    • Infundibulum: Funnel-shaped proximal part near the ovary, bearing finger-like projections called fimbriae that collect the ovum after ovulation.
    • Ampulla: Wider middle region where fertilisation occurs.
    • Isthmus: Narrow-lumened distal region joining the uterus.
  • Uterus (Womb): A single, inverted-pear-shaped organ supported by pelvic ligaments. It opens into the vagina via a narrow cervix; the cervical canal together with the vagina forms the birth canal. The uterine wall has three distinct tissue layers:
    Uterine Layer Tissue Nature Primary Physiological Role
    Perimetrium External thin membranous layer Protective outer covering of the uterus
    Myometrium Middle thick layer of smooth muscle Exhibits strong contractions during delivery (parturition)
    Endometrium Inner glandular lining of uterine cavity Undergoes cyclical changes during the menstrual cycle; site of implantation

Female External Genitalia and Mammary Glands

The female external genitalia include the mons pubis (fatty tissue cushion covered by skin and pubic hair), labia majora (fleshy folds extending from the mons pubis around the vaginal opening), labia minora (paired folds beneath the labia majora), hymen (membrane partially covering the vaginal opening), and clitoris (tiny finger-like structure at the upper junction of the labia minora above the urethral opening).

Clinical & Conceptual Clarification: The Hymen

Although the hymen is often torn during first coitus, it can also break due to a sudden fall or jolt, insertion of a tampon, or vigorous sports such as cycling and horseback riding. Conversely, it may persist even after coitus. Therefore, the presence or absence of the hymen is not a reliable indicator of virginity or sexual experience.

Mammary glands are paired structures containing glandular tissue and variable fat. The glandular tissue of each breast is divided into 15–20 mammary lobes containing clusters of milk-secreting cells called alveoli. Milk stored in alveolar lumens flows sequentially through: Alveoli → Mammary tubules → Mammary duct → Mammary ampulla → Lactiferous duct (from which milk is sucked out).

Gametogenesis: Spermatogenesis and Oogenesis

Gametogenesis is the formation of haploid gametes (\(n = 23\) chromosomes) from diploid germ cells (\(2n = 46\) chromosomes) in the primary sex organs.

Spermatogenesis and Hormonal Control

Spermatogenesis begins at puberty due to a marked rise in hypothalamic gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which stimulates the anterior pituitary to secrete two gonadotropins: LH and FSH.

  • LH acts on Leydig cells, stimulating synthesis and secretion of androgens, which in turn stimulate spermatogenesis.
  • FSH acts on Sertoli cells, stimulating secretion of factors that aid spermiogenesis.

Inside the seminiferous tubules, diploid spermatogonia (\(2n = 46\)) multiply by mitosis. Some differentiate into primary spermatocytes (\(2n = 46\)) and undergo Meiosis I (reduction division) to yield two equal haploid secondary spermatocytes (\(n = 23\)). Each secondary spermatocyte undergoes Meiosis II to form two equal haploid spermatids (\(n = 23\)); thus, one primary spermatocyte yields four spermatids. Spermatids transform into motile spermatozoa (sperms) via spermiogenesis. Finally, sperm heads embed in Sertoli cells and are released into the tubular lumen by spermiation.

Structure of a Human Sperm

A mature sperm is enveloped by a plasma membrane and divided into a head, neck, middle piece, and tail:

  • Head: Contains an elongated haploid nucleus capped anteriorly by the acrosome, which is filled with hydrolytic enzymes that aid ovum penetration during fertilisation.
  • Middle piece: Packed with numerous spirally arranged mitochondria that generate ATP for flagellar tail movement.
  • Tail: Facilitates motility essential for reaching the ovum.

A human male ejaculates 200–300 million sperms per coitus. For normal fertility, at least 60% of sperms must have normal shape and size, and at least 40% of them must exhibit vigorous motility.

Oogenesis and Follicular Development

Unlike spermatogenesis, oogenesis begins during embryonic (fetal) development, when a couple of million oogonia form in each fetal ovary; no new oogonia are formed after birth. Oogonia enter Meiosis I and arrest at Prophase I as primary oocytes (\(2n = 46\)). Each primary oocyte is surrounded by a layer of granulosa cells to form a primary follicle. Extensive follicular degeneration (atresia) occurs from birth to puberty, leaving only 60,000–80,000 primary follicles per ovary at puberty.

During reproductive cycles, primary follicles acquire additional granulosa layers and a new theca to become secondary follicles, then transform into tertiary follicles characterised by a fluid-filled cavity called the antrum and a differentiated theca (inner theca interna and outer theca externa). Inside the tertiary follicle, the primary oocyte grows and completes Meiosis I—an unequal division producing one large haploid secondary oocyte (\(n = 23\), retaining most of the nutrient-rich cytoplasm) and a tiny first polar body. The tertiary follicle matures into a Graafian follicle, while the secondary oocyte forms a surrounding glycoprotein layer called the zona pellucida. Rupture of the Graafian follicle releases the secondary oocyte (ovulation). Meiosis II is completed only if a sperm enters the secondary oocyte, yielding a haploid ovum (ootid) and a second polar body.

The Menstrual Cycle and Hormonal Regulation

The reproductive cycle in female primates is the menstrual cycle, averaging 28–29 days. First menstruation at puberty is menarche, and permanent cessation around age 50 is menopause. One ovum is released per cycle during mid-cycle.

Phase (Typical Days) Ovarian Events Uterine (Endometrial) Events Key Hormonal Drivers
Menstrual Phase (Days 1–5) Corpus luteum has degenerated from prior cycle Breakdown of endometrial lining and blood vessels; menstrual flow occurs Sharp drop in progesterone and estrogen
Follicular / Proliferative Phase (Days 6–13) Primary follicles grow into a mature Graafian follicle Endometrium regenerates through proliferation Gradual rise in FSH and LH; growing follicles secrete increasing estrogen
Ovulatory Phase (Around Day 14) Graafian follicle ruptures, releasing secondary oocyte Endometrium primed for potential implantation LH surge (rapid LH peak) along with FSH peak
Luteal / Secretory Phase (Days 15–28) Ruptured follicle transforms into corpus luteum Endometrium maintained, vascularised, and glandular High progesterone secreted by corpus luteum; if no fertilisation, corpus luteum degenerates

Note: While absence of menstruation is a classic sign of pregnancy, it can also result from stress or poor health.

Fertilisation, Sex Determination, and Implantation

During coitus, semen is deposited in the vagina (insemination). Motile sperms swim through the cervix and uterus into the ampullary region of the fallopian tube. Fertilisation occurs only if the ovum and sperms arrive simultaneously at the ampullary region.

  • Acrosomal Reaction & Polyspermy Block: When a sperm contacts the zona pellucida, it induces membrane changes that block entry of additional sperms (ensuring monospermy). Acrosomal enzymes help the sperm enter the ovum's cytoplasm through the zona pellucida and plasma membrane.
  • Completion of Meiosis II & Syngamy: Sperm entry triggers the secondary oocyte to complete Meiosis II, forming a second polar body and a haploid ovum (ootid). Fusion of the haploid sperm and ovum nuclei produces a diploid zygote (\(2n = 46\)).
  • Sex Determination: Human females are homogametic (XX; all ova carry X), whereas males are heterogametic (XY; 50% of sperms carry X, 50% carry Y). Thus, the chromosomal sex of the zygote (XX female or XY male) is determined by the father's sperm.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Mitotic cleavage of the zygote into morula and blastocyst, culminating in endometrial implantation.

As the zygote travels through the isthmus toward the uterus, rapid mitotic divisions called cleavage produce daughter cells termed blastomeres. At the 8–16 blastomere stage, the solid ball of cells is called a morula, which transforms into a hollow blastocyst in the uterus. The blastocyst has an outer layer, the trophoblast (which attaches to the endometrium), and an inner cell mass (ICM) (which differentiates into the embryo and contains pluripotent stem cells). Once uterine cells divide and cover the embedded blastocyst, implantation is complete and pregnancy begins.

Pregnancy, Embryonic Development, Parturition, and Lactation

Placenta and Pregnancy Hormones

After implantation, finger-like chorionic villi grow from the trophoblast and interdigitate with maternal uterine tissue to form the placenta, connected to the embryo via the umbilical cord. The placenta supplies oxygen and nutrients, removes \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and excretory wastes, and functions as an endocrine gland.

  • Hormones produced ONLY during pregnancy: hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin), hPL (human placental lactogen) from the placenta, and relaxin from the ovary (in later pregnancy).
  • Hormones elevated several-fold in maternal blood: Estrogens, progestogens, cortisol, prolactin, and thyroxine—essential for foetal growth, maternal metabolic adaptation, and maintaining pregnancy.

Germ Layers and Gestation Milestones

Immediately after implantation, the inner cell mass differentiates into an outer ectoderm and inner endoderm, followed by a middle mesoderm; these three germ layers give rise to all adult tissues and organs. Human gestation lasts approximately 9 months:

Gestational Time Major Developmental Milestones
End of 1st Month Embryo's heart is formed (first sign detectable via stethoscope heart sound)
End of 2nd Month Foetus develops limbs and digits
End of 12 Weeks (1st Trimester) Most major organ systems formed; limbs and external genital organs well-developed
5th Month First foetal movements and appearance of hair on the head
End of 24 Weeks (2nd Trimester) Body covered with fine hair, eyelids separate, and eyelashes are formed
End of 9 Months Foetus is fully developed and ready for delivery

Parturition and Lactation

Parturition (childbirth) is triggered by a complex neuroendocrine mechanism involving cortisol, estrogens, and oxytocin. Signals from the fully developed foetus and placenta induce mild uterine contractions called the foetal ejection reflex. This stimulates the maternal posterior pituitary to release oxytocin, which causes stronger myometrial contractions, which in turn stimulate further oxytocin release—a classic positive feedback loop—until the baby and placenta are expelled through the birth canal.

Lactation begins toward the end of pregnancy as mammary glands differentiate and secrete milk. The milk produced during the initial few days is called colostrum, which is rich in several antibodies essential for conferring passive immunity and resistance to the newborn.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Gamete Yield and Chromosome Numbers in Spermatogenesis

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Suppose a biopsy sample from a human seminiferous tubule contains \(150\) primary spermatocytes that complete both meiotic divisions synchronously without degeneration.

Questions: (a) How many secondary spermatocytes and spermatozoa will be formed? (b) What is the chromosome number per primary spermatocyte, secondary spermatocyte, and spermatid?

Solution:

Each diploid primary spermatocyte (\(2n = 46\)) undergoes Meiosis I to form \(2\) haploid secondary spermatocytes (\(n = 23\)). Each secondary spermatocyte then undergoes Meiosis II to form \(2\) haploid spermatids (\(n = 23\)), which differentiate into spermatozoa.

\[ \text{Secondary spermatocytes} = 150 \times 2 = 300 \quad ; \quad \text{Spermatozoa} = 150 \times 4 = 600 \]

Answer: (a) \(300\) secondary spermatocytes and \(600\) spermatozoa. (b) Primary spermatocyte: \(46\) chromosomes; secondary spermatocyte: \(23\) chromosomes; spermatid: \(23\) chromosomes.

Example 2: Evaluating Fertility Criteria from a Semen Analysis

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): A clinical semen sample has a total ejaculate count of \(2.4 \times 10^8\) sperms (\(240\text{ million}\)). Laboratory assessment shows \(1.56 \times 10^8\) sperms have normal morphology, and \(8.4 \times 10^7\) sperms show vigorous motility. Does this sample meet both standard fertility thresholds?

Solution:

For normal fertility, at least \(60\%\) of sperms must have normal shape and size, and at least \(40\%\) must show vigorous motility.

\[ \text{Normal morphology \%} = \frac{1.56 \times 10^8}{2.4 \times 10^8} \times 100 = 65\% \quad (\ge 60\% \text{ met}) \] \[ \text{Vigorous motility \%} = \frac{8.4 \times 10^7}{2.4 \times 10^8} \times 100 = 35\% \quad (< 40\% \text{ not met}) \]

Answer: No. While normal morphology (\(65\%\)) satisfies the \(\ge 60\%\) requirement, vigorous motility (\(35\%\)) falls below the \(40\%\) threshold.

Example 3: Predicting Ovulation Timing and Hormonal States in a Menstrual Cycle

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): A woman with a regular \(30\text{-day}\) menstrual cycle experienced the first day of menstrual bleeding on Day 1. Assuming a fixed \(14\text{-day}\) luteal phase, around which day is the LH surge expected, and which hormone dominates on Day 22?

Solution:

Because the luteal phase typically spans the final \(14\text{ days}\) before the next menstruation, ovulation occurs around \(\text{Cycle Length} - 14 = 30 - 14 = \text{Day } 16\). The LH surge triggers ovulation around Day 16. By Day 22 (mid-luteal/secretory phase), the corpus luteum is fully active and secretes large amounts of progesterone to maintain the endometrium.

Answer: LH surge around Day 16; progesterone dominates on Day 22.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following correctly traces the pathway of spermatozoa from their site of production to the urethra?

  1. (A) Seminiferous tubules → Vasa efferentia → Rete testis → Epididymis → Vas deferens → Ejaculatory duct
  2. (B) Seminiferous tubules → Rete testis → Vasa efferentia → Epididymis → Vas deferens → Ejaculatory duct
  3. (C) Seminiferous tubules → Rete testis → Epididymis → Vasa efferentia → Vas deferens → Ejaculatory duct
  4. (D) Seminiferous tubules → Vasa efferentia → Epididymis → Rete testis → Vas deferens → Ejaculatory duct
Answer: (B)

Solution: Spermatozoa produced in the seminiferous tubules open into the rete testis, pass through the vasa efferentia to leave the testis, enter the epididymis on the posterior testicular surface, ascend via the vas deferens, and join the seminal vesicle duct to form the ejaculatory duct opening into the urethra.

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: Leydig cells located in the interstitial spaces of the testis synthesise and secrete androgens under the influence of LH.

Statement II: Sertoli cells line the inside of seminiferous tubules and secrete factors that help in spermiogenesis under the influence of FSH.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  3. (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
Answer: (A)

Solution: LH acts on interstitial (Leydig) cells to stimulate androgen synthesis and secretion, whereas FSH acts on Sertoli cells inside the seminiferous tubules to stimulate factors aiding spermiogenesis. Both statements are accurate.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In human females, the first meiotic division of a primary oocyte is completed:

  1. (A) During fetal life before birth
  2. (B) Within the primary follicle at puberty
  3. (C) Within the tertiary follicle prior to ovulation
  4. (D) In the ampulla of the oviduct after sperm entry
Answer: (C)

Solution: Primary oocytes arrest at Prophase I during fetal life. The primary oocyte grows and completes Meiosis I inside the tertiary follicle, forming a large haploid secondary oocyte and a tiny first polar body prior to ovulation. Meiosis II is completed upon sperm entry.

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): In the absence of fertilisation, the endometrial lining breaks down, leading to menstruation.

Reason (R): Failure of fertilisation causes degeneration of the corpus luteum, resulting in a sharp decline in progesterone levels.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  3. (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
  4. (D) Both (A) and (R) are false
Answer: (A)

Solution: Progesterone secreted by the corpus luteum is essential for maintaining the secretory endometrium. Without fertilisation, the corpus luteum degenerates, progesterone falls, and the endometrium sloughs off as menstrual flow.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Spermiogenesis vs. Spermiation: Spermiogenesis is the transformation of spermatids into spermatozoa; spermiation is the release of mature spermatozoa from Sertoli cells into the seminiferous tubule lumen.
  • Egg Count in Twins: Identical (monozygotic) twins arise from a single fertilised egg (1 ovum), whereas fraternal (dizygotic) twins arise from two separately released and fertilised eggs (2 ova).
  • Ploidy of Secondary Oocyte/Spermatocyte: Both are haploid (\(n = 23\)) because Meiosis I is the reduction division.
  • Site of Fertilisation: Fertilisation occurs in the ampullary region of the fallopian tube, not in the uterus.
  • Breast Milk Pathway: Do not confuse the mammary ampulla (collecting duct dilation in the breast) with the fallopian ampulla (site of fertilisation).

High-Yield Revision Checklist

  • Scrotal temperature: \(2\text{–}2.5\text{ °C}\) below body temperature; ~\(250\) lobules/testis; \(1\text{–}3\) seminiferous tubules/lobule.
  • Seminal plasma: fructose, \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\), enzymes; bulbourethral gland lubricates penis.
  • Ovarian reserve: couple million oogonia in fetus → \(60{,}000\text{–}80{,}000\) primary follicles/ovary at puberty.
  • Menstrual cycle: LH surge on ~Day 14 triggers ovulation; corpus luteum secretes progesterone.
  • Parturition: foetal ejection reflex → maternal pituitary oxytocin → positive-feedback uterine contractions.
  • Colostrum: initial milk rich in antibodies for newborn immunity.

Concise Final Recap

Human reproduction integrates specialised gonads, accessory ducts, and endocrine feedback loops. Spermatogenesis continuously generates four haploid spermatozoa per primary spermatocyte from puberty onward, whereas oogenesis begins prenatally, arrests at Prophase I, and yields one functional ovum per primary oocyte via unequal meiotic divisions. Monthly pituitary-ovarian hormone oscillations coordinate ovulation and endometrial preparation for implantation. Upon fertilisation in the ampullary region, cleavage produces a blastocyst that implants in the endometrium, establishing a placenta that sustains foetal development until oxytocin-driven parturition and antibody-rich lactation.
