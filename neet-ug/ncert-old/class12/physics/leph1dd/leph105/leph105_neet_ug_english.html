Magnetism and Matter

Core principles of magnetic dipoles, Gauss's law for magnetism, magnetisation of materials, and exam-focused problem solving.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Magnetic interactions appear across every scale in nature, from planetary magnetic fields to atomic-level electron motion. When a bar magnet is suspended freely, it aligns roughly along the geographic north–south line because Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet whose magnetic field points approximately from geographic south to geographic north. The pole pointing toward geographic north is designated the north pole, and the opposite end is the south pole.

Target Competencies for NEET UG
  • Analyze magnetic field lines, dipole equivalence between a bar magnet and a finite solenoid, and the electrostatic–magnetostatic analogy.
  • Compute torque, potential energy, work done in rotation, and equilibrium stability for a magnetic dipole in a uniform magnetic field.
  • Apply Gauss's law for magnetism to evaluate magnetic flux through closed surfaces and identify valid field patterns.
  • Relate magnetisation (\(\mathbf{M}\)), magnetic intensity (\(\mathbf{H}\)), net magnetic field (\(\mathbf{B}\)), susceptibility (\(\chi\)), and relative permeability (\(\mu_r\)).
  • Classify diamagnetic, paramagnetic, and ferromagnetic materials by their atomic origin, field-line behaviour, susceptibility, and temperature response.

Magnetic Dipoles, Field Lines, and the Equivalent Solenoid

Two fundamental experimental facts govern permanent magnets: like poles repel while unlike poles attract, and magnetic poles always occur in north–south pairs. Slicing a bar magnet either transversely or longitudinally yields two smaller magnets, each possessing both a north and a south pole. Isolated magnetic charges, known as magnetic monopoles, have never been observed.

Properties of Magnetic Field Lines

Sprinkling iron filings over a glass sheet placed above a bar magnet reveals a characteristic pattern of curved paths around the magnet. These paths represent magnetic field lines, which satisfy four strict geometric rules:

  • Continuous closed loops: Magnetic field lines emerge from the north pole outside the magnet, curve around to enter the south pole, and continue back from south to north inside the magnet. Unlike electric field lines—which start on positive charges and end on negative charges—magnetic field lines have neither a beginning nor an end.
  • Tangent gives field direction: At any point in space, the tangent to a field line gives the direction of the net magnetic field vector \(\mathbf{B}\).
  • Line density indicates strength: Crowded field lines correspond to a stronger magnetic field magnitude, whereas widely spaced lines indicate a weaker field.
  • No intersection: Two magnetic field lines can never cross; an intersection would imply two different directions for \(\mathbf{B}\) at the same point, which is physically impossible.
Terminology Caution

Magnetic field lines should not be called "lines of force." For a charge \(q\) moving with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) in a magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), the magnetic force \(\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\) is always perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\), not tangent to the field line.

Bar Magnet as an Equivalent Solenoid

A current-carrying finite solenoid produces a magnetic field pattern remarkably similar to that of a bar magnet. According to Ampère's hypothesis, all magnetic phenomena arise from circulating electric currents. In a bar magnet, atomic-scale circulating currents behave collectively like a tightly wound solenoid. For a finite solenoid of \(N\) turns, cross-sectional area \(A\), and current \(I\), the magnetic dipole moment has magnitude \(m = N I A\), and its far axial magnetic field at distance \(r\) (much greater than the solenoid's length and radius) is identical to that of a bar magnet of equal magnetic moment:

\[ B_{\text{axial}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{2m}{r^3} \qquad (r \gg l) \]

The Electrostatic Analog for Short Dipoles

At distances \(r\) much larger than the physical size \(l\) of a bar magnet, its magnetic field expressions mirror those of an electric dipole of dipole moment \(\mathbf{p}\) under the systematic replacements \(\mathbf{E} \rightarrow \mathbf{B}\), \(\mathbf{p} \rightarrow \mathbf{m}\), and \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \rightarrow \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\).

Notice that for a given distance \(r \gg l\), the axial field magnitude is always twice the equatorial field magnitude (\(B_A = 2 B_E\)). Along the axis, \(\mathbf{B}_A\) points in the same direction as \(\mathbf{m}\); along the equatorial bisector, \(\mathbf{B}_E\) points opposite to \(\mathbf{m}\).

Torque, Potential Energy, and Equilibrium in a Uniform Field

When a small magnetic needle or bar magnet of magnetic moment \(\mathbf{m}\) is placed in a uniform external magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), the two poles experience equal and opposite forces, so the net translational force is zero. However, if \(\mathbf{m}\) makes an angle \(\theta\) with \(\mathbf{B}\), these forces form a couple that exerts a restoring torque tending to align \(\mathbf{m}\) with \(\mathbf{B}\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{B}, \qquad \tau = m B \sin\theta \]

By contrast, in a non-uniform magnetic field (such as near the pole of another magnet), an unmagnetised iron nail acquires an induced magnetic moment and experiences both a torque and a net attractive force because the nearer induced pole lies in a stronger field region than the farther pole.

Diagram 2: A magnetic dipole of moment m oriented at angle θ to a uniform magnetic field B experiences a restoring torque τ = mB sin θ.

Magnetic Potential Energy and Equilibrium States

Choosing the zero of potential energy at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (when \(\mathbf{m} \perp \mathbf{B}\)), the magnetic potential energy \(U_m\) of the dipole is:

\[ U_m = \int_{90^\circ}^{\theta} m B \sin\theta'\,d\theta' = -m B \cos\theta = -\mathbf{m} \cdot \mathbf{B} \]
  • Stable equilibrium (\(\theta = 0^\circ\), \(\mathbf{m} \parallel \mathbf{B}\)): Potential energy is minimum, \(U_{\min} = -mB\), and \(\tau = 0\). Any small angular displacement produces a restoring torque.
  • Unstable equilibrium (\(\theta = 180^\circ\), \(\mathbf{m}\) antiparallel to \(\mathbf{B}\)): Potential energy is maximum, \(U_{\max} = +mB\), and \(\tau = 0\). Any tiny disturbance causes the dipole to flip toward \(\theta = 0^\circ\).
  • Work done by an external torque: Rotating a dipole slowly from angle \(\theta_1\) to \(\theta_2\) requires external work \(W_{\text{ext}} = U(\theta_2) - U(\theta_1) = mB(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2)\).

Gauss's Law for Magnetism

Consider any closed surface \(S\) divided into tiny vector area elements \(\Delta \mathbf{S}\) pointing outward. The magnetic flux through an element is \(\Delta\phi_B = \mathbf{B} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{S}\). Because magnetic field lines always form continuous closed loops, every field line that enters the closed surface \(S\) must also exit it somewhere else. Consequently, the net magnetic flux through any closed surface is identically zero:

\[ \phi_B = \sum_{\text{all }\Delta S} \mathbf{B} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{S} = 0 \]

In contrast, Gauss's law for electrostatics states \(\sum \mathbf{E} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{S} = q_{\text{enc}} / \varepsilon_0\), which can be non-zero because isolated electric charges exist. The vanishing net magnetic flux is the direct mathematical expression of the non-existence of magnetic monopoles: there are no sources or sinks of \(\mathbf{B}\).

Testing Field Line Diagrams

In exam questions, you can immediately spot invalid magnetostatic field-line patterns using Gauss's law and Ampère's law:

  • Lines radiating outward from a point or emerging exclusively from a flat plate violate \(\phi_B = 0\) (such patterns represent electric charges, not magnetic fields).
  • Static magnetic field lines cannot form closed loops around empty space; any closed loop of \(\mathbf{B}\) must enclose an electric current (as inside a toroid) or pass through a magnetised body.
  • Field lines between pole pieces or at the ends of a finite solenoid cannot remain perfectly straight right up to the edges; realistic fringing must occur.

Magnetisation, Magnetic Intensity, and Permeability

In bulk matter, atomic orbital and spin magnetic moments add vectorially. The magnetisation \(\mathbf{M}\) of a sample is defined as its net magnetic dipole moment per unit volume:

\[ \mathbf{M} = \frac{\mathbf{m}_{\text{net}}}{V} \qquad (\text{SI unit: }\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}) \]

Consider a long solenoid having \(n\) turns per unit length carrying current \(I\). In vacuum, it produces a magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}_0 = \mu_0 n I\). When a material core is inserted inside the solenoid, the aligned atomic dipoles in the core produce an additional magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}_m = \mu_0 \mathbf{M}\). The total magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) inside the core becomes:

\[ \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}_0 + \mathbf{B}_m = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M}) \]

Here \(\mathbf{H}\) is the magnetic intensity (also measured in \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\)), defined by \(\mathbf{H} = \frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0} - \mathbf{M}\). In a long solenoid, \(\mathbf{H} = nI\) depends solely on the external free current in the windings, independent of the core material.

For linear magnetic materials, the induced magnetisation is directly proportional to \(\mathbf{H}\):

\[ \mathbf{M} = \chi \mathbf{H} \]

The dimensionless proportionality constant \(\chi\) is the magnetic susceptibility, which measures how strongly a material responds to an applied magnetising field. Substituting \(\mathbf{M} = \chi \mathbf{H}\) into the expression for \(\mathbf{B}\) gives:

\[ \mathbf{B} = \mu_0(1 + \chi)\mathbf{H} = \mu_0 \mu_r \mathbf{H} = \mu \mathbf{H} \]

where \(\mu_r = 1 + \chi\) is the dimensionless relative magnetic permeability (analogous to the dielectric constant in electrostatics) and \(\mu = \mu_0 \mu_r\) is the magnetic permeability of the medium. Knowing any one of \(\chi\), \(\mu_r\), or \(\mu\) determines the other two.

Classification of Magnetic Materials

Based on the sign and magnitude of susceptibility \(\chi\), magnetic substances fall into three principal categories: diamagnetic, paramagnetic, and ferromagnetic.

Diagram 3: Characteristic distortion of external magnetic field lines by diamagnetic, paramagnetic, and ferromagnetic specimens.

1. Diamagnetic Materials

Diamagnetic substances are weakly repelled by magnets and tend to move from stronger to weaker parts of a non-uniform magnetic field. Their atoms have zero net permanent magnetic dipole moment. When an external magnetic field is applied, orbiting electrons whose magnetic moment aligns with the field slow down while those with opposite moment speed up (consistent with Lenz's law), creating a small net induced dipole moment opposite to the applied field. Consequently, \(\mathbf{M}\) opposes \(\mathbf{H}\) and \(\chi\) is small and negative (typically \(\sim -10^{-5}\)).

  • Universality: Diamagnetism exists in all materials, though it is masked when stronger paramagnetic or ferromagnetic effects are present.
  • Examples: Bismuth, copper, lead, silicon, nitrogen (at STP), water, and sodium chloride.
  • Superconductors (Perfect Diamagnets): Metals cooled to very low temperatures exhibit both zero electrical resistance and complete expulsion of magnetic field lines—a phenomenon called the Meissner effect, explained by the BCS quantum theory. For superconductors, \(\chi = -1\), \(\mu_r = 0\), and \(\mu = 0\).

2. Paramagnetic Materials

Paramagnetic substances are weakly attracted by magnets and move from weaker to stronger regions of a non-uniform field. Their individual atoms or ions possess permanent magnetic dipole moments, which normally point in random directions due to thermal agitation. An external field partially aligns these dipoles along \(\mathbf{B}_0\), producing a small positive susceptibility (\(\chi \sim +10^{-5}\)). Increasing the applied field or lowering the temperature increases alignment until magnetic saturation is reached.

  • Examples: Aluminium, sodium, calcium, oxygen (at STP), and copper chloride.

3. Ferromagnetic Materials

Ferromagnetic substances are strongly attracted by magnets and move vigorously from weaker to stronger magnetic fields. Although their atoms carry permanent dipole moments like paramagnets, strong quantum-mechanical interactions cause neighbouring atomic moments to align spontaneously over macroscopic regions called domains (typical size \(\sim 1\,\mathrm{mm}\), containing roughly \(10^{11}\) atoms). In an unmagnetised sample, domains point in random directions so net magnetisation is zero. Applying an external field rotates domains into alignment and causes favourably oriented domains to grow, producing \(\mu_r \gg 1\) (often \(> 1000\)) and a non-linear \(\mathbf{B}\)–\(\mathbf{H}\) relationship.

  • Hard ferromagnets: Retain magnetisation after the external field is removed and are used for permanent magnets (e.g., Alnico—an alloy of Al, Ni, Co, Cu, and Fe—and naturally occurring lodestone).
  • Soft ferromagnets: Lose magnetisation readily when the external field is switched off (e.g., soft iron, used in electromagnets and transformer cores).
  • Temperature effect: Heating a ferromagnet to a sufficiently high temperature disintegrates its domain structure, turning it into a paramagnetic substance.
  • Examples: Iron, cobalt, nickel, and gadolinium.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Torque and Work Done in Rotating a Magnetic Dipole

Assumption: The following hypothetical values are chosen for illustration.

Problem: A short bar magnet of magnetic moment \(m = 2.4\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}\) is placed in a uniform magnetic field \(B = 0.30\,\mathrm{T}\). Calculate: (a) the torque when its axis makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the field, (b) the external work required to rotate it slowly from stable equilibrium to \(\theta = 60^\circ\), and (c) its potential energy in unstable equilibrium.

Solution:

(a) Torque magnitude: \[ \tau = m B \sin\theta = (2.4\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}})(0.30\,\mathrm{T})\sin 30^\circ = 0.72 \times 0.5 = 0.36\,\mathrm{N\,m} \]

(b) Stable equilibrium corresponds to \(\theta_1 = 0^\circ\). Rotating slowly to \(\theta_2 = 60^\circ\) requires external work equal to the change in potential energy: \[ W_{\text{ext}} = mB(\cos 0^\circ - \cos 60^\circ) = 0.72 \times (1 - 0.5) = 0.36\,\mathrm{J} \]

(c) Unstable equilibrium occurs at \(\theta = 180^\circ\): \[ U_{\max} = -mB\cos 180^\circ = +mB = +0.72\,\mathrm{J} \]

Example 2: Axial and Equatorial Fields of a Short Magnet

Assumption: The magnet is treated as an ideal short dipole (\(r \gg l\)) with hypothetical parameters.

Problem: A short bar magnet has a magnetic moment of \(0.80\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}\). Find the magnitude and direction of the magnetic field produced at a distance of \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) from its centre on (a) the axial line and (b) the equatorial line.

Solution:

Given \(m = 0.80\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}\), \(r = 20\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\), and \(\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} = 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}}\).

(a) On the axial line: \[ B_A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{2m}{r^3} = 10^{-7} \times \frac{2 \times 0.80}{(0.20)^3} = 10^{-7} \times \frac{1.6}{0.008} = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T} = 0.20\,\mathrm{G} \] Direction: along the magnetic dipole moment \(\mathbf{m}\) (from south pole to north pole).

(b) On the equatorial line at the same distance: \[ B_E = \frac{B_A}{2} = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T} = 0.10\,\mathrm{G} \] Direction: opposite to \(\mathbf{m}\) (from north pole to south pole).

Example 3: Solenoid Core Magnetisation and Susceptibility

Assumption: Hypothetical solenoid winding and core parameters are used.

Problem: A long solenoid with \(1500\,\mathrm{turns\,m^{-1}}\) carries a current of \(1.6\,\mathrm{A}\). Its core is filled with a linear magnetic material of relative permeability \(\mu_r = 250\). Determine (a) the magnetic intensity \(H\), (b) the susceptibility \(\chi\), (c) the magnetisation \(M\), and (d) the net magnetic field \(B\) inside the core.

Solution:

(a) Magnetic intensity depends only on free current: \[ H = nI = 1500 \times 1.6 = 2400\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}} \]

(b) Magnetic susceptibility: \[ \chi = \mu_r - 1 = 250 - 1 = 249 \]

(c) Magnetisation: \[ M = \chi H = 249 \times 2400 = 5.976 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}} \approx 5.98 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}} \]

(d) Net magnetic field inside the core: \[ B = \mu_0 \mu_r H = (4\pi \times 10^{-7}) \times 250 \times 2400 = 0.24\pi\,\mathrm{T} \approx 0.754\,\mathrm{T} \]

Example 4: Null Points and Earth's Horizontal Magnetic Field

Assumption: Hypothetical null-point distance and Earth's horizontal field value are chosen.

Problem: A short bar magnet is placed horizontally with its north pole pointing toward geographic north. Null points are obtained on its equatorial bisector at a distance of \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the centre of the magnet. If Earth's horizontal magnetic field is \(B_H = 0.40\,\mathrm{G} = 4.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}\), find (a) the magnetic moment of the magnet and (b) the net magnetic field on its axial line at the same distance of \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Solution:

(a) Because \(\mathbf{m}\) points north, the equatorial field \(\mathbf{B}_E\) points south, directly opposing \(B_H\). At the null point, \(B_E = B_H\): \[ \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{m}{r^3} = B_H \implies 10^{-7} \times \frac{m}{(0.10)^3} = 4.0 \times 10^{-5} \] \[ m = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-5} \times 10^{-3}}{10^{-7}} = 0.40\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}} \]

(b) On the axial line at \(r = 10\,\mathrm{cm}\), \(\mathbf{B}_A\) points north (parallel to \(B_H\)) and has magnitude \(B_A = 2B_E = 2B_H = 0.80\,\mathrm{G}\). Therefore, the net magnetic field is: \[ B_{\text{net}} = B_A + B_H = 0.80\,\mathrm{G} + 0.40\,\mathrm{G} = 1.20\,\mathrm{G} = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T} \]

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A closely wound solenoid of \(1200\) turns and cross-sectional area \(2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) carries a current of \(2.5\,\mathrm{A}\). It is suspended horizontally in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.40\,\mathrm{T}\) with its axis making an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the field. The magnitude of the torque acting on the solenoid is:

  • (A) \(0.06\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)
  • (B) \(0.12\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)
  • (C) \(0.24\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)
  • (D) \(0.48\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)
Answer: (B) \(0.12\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)

Worked Solution: The magnetic moment of the solenoid is \(m = N I A = 1200 \times 2.5 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-4} = 0.60\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}\). The torque is \(\tau = m B \sin 30^\circ = 0.60 \times 0.40 \times 0.5 = 0.12\,\mathrm{N\,m}\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A uniform bar magnet of magnetic moment \(m\) is first cut into two equal pieces along its length. One of these pieces is then cut into two equal parts perpendicular to its length. What is the magnetic moment of each of the resulting smallest pieces?

  • (A) \(m / 2\)
  • (B) \(m / 4\)
  • (C) \(m / 8\)
  • (D) \(m\)
Answer: (B) \(m / 4\)

Worked Solution: Cutting the magnet along its length halves its cross-sectional area and pole strength while keeping its length unchanged, giving magnetic moment \(m_1 = m/2\). Cutting that half transversely halves its length while keeping pole strength unchanged, giving \(m_2 = m_1/2 = m/4\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A magnetic dipole of magnetic moment \(0.50\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}\) initially lies parallel to a uniform magnetic field of \(0.20\,\mathrm{T}\). The work required by an external agent to turn the dipole so that it points opposite to the field is:

  • (A) \(0.05\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(0.10\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(0.20\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(0.40\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (C) \(0.20\,\mathrm{J}\)

Worked Solution: Rotating from \(\theta_1 = 0^\circ\) to \(\theta_2 = 180^\circ\) requires work \(W = mB(\cos 0^\circ - \cos 180^\circ) = 2mB = 2 \times 0.50 \times 0.20 = 0.20\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

According to Gauss's law for magnetism, the net magnetic flux through any closed surface is always zero. Which physical fact does this law directly reflect?

  • (A) Magnetic fields are always uniform inside a closed surface.
  • (B) Isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist.
  • (C) Electric charges cannot produce magnetic fields.
  • (D) Magnetic field lines always terminate on south poles.
Answer: (B) Isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist.

Worked Solution: Since magnetic monopoles do not exist, there are no sources or sinks of \(\mathbf{B}\). Every magnetic field line forms a continuous closed loop, so the number of lines entering any closed surface equals the number leaving it, yielding \(\phi_B = 0\).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

For a short bar magnet, the magnitude of the magnetic field on its axial line at a large distance \(r\) from its centre is \(B_1\), and the magnitude on its equatorial line at a distance \(r/2\) from its centre is \(B_2\). The ratio \(B_1 : B_2\) is:

  • (A) \(1 : 4\)
  • (B) \(1 : 2\)
  • (C) \(2 : 1\)
  • (D) \(4 : 1\)
Answer: (A) \(1 : 4\)

Worked Solution: Using short-dipole formulas, \(B_1 = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{r^3}\) and \(B_2 = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{m}{(r/2)^3} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{8m}{r^3}\). Therefore, \(\frac{B_1}{B_2} = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A substance has a magnetic susceptibility \(\chi = -2.5 \times 10^{-5}\). Its relative magnetic permeability \(\mu_r\) and magnetic nature are respectively:

  • (A) \(1.000025\), paramagnetic
  • (B) \(0.999975\), diamagnetic
  • (C) \(-0.999975\), diamagnetic
  • (D) \(0.999975\), paramagnetic
Answer: (B) \(0.999975\), diamagnetic

Worked Solution: Negative susceptibility (\(-1 \le \chi < 0\)) identifies a diamagnetic material. Using \(\mu_r = 1 + \chi\), we get \(\mu_r = 1 - 2.5 \times 10^{-5} = 0.999975\).

Practice Question 7 (Two-Statement MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: Diamagnetism is present in all materials, even though it may be masked by stronger paramagnetic or ferromagnetic effects.

Statement II: A superconductor cooled below its critical temperature exhibits perfect diamagnetism with \(\chi = -1\) and \(\mu_r = 0\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.

Worked Solution: Diamagnetism arises from the induced orbital response of electrons (Lenz's law) in all matter, making it universal. Superconductors exhibit the Meissner effect, completely expelling magnetic flux so that \(\mu_r = 0\) and \(\chi = \mu_r - 1 = -1\).

Practice Question 8 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): When placed in a non-uniform magnetic field, a paramagnetic specimen tends to move from the weaker field region toward the stronger field region.

Reason (R): Paramagnetic substances have a small positive susceptibility (\(\chi > 0\)), so their permanent atomic dipoles align partially along the external field and concentrate magnetic field lines inside the specimen.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Worked Solution: Because atomic dipoles align partially with \(\mathbf{B}_0\) (\(\chi > 0\)), the induced magnetisation \(\mathbf{M}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{H}\), lowering potential energy in stronger field regions and pulling the specimen from weaker to stronger fields.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements regarding ferromagnetic materials is incorrect?

  • (A) Atoms in a ferromagnet align spontaneously within macroscopic regions called domains.
  • (B) Soft iron loses its magnetisation readily when the external magnetising field is removed.
  • (C) The net magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) is strictly proportional to \(\mathbf{H}\) for all values of \(\mathbf{H}\).
  • (D) At sufficiently high temperatures, a ferromagnetic substance becomes paramagnetic.
Answer: (C) The net magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) is strictly proportional to \(\mathbf{H}\) for all values of \(\mathbf{H}\).

Worked Solution: Ferromagnetic materials are characterised by a non-linear relationship between \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{H}\) due to domain wall motion and saturation. All other statements are true.

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A long solenoid with \(500\,\mathrm{turns\,m^{-1}}\) carries a current of \(2.0\,\mathrm{A}\). Its interior is filled with a core of magnetic susceptibility \(\chi = 499\). Taking \(\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}}\), the magnetic field \(B\) inside the core is:

  • (A) \(0.1\pi\,\mathrm{T}\)
  • (B) \(0.2\pi\,\mathrm{T}\)
  • (C) \(0.4\pi\,\mathrm{T}\)
  • (D) \(2.0\pi\,\mathrm{T}\)
Answer: (B) \(0.2\pi\,\mathrm{T}\)

Worked Solution: Magnetic intensity \(H = nI = 500 \times 2.0 = 1000\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\). Relative permeability \(\mu_r = 1 + \chi = 1 + 499 = 500\). Therefore, \(B = \mu_0 \mu_r H = (4\pi \times 10^{-7}) \times 500 \times 1000 = 0.2\pi\,\mathrm{T}\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{H}\): In a solenoid with a core, \(\mathbf{H} = nI\) is unchanged by inserting the core, whereas \(\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mu_r H\) increases by a factor of \(\mu_r\).
  • Forgetting the factor of 2 in dipole fields: Always check whether the point lies on the axis (\(2m/r^3\)) or on the equatorial bisector (\(m/r^3\)).
  • Sign errors in potential energy and work: Remember \(U = -mB\cos\theta\), so \(U = -mB\) at \(\theta = 0^\circ\) and \(+mB\) at \(\theta = 180^\circ\). Rotating from \(0^\circ\) to \(180^\circ\) requires \(2mB\) of work, not \(mB\).
  • Mixing up \(\chi\) and \(\mu_r\): For diamagnets, \(\chi\) is negative (\(-1 \le \chi < 0\)), while \(\mu_r = 1 + \chi\) remains non-negative (\(0 \le \mu_r < 1\)).
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Can you write \(B_{\text{axial}}\), \(B_{\text{equatorial}}\), \(\tau\), and \(U_m\) from memory with correct signs?
  • Can you distinguish valid magnetic field-line diagrams from electrostatic diagrams using \(\phi_B = 0\)?
  • Do you remember the \(\chi\) and \(\mu_r\) ranges and examples for diamagnetic, paramagnetic, and ferromagnetic materials?
  • Can you convert between gauss and tesla (\(10^4\,\mathrm{G} = 1\,\mathrm{T}\)) quickly?

Concise Final Recap

Magnetic poles always exist in dipoles, so magnetic field lines form continuous closed loops and the net magnetic flux through any closed surface is zero. A bar magnet of moment \(\mathbf{m}\) behaves like an equivalent solenoid, producing an axial field twice as strong as its equatorial field at the same far distance, and experiencing torque \(\mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{B}\) with potential energy \(-\mathbf{m} \cdot \mathbf{B}\) in a uniform field. Inside matter, \(\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M}) = \mu_0(1 + \chi)\mathbf{H}\), separating substances into diamagnetic (\(-1 \le \chi < 0\)), paramagnetic (\(\chi > 0\) small), and domain-governed ferromagnetic (\(\chi \gg 1\)) materials.
