Moving Charges and Magnetism

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide covering magnetic forces, particle trajectories, Biot–Savart law, Ampere's circuital law, magnetic dipoles, and moving coil galvanometers.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Electric charges at rest create only electric fields, whereas charges in steady motion (electric currents) generate both electric and magnetic fields. This tutorial builds a unified understanding of how magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges and current-carrying conductors, how steady currents produce magnetic fields in symmetric geometries, and how these principles operate in measuring instruments.

  • Apply the Lorentz force equation to find the magnitude and direction of forces on moving charges and current-carrying wires.
  • Analyze circular and helical trajectories of charged particles in uniform magnetic fields, including radius, time period, cyclotron frequency, and pitch.
  • Compute magnetic fields produced by current elements, circular loops, straight wires, and long solenoids using the Biot–Savart law and Ampere's circuital law.
  • Evaluate forces between parallel current-carrying conductors and torques on current loops acting as magnetic dipoles.
  • Design and analyze moving coil galvanometers, ammeters, and voltmeters for circuit measurements.

Magnetic Fields and the Lorentz Force

Vector Conventions and Units of Magnetic Field

A magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r})\) is a vector field defined at every point in space and obeys the principle of superposition: the net magnetic field from multiple sources is the vector sum of the individual fields. To represent vectors perpendicular to the page in two-dimensional diagrams, two standard symbols are used:

  • Out of the page (\(\odot\)): A circle with a central dot represents a vector pointing toward the viewer.
  • Into the page (\(\otimes\)): A circle with a cross represents a vector pointing away from the viewer.

The SI unit of magnetic field is the tesla (\(\mathrm{T}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{T} = 1\,\mathrm{N\cdot s/(C\cdot m)} = 1\,\mathrm{N/(A\cdot m)}\). A smaller non-SI unit frequently encountered in problems is the gauss (\(\mathrm{G}\)), related by \(1\,\mathrm{G} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}\). The dimensional formula of \(\mathbf{B}\) is \([\mathrm{M\,T^{-2}\,A^{-1}}]\).

The Lorentz Force on a Moving Charge

When a point charge \(q\) moves with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) at a location where both an electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) and a magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) exist, the total electromagnetic force acting on it is the Lorentz force:

\[\mathbf{F} = q\left(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\right)\]

Focusing on the magnetic component \(\mathbf{F}_m = q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\), its magnitude is \(F_m = |q|vB\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\). Key physical consequences include:

  • Stationary charges feel no magnetic force: If \(v = 0\), then \(\mathbf{F}_m = \mathbf{0}\).
  • Parallel or antiparallel motion produces zero force: If \(\theta = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\), \(\sin\theta = 0\), so the charge passes undeflected.
  • Direction via the right-hand rule: Curling the fingers of the right hand from \(\mathbf{v}\) toward \(\mathbf{B}\) gives the direction of \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\) along the extended thumb. A positive charge experiences force in this direction; a negative charge experiences force in the exact opposite direction.
  • Zero work done: Because \(\mathbf{F}_m\) is always perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\), the instantaneous power \(\mathbf{F}_m \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\). A pure magnetic field can change the direction of velocity (and hence momentum) but cannot change the speed or kinetic energy of a particle.

Magnetic Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

A straight conductor of length \(l\) and cross-sectional area \(A\) carrying steady current \(I\) contains mobile charge carriers of number density \(n\), charge \(q\), and drift velocity \(\mathbf{v}_d\). Summing the magnetic forces on all \(nlA\) carriers yields:

\[\mathbf{F} = I\left(\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{B}\right), \qquad F = I l B \sin\theta\]

Here \(\mathbf{l}\) is a displacement vector of magnitude \(l\) pointing in the direction of conventional current \(I\), and \(\mathbf{B}\) is the external magnetic field. For a curved wire in a uniform magnetic field, \(\mathbf{l}\) is replaced by the straight displacement vector joining the two ends of the wire.

Motion of a Charged Particle in a Uniform Magnetic Field

Case 1: Velocity Perpendicular to the Magnetic Field (Circular Path)

When a particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) enters a uniform magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) with \(\mathbf{v} \perp \mathbf{B}\), the magnetic force \(qvB\) acts perpendicular to both \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\), serving as a constant-magnitude centripetal force. Equating \(qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r}\) gives:

\[r = \frac{mv}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB} = \frac{\sqrt{2mK}}{qB}\]

where \(p = mv\) is the linear momentum and \(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\) is the kinetic energy. The angular frequency (\(\omega\)), cyclotron frequency (\(\nu_c\)), and time period (\(T\)) of revolution are:

\[\omega = \frac{qB}{m}, \qquad \nu_c = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}\]

Crucially, \(\omega\), \(\nu_c\), and \(T\) depend only on the charge-to-mass ratio (\(q/m\)) and the magnetic field \(B\); they are completely independent of the particle's speed, radius, or kinetic energy (in the non-relativistic regime).

Case 2: Velocity at an Arbitrary Angle (Helical Path)

If \(\mathbf{v}\) makes an angle \(\theta\) (other than \(0^\circ\), \(90^\circ\), or \(180^\circ\)) with \(\mathbf{B}\), we resolve \(\mathbf{v}\) into two components: \(v_\parallel = v\cos\theta\) along \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(v_\perp = v\sin\theta\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\). The parallel component experiences zero magnetic force and remains constant, while the perpendicular component drives uniform circular motion. Combining both motions produces a helix:

  • Radius of the helix: \(r = \dfrac{m v_\perp}{qB} = \dfrac{mv\sin\theta}{qB}\)
  • Pitch of the helix (\(p\)): The distance advanced along \(\mathbf{B}\) in one complete revolution is \(p = v_\parallel T = \dfrac{2\pi m v\cos\theta}{qB}\).

Magnetic Fields from Steady Currents: Biot–Savart Law

Statement and Vector Form

The Biot–Savart law determines the infinitesimal magnetic field \(d\mathbf{B}\) produced at a point \(P\) (at displacement \(\mathbf{r}\)) by an infinitesimal current element \(I\,d\mathbf{l}\):

\[d\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\left(d\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{r}\right)}{r^3}, \qquad dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I\,dl\sin\theta}{r^2}\]

Here \(\theta\) is the angle between \(d\mathbf{l}\) and \(\mathbf{r}\), and \(\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}\) is the permeability of free space, so \(\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi} = 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}\). Notice that along the line of the current element (\(\theta = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\)), \(dB = 0\). Moreover, \(\mu_0\), the vacuum permittivity \(\varepsilon_0\), and the speed of light in vacuum \(c\) satisfy the fundamental relation:

\[\mu_0 \varepsilon_0 = \frac{1}{c^2}\]

Magnetic Field on the Axis of a Circular Current Loop

Consider a circular loop of radius \(R\) carrying a steady current \(I\), with its centre at the origin. At a point \(P\) on the axis at distance \(x\) from the centre, every element \(d\mathbf{l}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{r}\) (where \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + R^2}\)). By symmetry, components of \(d\mathbf{B}\) perpendicular to the axis cancel in pairs, leaving only the axial component. Integrating around the loop (and multiplying by \(N\) for a tightly wound \(N\)-turn coil) gives:

\[B_{\text{axis}} = \frac{\mu_0 N I R^2}{2\left(x^2 + R^2\right)^{3/2}}\]

Special cases frequently tested in NEET UG:

  • At the centre of the coil (\(x = 0\)): \(B_0 = \dfrac{\mu_0 N I}{2R}\).
  • At the centre of a circular arc subtending angle \(\alpha\) (in radians): \(B_{\text{arc}} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I \alpha}{4\pi R}\) (for a semicircle, \(\alpha = \pi\), giving \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4R}\)).

The direction of \(\mathbf{B}\) along the axis is given by the right-hand thumb rule for loops: curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of the current; the extended thumb points along \(\mathbf{B}\).

Ampere's Circuital Law, Solenoids, and Parallel Currents

Ampere's Circuital Law

For steady currents, Ampere's circuital law relates the line integral of \(\mathbf{B}\) around any closed Amperian loop \(C\) to the total net current \(I_e\) enclosed by the surface bounded by \(C\):

\[\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 I_e\]

The sign of each current is determined by curling the right-hand fingers along the loop traversal direction; currents pointing along the thumb are taken as positive. Whenever symmetry makes \(\mathbf{B}\) tangential and constant in magnitude over a path of length \(L\), this simplifies to \(BL = \mu_0 I_e\).

Magnetic Field of a Long Straight Conductor

  • Thin wire or outside a thick cylindrical wire (\(r \ge a\)): Choosing a concentric circular Amperian loop of radius \(r\) gives \(B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I\), so: \[B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} \quad \left(B \propto \frac{1}{r}\right)\]
  • Inside a solid cylindrical wire of radius \(a\) with uniform current density (\(r < a\)): The enclosed current is \(I_e = I\left(\frac{\pi r^2}{\pi a^2}\right) = \frac{Ir^2}{a^2}\), yielding: \[B = \frac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi a^2} \quad \left(B \propto r\right)\]

Thus, \(B\) increases linearly from zero at the wire's axis to a maximum \(\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\) at the surface \(r = a\), and then decreases inversely with \(r\) outside. The direction is tangential to concentric circles, found by grasping the wire with the right thumb along \(I\); curled fingers show the direction of \(\mathbf{B}\).

The Long Solenoid

A long solenoid consists of closely wound helical turns of insulated wire whose length \(L\) is much greater than its radius. Inside the solenoid (away from the ends), the magnetic field is strong, uniform, and parallel to the axis, while the exterior field is negligible. Applying Ampere's law to a rectangular loop with one side of length \(h\) inside the solenoid gives \(Bh = \mu_0 (nhI)\), or:

\[B = \mu_0 n I, \qquad \text{where } n = \frac{N}{L} \text{ (turns per unit length)}\]

Force Between Two Parallel Current-Carrying Conductors

Two long, straight, parallel conductors separated by a distance \(d\) and carrying steady currents \(I_1\) and \(I_2\) exert magnetic forces of equal magnitude and opposite direction on each other. The force per unit length \(f = F/L\) on each conductor is:

\[f = \frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}\]

Rule of interaction: Parallel currents (same direction) attract, whereas antiparallel currents (opposite directions) repel—the opposite of electrostatic charges, where like charges repel. This relation underpins the historical definition of the ampere: a steady current of \(1\,\mathrm{A}\) in two infinitely long, parallel wires \(1\,\mathrm{m}\) apart in vacuum produces a force of \(2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N/m}\).

Torque on a Current Loop, Magnetic Dipole, and Galvanometers

Current Loop as a Magnetic Dipole

A planar loop of area \(A\) with \(N\) closely wound turns carrying current \(I\) behaves as a magnetic dipole with magnetic dipole moment \(\mathbf{m}\):

\[\mathbf{m} = N I \mathbf{A}\]

The unit of \(m\) is \(\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}\) (or \(\mathrm{J/T}\)), and its direction follows the right-hand thumb rule along the area vector \(\mathbf{A}\). In a uniform magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), the net force on any closed current loop is zero, but it experiences a torque:

\[\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{B}, \qquad \tau = N I A B \sin\theta\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{m}\) (the normal to the coil) and \(\mathbf{B}\). At \(\theta = 0^\circ\) (\(\mathbf{m} \parallel \mathbf{B}\)), the loop is in stable equilibrium; at \(\theta = 180^\circ\), it is in unstable equilibrium.

At large distances (\(x \gg R\)), the magnetic field of a circular loop mirrors the electric field of an electric dipole: on the axis, \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{2m}{x^3}\); in the plane of the loop (equatorial), \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{m}{x^3}\).

Moving Coil Galvanometer (MCG)

A moving coil galvanometer detects and measures small currents. A multi-turn coil is pivoted around a cylindrical soft-iron core inside concave pole pieces of a permanent magnet. The soft-iron core strengthens the field and makes it radial, so the plane of the coil always stays parallel to the field lines (\(\theta = 90^\circ\), \(\sin\theta = 1\)). Balancing the magnetic torque \(NIAB\) against the restoring torque \(k\phi\) of a spring (torsion constant \(k\)) gives:

\[\phi = \left(\frac{NAB}{k}\right) I\]
  • Current sensitivity: \(S_I = \dfrac{\phi}{I} = \dfrac{NAB}{k}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{rad/A}\) or \(\mathrm{div/A}\)).
  • Voltage sensitivity: \(S_V = \dfrac{\phi}{V} = \dfrac{NAB}{k R_G}\), where \(R_G\) is the galvanometer coil resistance.
Key Conceptual Distinction Increasing the number of turns \(N\) (with the same wire gauge and area) increases current sensitivity proportionally, because \(S_I \propto N\). However, the coil resistance \(R_G\) also increases proportionally with length (\(R_G \propto N\)), so voltage sensitivity \(S_V = \frac{NAB}{kR_G}\) remains unchanged!

Conversion into an Ammeter and a Voltmeter

Suppose a galvanometer of resistance \(R_G\) gives full-scale deflection at current \(I_g\):

  • Ammeter (measures up to \(I > I_g\)): Connect a very small shunt resistance \(r_s\) in parallel with the galvanometer so most of the current bypasses the coil. Since \(I_g R_G = (I - I_g) r_s\): \[r_s = \frac{I_g R_G}{I - I_g}\] The effective resistance of the ammeter is \(R_A = \dfrac{R_G r_s}{R_G + r_s} < r_s\). An ideal ammeter has zero resistance and is connected in series.
  • Voltmeter (measures up to \(V > I_g R_G\)): Connect a very large series resistance \(R\) with the galvanometer to limit current. Since \(V = I_g(R_G + R)\): \[R = \frac{V}{I_g} - R_G\] The effective resistance is \(R_V = R_G + R\). An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance and is connected in parallel.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Balancing Gravity with Magnetic Force on a Conducting Rod

Assumed values for illustration: mass \(m = 150\,\mathrm{g}\), length \(l = 0.75\,\mathrm{m}\), current \(I = 4.0\,\mathrm{A}\), and \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\).

Problem: A horizontal copper rod of mass \(150\,\mathrm{g}\) and length \(0.75\,\mathrm{m}\) carries a steady current of \(4.0\,\mathrm{A}\) from west to east. What minimum uniform horizontal magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), directed from south to north, is required to keep the rod suspended in mid-air?

Solution:

The current flows east (\(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\)) and the magnetic field points north (\(\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)), so \(\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{B}\) points vertically upward (\(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\)), opposing gravity. Since \(\theta = 90^\circ\):

\[F = I l B \sin 90^\circ = I l B\]

For vertical equilibrium, \(I l B = m g\):

\[B = \frac{m g}{I l} = \frac{0.15\,\mathrm{kg} \times 9.8\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}{4.0\,\mathrm{A} \times 0.75\,\mathrm{m}} = \frac{1.47}{3.0} = 0.49\,\mathrm{T}\]

Example 2: Helical Trajectory of a Proton in a Uniform Magnetic Field

Assumed values for illustration: \(B = 0.20\,\mathrm{T}\), \(v = 4.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\theta = 30^\circ\); standard proton constants \(m = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(q = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\).

Problem: A proton enters a uniform magnetic field of \(0.20\,\mathrm{T}\) with a speed of \(4.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\) at an angle of \(30^\circ\) to the field direction. Find the radius and pitch of the resulting helical path.

Solution:

Resolve the velocity into perpendicular and parallel components:

\[v_\perp = v \sin 30^\circ = 4.0 \times 10^5 \times 0.5 = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\] \[v_\parallel = v \cos 30^\circ = 4.0 \times 10^5 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 3.464 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m/s}\]

Radius of the helix:

\[r = \frac{m v_\perp}{q B} = \frac{1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times 2.0 \times 10^5}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.20} = 1.044 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 1.04\,\mathrm{cm}\]

Time period of one revolution:

\[T = \frac{2\pi m}{q B} = \frac{2\pi \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.20} \approx 3.279 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{s}\]

Pitch of the helix:

\[p = v_\parallel T = 3.464 \times 10^5 \times 3.279 \times 10^{-7} \approx 0.114\,\mathrm{m} = 11.4\,\mathrm{cm}\]

Example 3: Magnetic Field at the Centre of a Circular Arc with Radial Leads

Assumed values for illustration: current \(I = 6.0\,\mathrm{A}\), arc radius \(R = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\), subtended angle \(\alpha = 270^\circ = \frac{3\pi}{2}\,\mathrm{rad}\).

Problem: A wire carrying a steady current of \(6.0\,\mathrm{A}\) is bent into a three-quarter circular arc of radius \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) with two straight segments extending radially outward from the centre. Calculate the magnitude of the magnetic field at the centre of the arc.

Solution:

For the two straight radial segments, every current element \(d\mathbf{l}\) is collinear with the displacement vector \(\mathbf{r}\) to the centre, so \(d\mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{0}\) and they contribute zero magnetic field.

For the circular arc subtending \(\alpha = \frac{3\pi}{2}\,\mathrm{rad}\):

\[B = \frac{\mu_0 I \alpha}{4\pi R} = 10^{-7} \times \frac{6.0 \times (3\pi / 2)}{0.04} = \frac{9\pi \times 10^{-7}}{0.04} = 2.25\pi \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 7.07 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T}\]

Example 4: Force Between Antiparallel Current-Carrying Wires

Assumed values for illustration: separation \(d = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}\), currents \(I_1 = 6.0\,\mathrm{A}\) and \(I_2 = 10\,\mathrm{A}\) in opposite directions.

Problem: Two long, straight, parallel conductors separated by \(5.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) in air carry steady currents of \(6.0\,\mathrm{A}\) and \(10\,\mathrm{A}\) in opposite directions. Determine the magnitude and nature of the magnetic force per unit length acting on each conductor.

Solution:

The force per unit length is:

\[f = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d} = \frac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 6.0 \times 10}{2\pi \times 0.05} = \frac{2 \times 10^{-7} \times 60}{0.05} = 2.4 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N/m}\]

Because the currents flow in opposite directions (antiparallel), the force is repulsive.

Example 5: Designing an Ammeter and a Voltmeter from a Galvanometer

Assumed values for illustration: \(R_G = 50\,\mathrm{Ω}\), \(I_g = 2.0\,\mathrm{mA}\), ammeter range \(5.0\,\mathrm{A}\), voltmeter range \(10\,\mathrm{V}\).

Problem: A moving coil galvanometer has a coil resistance of \(50\,\mathrm{Ω}\) and shows full-scale deflection for a current of \(2.0\,\mathrm{mA}\). How can it be converted into (a) an ammeter of range \(0\) to \(5.0\,\mathrm{A}\), and (b) a voltmeter of range \(0\) to \(10\,\mathrm{V}\)?

Solution:

(a) Conversion to Ammeter: Connect a small shunt resistance \(r_s\) in parallel:

\[r_s = \frac{I_g R_G}{I - I_g} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 50}{5.0 - 0.002} = \frac{0.1}{4.998} \approx 0.020\,\mathrm{Ω}\]

(b) Conversion to Voltmeter: Connect a high resistance \(R\) in series:

\[R = \frac{V}{I_g} - R_G = \frac{10}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}} - 50 = 5000 - 50 = 4950\,\mathrm{Ω}\]

NEET UG Practice Questions

These original practice questions are aligned with the NEET UG examination pattern. Attempt each question independently before checking the worked solution.

Practice Question 1 (Conceptual MCQ)

A proton enters a region containing only a uniform magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) with its velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\). Which of the following physical quantities changes during its subsequent motion?

  1. (A) Speed of the proton
  2. (B) Kinetic energy of the proton
  3. (C) Linear momentum of the proton
  4. (D) Cyclotron frequency of the proton
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: The magnetic force \(\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\) is always perpendicular to the velocity \(\mathbf{v}\), so it performs zero work. Consequently, the speed and kinetic energy remain constant, and the cyclotron frequency \(\nu_c = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}\) is constant. However, the direction of \(\mathbf{v}\) changes continuously, so the linear momentum vector \(\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}\) changes.

Practice Question 2 (Numerical MCQ)

A proton and an alpha particle (having mass \(4m_p\) and charge \(2e\)) enter a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the field lines with the same kinetic energy. What is the ratio of the radius of the proton's circular path to that of the alpha particle (\(r_p : r_\alpha\))?

  1. (A) \(1 : 1\)
  2. (B) \(1 : 2\)
  3. (C) \(2 : 1\)
  4. (D) \(1 : 4\)
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: The radius in terms of kinetic energy \(K\) is \(r = \frac{\sqrt{2mK}}{qB}\). Since \(K\) and \(B\) are identical for both particles:

\[\frac{r_p}{r_\alpha} = \frac{\sqrt{m_p}/e}{\sqrt{4m_p}/(2e)} = \frac{1}{2/2} = 1 : 1\]

Practice Question 3 (Numerical MCQ)

The magnetic field at the centre of a flat circular current-carrying loop of radius \(R\) is \(B_0\). What is the magnitude of the magnetic field at a point on the axis of the loop at a distance \(x = \sqrt{3}R\) from its centre?

  1. (A) \(B_0 / 2\)
  2. (B) \(B_0 / 4\)
  3. (C) \(B_0 / 8\)
  4. (D) \(B_0 / 16\)
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Using the axial field formula \(B(x) = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2(x^2 + R^2)^{3/2}}\) and \(B_0 = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}\):

\[B(\sqrt{3}R) = B_0 \frac{R^3}{\left(3R^2 + R^2\right)^{3/2}} = B_0 \frac{R^3}{\left(4R^2\right)^{3/2}} = \frac{B_0}{8}\]

Practice Question 4 (Numerical MCQ)

A long, straight, solid cylindrical conductor of radius \(a\) carries a steady current \(I\) distributed uniformly across its cross-section. What is the ratio of the magnetic field at a radial distance \(r = a/2\) to the magnetic field at \(r = 2a\) from the axis?

  1. (A) \(1 : 2\)
  2. (B) \(1 : 1\)
  3. (C) \(2 : 1\)
  4. (D) \(1 : 4\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Inside the wire (\(r = a/2 < a\)), \(B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I (a/2)}{2\pi a^2} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a}\). Outside the wire (\(r = 2a > a\)), \(B_2 = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi (2a)} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a}\). Hence, \(B_1 : B_2 = 1 : 1\).

Practice Question 5 (Numerical MCQ)

A long solenoid of length \(0.60\,\mathrm{m}\) has \(1200\) turns and carries a steady current of \(2.5\,\mathrm{A}\). What is the magnitude of the magnetic field inside the solenoid near its centre? (Take \(\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m/A}\))

  1. (A) \(3.14 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}\)
  2. (B) \(6.28 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}\)
  3. (C) \(1.26 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{T}\)
  4. (D) \(2.51 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: The number of turns per unit length is \(n = \frac{1200}{0.60} = 2000\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\). Inside the solenoid:

\[B = \mu_0 n I = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 2000 \times 2.5 = 2\pi \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 6.28 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{T}\]

Practice Question 6 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): Two long, straight, parallel conductors carrying steady currents in the same direction attract each other.
Reason (R): Each conductor experiences a Lorentz force directed toward the other due to the magnetic field produced by the current in the neighbouring conductor.

  1. (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  2. (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  3. (C) A is true, but R is false.
  4. (D) Both A and R are false.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: By the right-hand rule, the magnetic field of the first wire at the second wire is perpendicular to the second wire, and \(\mathbf{l}_2 \times \mathbf{B}_1\) points directly toward the first wire. Thus both statements are true and R correctly explains A.

Practice Question 7 (Numerical MCQ)

A circular coil of \(50\) turns and cross-sectional area \(2.0 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) carries a current of \(3.0\,\mathrm{A}\). It is placed in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.40\,\mathrm{T}\) such that the plane of the coil makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the magnetic field. What is the magnitude of the torque acting on the coil?

  1. (A) \(0.60\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  2. (B) \(0.60\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  3. (C) \(1.20\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  4. (D) \(0.30\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: The angle \(\theta\) in \(\tau = NIAB\sin\theta\) is between the normal to the coil and \(\mathbf{B}\). Since the plane makes \(30^\circ\) with \(\mathbf{B}\), the normal makes \(\theta = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ\):

\[\tau = 50 \times 3.0 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-2}) \times 0.40 \times \sin 60^\circ = 1.20 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 0.60\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\]

Practice Question 8 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: In a moving coil galvanometer, a radial magnetic field ensures that the deflecting torque on the coil remains constant in magnitude regardless of its angular position.
Statement II: Doubling the number of turns in a galvanometer coil (using the same wire gauge, coil area, magnetic field, and spring) doubles its voltage sensitivity.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: In a radial field, the coil's plane always lies along the field lines (\(\sin\theta = 1\)), so \(\tau = NIAB\) is independent of deflection angle (Statement I is correct). Doubling \(N\) doubles \(R_G\) as well, so voltage sensitivity \(S_V = \frac{NAB}{kR_G}\) remains unchanged (Statement II is incorrect).

Practice Question 9 (Numerical MCQ)

A galvanometer of coil resistance \(90\,\mathrm{Ω}\) gives full-scale deflection for a current of \(10\,\mathrm{mA}\). What shunt resistance is required to convert this galvanometer into an ammeter of range \(0\) to \(1.0\,\mathrm{A}\)?

  1. (A) \(0.91\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  2. (B) \(1.00\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  3. (C) \(9.09\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  4. (D) \(0.09\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Using \(I_g = 10\,\mathrm{mA} = 0.01\,\mathrm{A}\), \(R_G = 90\,\mathrm{Ω}\), and \(I = 1.0\,\mathrm{A}\):

\[r_s = \frac{I_g R_G}{I - I_g} = \frac{0.01 \times 90}{1.0 - 0.01} = \frac{0.90}{0.99} = \frac{10}{11}\,\mathrm{Ω} \approx 0.91\,\mathrm{Ω}\]

Practice Question 10 (Numerical MCQ)

A flat circular coil of \(20\) turns and radius \(5.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) carries a steady current of \(2.0\,\mathrm{A}\). What is the magnitude of its magnetic dipole moment? (Take \(\pi = 3.14\))

  1. (A) \(0.157\,\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}\)
  2. (B) \(0.314\,\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}\)
  3. (C) \(0.628\,\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}\)
  4. (D) \(3.14\,\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: The magnetic dipole moment is \(m = N I A = N I (\pi R^2)\):

\[m = 20 \times 2.0 \times 3.14 \times (0.05\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 40 \times 3.14 \times 0.0025 = 0.314\,\mathrm{A\cdot m^2}\]

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Angle confusion in torque vs. trajectory: In \(\tau = NIAB\sin\theta\), \(\theta\) is the angle between the normal to the coil and \(\mathbf{B}\), not between the plane of the coil and \(\mathbf{B}\).
  • Sign of charge in Lorentz force: Always reverse the direction of \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\) when the moving particle is negatively charged (such as an electron).
  • Parallel currents vs. like charges: Like static charges repel, whereas parallel currents attract and antiparallel currents repel.
  • Unit conversions: Convert gauss to tesla (\(1\,\mathrm{G} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}\)), centimetres to metres, and milliamperes to amperes before substituting into SI equations.
  • Galvanometer sensitivities: Remember that increasing \(N\) increases current sensitivity but does not automatically increase voltage sensitivity if coil resistance scales with \(N\).

Concise Final Recap

Moving charges generate magnetic fields described by the Biot–Savart law and Ampere's circuital law, and experience velocity-dependent magnetic forces that do zero work. In uniform magnetic fields, charges follow circular or helical paths with energy-independent cyclotron frequency, while current loops act as magnetic dipoles experiencing torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{B}\)—the operating principle behind moving coil galvanometers, ammeters, and voltmeters.
