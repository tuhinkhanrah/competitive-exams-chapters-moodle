Electrostatic Potential and Capacitance

A complete conceptual guide, formula reference, worked examples, and NEET UG practice assessment.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Electrostatics can be analyzed through vector fields (\(\mathbf{E}\)) or through scalar energy quantities—electrostatic potential (\(V\)) and potential energy (\(U\)). Because potential is a scalar, it simplifies multi-charge calculations and forms the foundation for understanding conductors, dielectrics, and capacitors.

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Relate electrostatic work, potential energy difference, and electrostatic potential using the conservative nature of Coulomb forces.
  • Calculate electric potential due to point charges, dipoles, multi-charge systems, and uniformly charged spherical shells.
  • Interpret equipotential surfaces and apply the gradient relation between electric field and potential.
  • Explain the electrostatic behavior of conductors (including shielding) and linear isotropic dielectrics.
  • Analyze parallel-plate capacitors with and without dielectrics, solve series-parallel networks, and compute stored energy and energy loss during charge sharing.

Conservative Forces, Potential Energy, and Potential

The electrostatic force between stationary charges is a conservative force. When an external agent moves a test charge \(q\) very slowly (without acceleration, so \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} = -\mathbf{F}_E\)) from point \(R\) to point \(P\), the work done depends only on the initial and final positions and is completely independent of the path taken.

This external work is stored as the change in electrostatic potential energy \(\Delta U\):

Potential Energy Difference & Electrostatic Potential
\[ \Delta U = U_P - U_R = W_{RP} = \int_R^P \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -q \int_R^P \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \] \[ V_P - V_R = \frac{U_P - U_R}{q} = -\int_R^P \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \]

Only differences in potential energy and potential are physically measurable. By choosing the reference point \(R\) at infinity with \(U_\infty = 0\) and \(V_\infty = 0\), the electrostatic potential \(V\) at any point is defined as the external work done per unit positive test charge in bringing it quasi-statically from infinity to that point.

  • SI Unit of Potential: volt (\(\mathrm{V}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{V} = 1\,\mathrm{J\,C^{-1}}\).
  • Electron-Volt (\(\mathrm{eV}\)): A convenient energy unit in atomic and nuclear physics, defined as the kinetic energy gained by an electron accelerated across a \(1\,\mathrm{V}\) potential difference: \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\).

Potential Due to a Point Charge and Superposition

Integrating the radial force on a unit positive test charge from \(\infty\) to distance \(r\) from a point charge \(Q\) yields:

\[ V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r} \]

Unlike electric field magnitude (\(E \propto 1/r^2\)), potential falls off more slowly as \(1/r\) and carries the algebraic sign of \(Q\) (\(V > 0\) for \(Q > 0\); \(V < 0\) for \(Q < 0\)).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Normalized comparison showing potential \(V \propto 1/r\) decaying more gradually than electric field \(E \propto 1/r^2\) for \(r > 1\).

By the superposition principle, the net potential at a point \(P\) due to discrete charges \(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\) at distances \(r_{1P}, r_{2P}, \dots, r_{nP}\) is the scalar algebraic sum:

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{q_i}{r_{iP}} \]

For a uniformly charged spherical shell of radius \(R\) and total charge \(Q\):

  • Outside (\(r \ge R\)): \(V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r}\) (behaves as if all charge is at the center).
  • Inside (\(r < R\)): Since \(E = 0\) inside, no work is done moving a charge inside, so potential is constant and equals the surface value: \(V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{R}\).

Electric Dipoles and Equipotential Surfaces

Potential Due to an Electric Dipole

An electric dipole consists of charges \(+q\) and \(-q\) separated by a small distance \(2a\), with dipole moment \(\mathbf{p}\) (magnitude \(p = 2qa\), directed from \(-q\) to \(+q\)). At a point \(P\) with distance \(r \gg a\) and angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf{p}\) and position vector \(\mathbf{r}\):

\[ V(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{p \cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\mathbf{p} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2} \quad (r \gg a) \]
  • Axial line (\(\theta = 0°\) or \(180°\)): \(V = \pm \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\).
  • Equatorial plane (\(\theta = 90°\)): \(V = 0\) everywhere on the equatorial plane, even though the electric field there is non-zero.
  • Distance dependence: Dipole potential falls as \(1/r^2\) (faster than \(1/r\) for a monopole) and depends on angle \(\theta\).

Equipotential Surfaces and Field–Potential Relation

An equipotential surface is a locus of points having the same electrostatic potential. Key properties tested frequently in NEET UG:

  1. Zero Work: No work is done by the electric field or an external agent in moving a charge between any two points on an equipotential surface (\(\Delta W = q\Delta V = 0\)).
  2. Orthogonality: Electric field lines are always perpendicular (normal) to equipotential surfaces at every point. If a tangential component existed, moving a charge along the surface would require work, contradicting constant potential.
  3. No Intersection: Two equipotential surfaces with different potentials can never intersect, because a single point cannot have two distinct potential values.
  4. Spacing: Equipotential surfaces are closer together in regions of stronger electric field, because \(|\mathbf{E}| = -\frac{dV}{dl}\) (or for small steps, \(|\mathbf{E}| = \frac{|\delta V|}{\delta l}\)).
Tutorial Diagram 2: Equipotential surfaces (teal) are always normal to electric field lines (blue), which point in the direction of decreasing potential.

The electric field vector always points in the direction of the steepest decrease in potential. In Cartesian coordinates, if \(V(x,y,z)\) is known, the field components are \(E_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\), \(E_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\), and \(E_z = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\).

Electrostatic Potential Energy of Systems and External Fields

System of Point Charges

The potential energy \(U\) of a system of point charges is the total external work required to assemble them from infinity to their positions. Because electrostatic forces are conservative, \(U\) is independent of the order of assembly:

\[ U_{\text{2-charge}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} \] \[ U_{\text{3-charge}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1 q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2 q_3}{r_{23}} \right) \]

Like charges (\(q_1 q_2 > 0\)) have positive potential energy (repulsion); unlike charges (\(q_1 q_2 < 0\)) have negative potential energy (bound/attractive system).

Charges and Dipoles in an External Field

  • Single charge \(q\) in external potential \(V(\mathbf{r})\): \(U = qV(\mathbf{r})\).
  • Two charges \(q_1, q_2\) in external potential \(V(\mathbf{r})\): \[ U = q_1 V(\mathbf{r}_1) + q_2 V(\mathbf{r}_2) + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} \]
  • Dipole \(\mathbf{p}\) in a uniform external electric field \(\mathbf{E}\): The net force is zero, but a torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E}\) acts on it. Taking \(U = 0\) at \(\theta = 90°\), the potential energy and work done in rotating from \(\theta_1\) to \(\theta_2\) are:
    \[ U(\theta) = -\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{E} = -pE\cos\theta \] \[ W_{\theta_1 \to \theta_2} = U(\theta_2) - U(\theta_1) = pE(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2) \]
    At \(\theta = 0°\), \(U = -pE\) (minimum, stable equilibrium). At \(\theta = 180°\), \(U = +pE\) (maximum, unstable equilibrium).

Electrostatics of Conductors and Dielectrics

Six Fundamental Properties of Metallic Conductors in Equilibrium

  1. Zero Interior Electric Field: Under electrostatic equilibrium, \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}\) everywhere inside the conducting material; free electrons redistribute until internal fields cancel any external field.
  2. Normal Surface Field: At the surface of a charged conductor, \(\mathbf{E}\) is strictly perpendicular to the surface; any tangential component would cause surface currents.
  3. Surface-Only Excess Charge: By Gauss's law, since \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}\) inside, the interior volume charge density is zero (\(\rho = 0\)). Any excess charge resides entirely on the outer surface.
  4. Constant Potential: Because \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}\) inside and has no tangential surface component, the entire conductor (interior and surface) is an equipotential volume at a single constant potential \(V_0\).
  5. Field Just Outside the Surface: Applying Gauss's law to a small pillbox straddling the surface gives: \[ \mathbf{E} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf{n}} \] where \(\sigma\) is the local surface charge density and \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is the outward unit normal.
  6. Electrostatic Shielding: Inside any charge-free cavity within a conductor, \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}\) regardless of external charges or fields. Sensitive electronic instruments are enclosed in metallic shells to shield them from external electric fields.

Dielectrics and Polarisation

Dielectrics are insulators with negligible free charge carriers. When placed in an external electric field \(\mathbf{E}_0\), they do not conduct; instead, they undergo polarisation:

  • Non-polar molecules (e.g., \(\mathrm{H}_2, \mathrm{O}_2, \mathrm{CO}_2\)) have coinciding centers of positive and negative charge when \(E_0 = 0\). An external field stretches them to create an induced dipole moment.
  • Polar molecules (e.g., \(\mathrm{HCl}, \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)) possess a permanent dipole moment. Thermal agitation randomizes their orientations at \(E_0 = 0\), and an external field partially aligns them.

The dipole moment per unit volume is the polarisation vector \(\mathbf{P} = \varepsilon_0 \chi_e \mathbf{E}\) (for linear isotropic dielectrics, where \(\chi_e\) is electric susceptibility). Polarisation produces bound surface charge densities \(\pm\sigma_p\) that create an opposing internal field, reducing—but not cancelling—the external field:

\[ E_{\text{net}} = \frac{E_0}{K}, \quad \text{where } K = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0} > 1 \text{ is the dielectric constant.} \]

Charge carriers Free electrons Bound charges (no free migration)

Capacitors, Combinations, and Energy Storage

A capacitor is a pair of conductors separated by an insulator (or vacuum) holding equal and opposite charges \(+Q\) and \(-Q\) at a potential difference \(V\). Its capacitance \(C\) is:

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} \quad (\text{SI unit: farad, } 1\,\mathrm{F} = 1\,\mathrm{C\,V^{-1}}) \]

Capacitance depends strictly on geometry (plate area, separation, shape) and the intervening dielectric medium—never on \(Q\) or \(V\). Common sub-multiples are \(1\,\mathrm{µF} = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{F}\), \(1\,\mathrm{nF} = 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{F}\), and \(1\,\mathrm{pF} = 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F}\). The maximum electric field an insulator can sustain without breakdown (ionization) is its dielectric strength (about \(3 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) for air).

Parallel-Plate Capacitor (Vacuum and Dielectric)

For two large parallel plates of area \(A\) and separation \(d\) (\(d^2 \ll A\)), the electric field is uniform between the plates (\(E_0 = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)\)) and zero outside:

\[ C_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} \quad (\text{vacuum/air}) \] \[ C = K C_0 = \frac{K \varepsilon_0 A}{d} \quad (\text{fully filled with dielectric } K) \] \[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t + \frac{t}{K}} \quad (\text{partially filled with slab of thickness } t < d) \]

Series and Parallel Combinations

\[ \text{Series: } \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{eq}}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{C_n} \qquad \text{Parallel: } C_{\mathrm{eq}} = C_1 + C_2 + \dots + C_n \]

Energy Stored, Energy Density, and Charge Redistribution

The work done in charging a capacitor from \(0\) to \(Q\) is stored in the electric field between its plates:

\[ U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}QV \] \[ \text{Energy density (per unit volume): } u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \quad (\text{or } \frac{1}{2}K\varepsilon_0 E^2 \text{ in a dielectric}) \]

When two capacitors \((C_1, V_1)\) and \((C_2, V_2)\) are connected in parallel after disconnecting from their sources, total charge is conserved, giving a common potential \(V_c\) and an energy loss \(\Delta U\) (dissipated as heat and electromagnetic radiation during the transient current):

\[ V_c = \frac{C_1 V_1 + C_2 V_2}{C_1 + C_2}, \qquad \Delta U_{\text{loss}} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2} (V_1 - V_2)^2 \]
Quantity (Dielectric \(K\) inserted) Battery Disconnected (\(Q = \text{constant}\)) Battery Connected (\(V = \text{constant}\))
Capacitance \(C\) Increases to \(KC_0\) Increases to \(KC_0\)
Charge \(Q\) Remains \(Q_0\) Increases to \(KQ_0\)
Potential Difference \(V\) Decreases to \(V_0/K\) Remains \(V_0\)
Electric Field \(E\) Decreases to \(E_0/K\) Remains \(E_0\)
Stored Energy \(U\) Decreases to \(U_0/K\) Increases to \(KU_0\)

Worked Examples

Example 1: Finding the Zero-Potential Point for Two Point Charges

Problem (Assumed values): Two point charges \(q_1 = +6\,\mathrm{µC}\) and \(q_2 = -2\,\mathrm{µC}\) are fixed \(24\,\mathrm{cm}\) apart on the \(x\)-axis, with \(q_1\) at \(x = 0\) and \(q_2\) at \(x = 24\,\mathrm{cm}\). Find all points on the \(x\)-axis where the net electrostatic potential is zero (taking \(V = 0\) at infinity).

Solution:

Let \(P\) be a point on the \(x\)-axis where \(V = V_1 + V_2 = 0\). Because \(|q_1| > |q_2|\), \(P\) must lie closer to the smaller-magnitude charge \(q_2\). There are two possible regions: between the charges (\(0 < x < 24\,\mathrm{cm}\)) and to the right of \(q_2\) (\(x > 24\,\mathrm{cm}\)).

Case 1 (Between the charges):

\[ \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{6 \times 10^{-6}}{x} - \frac{2 \times 10^{-6}}{24 - x}\right) = 0 \implies \frac{6}{x} = \frac{2}{24 - x} \implies 6(24 - x) = 2x \implies 144 = 8x \implies x = 18\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Case 2 (Beyond \(q_2\), \(x > 24\,\mathrm{cm}\)):

\[ \frac{6}{x} = \frac{2}{x - 24} \implies 6(x - 24) = 2x \implies 4x = 144 \implies x = 36\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Thus, the electric potential is zero at \(x = 18\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(x = 36\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the \(+6\,\mathrm{µC}\) charge.

Example 2: Assembling a Three-Charge Triangular System

Problem (Assumed values): Three point charges \(q_1 = +2\,\mathrm{µC}\), \(q_2 = +2\,\mathrm{µC}\), and \(q_3 = -4\,\mathrm{µC}\) are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side \(d = 30\,\mathrm{cm}\). Calculate the total electrostatic potential energy of the system.

Solution:

Using the pairwise potential energy formula with \(d = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\):

\[ U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 d}(q_1 q_2 + q_1 q_3 + q_2 q_3) \] \[ q_1 q_2 + q_1 q_3 + q_2 q_3 = (2)(2) + (2)(-4) + (2)(-4) = 4 - 8 - 8 = -12\,\mathrm{µC^2} = -12 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2} \] \[ U = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times (-12 \times 10^{-12})}{0.30} = -0.36\,\mathrm{J} \]

The negative sign indicates that the configuration is net bound and \(0.36\,\mathrm{J}\) of work would be required by an external agent to separate all three charges to infinity.

Example 3: Rotating an Electric Dipole in a Uniform Field

Problem (Assumed values): An electric dipole with dipole moment \(p = 4 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m}\) is aligned parallel to a uniform electric field \(E = 5 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\). Calculate the work done by an external agent in slowly rotating the dipole through \(120°\).

Solution:

Here \(\theta_1 = 0°\) and \(\theta_2 = 120°\). The external work done equals the change in potential energy:

\[ W = pE(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2) = (4 \times 10^{-8})(5 \times 10^4)\left(\cos 0° - \cos 120°\right) \] \[ W = 2 \times 10^{-3} \times \left(1 - (-0.5)\right) = 2 \times 10^{-3} \times 1.5 = 3 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J} = 3\,\mathrm{mJ} \]
Example 4: Partially Filled Parallel-Plate Capacitor

Problem (Assumed values): An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has a capacitance \(C_0 = 20\,\mathrm{pF}\). A dielectric slab of dielectric constant \(K = 4\) and thickness equal to half the plate separation (\(t = d/2\)) is inserted parallel to the plates. Find the new capacitance.

Solution:

Using the partial-dielectric formula with \(t = d/2\) and \(K = 4\):

\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t + \frac{t}{K}} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - \frac{d}{2} + \frac{d}{8}} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{\frac{5}{8}d} = \frac{8}{5}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\right) = 1.6\,C_0 \] \[ C = 1.6 \times 20\,\mathrm{pF} = 32\,\mathrm{pF} \]
Example 5: Energy Loss During Charge Sharing

Problem (Assumed values): A \(12\,\mathrm{µF}\) capacitor is charged to \(50\,\mathrm{V}\) and then disconnected from the battery. It is now connected in parallel across an uncharged \(8\,\mathrm{µF}\) capacitor. Find the common potential and the electrostatic energy lost in the process.

Solution:

Given \(C_1 = 12\,\mathrm{µF}\), \(V_1 = 50\,\mathrm{V}\), \(C_2 = 8\,\mathrm{µF}\), and \(V_2 = 0\,\mathrm{V}\):

\[ V_c = \frac{C_1 V_1 + C_2 V_2}{C_1 + C_2} = \frac{12 \times 50 + 0}{12 + 8} = \frac{600}{20} = 30\,\mathrm{V} \]

The energy lost during redistribution is:

\[ \Delta U_{\text{loss}} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}(V_1 - V_2)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{12 \times 8}{12 + 8} \times 10^{-6} \times (50 - 0)^2 \] \[ \Delta U_{\text{loss}} = \frac{1}{2} \times 4.8 \times 10^{-6} \times 2500 = 6.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J} = 6.0\,\mathrm{mJ} \]

NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are modeled on the NEET UG Physics examination pattern (single-correct MCQs, statement-based, and assertion-reason questions).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A hollow metallic sphere of radius \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) is charged such that the potential on its surface is \(80\,\mathrm{V}\). What is the electrostatic potential at a distance of \(4\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the center of the sphere?

  • (A) \(0\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(32\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(80\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(200\,\mathrm{V}\)

Answer: (C) \(80\,\mathrm{V}\)

Worked Solution: Inside a uniformly charged hollow spherical conductor, the electric field is zero everywhere (\(r < R\)). Since \(E = -dV/dr = 0\), the potential remains constant throughout the interior and equals the potential at the surface, which is \(80\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a region of space, the electrostatic potential is given by \(V(x, y) = 4x^2 - 3y\,\mathrm{V}\), where \(x\) and \(y\) are in meters. What is the magnitude of the electric field at the point \((1\,\mathrm{m}, 2\,\mathrm{m})\)?

  • (A) \(5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(\sqrt{73}\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(11\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(\sqrt{55}\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)

Answer: (B) \(\sqrt{73}\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)

Worked Solution: Using \(E_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = -8x\) and \(E_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} = 3\). At \((1, 2)\), \(E_x = -8\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) and \(E_y = 3\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\). Thus, \(|\mathbf{E}| = \sqrt{(-8)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{64 + 9} = \sqrt{73}\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Four point charges \(+q, +q, -q,\) and \(-q\) are placed in order at the four corners of a square of side \(a\). What is the electrostatic potential at the center of the square?

  • (A) \(\frac{4q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}\)
  • (B) \(\frac{2\sqrt{2}q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}\)
  • (C) Zero
  • (D) \(\frac{-2q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}\)

Answer: (C) Zero

Worked Solution: All four corners are equidistant (\(r = a/\sqrt{2}\)) from the center. By superposition, \(V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}(+q + q - q - q) = 0\).

Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): No work is done in moving a test charge from one point to another on an equipotential surface.
Reason (R): The electric field is always perpendicular to the equipotential surface at every point.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: On an equipotential surface, \(\Delta V = 0\), so \(W = q\Delta V = 0\). Because \(\mathbf{E}\) is everywhere perpendicular to any infinitesimal displacement \(d\mathbf{l}\) along the surface, \(\mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0\), which directly explains why zero work is done.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

An electric dipole of moment \(p\) is placed in a uniform electric field \(E\). If it is displaced slightly from its position of stable equilibrium, what is the potential energy of the dipole in the stable equilibrium position?

  • (A) \(+pE\)
  • (B) \(0\)
  • (C) \(-pE\)
  • (D) \(-pE/2\)

Answer: (C) \(-pE\)

Worked Solution: Potential energy of a dipole is \(U = -pE\cos\theta\). Stable equilibrium corresponds to minimum potential energy, which occurs at \(\theta = 0°\), giving \(U = -pE\cos 0° = -pE\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A parallel-plate capacitor has capacitance \(C_0\) with air between its plates. If the plate separation is doubled and the space is completely filled with a dielectric of dielectric constant \(K = 6\), the new capacitance becomes:

  • (A) \(3C_0\)
  • (B) \(12C_0\)
  • (C) \(C_0/3\)
  • (D) \(6C_0\)

Answer: (A) \(3C_0\)

Worked Solution: Initially \(C_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\). With \(d' = 2d\) and \(K = 6\), \(C' = \frac{K\varepsilon_0 A}{d'} = \frac{6\varepsilon_0 A}{2d} = 3\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = 3C_0\).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A parallel-plate capacitor is charged by a battery, and the battery is then disconnected. A dielectric slab of constant \(K\) is now inserted to fill the space between the plates. Which of the following statements is correct?

  • (A) Charge on the plates increases by a factor of \(K\).
  • (B) Potential difference between the plates increases by a factor of \(K\).
  • (C) Energy stored in the capacitor decreases by a factor of \(K\).
  • (D) Electric field between the plates remains unchanged.

Answer: (C) Energy stored in the capacitor decreases by a factor of \(K\).

Worked Solution: Since the battery is disconnected, the charge \(Q\) remains constant. Capacitance increases to \(C = KC_0\), so \(V = Q/C = V_0/K\) and \(E = E_0/K\). Stored energy becomes \(U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{Q^2}{2KC_0} = \frac{U_0}{K}\).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Three capacitors of capacitances \(3\,\mathrm{µF}\), \(6\,\mathrm{µF}\), and \(6\,\mathrm{µF}\) are connected in series across a \(120\,\mathrm{V}\) DC source. What is the potential difference across the \(3\,\mathrm{µF}\) capacitor?

  • (A) \(30\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(40\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(60\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(80\,\mathrm{V}\)

Answer: (C) \(60\,\mathrm{V}\)

Worked Solution: Equivalent capacitance: \(\frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{eq}}} = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3} \implies C_{\mathrm{eq}} = 1.5\,\mathrm{µF}\). Charge on each capacitor in series is \(Q = C_{\mathrm{eq}} V = 1.5\,\mathrm{µF} \times 120\,\mathrm{V} = 180\,\mathrm{µC}\). Therefore, \(V_{3\,\mathrm{µF}} = \frac{Q}{3\,\mathrm{µF}} = \frac{180}{3} = 60\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 9 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Consider the following two statements regarding electrostatic shielding:
Statement I: The electric field inside a charge-free cavity of a conductor is always zero, even if external electric fields are present.
Statement II: If a charge \(+q\) is placed inside the cavity of a neutral conductor, the exterior of the conductor is completely shielded from the electric field of \(+q\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.

Answer: (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.

Worked Solution: A conductor shields a charge-free cavity from external fields (Statement I is true). However, electrostatic shielding does not work in reverse: a charge \(+q\) placed inside the cavity induces \(-q\) on the inner cavity wall and \(+q\) on the outer surface of the conductor, creating an external field (Statement II is false).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A \(10\,\mathrm{µF}\) capacitor is charged to \(100\,\mathrm{V}\) and disconnected from the supply. It is then connected in parallel to another uncharged \(10\,\mathrm{µF}\) capacitor. How much electrostatic energy is lost in the process?

  • (A) \(0.050\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(0.025\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(0.0125\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(0\,\mathrm{J}\)

Answer: (B) \(0.025\,\mathrm{J}\)

Worked Solution: Using \(\Delta U_{\text{loss}} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}(V_1 - V_2)^2\): \[ \Delta U_{\text{loss}} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{10 \times 10}{10 + 10} \times 10^{-6} \times (100 - 0)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 10^{-6} \times 10^4 = 0.025\,\mathrm{J} \]

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Forgetting the sign of charge: Unlike electric field magnitude, potential \(V\) and potential energy \(U\) are scalar quantities that must include the algebraic sign of \(q\).
  • Confusing \(E = 0\) with \(V = 0\): Inside a charged spherical shell, \(E = 0\) but \(V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \ne 0\). Conversely, on the equatorial plane of a dipole, \(V = 0\) but \(E \ne 0\).
  • Battery Connected vs. Disconnected: Always check whether \(V\) is held constant (battery connected) or \(Q\) is held constant (battery disconnected) before computing changes in \(E\) and \(U\).
  • Energy Conservation in Charge Sharing: When two capacitors at different potentials are connected, total charge is conserved, not electrostatic energy—half or part of the energy is lost as heat/radiation.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Point charge potential: \(V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\); Dipole potential: \(V = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\).
  • Field–potential link: \(E = -\frac{dV}{dr}\) (points toward decreasing potential).
  • Dipole energy in uniform field: \(U = -\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{E}\).
  • Surface field of a conductor: \(E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\); interior \(E = 0\), \(V = \text{constant}\).
  • Parallel-plate capacitor: \(C = \frac{K\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\); energy \(U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C}\); energy density \(u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2\).

Concise Final Recap

Electrostatic potential provides a scalar framework for conservative electric fields, where potential difference measures work per unit charge and electric field points along the steepest potential drop. Conductors maintain constant potential throughout their volume and shield charge-free cavities, whereas dielectrics polarise to reduce internal fields by a factor \(K\). Capacitors store energy in the electric field between conductors, with their capacitance governed by geometry and dielectric constant, and their network behavior governed by charge conservation and common potential.
