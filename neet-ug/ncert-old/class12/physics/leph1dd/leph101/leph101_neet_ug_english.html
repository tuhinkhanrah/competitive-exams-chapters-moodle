NEET UG Physics • Electrostatics

Electric Charges and Fields

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide covering charge properties, Coulomb's law, electric fields, dipoles, electric flux, and Gauss's law.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Electrostatics examines forces, fields, and interactions produced by electric charges that remain stationary. For the NEET UG examination, this chapter builds the mathematical and conceptual foundation for all of electricity and magnetism, with frequent questions on vector superposition, dipole behaviour, electric flux, and symmetric Gauss's law applications.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Explain electrification by friction and conduction, and distinguish conductors, insulators, and semiconductors.
  • Apply additivity, conservation, and quantisation of electric charge (\(q = ne\)) to microscopic and macroscopic systems.
  • Calculate electrostatic forces between point charges using Coulomb's law in scalar and vector forms, along with the superposition principle.
  • Determine electric fields for discrete charges, dipoles (axial and equatorial points), and symmetric continuous charge distributions.
  • Interpret electric field lines and compute torque on an electric dipole in a uniform external field.
  • Evaluate electric flux and apply Gauss's law to infinitely long charged wires, infinite plane sheets, and thin spherical shells.

1. Electric Charge, Electrification, and Materials

Matter is composed of atoms containing positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons in equal numbers, making bulk objects electrically neutral. When two insulating surfaces are rubbed together, a small fraction of loosely bound electrons transfers from one body to the other. The body losing electrons acquires a net positive charge, while the body gaining electrons acquires an equal net negative charge.

  • Polarity of Charge: There are exactly two kinds of electric charge—positive and negative. Like charges repel each other, whereas unlike charges attract each other.
  • Conductors: Materials such as metals, the human body, and Earth possess mobile charge carriers (free electrons in metals, or ions in electrolytes) that allow charge to flow readily and distribute over the outer surface.
  • Insulators: Materials such as glass, plastic, nylon, porcelain, and dry wood strongly resist charge flow; any charge placed on an insulator remains localized at the spot where it was deposited.
  • Semiconductors: A third class of materials offering electrical resistance intermediate between conductors and insulators.

2. Fundamental Properties of Electric Charge

Electric charge obeys three universal laws that hold across all physical processes:

  • Additivity: Electric charge is a scalar quantity (invariant under rotation and relative motion of reference frames). The net charge of a system of point charges \(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\) is their algebraic sum: \(q_{\mathrm{total}} = q_1 + q_2 + \dots + q_n\), keeping proper positive and negative signs.
  • Conservation of Charge: The total electric charge of an isolated system always remains constant over time. Charges can be transferred between bodies, and charged particles can even be created or annihilated in pairs (such as a neutron transforming into a proton and an electron), but net charge is never created or destroyed.
  • Quantisation of Charge: Any free electric charge \(q\) exists only as an integral multiple of the elementary charge \(e\):
\[ q = n e, \quad \text{where } n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \dots \quad \text{and} \quad e \approx 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C} \]

Because \(e\) is extremely small, a macroscopic charge of \(1\,\mathrm{µC}\) corresponds to roughly \(6.25 \times 10^{12}\) elementary charges. At macroscopic scales, the discrete steps of \(e\) are imperceptible and charge behaves as if it were continuous. At atomic and subatomic scales, quantisation cannot be ignored.

3. Coulomb's Law and the Superposition Principle

When the physical dimensions of charged bodies are negligible compared to the distance \(r\) separating them, they are modelled as point charges. Coulomb's law states that the magnitude of the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) in vacuum is directly proportional to the product of their magnitudes and inversely proportional to the square of their separation:

\[ F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2} \]

Here, \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2\,N^{-1}\,m^{-2}}\) is the permittivity of free space, and \(k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\). In vector form, if \(\mathbf{r}_{21} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1\) is the vector from \(q_1\) to \(q_2\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21} = \mathbf{r}_{21}/r_{21}\), the force on \(q_2\) due to \(q_1\) is:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{21}^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21} = -\mathbf{F}_{12} \]

This single vector equation handles both attraction (\(q_1 q_2 < 0\)) and repulsion (\(q_1 q_2 > 0\)) and automatically satisfies Newton's third law (\(\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21}\)).

Comparison with Gravity: Both Coulomb's law and Newton's law of gravitation follow an inverse-square dependence on distance. However, electrostatic forces can be either attractive or repulsive, whereas gravity is always attractive. Furthermore, electrical forces are vastly stronger: for an electron and a proton, \(|F_e / F_G| \approx 2.4 \times 10^{39}\); for two protons, \(|F_e / F_G| \approx 1.3 \times 10^{36}\).

Principle of Superposition

When more than two charges are present, the net electrostatic force on any given charge \(q_1\) is the vector sum of the individual Coulomb forces exerted on it by every other charge taken one at a time. Crucially, the pairwise force between any two charges is completely unaffected by the presence of additional charges:

\[ \mathbf{F}_1 = \mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{13} + \dots + \mathbf{F}_{1n} = \frac{q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{q_i}{r_{1i}^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{1i} \]

4. Electric Field and Electric Field Lines

A source charge \(Q\) modifies the space around it by establishing an electric field \(\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})\). Operationally, the electric field at a point is defined as the electrostatic force experienced per unit positive test charge \(q_0\) placed at that point, in the limit \(q_0 \to 0\) (so the test charge does not disturb the source charges):

\[ \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \lim_{q_0 \to 0}\frac{\mathbf{F}}{q_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}} \]

The SI unit of electric field is \(\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) (or equivalently \(\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\)). A charge \(q\) placed in a field \(\mathbf{E}\) experiences a force \(\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{E}\). For a positive source charge, \(\mathbf{E}\) points radially outward; for a negative source charge, \(\mathbf{E}\) points radially inward. Beyond electrostatics, accelerated charges generate propagating electromagnetic waves travelling at speed \(c\), showing that electric fields are physical entities capable of transporting energy.

Properties of Electric Field Lines

Electric field lines provide a geometric map of the electric field in 3D space:

  • The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of the net electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) at that point.
  • Field lines originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges (or extend to/from infinity for isolated charges).
  • In a charge-free region, field lines are continuous curves without sudden breaks.
  • Two field lines can never intersect; if they did, the electric field at the intersection would have two different directions simultaneously, which is impossible.
  • Electrostatic field lines never form closed loops, reflecting the conservative nature of the electrostatic field.
  • The relative closeness (density) of field lines perpendicular to a unit area represents the relative magnitude of \(\mathbf{E}\): crowded lines indicate a strong field, while widely spaced lines indicate a weak field.

5. Electric Dipole and Behaviour in External Fields

An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite point charges \(+q\) and \(-q\) separated by a small distance \(2a\). Although the net charge of a dipole is zero, its electric field is non-zero because the charges do not coincide. The strength and orientation of the dipole are described by the electric dipole moment vector \(\mathbf{p}\), directed by convention from \(-q\) to \(+q\):

\[ \mathbf{p} = q (2a)\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}, \quad \text{SI unit: } \mathrm{C\cdot m} \]

Electric Field of a Dipole

  • On the Dipole Axis (at distance \(r\) from centre): \[ \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2\mathbf{p}r}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \approx \frac{2\mathbf{p}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3} \quad (r \gg a) \] The axial field points in the same direction as \(\mathbf{p}\).
  • On the Equatorial Plane (perpendicular bisector at distance \(r\)): \[ \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eq}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\mathbf{p}}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \approx -\frac{\mathbf{p}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3} \quad (r \gg a) \] The equatorial field points opposite to \(\mathbf{p}\), and for a short dipole (\(r \gg a\)), \(E_{\mathrm{axial}} = 2 E_{\mathrm{eq}}\). Notice that dipole fields fall off as \(1/r^3\), faster than the \(1/r^2\) decay of a point charge.

Molecules in which the centres of positive and negative charge coincide (such as \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\)) are non-polar (zero permanent dipole moment), whereas molecules with separated charge centres (such as \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) are polar and possess a permanent dipole moment.

Dipole in Uniform and Non-Uniform External Fields

When a permanent dipole \(\mathbf{p}\) is placed in a uniform electric field \(\mathbf{E}\), the forces \(+q\mathbf{E}\) and \(-q\mathbf{E}\) are equal and opposite, so the net force is zero. However, their lines of action are separated by \(2a \sin\theta\), producing a restoring torque that tends to align \(\mathbf{p}\) with \(\mathbf{E}\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E}, \quad \tau = pE\sin\theta \]

In a non-uniform electric field, the forces on \(+q\) and \(-q\) have different magnitudes, so the dipole experiences both a torque and a net translational force. When \(\mathbf{p}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{E}\), the net force acts toward the region of increasing field; when antiparallel, it acts toward the region of decreasing field. This explains why an uncharged bit of paper is polarised by a charged comb's non-uniform field and attracted toward it.

6. Continuous Charge Distributions

For macroscopic charged wires, surfaces, or solids, it is practical to treat charge as continuously distributed using three charge densities:

  • Linear charge density (\(\lambda\)): \(\lambda = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta l}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-1}}\), dimensions: \([\mathrm{L^{-1} T A}]\))
  • Surface charge density (\(\sigma\)): \(\sigma = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta S}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}\), dimensions: \([\mathrm{L^{-2} T A}]\))
  • Volume charge density (\(\rho\)): \(\rho = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta V}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-3}}\), dimensions: \([\mathrm{L^{-3} T A}]\))

7. Electric Flux and Gauss's Law

An area element \(\Delta S\) has both magnitude and orientation, represented as an area vector \(\Delta \mathbf{S} = \Delta S\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}\). For a closed surface, \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is always chosen along the outward normal. The electric flux \(\Delta\phi\) passing through \(\Delta\mathbf{S}\) is the scalar product:

\[ \Delta\phi = \mathbf{E} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{S} = E\,\Delta S \cos\theta, \quad \text{SI unit: } \mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}} \text{ (or } \mathrm{V\cdot m}\text{)} \]

Here \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\). When \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (field parallel to the surface), the flux is zero.

Gauss's Law

Gauss's law states that the total outward electric flux through any closed surface \(S\) (called a Gaussian surface) equals \(1/\varepsilon_0\) times the total net charge \(q_{\mathrm{enc}}\) enclosed within \(S\):

\[ \phi = \oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \frac{q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} \]
  • Gauss's law is valid for any closed surface of arbitrary shape or size, provided the surface does not pass through a discrete point charge.
  • The electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) on the left-hand side is the net field due to all charges (both inside and outside \(S\)), whereas \(q_{\mathrm{enc}}\) on the right-hand side includes only the charges enclosed inside \(S\).
  • If \(\phi = 0\) for a closed surface, it means the net enclosed charge is zero (there may still be equal positive and negative charges inside, or external fields passing through).

Key Symmetric Applications of Gauss's Law

  1. Infinitely long straight uniformly charged wire (linear density \(\lambda\)): Choosing a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\) and length \(l\) gives zero flux through the flat ends and radial flux \(E(2\pi r l)\) through the curved wall: \[ \mathbf{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\hat{\mathbf{n}} \]
  2. Uniformly charged infinite plane sheet (surface density \(\sigma\)): Choosing a pillbox/rectangular Gaussian surface piercing the sheet with cross-sectional area \(A\) gives flux \(2EA\) through the two parallel faces: \[ \mathbf{E} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf{n}} \] Note that \(E\) is independent of the distance from the sheet.
  3. Uniformly charged thin spherical shell of radius \(R\) and total charge \(q = 4\pi R^2\sigma\): \[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2} \quad (r \ge R), \qquad E = 0 \quad (r < R) \] Outside the shell, the field is identical to that of a point charge \(q\) at the centre. Inside the shell, the enclosed charge is zero, so the electric field vanishes everywhere.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Quantisation and Mass Transfer During Charging

Problem: Suppose \(2.5 \times 10^{12}\) electrons are transferred from an initially neutral glass rod to a silk cloth (assumed values). Calculate (a) the net charge acquired by the silk cloth and the glass rod, and (b) the mass transferred to the silk cloth. (Take \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), \(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)).

Solution:

(a) Since the silk cloth gains \(n = 2.5 \times 10^{12}\) electrons, its net charge is negative:

\[ q_{\mathrm{silk}} = -n e = -(2.5 \times 10^{12}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}) = -4.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{C} = -0.40\,\mathrm{µC} \]

By conservation of charge, the glass rod acquires an equal and opposite charge: \(q_{\mathrm{rod}} = +0.40\,\mathrm{µC}\).

(b) Each electron has mass \(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\), so the mass transferred from the rod to the silk cloth is:

\[ \Delta m = n m_e = (2.5 \times 10^{12}) \times (9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}) \approx 2.28 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{kg} \]

Example 2: Applying Coulomb's Law and Superposition

Problem: Three point charges are placed along the \(x\)-axis in vacuum (assumed values): \(q_1 = +4.0\,\mathrm{µC}\) at \(x = 0\), \(q_2 = -2.0\,\mathrm{µC}\) at \(x = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\), and \(q_3 = +6.0\,\mathrm{µC}\) at \(x = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}\). Find the magnitude and direction of the net electrostatic force on \(q_2\).

Solution:

The force on \(q_2\) due to \(q_1\) is attractive (directed along \(-x\), toward \(q_1\)) with magnitude:

\[ F_{21} = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times (4.0 \times 10^{-6}) \times (2.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.20)^2} = \frac{0.072}{0.04} = 1.8\,\mathrm{N} \]

The distance between \(q_2\) and \(q_3\) is \(r_{23} = 0.50 - 0.20 = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}\). The force on \(q_2\) due to \(q_3\) is attractive (directed along \(+x\), toward \(q_3\)) with magnitude:

\[ F_{23} = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-6}) \times (6.0 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.30)^2} = \frac{0.108}{0.09} = 1.2\,\mathrm{N} \]

By the superposition principle, the net force on \(q_2\) is:

\[ \mathbf{F}_2 = (-1.8 + 1.2)\,\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{N} = -0.6\,\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{N} \]

Thus, the net force on \(q_2\) has magnitude \(0.6\,\mathrm{N}\) and is directed along the negative \(x\)-axis.

Example 3: Calculating the Electric Field of a Short Dipole

Problem: An electric dipole consists of two charges \(\pm 5.0\,\mathrm{µC}\) separated by \(4.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) (assumed values). Determine the magnitude of the electric field at a distance of \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the centre of the dipole (a) on the dipole axis, and (b) on the equatorial line.

Solution:

Here \(q = 5.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C}\) and \(2a = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}\), so the dipole moment is:

\[ p = q(2a) = (5.0 \times 10^{-6}) \times (4.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m} \]

Since \(r = 0.20\,\mathrm{m} \gg a = 0.002\,\mathrm{m}\), we apply the short-dipole formulas:

(a) On the axis:

\[ E_{\mathrm{axial}} = \frac{2kp}{r^3} = \frac{2 \times (9 \times 10^9) \times (2.0 \times 10^{-8})}{(0.20)^3} = \frac{360}{8.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 4.5 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}} \]

(b) On the equatorial line:

\[ E_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{kp}{r^3} = \frac{1}{2} E_{\mathrm{axial}} = 2.25 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}} \]

Example 4: Torque on a Dipole in a Uniform Electric Field

Problem: An electric dipole with dipole moment \(p = 3.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m}\) is oriented at an angle of \(30^\circ\) with respect to a uniform electric field of magnitude \(E = 4.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) (assumed values). Calculate the magnitude of the torque and the net force acting on the dipole.

Solution:

Because the external electric field is uniform, the forces on \(+q\) and \(-q\) are equal and opposite, so the net translational force is \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = 0\).

The magnitude of the torque is:

\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta = (3.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m}) \times (4.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}) \times \sin 30^\circ = 1.2 \times 10^{-2} \times 0.5 = 6.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m} \]

Example 5: Electric Flux and Enclosed Charge Using Gauss's Law

Problem: A region contains a non-uniform electric field \(\mathbf{E} = (500 x)\,\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\), where \(x\) is in metres (assumed field). A cube of side \(a = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\) has its faces parallel to the coordinate planes, with its left face at \(x = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}\) and right face at \(x = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}\). Find (a) the net outward electric flux through the cube, and (b) the net charge enclosed within the cube.

Solution:

(a) Since \(\mathbf{E}\) is purely along the \(x\)-axis, it is perpendicular to the outward normals of the four faces parallel to the \(xz\)- and \(xy\)-planes, giving zero flux through those four faces.

For the left face (\(x = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}\)), \(E_L = 500(0.10) = 50\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) and the outward normal is \(-\hat{\mathbf{i}}\):

\[ \phi_L = -E_L a^2 = -50 \times (0.20)^2 = -2.0\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}} \]

For the right face (\(x = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}\)), \(E_R = 500(0.30) = 150\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) and the outward normal is \(+\hat{\mathbf{i}}\):

\[ \phi_R = +E_R a^2 = +150 \times (0.20)^2 = +6.0\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}} \]

Net outward flux: \(\phi = \phi_L + \phi_R = -2.0 + 6.0 = 4.0\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\).

(b) By Gauss's law, the enclosed charge is:

\[ q_{\mathrm{enc}} = \varepsilon_0 \phi = (8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2\,N^{-1}\,m^{-2}}) \times (4.0\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}) \approx 3.54 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{C} \]

Practice Question 1

Which of the following electric charges cannot be possessed by an isolated body?

  • (A) \(3.2 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (B) \(8.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (C) \(2.4 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) \(1.6 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{C}\)
Answer: (C)

By quantisation of charge, \(q = ne\), where \(n\) must be an integer and \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\). Dividing \(2.4 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) by \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) gives \(n = 1.5\), which is not an integer. The other options correspond to \(n = 2, 5,\) and \(10\).

Practice Question 2

Two identical conducting spheres A and B carry charges \(+6\,\mathrm{µC}\) and \(-2\,\mathrm{µC}\) respectively, and experience an attractive force \(F\) when separated by a distance \(d\) (much larger than their radii). If they are touched together and then placed at a separation \(d/2\), the new electrostatic force between them is:

  • (A) \(F/3\), attractive
  • (B) \(F/3\), repulsive
  • (C) \(4F/3\), repulsive
  • (D) \(3F/4\), repulsive
Answer: (C)

Initially, \(F = \frac{k (6\,\mathrm{µC})(2\,\mathrm{µC})}{d^2} = \frac{12k\,\mathrm{µC}^2}{d^2}\). When touched together, the total charge \(+6 - 2 = +4\,\mathrm{µC}\) distributes equally, leaving \(+2\,\mathrm{µC}\) on each sphere. At separation \(d/2\), the new force is \(F' = \frac{k (2\,\mathrm{µC})(2\,\mathrm{µC})}{(d/2)^2} = \frac{16k\,\mathrm{µC}^2}{d^2} = \frac{16}{12}F = \frac{4}{3}F\), and it is repulsive since both spheres now carry positive charge.

Practice Question 3

Two point charges \(+q\) and \(+9q\) are fixed at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\), respectively. At what position on the \(x\)-axis will a third point charge \(Q\) be in electrostatic equilibrium?

  • (A) \(x = L/4\)
  • (B) \(x = L/3\)
  • (C) \(x = 3L/4\)
  • (D) \(x = L/10\)
Answer: (A)

For the forces from \(+q\) and \(+9q\) on \(Q\) to be opposite in direction, \(Q\) must lie between them (\(0 < x < L\)). Equating magnitudes gives \(\frac{kq|Q|}{x^2} = \frac{k(9q)|Q|}{(L-x)^2}\). Taking the positive square root: \(\frac{1}{x} = \frac{3}{L-x} \implies L - x = 3x \implies x = \frac{L}{4}\).

Practice Question 4

An electron and a proton are released from rest in the same uniform electric field (ignoring gravity). After falling through the same distance \(h\), the ratio of the time taken by the electron to that taken by the proton (\(t_e / t_p\)) is:

  • (A) \(1\)
  • (B) \(m_p / m_e\)
  • (C) \(\sqrt{m_e / m_p}\)
  • (D) \(\sqrt{m_p / m_e}\)
Answer: (C)

Both particles experience a force of magnitude \(eE\), giving acceleration \(a = eE/m\). Starting from rest, \(h = \frac{1}{2}at^2 \implies t = \sqrt{\frac{2hm}{eE}}\). Since \(h\), \(e\), and \(E\) are identical for both, \(t \propto \sqrt{m}\), so \(\frac{t_e}{t_p} = \sqrt{\frac{m_e}{m_p}}\).

Practice Question 5

Which of the following statements about electrostatic field lines is incorrect?

  • (A) The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of the net electric field at that point.
  • (B) Two electrostatic field lines can never intersect each other.
  • (C) Electrostatic field lines can form closed loops in a region of non-uniform electric field.
  • (D) The relative closeness of field lines indicates the relative strength of the electric field.
Answer: (C)

Electrostatic field lines can never form closed loops because the electrostatic field is conservative; they always start on positive charges (or infinity) and end on negative charges (or infinity).

Practice Question 6

For a short electric dipole, the ratio of the magnitude of the electric field at an axial point to that at an equatorial point at the same distance \(r\) (\(r \gg a\)) from the centre of the dipole is:

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(2 : 1\)
  • (C) \(4 : 1\)
  • (D) \(1 : 4\)
Answer: (B)

For a short dipole (\(r \gg a\)), \(E_{\mathrm{axial}} = \frac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\). Therefore, \(E_{\mathrm{axial}} : E_{\mathrm{eq}} = 2 : 1\).

Practice Question 7

An electric dipole of dipole moment \(5 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m}\) is placed in a uniform electric field of \(2 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\). The maximum torque that the dipole can experience in this field is:

  • (A) \(0.05\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  • (B) \(0.10\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  • (C) \(0.20\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
  • (D) \(2.5 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\)
Answer: (B)

Torque on a dipole is \(\tau = pE\sin\theta\). It is maximum when \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (\(\sin 90^\circ = 1\)): \(\tau_{\max} = (5 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C\cdot m}) \times (2 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}) = 0.10\,\mathrm{N\cdot m}\).

Practice Question 8

A point charge \(+q\) is placed at the centre of a cube of side \(a\). What is the electric flux passing through any one face of the cube?

  • (A) \(q / \varepsilon_0\)
  • (B) \(q / (4\varepsilon_0)\)
  • (C) \(q / (6\varepsilon_0)\)
  • (D) Zero
Answer: (C)

By Gauss's law, the total outward flux through the entire closed cube is \(q/\varepsilon_0\). Because the charge is at the centre, all 6 identical faces are symmetric with respect to the charge, so the flux through each face is \(\frac{q}{6\varepsilon_0}\).

Practice Question 9

Consider the following two statements regarding a uniformly charged thin spherical shell of radius \(R\) carrying total charge \(+Q\):

Statement I: The electric field at any point inside the shell (\(r < R\)) is zero.
Statement II: The electric field at any point outside the shell (\(r > R\)) is the same as if the entire charge \(Q\) were concentrated at its centre.

Choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)

By Gauss's law, a concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius \(r < R\) encloses zero charge, so \(E = 0\) everywhere inside. A concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius \(r > R\) encloses the entire charge \(Q\), yielding \(E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\), identical to a point charge \(Q\) at the centre.

Practice Question 10

An infinitely long straight wire has uniform linear charge density \(\lambda = 4.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C\,m^{-1}}\). What is the magnitude of the electric field at a perpendicular distance of \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the wire? (Take \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\))

  • (A) \(1.8 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(3.6 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(7.2 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(9.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)

Using \(E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}\) with \(r = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\):
\(E = \frac{2 \times (9 \times 10^9) \times (4.0 \times 10^{-6})}{0.20} = \frac{7.2 \times 10^4}{0.20} = 3.6 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent NEET UG Mistakes
  • Dipole Moment Direction: Always take \(\mathbf{p}\) from \(-q\) to \(+q\), not from \(+q\) to \(-q\).
  • Zero Flux vs. Zero Enclosed Charge: Zero net flux through a closed surface implies \(q_{\mathrm{enc}} = 0\) (net charge is zero), which does not mean there are no charges inside (e.g., a dipole inside gives zero net flux).
  • Source of \(\mathbf{E}\) in Gauss's Law: Even though only \(q_{\mathrm{enc}}\) appears on the right-hand side of \(\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\), the field \(\mathbf{E}\) on the left-hand side is produced by all charges inside and outside the Gaussian surface.
  • Angle in Flux Formula: \(\theta\) in \(\phi = E S \cos\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{E}\) and the normal to the surface, not the angle between \(\mathbf{E}\) and the plane of the surface.
  • Distance Dependence: Point charge field falls as \(1/r^2\), infinite line charge field falls as \(1/r\), short dipole field falls as \(1/r^3\), and infinite plane sheet field is independent of \(r\).

Concise Final Recap

  • Electric charge is scalar, additive, conserved in isolated systems, and quantised in steps of \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\).
  • Coulomb's law gives the pairwise inverse-square force; multi-charge problems are solved by vector addition via the superposition principle.
  • Electric field lines never intersect and never form closed loops in electrostatics; their density measures field strength.
  • An electric dipole experiences zero net force and torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E}\) in a uniform field, and its far-field decays as \(1/r^3\).
  • Gauss's law (\(\phi = q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\)) simplifies field calculations for symmetric wires, sheets, and spherical shells.
