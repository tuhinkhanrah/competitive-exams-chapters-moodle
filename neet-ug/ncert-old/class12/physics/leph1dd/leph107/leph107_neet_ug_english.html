Alternating Current (AC) Circuits & Transformers

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide covering sinusoidal waveforms, phasors, reactance, series LCR resonance, AC power, and transformers.

Learning Objectives & Overview

Unlike direct current (DC), which flows steadily in one direction, alternating current (AC) reverses its direction periodically and varies sinusoidally with time. Power grids rely on AC because transformers can step alternating voltages up or down with very little energy loss, enabling economical long-distance transmission.

  • Distinguish between instantaneous, peak, average, and root-mean-square (rms) values of AC voltage and current.
  • Represent harmonically varying voltages and currents using rotating phasors.
  • Analyze purely resistive, purely inductive, and purely capacitive AC circuits, including phase differences and reactances.
  • Solve series LCR circuits for impedance, phase angle, resonance frequency, and quality factor.
  • Compute average power, power factor, and wattless current in AC networks.
  • Apply ideal transformer equations and identify real-world energy loss mechanisms.

1. Sinusoidal AC, Average, and RMS Values

A standard sinusoidal alternating voltage of amplitude (peak value) \(v_m\) and angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f = \frac{2\pi}{T}\) is written as:

\[ v(t) = v_m \sin(\omega t) \qquad \text{and} \qquad i(t) = i_m \sin(\omega t + \phi) \]

Over one complete cycle (\(0\) to \(T\)), the positive and negative halves of \(\sin(\omega t)\) cancel out, so the full-cycle average of both AC voltage and AC current is zero. However, electrical heating in a resistor depends on \(i^2 R\), which is always positive. To compare AC heating directly with DC heating, we define the root-mean-square (rms) or effective value.

  • RMS Current (\(I\) or \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}\)): That steady DC current which produces the same average Joule heating in a given resistor over one complete cycle as the alternating current. \[ I_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle i^2 \rangle} = \frac{i_m}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\,i_m \]
  • RMS Voltage (\(V\) or \(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\)): \[ V_{\mathrm{rms}} = \frac{v_m}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\,v_m \]
  • Half-Cycle Average: Over a positive half-cycle (\(0\) to \(T/2\)), the mean value is \(I_{\mathrm{avg,half}} = \frac{2i_m}{\pi} \approx 0.637\,i_m\) and \(V_{\mathrm{avg,half}} = \frac{2v_m}{\pi} \approx 0.637\,v_m\).
Exam Convention Unless a question explicitly states "peak" or "maximum" value, any AC voltage or current rating (such as a \(220\,\mathrm{V}\), \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\) domestic supply) is always an rms value. For \(V_{\mathrm{rms}} = 220\,\mathrm{V}\), the peak voltage is \(v_m = 220\sqrt{2} \approx 311\,\mathrm{V}\).

2. Phasors & Single-Element AC Circuits

Although alternating voltage and current are scalar quantities, their amplitudes and phase angles combine mathematically just like two-dimensional vectors. A phasor is a rotating vector whose length equals the peak amplitude (\(v_m\) or \(i_m\)) and which rotates counterclockwise at angular speed \(\omega\). Its vertical projection at any instant \(t\) gives the instantaneous value.

Purely Resistive, Inductive, and Capacitive Circuits

  • Pure Resistor (\(R\)): By Kirchhoff's loop rule, \(v_m \sin(\omega t) = iR\), so \(i = i_m \sin(\omega t)\) with \(i_m = \frac{v_m}{R}\). Voltage and current are in phase (\(\phi = 0^\circ\)). Average power dissipated is \(P = I^2 R = VI\).
  • Pure Inductor (\(L\)): The self-induced back emf opposes changes in current: \(v - L\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0\). Integrating gives \(i = i_m \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\), where \(i_m = \frac{v_m}{X_L}\). Here inductive reactance is \(X_L = \omega L = 2\pi f L\) (in \(\Omega\)). Current lags voltage by \(90^\circ\) (\(\pi/2\,\mathrm{rad}\)). Average power over a cycle is zero.
  • Pure Capacitor (\(C\)): Since \(q = Cv = C v_m \sin(\omega t)\), differentiating gives \(i = \frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}t} = i_m \sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\), where \(i_m = \frac{v_m}{X_C}\). Here capacitive reactance is \(X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}\) (in \(\Omega\)). Current leads voltage by \(90^\circ\) (\(\pi/2\,\mathrm{rad}\)). Average power over a cycle is zero.

3. Series LCR Circuit & Impedance

When a resistor \(R\), inductor \(L\), and capacitor \(C\) are connected in series to an AC source \(v = v_m \sin(\omega t)\), the same instantaneous current \(i = i_m \sin(\omega t + \phi)\) flows through all three elements. Because \(V_L\) leads \(I\) by \(90^\circ\) and \(V_C\) lags \(I\) by \(90^\circ\), the inductor and capacitor voltages are \(180^\circ\) out of phase with each other and directly subtract.

\[ V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2} \qquad Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2} \qquad i_m = \frac{v_m}{Z}, \quad I = \frac{V}{Z} \]

The phase angle \(\phi\) by which the current leads the source voltage is determined from the impedance triangle:

\[ \tan\phi = \frac{X_C - X_L}{R} = \frac{V_C - V_L}{V_R} \]
  • If \(X_C > X_L\), \(\phi > 0\): the circuit is predominantly capacitive, and current leads voltage.
  • If \(X_L > X_C\), \(\phi < 0\): the circuit is predominantly inductive, and current lags voltage.
  • If \(X_L = X_C\), \(\phi = 0\): the circuit is purely resistive (resonance).

4. Electrical Resonance & Quality Factor

Resonance occurs in a series LCR circuit when the inductive reactance exactly balances the capacitive reactance (\(X_L = X_C\)). Both \(L\) and \(C\) must be present for resonance; an RL or RC circuit cannot resonate.

\[ \omega_0 L = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C} \implies \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \]
  • Minimum Impedance & Maximum Current: At \(\omega = \omega_0\), \(Z_{\min} = R\), so current amplitude reaches its peak \(i_{m,\max} = \frac{v_m}{R}\).
  • Voltage Cancellation: Although \(V_L = I X_L\) and \(V_C = I X_C\) can individually exceed the source voltage \(V\), they are \(180^\circ\) out of phase, so the net voltage across the \(LC\) combination is zero (\(V_L - V_C = 0\)) and the entire source voltage appears across \(R\).
  • Quality Factor (\(Q\)): Measures the sharpness of resonance (selectivity in radio tuning). Higher \(Q\) (smaller \(R\)) gives a sharper, narrower peak: \[ Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C R} = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]

5. Power in AC Circuits & Wattless Current

The instantaneous power supplied by an AC source is \(p = vi\). Averaging over one full cycle eliminates the oscillating \(2\omega t\) term, giving the average power:

\[ P = V I \cos\phi = I^2 Z \cos\phi = I^2 R \]
  • Power Factor (\(\cos\phi\)): Defined as \(\cos\phi = \frac{R}{Z} = \frac{\text{True Power}}{\text{Apparent Power}}\). It ranges from \(0\) to \(1\).
  • Wattless Current: The component of current \(I \sin\phi\) that is \(90^\circ\) out of phase with the voltage consumes zero average power over a cycle. Pure inductors and capacitors draw only wattless current (\(\cos 90^\circ = 0\)).
  • Power Factor Correction: Inductive industrial loads cause current to lag, lowering \(\cos\phi\) and increasing \(I^2 R_{\text{line}}\) transmission losses. Connecting a capacitor in parallel supplies leading wattless current to cancel the lagging component, bringing \(\cos\phi\) closer to \(1\).

6. Transformers & Energy Losses

A transformer operates on mutual induction to step alternating voltages up or down without changing the frequency. Two insulated coils—primary (\(N_p\) turns) and secondary (\(N_s\) turns)—are wound on a laminated soft-iron core so that nearly all magnetic flux \(\Phi\) links both coils.

\[ \frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} \qquad \text{and for an ideal transformer (100% efficient):} \qquad V_p I_p = V_s I_s \implies \frac{I_p}{I_s} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} \]
  • Step-Up Transformer (\(N_s > N_p\)): Increases voltage (\(V_s > V_p\)) and decreases current (\(I_s < I_p\)).
  • Step-Down Transformer (\(N_s < N_p\)): Decreases voltage (\(V_s < V_p\)) and increases current (\(I_s > I_p\)).
  • Efficiency (\(\eta\)): For a practical transformer, \(\eta = \frac{P_{\mathrm{out}}}{P_{\mathrm{in}}} = \frac{V_s I_s}{V_p I_p} \times 100\%\).

Tutorial Diagram 5: Transformer showing primary (\(N_p\)) and secondary (\(N_s\)) coils linked by magnetic flux \(\Phi\) in a laminated soft-iron core.

Energy Loss Mechanism Physical Cause Mitigation Method
Flux Leakage Not all primary magnetic flux links the secondary coil. Wind primary and secondary coils concentrically on the same core limb.
Copper (Joule) Loss \(I^2 R\) heating in the resistance of the copper windings. Use thick copper wires for high-current, low-voltage windings.
Eddy Current Loss Alternating flux induces circulating currents in the iron core. Construct the core from thin, insulated laminated sheets.
Hysteresis Loss Repeated magnetization and demagnetization of the core per cycle. Use soft iron or silicon steel with a narrow hysteresis loop.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Relating Peak, RMS, and Average Values

Problem: Suppose a laboratory AC generator produces a voltage given by \(v(t) = 200\sqrt{2}\sin(100\pi t)\,\mathrm{V}\) (assumed values). Calculate: (a) the rms voltage, (b) the frequency of the supply, and (c) the average voltage over a positive half-cycle.

Solution:

(a) Comparing with \(v(t) = v_m \sin(\omega t)\), the peak voltage is \(v_m = 200\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{V}\). Therefore: \[ V_{\mathrm{rms}} = \frac{v_m}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{200\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = 200\,\mathrm{V} \]

(b) The angular frequency is \(\omega = 100\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). The linear frequency is: \[ f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{100\pi}{2\pi} = 50\,\mathrm{Hz} \]

(c) The average voltage over a positive half-cycle is: \[ V_{\mathrm{avg,half}} = \frac{2v_m}{\pi} = \frac{400\sqrt{2}}{\pi} \approx 180.1\,\mathrm{V} \]

Example 2: Comparing Inductive and Capacitive Reactance

Problem: Assume a \(50\,\mathrm{mH}\) pure inductor and a \(20\,\mathrm{µF}\) pure capacitor are each connected separately to a \(100\,\mathrm{V}\) (rms) AC source operating at \(f = \frac{500}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Find \(X_L\), \(X_C\), and the rms current in each circuit.

Solution:

First, find the angular frequency: \[ \omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \left(\frac{500}{\pi}\right) = 1000\,\mathrm{rad/s} \]

For the inductor: \[ X_L = \omega L = 1000 \times 50 \times 10^{-3} = 50\,\Omega \implies I_L = \frac{V}{X_L} = \frac{100}{50} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A} \text{ (lags } V \text{ by } 90^\circ\text{)} \]

For the capacitor: \[ X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{1000 \times 20 \times 10^{-6}} = 50\,\Omega \implies I_C = \frac{V}{X_C} = \frac{100}{50} = 2.0\,\mathrm{A} \text{ (leads } V \text{ by } 90^\circ\text{)} \]

Example 3: Analyzing a Series LCR Circuit

Problem: A series LCR circuit has \(R = 30\,\Omega\), \(X_L = 70\,\Omega\), and \(X_C = 30\,\Omega\) connected across a \(250\,\mathrm{V}\) (rms) AC source (assumed values). Determine: (a) the impedance \(Z\), (b) the rms current \(I\), (c) the power factor \(\cos\phi\), and (d) the average power dissipated.

Solution:

(a) Impedance: \[ Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2} = \sqrt{30^2 + (30 - 70)^2} = \sqrt{900 + 1600} = 50\,\Omega \]

(b) RMS current: \[ I = \frac{V}{Z} = \frac{250}{50} = 5.0\,\mathrm{A} \]

(c) Power factor: \[ \cos\phi = \frac{R}{Z} = \frac{30}{50} = 0.6 \quad (\text{since } X_L > X_C, \text{ current lags voltage by } 53.1^\circ) \]

(d) Average power dissipated: \[ P = V I \cos\phi = 250 \times 5.0 \times 0.6 = 750\,\mathrm{W} \]

Example 4: Determining Resonant Frequency and Quality Factor

Problem: Consider a series LCR tuning circuit with \(L = 0.4\,\mathrm{H}\), \(C = 10\,\mathrm{µF}\), and \(R = 20\,\Omega\) (assumed values). Calculate the resonant angular frequency \(\omega_0\), resonant linear frequency \(f_0\), and quality factor \(Q\).

Solution:

Resonant angular frequency: \[ \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.4 \times 10 \times 10^{-6}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4 \times 10^{-6}}} = \frac{1}{2 \times 10^{-3}} = 500\,\mathrm{rad/s} \]

Resonant linear frequency: \[ f_0 = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi} = \frac{500}{2\pi} \approx 79.6\,\mathrm{Hz} \]

Quality factor: \[ Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{500 \times 0.4}{20} = 10 \]

Example 5: Calculating Transformer Ratios and Power

Problem: Suppose an ideal step-down transformer has \(N_p = 2000\) primary turns and \(N_s = 100\) secondary turns. The primary is connected to a \(4400\,\mathrm{V}\) (rms) line, and the secondary supplies a load drawing \(20\,\mathrm{A}\) (rms) (assumed values). Find: (a) the secondary voltage \(V_s\), (b) the primary current \(I_p\), and (c) the output power.

Solution:

(a) Secondary voltage: \[ V_s = V_p \left(\frac{N_s}{N_p}\right) = 4400 \times \frac{100}{2000} = 220\,\mathrm{V} \]

(b) For an ideal transformer, \(V_p I_p = V_s I_s\): \[ I_p = I_s \left(\frac{N_s}{N_p}\right) = 20 \times \frac{100}{2000} = 1.0\,\mathrm{A} \]

(c) Output power: \[ P_{\mathrm{out}} = V_s I_s = 220 \times 20 = 4400\,\mathrm{W} = 4.4\,\mathrm{kW} \]

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

An alternating current in a circuit is described by \(i(t) = 10\sqrt{2}\sin\left(100\pi t + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)\,\mathrm{A}\). What are the rms value and the frequency of this current?

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{A},\; 50\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (B) \(14.14\,\mathrm{A},\; 50\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (C) \(10\,\mathrm{A},\; 100\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (D) \(5\,\mathrm{A},\; 25\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
Answer: (A)

The peak current is \(i_m = 10\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{A}\), so \(I_{\mathrm{rms}} = \frac{i_m}{\sqrt{2}} = 10\,\mathrm{A}\). Since \(\omega = 100\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), the frequency is \(f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A \(10\,\mathrm{µF}\) capacitor is connected across an AC source of constant rms voltage. If the frequency of the AC source is tripled, the rms current in the circuit:

  • (A) becomes one-third of its initial value
  • (B) becomes three times its initial value
  • (C) remains unchanged
  • (D) becomes nine times its initial value
Answer: (B)

Capacitive reactance is \(X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}\), so the rms current is \(I = \frac{V}{X_C} = 2\pi f C V\). Because \(I \propto f\) at constant \(V\), tripling \(f\) triples the rms current.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a series LCR circuit, the rms voltages measured across the resistor, inductor, and capacitor are \(40\,\mathrm{V}\), \(60\,\mathrm{V}\), and \(30\,\mathrm{V}\), respectively. What is the rms voltage of the AC source?

  • (A) \(130\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(70\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(50\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(10\,\mathrm{V}\)
Answer: (C)

Taking phase differences into account, \(V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2} = \sqrt{40^2 + (60 - 30)^2} = \sqrt{1600 + 900} = 50\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A series LCR circuit is driven at resonance with \(L = 2\,\mathrm{H}\), \(C = 8\,\mathrm{µF}\), and \(R = 10\,\Omega\). What is the quality factor \(Q\) of the circuit?

  • (A) \(25\)
  • (B) \(50\)
  • (C) \(100\)
  • (D) \(250\)
Answer: (B)

The resonant angular frequency is \(\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \times 8 \times 10^{-6}}} = \frac{1}{4 \times 10^{-3}} = 250\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). Thus, \(Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{250 \times 2}{10} = 50\).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

An AC circuit draws an rms current of \(4\,\mathrm{A}\) from a \(220\,\mathrm{V}\) (rms) source, and the current lags the source voltage by \(60^\circ\). What is the average power consumed in the circuit?

  • (A) \(880\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (B) \(440\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (C) \(440\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (D) \(0\,\mathrm{W}\)
Answer: (B)

Average power is \(P = VI\cos\phi = 220 \times 4 \times \cos 60^\circ = 880 \times 0.5 = 440\,\mathrm{W}\).

Practice Question 6 (Conceptual MCQ)

An incandescent lamp is connected in series with an air-core solenoid and an AC power supply. If a soft-iron rod is inserted into the solenoid, the brightness of the lamp:

  • (A) increases
  • (B) decreases
  • (C) remains unchanged
  • (D) first increases and then drops to zero
Answer: (B)

Inserting a soft-iron core increases the self-inductance \(L\) of the solenoid, which increases its inductive reactance \(X_L = \omega L\) and the total circuit impedance \(Z = \sqrt{R^2 + X_L^2}\). Consequently, the current decreases and the lamp glows less brightly.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A step-down transformer reduces \(2200\,\mathrm{V}\) to \(220\,\mathrm{V}\). If the secondary coil delivers \(10\,\mathrm{A}\) to a load and the transformer's efficiency is \(80\%\), what is the rms current in the primary coil?

  • (A) \(1.00\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (B) \(1.25\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (C) \(0.80\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (D) \(1.50\,\mathrm{A}\)
Answer: (B)

Output power is \(P_{\mathrm{out}} = V_s I_s = 220 \times 10 = 2200\,\mathrm{W}\). Using \(\eta = \frac{P_{\mathrm{out}}}{P_{\mathrm{in}}} = 0.80\), we get \(P_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{2200}{0.80} = 2750\,\mathrm{W}\). Hence, \(I_p = \frac{P_{\mathrm{in}}}{V_p} = \frac{2750}{2200} = 1.25\,\mathrm{A}\).

Practice Question 8 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): In a series LCR circuit at resonance, the net impedance equals the ohmic resistance \(R\) and the power factor is \(1\).
Reason (R): At resonance, \(X_L = X_C\), so the potential drops across the inductor and capacitor are equal in magnitude and \(180^\circ\) out of phase.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Because \(X_L = X_C\) at resonance, \(V_L\) and \(V_C\) cancel each other completely. Therefore \(Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2} = R\) and \(\cos\phi = R/Z = 1\). (R) directly explains (A).

Practice Question 9 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Statement I: An AC ammeter measures the rms value of an alternating current using its Joule heating effect.
Statement II: Because the average value of a sinusoidal AC over one complete cycle is zero, no net heat is dissipated in a resistor over a full cycle.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
Answer: (B)

Statement I is true: AC meters are calibrated to read rms values based on heating (\(i^2 R\)), which is independent of current direction. Statement II is false: although \(\langle i \rangle = 0\) over a full cycle, \(\langle i^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{2}i_m^2 \neq 0\), so net heat \(I_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 R T\) is dissipated.

Practice Question 10 (Conceptual MCQ)

Which design feature specifically minimizes eddy current losses in the iron core of a power transformer?

  • (A) Using thick copper wires for the secondary winding
  • (B) Winding the primary and secondary coils one over the other
  • (C) Building the core from thin, insulated laminated iron sheets
  • (D) Selecting a core alloy with a narrow hysteresis loop
Answer: (C)

Laminating the soft-iron core breaks up the conducting paths for induced circulating currents, increasing electrical resistance perpendicular to the laminations and drastically reducing eddy current heating.

Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in NEET UG:
  • Adding AC Voltages Algebraically: Never write \(V = V_R + V_L + V_C\). Always combine them vectorially: \(V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2}\).
  • Confusing Peak and RMS Values: Check whether the question gives \(v_m\) (from \(v = v_m \sin\omega t\)) or \(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\) (from "220 V supply").
  • Using \(f\) instead of \(\omega\): Remember \(\omega = 2\pi f\). In \(X_L = \omega L\) and \(X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C}\), omitting \(2\pi\) when given frequency in Hz is a frequent error.
  • Transformers with DC: A transformer cannot step up or step down a steady DC voltage because \(\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0\).
  • RMS & Half-Cycle Mean: \(I_{\mathrm{rms}} = i_m/\sqrt{2}\); \(I_{\mathrm{avg,half}} = 2i_m/\pi\); full-cycle average = \(0\).
  • Phase Rules: Resistor (\(\phi = 0^\circ\)); Inductor (\(I\) lags \(V\) by \(90^\circ\)); Capacitor (\(I\) leads \(V\) by \(90^\circ\)).
  • LCR & Resonance: \(Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2}\), \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\), \(Q = \omega_0 L / R\).
  • Power & Transformers: \(P = VI\cos\phi\); \(\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}\).

Concise Final Recap

Alternating current analysis relies on replacing time-varying sinusoids with rms magnitudes and rotating phasors. In any series AC circuit, resistive opposition (\(R\)) dissipates real power, whereas inductive (\(X_L = \omega L\)) and capacitive (\(X_C = 1/\omega C\)) reactances store and return energy with zero net power loss. At resonance (\(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\)), reactances cancel, impedance drops to \(R\), current peaks, and the power factor reaches unity. Finally, transformers harness mutual induction to scale AC voltages and currents inversely, enabling efficient power distribution.
