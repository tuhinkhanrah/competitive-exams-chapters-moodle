Current Electricity

Microscopic Drift, Ohm's Law, Electrical Power, Cells, Kirchhoff's Rules, and Bridge Circuits

Learning Objectives and Overview

Steady electric currents govern everything from household appliances to laboratory measuring instruments. For NEET UG, Current Electricity is a high-weightage chapter that blends conceptual reasoning with quick, accurate algebraic problem-solving.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between random thermal motion and electric-field-driven drift velocity of conduction electrons, and relate current density to conductivity.
  • Apply Ohm's law, identify non-ohmic behavior, and predict resistance changes under wire stretching or temperature variation.
  • Compute electrical energy and power dissipation, including why high-voltage lines reduce transmission losses.
  • Analyze single cells and series or parallel cell combinations, accounting for internal resistance during discharging and charging.
  • Solve multi-loop networks systematically using Kirchhoff's junction and loop rules, and apply the Wheatstone bridge balance condition.

Electric Current and Microscopic Drift of Electrons

An electric current exists whenever net electric charge crosses a given surface per unit time. If a net charge \(\Delta Q\) passes across a conductor's cross-section during a small interval \(\Delta t\), the instantaneous current at time \(t\) is:

\[I(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \frac{dQ}{dt}\]

For a steady flow where net charge \(q\) crosses in time \(t\), this simplifies to \(I = q / t\). The SI unit of current is the ampere (\(\mathrm{A}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{A} = 1\,\mathrm{C\,s^{-1}}\). Note that even though current is drawn with directional arrows in circuit diagrams, electric current is a scalar quantity because currents combine algebraically at junctions rather than following vector addition.

Why Drift Velocity Exists in Metals

In a solid metallic conductor, positive ions are fixed in a crystal lattice while outer valence electrons move freely through the bulk. Without an external electric field, these free electrons undergo rapid, random thermal motion (speeds around \(10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)) and collide repeatedly with the lattice ions. Because velocities after each collision are randomly oriented, the average thermal velocity across all \(N\) electrons is zero:

\[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}\]

When a steady electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) is maintained inside the conductor (by a battery or cell), each electron of charge \(-e\) and mass \(m\) experiences an acceleration \(\mathbf{a} = -e\mathbf{E}/m\) between collisions. If \(\tau\) is the relaxation time (the average time interval between successive collisions), the average velocity of the electrons settles to a constant drift velocity \(\mathbf{v}_d\):

\[\mathbf{v}_d = -\frac{e\mathbf{E}}{m}\tau\]

Although the drift speed \(v_d\) is tiny (typically around \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)), a circuit responds almost instantaneously when switched on because the electric field propagates along the conductor at nearly the speed of light, initiating local electron drift everywhere at once.

Current, Current Density, and Mobility

If \(n\) is the number of free electrons per unit volume (carrier number density), the total charge crossing area \(A\) in time \(\Delta t\) gives the magnitude of current:

\[I = n e A v_d\]

The current density \(\mathbf{j}\) is a vector of magnitude \(j = I/A\) directed along the electric field (for positive charge flow, or opposite to \(\mathbf{v}_d\) for electrons). In vector form, \(I = \mathbf{j} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{S}\). Substituting \(v_d = eE\tau/m\) yields the microscopic form of Ohm's law:

\[\mathbf{j} = \frac{n e^2 \tau}{m}\mathbf{E} = \sigma \mathbf{E}, \quad \text{where } \sigma = \frac{n e^2 \tau}{m} \text{ and } \rho = \frac{1}{\sigma} = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\]

Here, \(\sigma\) is electrical conductivity (\(\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\)) and \(\rho\) is electrical resistivity (\(\mathrm{Ω\,m}\)). Another key parameter is mobility \(\mu\), defined as the magnitude of drift velocity per unit electric field:

\[\mu = \frac{|\mathbf{v}_d|}{E} = \frac{e\tau}{m} \quad (\text{SI unit: } \mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}\,s^{-1}})\]

Ohm's Law, Resistance Scaling, and Temperature Effects

Ohm's law states that for a conductor at constant physical conditions (especially temperature), the potential difference \(V\) across its ends is directly proportional to the steady current \(I\) through it:

\[V = I R\]

The constant \(R\) is the resistance in ohms (\(\mathrm{Ω}\)). Note a subtle distinction: the equation \(R = V/I\) defines resistance for any device at a given operating point, whereas Ohm's law specifically asserts that \(R\) remains constant (independent of \(V\) and \(I\)).

Geometric Dependence and Wire-Stretching Rule

For a uniform conductor of length \(l\) and cross-sectional area \(A\), resistance depends on both material and geometry:

\[R = \rho \frac{l}{A}\]

Resistivity \(\rho\) is an intrinsic material property (independent of \(l\) and \(A\)). In NEET UG problems, a wire is often stretched or compressed while keeping its total volume \(V_{\text{vol}} = A l\) constant. Under constant volume and temperature:

\[R = \rho \frac{l^2}{V_{\text{vol}}} = \rho \frac{V_{\text{vol}}}{A^2} \implies R \propto l^2 \propto \frac{1}{A^2} \propto \frac{1}{r^4}\]

Thus, doubling a wire's length by uniform stretching increases its resistance by a factor of \(4\), while halving its radius increases resistance by a factor of \(16\).

Limitations of Ohm's Law

Not all circuit elements obey \(V \propto I\). Departures from Ohm's law fall into three broad categories:

  • Non-linear \(V\)-\(I\) relation: Even in good metallic conductors, large currents cause Joule heating, raising temperature and increasing \(R\), so \(V\) curves upward relative to \(I\).
  • Asymmetric (unilateral) conduction: In a semiconductor diode, reversing the polarity of \(V\) changes the current magnitude dramatically (forward bias passes \(\mathrm{mA}\), reverse bias passes \(\mathrm{µA}\)).
  • Non-unique \(V\)-\(I\) relation: In materials such as gallium arsenide (\(\mathrm{GaAs}\)), more than one voltage can correspond to the same current, producing a negative differential resistance region.

Temperature Dependence of Resistivity

Over a moderate temperature range around a reference temperature \(T_0\), the resistivity (and resistance, ignoring minor thermal expansion) of a conductor varies approximately linearly:

\[\rho_T = \rho_0 \left[1 + \alpha (T - T_0)\right] \quad \text{and} \quad R_T = R_0 \left[1 + \alpha (T - T_0)\right]\]

Here \(\alpha\) is the temperature coefficient of resistivity (unit: \(\mathrm{°C^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{K^{-1}}\)). From \(\rho = m / (n e^2 \tau)\), we can explain why different materials behave differently:

  • Pure metals (e.g., copper, silver, platinum): Free electron density \(n\) stays nearly constant, while higher thermal speeds cause more frequent collisions, reducing \(\tau\). Thus \(\rho\) increases with \(T\) (\(\alpha > 0\)).
  • Alloys (e.g., Nichrome, Manganin, Constantan): They have high residual resistivity and very small \(\alpha\). Because their resistance barely shifts with temperature, Manganin and Constantan are ideal for standard resistors.
  • Semiconductors (e.g., silicon, germanium): Raising temperature frees many more charge carriers, causing \(n\) to grow exponentially. This increase in \(n\) far outweighs the drop in \(\tau\), so \(\rho\) decreases rapidly with \(T\) (\(\alpha < 0\)).

Electrical Energy, Power, and High-Voltage Transmission

When a steady current \(I\) flows from a higher potential \(V_A\) to a lower potential \(V_B\) across a resistor \(R\) with potential difference \(V = V_A - V_B\), a charge \(\Delta Q = I\Delta t\) loses potential energy \(I V \Delta t\). Because carriers move at a steady drift speed rather than accelerating indefinitely, this energy is transferred to lattice ions through collisions and dissipated as heat (Joule heating):

\[\Delta W = I V \Delta t \implies P = \frac{\Delta W}{\Delta t} = I V = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}\]

Application to Power Transmission: To deliver electrical power \(P = V I\) to a distant load through transmission cables of total resistance \(R_c\), the current in the cables is \(I = P / V\). Therefore, the power wasted as heat in the cables is:

\[P_c = I^2 R_c = \frac{P^2 R_c}{V^2}\]

Because cable loss scales inversely with \(V^2\), power stations step up voltage to hundreds of kilovolts for long-distance transmission and step it down near homes and factories using transformers.

Cells, EMF, Internal Resistance, and Cell Combinations

An electrolytic cell maintains a steady current by converting chemical energy into electrical potential energy. Its electromotive force (emf) \(\varepsilon\) is the potential difference between its positive and negative terminals in an open circuit (when no current flows). Despite its name, emf is a potential difference (unit: volt, \(\mathrm{V}\)), not a mechanical force.

When the cell drives a current \(I\) through an external resistor \(R\), the electrolyte itself opposes the flow with an internal resistance \(r\). The terminal potential difference \(V\) across the cell terminals is:

\[V = \varepsilon - I r \quad \text{(during discharging)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r}\]

If an external charger forces current into the positive terminal of the cell (charging mode), the terminal voltage exceeds the emf: \(V = \varepsilon + I r\). The theoretical maximum current drawn from a short-circuited cell (\(R = 0\)) is \(I_{\max} = \varepsilon / r\).

Equivalent EMF and Internal Resistance

  • Series Combination: For two cells of emfs \(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2\) and internal resistances \(r_1, r_2\) connected end-to-end so currents flow in the same sense:
    \[\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2, \qquad r_{\text{eq}} = r_1 + r_2\]
    If one cell opposes the other (like terminals joined together), \(\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = |\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2|\), while \(r_{\text{eq}}\) still adds as \(r_1 + r_2\).
  • Parallel Combination: For \(n\) cells connected in parallel with positive terminals joined together at one node and negative terminals at the other:
    \[\frac{1}{r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{r_n}, \qquad \frac{\varepsilon_{\text{eq}}}{r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{\varepsilon_1}{r_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{r_2} + \dots + \frac{\varepsilon_n}{r_n}\]
    For two cells, this gives \(\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = \frac{\varepsilon_1 r_2 + \varepsilon_2 r_1}{r_1 + r_2}\) and \(r_{\text{eq}} = \frac{r_1 r_2}{r_1 + r_2}\). If the second cell is reversed, replace \(\varepsilon_2\) with \(-\varepsilon_2\).

Kirchhoff's Rules and the Wheatstone Bridge

Complex networks that cannot be simplified into pure series or parallel combinations are solved using Kirchhoff's two rules:

1. Junction Rule (Conservation of Charge)

At any node or junction in a steady-state circuit, the sum of currents entering the junction equals the sum of currents leaving it: \(\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}\). Charge does not accumulate at any junction.

2. Loop Rule (Conservation of Energy)

Around any closed loop, the algebraic sum of all potential changes is zero: \(\sum \Delta V = 0\). Traversing a resistor \(R\) in the direction of assumed current \(I\) gives \(\Delta V = -IR\); traversing against \(I\) gives \(+IR\). Crossing a cell from negative to positive terminal gives \(+\varepsilon\); from positive to negative gives \(-\varepsilon\).

The Wheatstone Bridge

A Wheatstone bridge consists of four resistors \(R_1, R_2, R_3, R_4\) arranged in a diamond loop A-B-C-D, with a voltage source connected across one diagonal (AC, the battery arm) and a sensitive galvanometer G across the other diagonal (BD, the galvanometer arm).

The bridge is balanced when the galvanometer shows zero deflection (\(I_g = 0\)), meaning nodes B and D are at the same electric potential (\(V_{\text{B}} = V_{\text{D}}\)). Applying Kirchhoff's rules under \(I_g = 0\) yields \(I_1 = I_3\) and \(I_2 = I_4\), and the balance condition:

\[\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_3}{R_4} \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad R_1 R_4 = R_2 R_3\]

When balanced, the galvanometer branch carries no current and can be removed when computing equivalent resistance. This null-point principle is used in the meter bridge to measure an unknown resistance accurately.

Worked Examples

Calculating Drift Speed and Carrier Transit Time

Assume a silver wire of uniform cross-sectional area \(A = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) carries a steady current \(I = 4.0\,\mathrm{A}\). Assume the free electron number density of silver is \(n = 5.8 \times 10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) and \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\). Find (a) the average drift speed of electrons and (b) the time an electron takes to drift along a \(1.08\,\mathrm{m}\) length of the wire.

Solution

(a) Using \(I = n e A v_d\):

\[v_d = \frac{I}{n e A} = \frac{4.0}{(5.8 \times 10^{28})(1.6 \times 10^{-19})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})} = \frac{4.0}{1.856 \times 10^4} \approx 2.16 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

(b) The average transit time over length \(l = 1.08\,\mathrm{m}\) is:

\[t = \frac{l}{v_d} = \frac{1.08}{2.155 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 5.01 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{s} \quad (\text{about } 83.5\,\mathrm{min})\]

Determining Wire Resistance After Uniform Stretching

Assume a uniform cylindrical copper wire has an initial resistance of \(R_1 = 8.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\). It is drawn uniformly through a die so that its length increases by \(50\%\) at constant volume and temperature. Calculate its new resistance \(R_2\).

Solution

A \(50\%\) increase in length gives \(l_2 = 1.5\,l_1\). Since volume \(V_{\text{vol}} = A_1 l_1 = A_2 l_2\) remains unchanged, the new cross-sectional area is \(A_2 = A_1 / 1.5\). Using \(R = \rho l / A\):

\[R_2 = \rho \frac{l_2}{A_2} = \rho \frac{1.5\,l_1}{A_1 / 1.5} = (1.5)^2 \left(\rho \frac{l_1}{A_1}\right) = 2.25\,R_1 = 2.25 \times 8.0\,\mathrm{Ω} = 18.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\]

Finding Temperature Using Resistance Thermometry

Assume a platinum sensing coil has a resistance \(R_0 = 20.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\) at \(T_0 = 25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and its average temperature coefficient of resistance is \(\alpha = 3.8 \times 10^{-3}\,^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1}\). When placed inside an oven, its resistance rises to \(R_T = 27.6\,\mathrm{Ω}\). Determine the oven temperature \(T\).

Solution

From \(R_T = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]\):

\[T - T_0 = \frac{R_T - R_0}{R_0 \alpha} = \frac{27.6 - 20.0}{20.0 \times 3.8 \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{7.6}{0.076} = 100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\]

Therefore, \(T = 25\,^\circ\mathrm{C} + 100\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 125\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Analyzing Parallel Cells Connected to an External Load

Assume two cells with emfs \(\varepsilon_1 = 6.0\,\mathrm{V}\), \(\varepsilon_2 = 3.0\,\mathrm{V}\) and internal resistances \(r_1 = 1.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\), \(r_2 = 2.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\) are connected in parallel with like polarities joined together. This combination supplies an external resistor \(R = 4.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\). Find the current through \(R\) and the terminal voltage across the combination.

Solution

First, find the equivalent emf and internal resistance of the parallel pair:

\[r_{\text{eq}} = \frac{r_1 r_2}{r_1 + r_2} = \frac{1.0 \times 2.0}{1.0 + 2.0} = \frac{2}{3}\,\mathrm{Ω}\] \[\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = \frac{\varepsilon_1 r_2 + \varepsilon_2 r_1}{r_1 + r_2} = \frac{(6.0)(2.0) + (3.0)(1.0)}{1.0 + 2.0} = \frac{15.0}{3.0} = 5.0\,\mathrm{V}\]

Now connect the equivalent cell to \(R = 4.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\):

\[I = \frac{\varepsilon_{\text{eq}}}{R + r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{5.0}{4.0 + 2/3} = \frac{15}{14}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 1.07\,\mathrm{A}\] \[V = I R = \frac{15}{14} \times 4.0 = \frac{30}{7}\,\mathrm{V} \approx 4.29\,\mathrm{V}\]

Applying Kirchhoff's Rules to a Two-Loop Network

Assume a two-loop circuit has three parallel vertical branches connected between a common top rail (A-B-C) and a common bottom rail (D-E-F). The left branch contains a \(12\,\mathrm{V}\) cell (positive terminal up) in series with a \(2\,\mathrm{Ω}\) resistor. The middle branch contains a \(6\,\mathrm{V}\) cell (positive terminal up) in series with a \(3\,\mathrm{Ω}\) resistor. The right branch contains a \(6\,\mathrm{Ω}\) resistor. Determine the current in each branch.

Solution

Let \(I_1\) flow upward in the left branch, \(I_2\) flow upward in the middle branch, and \(I_3 = I_1 + I_2\) flow downward through the \(6\,\mathrm{Ω}\) resistor in the right branch.

Applying Kirchhoff's loop rule to the left loop (clockwise from bottom-left):

\[+12 - 2I_1 + 3I_2 - 6 = 0 \implies 2I_1 - 3I_2 = 6 \quad \text{(Equation 1)}\]

Applying the loop rule to the right loop (clockwise from bottom-middle):

\[+6 - 6(I_1 + I_2) - 3I_2 = 0 \implies 6I_1 + 9I_2 = 6 \implies 2I_1 + 3I_2 = 2 \quad \text{(Equation 2)}\]

Adding Equations 1 and 2 gives \(4I_1 = 8 \implies I_1 = 2.0\,\mathrm{A}\). Substituting back into Equation 2 gives \(4 + 3I_2 = 2 \implies I_2 = -2/3\,\mathrm{A}\). The negative sign shows that \(2/3\,\mathrm{A}\) actually flows downward into the \(6\,\mathrm{V}\) cell (charging it). Finally, \(I_3 = 2.0 - 2/3 = 4/3\,\mathrm{A} \approx 1.33\,\mathrm{A}\) downward through the \(6\,\mathrm{Ω}\) resistor.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A metallic conductor of non-uniform circular cross-section carries a steady electric current \(I\). At two cross-sections P and Q, the diameters are \(d\) and \(2d\) respectively. The ratio of electron drift speeds \(v_{d,\mathrm{P}} : v_{d,\mathrm{Q}}\) is:

  • (A) \(1 : 4\)
  • (B) \(4 : 1\)
  • (C) \(1 : 2\)
  • (D) \(2 : 1\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: In a steady-state series conductor, current \(I = n e A v_d\) is the same at every cross-section. Since \(n\) and \(e\) are constant for the metal, \(v_d \propto 1/A \propto 1/d^2\). Therefore, \(\frac{v_{d,\mathrm{P}}}{v_{d,\mathrm{Q}}} = \left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{Q}}}{d_{\mathrm{P}}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{2d}{d}\right)^2 = 4:1\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A uniform metallic wire of resistance \(12\,\mathrm{Ω}\) is melted and recast into a wire of half its original radius, keeping its volume and temperature constant. What is the resistance of the new wire?

  • (A) \(48\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  • (B) \(96\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  • (C) \(192\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  • (D) \(3\,\mathrm{Ω}\)

Answer: (C)

Solution: At constant volume \(V_{\text{vol}} = A l\), halving the radius (\(r_2 = r_1/2\)) reduces the area to \(A_2 = A_1/4\) and increases the length to \(l_2 = 4l_1\). Since \(R = \rho l / A \propto 1/r^4\), we get \(R_2 = 16 R_1 = 16 \times 12\,\mathrm{Ω} = 192\,\mathrm{Ω}\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements correctly describes a material with a negative temperature coefficient of resistivity (\(\alpha < 0\)) near room temperature?

  • (A) Copper, because free electron density increases rapidly with temperature.
  • (B) Nichrome, because its relaxation time remains strictly independent of temperature.
  • (C) Silicon, because the rapid increase in charge carrier density \(n\) outweighs the decrease in relaxation time \(\tau\).
  • (D) Manganin, because its resistivity decreases exponentially with rising temperature.

Answer: (C)

Solution: From \(\rho = m/(n e^2 \tau)\), in semiconductors like silicon, increasing temperature generates many more electron-hole pairs, so \(n\) rises exponentially. This more than compensates for the shorter relaxation time \(\tau\), causing \(\rho\) to fall (\(\alpha < 0\)).

Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Although electric current has both magnitude and direction, it is a scalar quantity.
Reason (R): Electric currents meeting at a circuit junction combine algebraically rather than obeying the law of vector addition.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.

Answer: (A)

Solution: A physical quantity is a vector only if it obeys vector addition laws. Because currents at a junction add as ordinary real numbers (and \(I = \mathbf{j} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{S}\) is a dot product), current is a scalar. Thus R is true and directly explains A.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A power station delivers \(20\,\mathrm{kW}\) of electrical power to a factory through transmission cables of total resistance \(5.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\). What are the power losses in the cables when transmitted at \(200\,\mathrm{V}\) and at \(2000\,\mathrm{V}\), respectively?

  • (A) \(50\,\mathrm{kW},\; 0.5\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (B) \(5\,\mathrm{kW},\; 0.5\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (C) \(50\,\mathrm{kW},\; 5\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (D) \(10\,\mathrm{kW},\; 0.1\,\mathrm{kW}\)

Answer: (A)

Solution: Cable power loss is \(P_c = I^2 R_c = (P/V)^2 R_c\). At \(V_1 = 200\,\mathrm{V}\), \(I_1 = 20{,}000 / 200 = 100\,\mathrm{A}\), so \(P_{c1} = (100)^2 \times 5.0 = 50{,}000\,\mathrm{W} = 50\,\mathrm{kW}\). At \(V_2 = 2000\,\mathrm{V}\), \(I_2 = 10\,\mathrm{A}\), so \(P_{c2} = (10)^2 \times 5.0 = 500\,\mathrm{W} = 0.5\,\mathrm{kW}\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A storage battery of emf \(12\,\mathrm{V}\) and internal resistance \(0.5\,\mathrm{Ω}\) is being charged by a \(60\,\mathrm{V}\) DC supply using a series limiting resistor of \(7.5\,\mathrm{Ω}\). What is the terminal potential difference across the battery during charging?

  • (A) \(9.0\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(12.0\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(15.0\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(18.0\,\mathrm{V}\)

Answer: (C)

Solution: Net driving voltage in the charging circuit is \(60\,\mathrm{V} - 12\,\mathrm{V} = 48\,\mathrm{V}\), and total resistance is \(7.5\,\mathrm{Ω} + 0.5\,\mathrm{Ω} = 8.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\). Thus, charging current \(I = 48 / 8.0 = 6.0\,\mathrm{A}\). Since current enters the positive terminal of the battery being charged, \(V = \varepsilon + I r = 12 + (6.0)(0.5) = 15.0\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 7 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Kirchhoff's junction rule is a direct consequence of the conservation of electric charge in steady-state circuits.
Statement II: The relation \(V = I R\) is valid only for ohmic conductors and cannot be used to define resistance for a semiconductor diode.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (C)

Solution: Statement I is correct because steady current implies no charge accumulation at junctions. Statement II is incorrect because \(R = V/I\) defines resistance at a given voltage and current for any conducting device; Ohm's law specifically requires \(R\) to be constant.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a Wheatstone bridge, the four arms AB, BC, CD, and DA have resistances \(12\,\mathrm{Ω}\), \(6\,\mathrm{Ω}\), \(4\,\mathrm{Ω}\), and \(8\,\mathrm{Ω}\), respectively. A galvanometer of resistance \(20\,\mathrm{Ω}\) is connected across BD, and an ideal \(10\,\mathrm{V}\) battery is connected across AC. What is the current through the galvanometer?

  • (A) \(0\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (B) \(0.25\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (C) \(0.50\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (D) \(1.0\,\mathrm{A}\)

Answer: (A)

Solution: Check the bridge balance ratio: \(\frac{R_{\text{AB}}}{R_{\text{BC}}} = \frac{12}{6} = 2\), and \(\frac{R_{\text{AD}}}{R_{\text{DC}}} = \frac{8}{4} = 2\). Since \(\frac{R_{\text{AB}}}{R_{\text{BC}}} = \frac{R_{\text{AD}}}{R_{\text{DC}}}\), the bridge is balanced, \(V_{\text{B}} = V_{\text{D}}\), and the galvanometer current is \(0\,\mathrm{A}\).

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent Exam Traps
  • Confusing \(V = IR\) with Ohm's Law: \(V = IR\) defines resistance \(R\) at an instant; Ohm's law holds only if \(R\) stays constant as \(V\) changes.
  • Resistivity vs. Resistance: Stretching a wire changes \(R\) (via \(R \propto l^2\)), but resistivity \(\rho\) depends only on material and temperature, not dimensions.
  • Charging vs. Discharging Terminal Voltage: Use \(V = \varepsilon - Ir\) when a cell supplies current, and \(V = \varepsilon + Ir\) when a stronger source forces current into its positive terminal.
  • Opposing Cells in Series: While opposing emfs subtract (\(\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = |\varepsilon_1 - \varepsilon_2|\)), internal resistances always add (\(r_{\text{eq}} = r_1 + r_2\)).
  • Wheatstone Bridge Arm Order: Always verify the ratio of adjacent arms connected to the same battery terminal (\(R_1/R_2 = R_3/R_4\)), not arbitrary pairs.

Concise Final Recap

Electric current arises from the slow, field-driven drift of charge carriers superposed on rapid thermal motion, linking microscopic parameters (\(n, \tau, \mu\)) to macroscopic conductivity and Ohm's law. Resistivity rises with temperature in metals due to more frequent collisions, stays nearly flat in standard alloys, and drops in semiconductors as carrier concentration grows. In practical circuits, cells exhibit terminal voltages shifted by internal resistance drops, power losses in cables scale as \(1/V^2\), and multi-loop or bridge networks yield cleanly to Kirchhoff's charge- and energy-conservation rules.
