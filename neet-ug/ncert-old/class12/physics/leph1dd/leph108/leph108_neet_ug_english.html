Electromagnetic Waves

Complete Concept Tutorial, Mathematical Framework, Spectrum Guide, and NEET UG Practice Assessment

Learning Objectives and Overview

Electricity and magnetism are deeply intertwined: steady currents generate magnetic fields, and time-varying magnetic fields induce electric fields. Completing this symmetry requires that a time-varying electric field also generates a magnetic field. This mutual regeneration of changing electric and magnetic fields gives rise to self-propagating electromagnetic (EM) waves that travel through vacuum at the speed of light.

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Explain why Ampere's circuital law needed modification and calculate displacement current (\(i_d\)) in charging or AC capacitors.
  • Apply the Ampere-Maxwell law to find the magnetic field inside and outside a parallel-plate capacitor.
  • State Maxwell's four equations in vacuum and identify accelerated charges as the source of electromagnetic waves.
  • Analyze sinusoidal wave equations for \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\), determine propagation direction using \(\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}\), and compute wave speed, wavelength, frequency, and energy density.
  • Classify the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum by wavelength, frequency, production mechanism, and practical applications.

1. Displacement Current and the Ampere-Maxwell Law

The Inconsistency in Ampere's Circuital Law

Consider a parallel-plate capacitor \(C\) being charged by a time-dependent conduction current \(i_c(t)\). Suppose we want to find the magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) at a point \(P\) located at a radial distance \(r\) from the connecting wire, just outside the left plate. We draw a circular Amperian loop of radius \(r\) centered on the wire and perpendicular to it.

According to the original Ampere's circuital law, \(\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 i_{\text{enclosed}}\), where \(i_{\text{enclosed}}\) is the current piercing any open surface bounded by the loop:

  • Flat circular surface: The conducting wire pierces this surface, so \(i_{\text{enclosed}} = i_c(t)\), giving \(B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 i_c(t)\) (a non-zero magnetic field at \(P\)).
  • Bulging surface passing between the plates: Because the space between the capacitor plates is an insulator (vacuum or dielectric), no conduction current crosses this surface. Thus \(i_{\text{enclosed}} = 0\), which falsely predicts \(B = 0\) at the exact same point \(P\).
Tutorial Diagram 1: Two surfaces (\(S_1\) and \(S_2\)) sharing the same circular Amperian loop at point \(P\). Conduction current \(i_c\) passes through \(S_1\), whereas changing electric flux \(\Phi_E\) passes through \(S_2\) between the capacitor plates.

Maxwell's Resolution: Displacement Current

James Clerk Maxwell resolved this contradiction by noting that although no charge crosses the gap, a time-varying electric field \(E(t)\) passes through surface \(S_2\). If the capacitor plates have area \(A\) and instantaneous charge \(Q(t)\), the uniform electric field between the plates is \(E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}\). The electric flux through the gap is:

\[ \Phi_E = E A = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \]

Differentiating with respect to time \(t\) gives:

\[ \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\frac{dQ}{dt} \implies \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \frac{dQ}{dt} = i_c \]

Maxwell called this term displacement current (\(i_d\)). Adding \(i_d\) to the conduction current \(i_c\) yields the Ampere-Maxwell Law, which gives the exact same magnetic field regardless of which surface is chosen.

Key Equations: Displacement Current & Ampere-Maxwell Law
\[ i_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \] \[ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 (i_c + i_d) = \mu_0 i_c + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \]
Crucial NEET Insights on Displacement Current:
  • Continuity of Current: In the connecting wires, \(i_c = i\) and \(i_d = 0\). Inside the ideal capacitor gap, \(i_c = 0\) and \(i_d = i\). Thus, total current \(i_{\text{total}} = i_c + i_d\) is continuous everywhere, preserving Kirchhoff's junction rule at each plate.
  • Magnetic Field Inside a Circular Capacitor (Plate Radius \(R\)): For a radial distance \(r \le R\) from the central axis, only a fraction \(\left(\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)\) of the total electric flux is enclosed, so: \[ B_{\text{inside}} = \frac{\mu_0 i_d r}{2\pi R^2} \quad (r \le R) \] For \(r > R\) (outside the plate edge or around the wire), all flux/current is enclosed: \[ B_{\text{outside}} = \frac{\mu_0 i_d}{2\pi r} \quad (r > R) \] Notice that \(B\) is zero on the axis (\(r = 0\)), increases linearly with \(r\) up to \(r = R\), and decreases as \(1/r\) for \(r > R\).

2. Maxwell's Equations and Sources of Electromagnetic Waves

Maxwell unified all laws of electricity and magnetism into four fundamental equations. In vacuum (free space), they take the following integral forms:

Law Integral Equation (in Vacuum) Physical Meaning
Gauss's Law for Electricity \(\oint \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \dfrac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}\) Electric charges are sources/sinks of electric field lines.
Gauss's Law for Magnetism \(\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = 0\) Isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist; magnetic field lines form closed loops.
Faraday's Law of Induction \(\oint \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt}\) A time-varying magnetic field induces an electric field.
Ampere-Maxwell Law \(\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 i_c + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \dfrac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\) Conduction currents and time-varying electric fields generate magnetic fields.

How Electromagnetic Waves Are Produced

  • Stationary charge: Produces only an electrostatic field (\(\mathbf{E}\)) that does not vary with time; no EM waves are radiated.
  • Charge in uniform motion (steady current): Produces both electric and magnetic fields, but they are steady in time; no EM waves are radiated.
  • Accelerated charge: Produces time-varying \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) fields that continuously regenerate each other and propagate outward as electromagnetic waves. An oscillating electric dipole (harmonically oscillating charge) radiates EM waves of frequency equal to the oscillation frequency of the charge.

Experimental Verification: While Maxwell predicted EM waves theoretically in 1865, Heinrich Hertz first generated and detected radio-frequency EM waves in 1887 using a spark-gap oscillator. Jagadish Chandra Bose later produced shorter millimetre-wavelength waves (\(5\,\mathrm{mm}\) to \(25\,\mathrm{mm}\)), and Guglielmo Marconi pioneered long-distance wireless radio communication.

3. Nature and Mathematical Description of Electromagnetic Waves

Solutions to Maxwell's equations in source-free space reveal that electromagnetic waves are transverse waves: the oscillating electric field vector \(\mathbf{E}\) and magnetic field vector \(\mathbf{B}\) are mutually perpendicular to each other, and both are perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation.

Tutorial Diagram 2: A linearly polarized plane electromagnetic wave propagating along the \(+z\)-axis. The electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) oscillates along the \(x\)-axis and the magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) oscillates along the \(y\)-axis, in phase with \(\mathbf{E}\).

Wave Equations and Fundamental Relations

For a plane EM wave propagating along the \(+z\)-direction with \(\mathbf{E}\) along \(x\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) along \(y\):

\[ E_x = E_0 \sin(kz - \omega t), \qquad B_y = B_0 \sin(kz - \omega t) \]

where \(k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\) is the propagation constant (wave number) and \(\omega = 2\pi \nu = \frac{2\pi}{T}\) is the angular frequency. Notice that \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) reach their maxima and minima simultaneously (zero phase difference).

Essential EM Wave Formulas for NEET UG
  • Speed in vacuum: \(c = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} \approx 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (independent of wavelength or frequency).
  • Speed in a material medium: \(v = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu \varepsilon}} = \dfrac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r \varepsilon_r}} = \dfrac{c}{n}\), where \(n = \sqrt{\mu_r \varepsilon_r}\) is the refractive index.
  • Field amplitude ratio (in vacuum): \(\dfrac{E_0}{B_0} = \dfrac{E}{B} = c\).
  • Propagation direction: Along the vector \(\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}\).
  • Wave relation: \(c = \nu \lambda = \dfrac{\omega}{k}\).
  • Average energy density: The electric and magnetic fields share energy equally: \[ \langle u_E \rangle = \frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2, \qquad \langle u_B \rangle = \frac{B_0^2}{4\mu_0} = \frac{B_{\text{rms}}^2}{2\mu_0} \] \[ \langle u_E \rangle = \langle u_B \rangle \implies u_{\text{total, avg}} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \frac{B_0^2}{2\mu_0} \]

4. The Electromagnetic Spectrum

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed \(c\) in vacuum, but they differ vastly in wavelength (\(\lambda\)) and frequency (\(\nu\)), which determines how they are produced, detected, and interact with matter. There are no sharp boundaries between adjacent bands; regions overlap slightly.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Overview of the electromagnetic spectrum ordered from long-wavelength radio waves to high-energy gamma rays.
Wave Type Wavelength Range Production Detection & Key Applications
Radio waves \(> 0.1\,\mathrm{m}\) Rapid acceleration/deceleration of electrons in aerials Receiver aerials. AM radio (\(530\text{–}1710\,\mathrm{kHz}\)), FM radio (\(88\text{–}108\,\mathrm{MHz}\)), TV (\(54\text{–}890\,\mathrm{MHz}\)), cellular UHF communication.
Microwaves \(0.1\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(1\,\mathrm{mm}\) Klystron, magnetron, or Gunn diode Point-contact diodes. Aircraft radar, speed guns, and microwave ovens (frequency matches resonant frequency of water molecules).
Infrared (IR) \(1\,\mathrm{mm}\) to \(700\,\mathrm{nm}\) Vibration of atoms and hot bodies/molecules Thermopiles, bolometer, IR film. Called heat waves; absorbed by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\); greenhouse effect, physical therapy, TV remote LEDs, Earth observation.
Visible light \(700\,\mathrm{nm}\) to \(400\,\mathrm{nm}\) Valence electron transitions in atoms Human eye, photocells, photographic film. Frequency range \(\approx 4 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\) to \(7 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\).
Ultraviolet (UV) \(400\,\mathrm{nm}\) to \(1\,\mathrm{nm}\) Inner-shell electron transitions, very hot bodies, welding arcs Photocells, photographic film. Absorbed by atmospheric ozone (\(40\text{–}50\,\mathrm{km}\) altitude) and ordinary glass; used in LASIK eye surgery and water purifiers.
X-rays \(1\,\mathrm{nm}\) to \(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{nm}\) Bombarding a metal target with high-energy electrons (X-ray tubes) Photographic film, Geiger tubes, ionization chambers. Medical imaging (diagnostics) and targeted cancer therapy.
Gamma rays \(< 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{nm}\) Radioactive nuclear decay and nuclear reactions Geiger tubes, ionization chambers, photographic film. Used in medicine to destroy cancer cells.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Displacement Current and Magnetic Field Inside a Capacitor

Problem (Hypothetical Assumption): A parallel-plate capacitor with circular plates of radius \(R = 10\,\mathrm{cm}\) is being charged such that the uniform electric field between the plates increases at a steady rate of \(\frac{dE}{dt} = 4.5 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\). Calculate (a) the total displacement current between the plates and (b) the magnitude of the induced magnetic field at a radial distance \(r = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the central axis.

Solution:

(a) The area of each circular plate is \(A = \pi R^2 = \pi (0.10\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 3.1416 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}\). The displacement current is: \[ i_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \varepsilon_0 A \frac{dE}{dt} \] \[ i_d = (8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2\,N^{-1}\,m^{-2}}) \times (3.1416 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}) \times (4.5 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}) \approx 0.125\,\mathrm{A} \]

(b) For a circular Amperian loop of radius \(r = 0.04\,\mathrm{m}\) (\(r < R\)) inside the capacitor, the enclosed electric flux is \(\Phi_{E,\text{enc}} = E(\pi r^2)\). Applying the Ampere-Maxwell law with \(i_c = 0\): \[ B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \pi r^2 \frac{dE}{dt} \implies B = \frac{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0 r}{2}\frac{dE}{dt} \] Using \(\mu_0 \varepsilon_0 = \frac{1}{c^2} = \frac{1}{(3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})^2} = \frac{1}{9.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}}\): \[ B = \frac{0.04\,\mathrm{m}}{2 \times 9.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}} \times (4.5 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}) = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T} \]

Example 2: Extracting Wave Parameters and Field Vectors from a Wave Equation

Problem (Hypothetical Assumption): The electric field of a plane electromagnetic wave travelling in vacuum is given by: \[ E_y = 90 \sin(1.2 \times 10^7 x + 3.6 \times 10^{15} t)\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} \] Determine (a) the direction of propagation, (b) the wavelength \(\lambda\) and frequency \(\nu\), and (c) the complete vector expression for the magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\).

Solution:

(a) Comparing the phase \((1.2 \times 10^7 x + 3.6 \times 10^{15} t)\) with \((kx + \omega t)\), the plus sign between \(kx\) and \(\omega t\) indicates that the wave propagates along the \(-x\)-direction.

(b) Here \(k = 1.2 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\) and \(\omega = 3.6 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\): \[ \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{1.2 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}} \approx 5.24 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 524\,\mathrm{nm} \] \[ \nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{3.6 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}}{2\pi} \approx 5.73 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz} \]

(c) The magnetic field amplitude is \(B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{90\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}}{3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T}\). Because propagation is along \(\hat{\mathbf{E}} \times \hat{\mathbf{B}} = -\hat{\mathbf{i}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{E}} = +\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), we require \(\hat{\mathbf{j}} \times \hat{\mathbf{B}} = -\hat{\mathbf{i}}\), which gives \(\hat{\mathbf{B}} = -\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) (along the \(-z\)-axis). Therefore: \[ \mathbf{B} = -3.0 \times 10^{-7} \sin(1.2 \times 10^7 x + 3.6 \times 10^{15} t)\,\hat{\mathbf{k}}\,\mathrm{T} \]

Example 3: Energy Density and Speed in a Dielectric Medium

Problem (Hypothetical Assumption): A plane electromagnetic wave in vacuum has a magnetic field amplitude \(B_0 = 4.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{T}\). (a) Find the total average energy density of the wave in vacuum. (b) If the wave enters a non-magnetic transparent medium (\(\mu_r = 1\)) with relative permittivity \(\varepsilon_r = 2.25\), find its speed in the medium.

Solution:

(a) The total average energy density is equally divided between the electric and magnetic fields: \[ u_{\text{total, avg}} = \frac{B_0^2}{2\mu_0} = \frac{(4.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{T})^2}{2 \times 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\cdot m\,A^{-1}}} = \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-15}}{8\pi \times 10^{-7}} \approx 6.37 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}} \]

(b) In the dielectric medium, the wave speed is: \[ v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r \varepsilon_r}} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{\sqrt{1 \times 2.25}} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{1.5} = 2.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Practice Question 1

A parallel-plate capacitor with circular plates of radius \(R\) is charged by a constant current \(I\). Let \(B_1\) be the magnetic field at a point between the plates at a radial distance \(r = R/2\) from the axis, and let \(B_2\) be the magnetic field around the connecting wire at a radial distance \(r = 2R\) from the axis. The ratio \(B_1 / B_2\) is:

  • (A) \(1 : 4\)
  • (B) \(1 : 2\)
  • (C) \(1 : 1\)
  • (D) \(2 : 1\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Inside the capacitor (\(r \le R\)), \(B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi R^2}\). Substituting \(r = R/2\) gives \(B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I (R/2)}{2\pi R^2} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi R}\). Outside around the wire at \(r = 2R\), \(B_2 = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi (2R)} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi R}\). Therefore, \(B_1 / B_2 = 1 : 1\).

Practice Question 2

At a certain instant and location in free space, the electric field vector of an electromagnetic wave points along the \(+z\)-direction and its magnetic field vector points along the \(+x\)-direction. The wave is propagating along the:

  • (A) \(+y\)-direction
  • (B) \(-y\)-direction
  • (C) \(+x\)-direction
  • (D) \(-z\)-direction
Answer: (A)

Solution: The direction of propagation is given by \(\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}\). Since \(\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{i}} = +\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), the wave propagates along the \(+y\)-direction.

Practice Question 3

The magnetic field component of a plane electromagnetic wave in vacuum is described by \(B_x = 2.0 \times 10^{-7} \sin(400 y - 1.2 \times 10^{11} t)\,\mathrm{T}\). The amplitude of the electric field \(E_0\) and the wavelength \(\lambda\) of the wave are approximately:

  • (A) \(60\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) and \(1.57\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (B) \(60\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) and \(3.14\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (C) \(30\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) and \(1.57\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (D) \(600\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) and \(0.78\,\mathrm{cm}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: \(E_0 = c B_0 = (3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})(2.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T}) = 60\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\). From \(k = 400\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), the wavelength is \(\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{400} \approx 0.0157\,\mathrm{m} = 1.57\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Practice Question 4

An electromagnetic wave travels through a medium having relative permittivity \(\varepsilon_r = 4.0\) and relative permeability \(\mu_r = 1.44\). The speed of the wave in this medium is:

  • (A) \(2.5 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(1.5 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(1.25 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(2.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: The speed in the medium is \(v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r \varepsilon_r}} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{\sqrt{1.44 \times 4.0}} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{1.2 \times 2.0} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{2.4} = 1.25 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 5

Which of the following charge configurations will radiate electromagnetic waves?

  • (A) A point charge held stationary in a uniform electric field
  • (B) A proton moving with constant velocity in a straight line
  • (C) An electron moving in a circular path with constant speed
  • (D) A steady direct current flowing through a straight copper wire
Answer: (C)

Solution: Only accelerated charges radiate electromagnetic waves. An electron moving in a circular path experiences continuous centripetal acceleration, so it radiates EM waves. Stationary charges and steady currents do not radiate.

Practice Question 6

In a plane electromagnetic wave propagating in vacuum, the electric field amplitude is \(E_0 = 60\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\). The average energy density associated with the magnetic field alone is (take \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2\,N^{-1}\,m^{-2}}\)):

  • (A) \(4.0 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\)
  • (B) \(8.0 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\)
  • (C) \(1.6 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\)
  • (D) \(3.2 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: In an EM wave, the average magnetic energy density equals the average electric energy density: \(\langle u_B \rangle = \langle u_E \rangle = \frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \frac{1}{4}(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(60)^2 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 900 \approx 7.97 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}} \approx 8.0 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\).

Which of the following lists correctly arranges electromagnetic waves in decreasing order of wavelength?

  • (B) Microwaves, Infrared, Ultraviolet, X-rays
  • (C) Infrared, Microwaves, X-rays, Ultraviolet
  • (D) Ultraviolet, X-rays, Microwaves, Infrared

Practice Question 8

Given below are two statements:

Statement I: Displacement current arises whenever the electric flux through a surface changes with time, even in empty space where no conduction charges are present.

Statement II: In an electromagnetic wave travelling through vacuum, the average energy density of the electric field is greater than the average energy density of the magnetic field.

In light of the above statements, choose the most appropriate option:

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Solution: Statement I is correct by definition of displacement current (\(i_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\)). Statement II is incorrect because the average electric and magnetic energy densities in a vacuum EM wave are strictly equal (\(\langle u_E \rangle = \langle u_B \rangle\)).

Practice Question 9

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as Assertion (A) and the other is labelled as Reason (R).

Assertion (A): A person sitting behind a closed ordinary glass window does not get sunburned or tanned by sunlight.

Reason (R): Ordinary glass absorbs ultraviolet radiation while allowing visible light to pass through.

Choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Practice Question 10

A radio receiver can tune in to any station in the frequency band \(6.0\,\mathrm{MHz}\) to \(15.0\,\mathrm{MHz}\). The corresponding wavelength band is:

  • (A) \(20\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(50\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(25\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(30\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(60\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(40\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(100\,\mathrm{m}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Using \(\lambda = \frac{c}{\nu}\): for \(\nu_1 = 6.0 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{Hz}\), \(\lambda_1 = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{6.0 \times 10^6} = 50\,\mathrm{m}\). For \(\nu_2 = 15.0 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{Hz}\), \(\lambda_2 = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{15.0 \times 10^6} = 20\,\mathrm{m}\). Thus, the wavelength band is \(20\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(50\,\mathrm{m}\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Confusing peak and RMS values in energy density: Remember \(u_{\text{total, avg}} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2\). Do not mix \(E_0\) with the \(\varepsilon_0 E_{\text{rms}}^2\) factor.
  • Forgetting the \(r/R\) scaling inside a capacitor: Between circular plates of radius \(R\), \(B \propto r\) for \(r \le R\), not \(1/r\).
  • Sign error in wave propagation direction: In \(\sin(kx - \omega t)\), propagation is along \(+x\); in \(\sin(kx + \omega t)\), propagation is along \(-x\). Always verify \(\hat{\mathbf{E}} \times \hat{\mathbf{B}}\) using the right-hand rule.
  • Assuming EM waves need a medium: Unlike sound waves, EM waves are self-sustaining field oscillations that propagate through vacuum.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • \(i_d = \varepsilon_0 \dfrac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = C \dfrac{dV}{dt}\) (for an ideal capacitor).
  • \(c = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} = 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(v = \dfrac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r \varepsilon_r}}\).
  • \(E_0 = c B_0\); \(\mathbf{E}\), \(\mathbf{B}\), and \(\mathbf{k}\) are mutually perpendicular and \(\mathbf{E}\), \(\mathbf{B}\) oscillate in phase.
  • \(\langle u_E \rangle = \langle u_B \rangle = \dfrac{1}{4}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \dfrac{B_0^2}{4\mu_0}\).
  • Spectrum order (increasing \(\nu\)): Radio \(\rightarrow\) Microwave \(\rightarrow\) Infrared \(\rightarrow\) Visible (\(700\text{–}400\,\mathrm{nm}\)) \(\rightarrow\) UV \(\rightarrow\) X-rays \(\rightarrow\) Gamma rays.

Concise Final Recap

Maxwell's introduction of displacement current (\(i_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\)) completed the symmetry of electromagnetism and predicted that accelerated charges radiate transverse electromagnetic waves. These waves carry energy equally in mutually perpendicular, in-phase electric and magnetic fields at speed \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\) in vacuum. Across the electromagnetic spectrum—from long radio waves to high-energy gamma rays—every band obeys the same fundamental wave relations while differing in wavelength, frequency, and interaction with matter.
