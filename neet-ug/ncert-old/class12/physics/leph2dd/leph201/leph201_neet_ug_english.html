Ray Optics and Optical Instruments

A complete, exam-oriented study guide covering reflection, refraction, total internal reflection, lenses, prisms, and optical instruments.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Light consists of electromagnetic waves to which the human retina is sensitive, spanning wavelengths roughly from \(400\,\mathrm{nm}\) to \(750\,\mathrm{nm}\). In vacuum, light travels at \(c = 2.99792458 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (commonly approximated as \(3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)), the highest attainable speed in nature.

When light interacts with everyday objects—mirrors, lenses, prisms, and apertures—whose dimensions are much larger than the wavelength of light, diffraction effects are negligible. Under this condition, light can be treated as travelling along straight-line paths called rays, and a collection of rays forms a beam. This geometrical model is known as ray optics.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Apply the Cartesian sign convention consistently to spherical mirrors, spherical refracting surfaces, and thin lenses.
  • Use the mirror equation, lens maker's formula, thin lens formula, and lens combination relations to locate images and compute magnifications.
  • Analyse refraction at plane interfaces, lateral shift, apparent depth, and total internal reflection (including critical angle and optical fibres).
  • Derive and apply the prism deviation relations and minimum deviation formula.
  • Calculate the magnifying power and tube length of simple microscopes, compound microscopes, and astronomical telescopes under different viewing adjustments.

Reflection and the Cartesian Sign Convention

Laws of Reflection

At any smooth reflecting surface—plane or curved—two fundamental laws govern reflection:

  • The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence (\(i = r'\)), where both angles are measured between the ray and the normal to the surface at the point of incidence.
  • The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.

For a spherical mirror, the normal at any point on the reflecting surface is the radial line joining that point to the centre of curvature \(C\). The geometric centre of the mirror surface is its pole \(P\), and the straight line passing through \(P\) and \(C\) is the principal axis.

Cartesian Sign Convention

To ensure that a single mirror or lens formula works across all object and image positions, distances and heights are assigned algebraic signs using the Cartesian sign convention:

  1. All distances are measured from the pole \(P\) (for mirrors) or the optical centre \(O\) (for lenses).
  2. Distances measured in the same direction as the incident light are taken as positive.
  3. Distances measured opposite to the direction of incident light are taken as negative.
  4. Heights measured upwards perpendicular to the principal axis are positive; heights measured downwards are negative.

Image Formation by Spherical Mirrors

For paraxial rays—rays incident close to the pole and making small angles with the principal axis—a beam parallel to the principal axis converges at the principal focus \(F\) of a concave mirror, or appears to diverge from \(F\) behind a convex mirror. If parallel paraxial rays arrive at an angle to the principal axis, they focus at a point on the focal plane (the plane through \(F\) perpendicular to the principal axis).

Under the small-angle approximation, the focal length \(f\) (distance \(PF\)) is related to the radius of curvature \(R\) (distance \(PC\)) by:

Focal Length, Mirror Equation, and Linear Magnification
\[f = \frac{R}{2}\] \[\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}\] \[m = \frac{h'}{h} = -\frac{v}{u}\]

Here \(u\) is the object distance, \(v\) is the image distance, \(h\) is the object height, and \(h'\) is the image height. By convention, \(f\) is negative for a concave mirror and positive for a convex mirror.

Key Insight: Covering half of a spherical mirror's aperture with an opaque material does not cut off half the image. Every remaining reflecting point still obeys the laws of reflection and forms the complete image, though the image brightness (intensity) decreases proportionally to the reduced reflecting area.

Refraction and Total Internal Reflection

Snell's Law and Optical Density

When a light ray crosses the boundary between two transparent media obliquely (\(0^\circ < i < 90^\circ\)), its speed changes, causing a change in direction called refraction. Snell's laws state:

  • The incident ray, refracted ray, and normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.
  • For a given pair of media and wavelength, the ratio of the sines of the angles of incidence and refraction is constant:
Snell's Law and Refractive Index Relations
\[\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = n_{21} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} \implies n_1 \sin i = n_2 \sin r\] \[n_{12} = \frac{1}{n_{21}}, \qquad n_{32} = n_{31} \times n_{12}\]

If \(n_{21} > 1\), the ray bends towards the normal (\(r < i\)), and medium 2 is optically denser than medium 1. Remember that optical density (determined by the refractive index, i.e., the ratio of light speeds) is distinct from mass density (mass per unit volume). For instance, turpentine has a lower mass density than water, yet its refractive index (optical density) is higher.

Plane-Parallel Slab and Apparent Depth

  • Parallel-Sided Slab: At the two parallel faces (e.g., air–glass and glass–air), the emergent angle equals the initial angle of incidence (\(r_2 = i_1\)). Thus, there is zero angular deviation, though the emergent ray undergoes a perpendicular lateral shift.
  • Apparent Depth: An object at real depth \(h_{\text{real}}\) inside a medium of refractive index \(n\) viewed near-normally from air (\(n_{\text{air}} \approx 1\)) appears raised to an apparent depth: \[h_{\text{app}} = \frac{h_{\text{real}}}{n}, \qquad \text{Apparent shift } \Delta h = h_{\text{real}}\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)\]

Total Internal Reflection (TIR) and Applications

When light travels from an optically denser medium (\(n_1\)) toward an optically rarer medium (\(n_2 < n_1\)), the refracted ray bends away from the normal (\(r > i\)). As \(i\) increases, \(r\) reaches \(90^\circ\) at a specific angle of incidence called the critical angle \(i_c\). For any \(i > i_c\), refraction is impossible and 100% of the incident light is reflected back into the denser medium—a phenomenon called total internal reflection.

Critical Angle Condition
\[\sin i_c = \frac{n_{\text{rarer}}}{n_{\text{denser}}} = n_{21} \quad (\text{where } n_1 > n_2)\]

For a medium of refractive index \(n\) bounded by air (\(n_{\text{air}} \approx 1\)), \(\sin i_c = \frac{1}{n}\).

Tutorial Diagram 3: Transition from partial refraction (\(i < i_c\)) to grazing emergence (\(i = i_c\)) and total internal reflection (\(i > i_c\)).

  • Typical Critical Angles (with respect to air): Water (\(n = 1.33\), \(i_c \approx 48.75^\circ\)), Crown glass (\(n = 1.52\), \(i_c \approx 41.14^\circ\)), Dense flint glass (\(n = 1.62\), \(i_c \approx 37.31^\circ\)), Diamond (\(n = 2.42\), \(i_c \approx 24.41^\circ\)).
  • Totally Reflecting Prisms: Right-angled glass prisms (\(45^\circ\text{–}90^\circ\text{–}45^\circ\)) bend light by \(90^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\) without intensity loss because \(45^\circ > i_c\) for glass.
  • Optical Fibres: Consist of a high-refractive-index core surrounded by a lower-refractive-index cladding. Light entering within the acceptance angle undergoes successive total internal reflections along the fibre, even around bends, enabling low-loss signal transmission and medical endoscopy.

Refraction at Spherical Surfaces and Thin Lenses

Single Spherical Refracting Surface

When paraxial rays from an object in a medium of refractive index \(n_1\) refract across a spherical boundary of radius of curvature \(R\) into a medium of refractive index \(n_2\), the object distance \(u\) and image distance \(v\) satisfy:

Spherical Surface Refraction Formula
\[\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}\]

Thin Lenses and the Lens Maker's Formula

A thin lens is bounded by two surfaces (at least one spherical) whose thickness is negligible compared to the radii of curvature and object/image distances. Applying spherical surface refraction successively at both faces yields:

Lens Maker's Formula, Thin Lens Formula, and Power
\[\frac{1}{f} = \left(\frac{n_2}{n_1} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right) = (n_{21} - 1)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\] \[\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}, \qquad m = \frac{h'}{h} = \frac{v}{u}\] \[P = \frac{1}{f\,(\text{in metres})} \quad (\text{SI unit: dioptre, } 1\,\mathrm{D} = 1\,\mathrm{m}^{-1})\]

Here \(n_2\) is the refractive index of the lens material and \(n_1\) is that of the surrounding medium. Notice that if a convex glass lens (\(n_2 = 1.5\)) is immersed in a liquid with \(n_1 > n_2\), \((n_{21} - 1)\) becomes negative, so the focal length flips sign and the converging lens behaves as a diverging lens. If \(n_1 = n_2\), \(\frac{1}{f} = 0\) (\(f \to \infty\)), and the lens becomes optically invisible.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Image formation by a thin converging lens showing a real, inverted image when the object is beyond \(F\).

Combination of Thin Lenses

When thin lenses of focal lengths \(f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots\) are placed coaxially in contact:

Coaxial Thin Lenses in Contact
\[\frac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} + \frac{1}{f_3} + \cdots\] \[P_{\text{eq}} = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + \cdots, \qquad m_{\text{total}} = m_1 m_2 m_3 \cdots\]

If two thin lenses are separated by a distance \(d\) in air, the equivalent focal length for an incident parallel beam is given by \(\frac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} - \frac{d}{f_1 f_2}\). However, for finite object distances in separated multi-lens systems, always trace the image step-by-step: the image formed by the first lens acts as the object (real or virtual) for the second lens.

Refraction Through a Triangular Prism

Consider a triangular glass prism of refracting angle \(A\). A ray incident on the first face at angle \(i\) refracts at angle \(r_1\), strikes the second face at angle \(r_2\), and emerges into air at angle \(e\). The total angular deviation \(\delta\) between the emergent and incident rays satisfies:

Prism Relations and Minimum Deviation
\[r_1 + r_2 = A, \qquad \delta = i + e - A\]

At minimum deviation (\(\delta = D_m\)), the ray passes symmetrically through the prism (\(i = e\) and \(r_1 = r_2 = A/2\)), parallel to the base:

\[n_{21} = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A + D_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)}\]

For a thin prism (small refracting angle \(A\)): \(D_m \approx (n_{21} - 1)A\).

Optical Instruments: Microscopes and Telescopes

Optical instruments aid the human eye by increasing the angular size of objects. For a normal human eye, the least distance of distinct vision (near point) is \(D = 25\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Simple Microscope (Magnifying Glass)

A converging lens of small focal length \(f\) held close to the eye allows an object to be placed at \(u \le f\), producing an erect, virtual, magnified image:

  • Image at near point (\(v = -D\)): \(m = 1 + \frac{D}{f}\) (maximum magnifying power, slight eye strain).
  • Image at infinity (relaxed eye): \(m = \frac{D}{f}\).

Compound Microscope

For higher magnifications, two converging lenses of short focal lengths are used: an objective (focal length \(f_o\)) near the object and an eyepiece (focal length \(f_e\)) near the eye. The objective forms a real, inverted, magnified intermediate image near the first focal point of the eyepiece, which then acts as a simple magnifier.

Compound Microscope Magnifying Power
\[m = m_o \times m_e\]

Using the tube length \(L\) (distance between the second focal point of the objective and the first focal point of the eyepiece), \(m_o \approx \frac{L}{f_o}\):

\[m = \left(\frac{L}{f_o}\right)\left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right) \quad (\text{final image at } D)\] \[m = \left(\frac{L}{f_o}\right)\left(\frac{D}{f_e}\right) \quad (\text{final image at } \infty)\]

Astronomical Telescopes (Refracting and Reflecting)

An astronomical telescope provides angular magnification of distant objects. Its objective has a large focal length \(f_o\) and large aperture (to gather more light and improve resolving power), while the eyepiece has a small focal length \(f_e\).

Refracting Telescope Magnifying Power and Tube Length
\[m = \frac{f_o}{f_e}, \qquad \text{Tube length } L = f_o + f_e \quad (\text{normal adjustment, final image at } \infty)\] \[m = \frac{f_o}{f_e}\left(1 + \frac{f_e}{D}\right) \quad (\text{final image at near point } D)\]

Reflecting Telescopes (Cassegrain Design): Modern large telescopes replace the heavy glass objective lens with a large concave parabolic mirror (primary) and a small convex secondary mirror that directs light through a central hole to the eyepiece. Reflecting telescopes are completely free from chromatic aberration, lighter, and easier to support mechanically across their entire back surface.

Worked Examples

1. Locating Images and Calculating Magnification with a Concave Mirror

Problem: Assume an object of height \(2.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) is placed \(18\,\mathrm{cm}\) in front of a concave mirror whose radius of curvature is \(24\,\mathrm{cm}\). Determine the position, nature, and height of the image.

Solution:

Using the Cartesian sign convention, for a concave mirror:

\[f = -\frac{R}{2} = -\frac{24}{2} = -12\,\mathrm{cm}, \qquad u = -18\,\mathrm{cm}, \qquad h = +2.0\,\mathrm{cm}\]

Applying the mirror equation \(\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}\):

\[\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{-12} - \frac{1}{-18} = -\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{18} = \frac{-3 + 2}{36} = -\frac{1}{36}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}\] \[v = -36\,\mathrm{cm}\]

Linear magnification:

\[m = -\frac{v}{u} = -\left(\frac{-36}{-18}\right) = -2\] \[h' = m \times h = -2 \times 2.0\,\mathrm{cm} = -4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\]

The image is formed \(36\,\mathrm{cm}\) in front of the mirror, and it is real, inverted, and \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) tall.

2. Apparent Depth and Critical Angle in a Liquid Tank

Problem: Suppose a transparent liquid of refractive index \(n = 1.60\) fills a tank to a depth of \(24\,\mathrm{cm}\). Calculate (a) the apparent depth of a coin at the bottom when viewed nearly normally from air, and (b) the critical angle for the liquid–air interface.

Solution:

(a) Apparent depth for near-normal viewing:

\[h_{\text{app}} = \frac{h_{\text{real}}}{n} = \frac{24\,\mathrm{cm}}{1.60} = 15\,\mathrm{cm}\]

(b) Critical angle \(i_c\) at the liquid–air interface (\(n_{\text{air}} = 1\)):

\[\sin i_c = \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{1.60} = 0.625 \implies i_c = \arcsin(0.625) \approx 38.7^\circ\]

3. Applying the Lens Maker's Formula in Air and in a Liquid

Problem: Assume an equiconvex glass lens (\(n_g = 1.50\)) has surfaces of radius of curvature \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) each. Find its focal length (a) in air (\(n = 1.00\)), and (b) when immersed in a liquid of refractive index \(n_l = 1.25\).

Solution:

By sign convention for a biconvex lens, \(R_1 = +20\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(R_2 = -20\,\mathrm{cm}\).

(a) In air:

\[\frac{1}{f_{\text{air}}} = (1.50 - 1)\left(\frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{-20}\right) = 0.50 \times \frac{2}{20} = \frac{1}{20}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1} \implies f_{\text{air}} = +20\,\mathrm{cm}\]

(b) In the liquid (\(n_l = 1.25\)):

\[\frac{1}{f_{\text{liq}}} = \left(\frac{1.50}{1.25} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{-20}\right) = (1.20 - 1)\left(\frac{1}{10}\right) = 0.02\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1} \implies f_{\text{liq}} = +50\,\mathrm{cm}\]

4. Determining Refractive Index from Minimum Deviation in a Prism

Problem: An equilateral glass prism (refracting angle \(A = 60^\circ\)) is observed to produce an angle of minimum deviation \(D_m = 30^\circ\) in air. Calculate the refractive index of the prism material.

Solution:

Using the prism minimum deviation formula:

\[n = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A + D_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)} = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{60^\circ + 30^\circ}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{60^\circ}{2}\right)} = \frac{\sin 45^\circ}{\sin 30^\circ} = \frac{1/\sqrt{2}}{1/2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\]

5. Calculating Magnifying Power of a Compound Microscope

Problem: A compound microscope is assumed to have an objective of focal length \(f_o = 1.5\,\mathrm{cm}\), an eyepiece of focal length \(f_e = 5.0\,\mathrm{cm}\), and a tube length \(L = 15\,\mathrm{cm}\). Find its magnifying power when the final image is formed (a) at infinity, and (b) at the least distance of distinct vision (\(D = 25\,\mathrm{cm}\)).

Solution:

(a) Final image at infinity (relaxed eye):

\[m = \left(\frac{L}{f_o}\right)\left(\frac{D}{f_e}\right) = \left(\frac{15}{1.5}\right)\left(\frac{25}{5.0}\right) = 10 \times 5 = 50\]

(b) Final image at the near point (\(D = 25\,\mathrm{cm}\)):

\[m = \left(\frac{L}{f_o}\right)\left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right) = 10 \times \left(1 + \frac{25}{5.0}\right) = 10 \times 6 = 60\]

Practice Question 1

A small object is placed \(15\,\mathrm{cm}\) in front of a concave mirror of focal length \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\). Where is the image formed and what is its magnification?

  • (A) \(30\,\mathrm{cm}\) in front of the mirror, \(m = -2\)
  • (B) \(30\,\mathrm{cm}\) behind the mirror, \(m = +2\)
  • (C) \(6\,\mathrm{cm}\) in front of the mirror, \(m = -0.4\)
  • (D) \(6\,\mathrm{cm}\) behind the mirror, \(m = +0.4\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Here \(u = -15\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(f = -10\,\mathrm{cm}\). From \(\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}\), we get \(\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{-10} - \frac{1}{-15} = -\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{15} = -\frac{1}{30}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}\), so \(v = -30\,\mathrm{cm}\) (in front of the mirror). Magnification \(m = -\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{-30}{-15} = -2\).

Practice Question 2

An object is placed \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) in front of a convex mirror of radius of curvature \(40\,\mathrm{cm}\). The image is formed at:

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) in front of the mirror, real
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) behind the mirror, virtual
  • (C) \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) behind the mirror, virtual
  • (D) infinity
Answer: (B)

Solution: For a convex mirror, \(f = +\frac{R}{2} = +20\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(u = -20\,\mathrm{cm}\). Using the mirror equation: \(\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{-20} = \frac{2}{20} = \frac{1}{10}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1} \implies v = +10\,\mathrm{cm}\). Positive \(v\) indicates a virtual image \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) behind the mirror.

Practice Question 3

A point source of light is placed at a depth of \(4.0\,\mathrm{m}\) below the horizontal surface of a liquid of refractive index \(n = \frac{5}{3}\). What is the radius of the circular patch on the liquid surface through which light emerges into air?

  • (A) \(2.4\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(3.0\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(3.2\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(5.3\,\mathrm{m}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Light emerges only within the critical angle cone: \(\sin i_c = \frac{1}{n} = \frac{3}{5}\). Therefore \(\cos i_c = \frac{4}{5}\) and \(\tan i_c = \frac{3}{4}\). The radius of the circular patch is \(R = h \tan i_c = 4.0 \times \frac{3}{4} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m}\).

Practice Question 4

An optical fibre has a glass core of refractive index \(1.60\) surrounded by a cladding of refractive index \(1.44\). The sine of the critical angle at the core–cladding interface is:

  • (A) \(0.80\)
  • (B) \(0.85\)
  • (C) \(0.90\)
  • (D) \(0.95\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: For total internal reflection from core (\(n_1 = 1.60\)) to cladding (\(n_2 = 1.44\)), \(\sin i_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{1.44}{1.60} = 0.90\).

Practice Question 5

A point object in air (\(n_1 = 1.0\)) lies \(30\,\mathrm{cm}\) in front of a convex spherical glass surface (\(n_2 = 1.5\)) of radius of curvature \(15\,\mathrm{cm}\). Where is the image formed?

  • (A) At \(30\,\mathrm{cm}\) inside the glass
  • (B) At \(45\,\mathrm{cm}\) inside the glass
  • (C) At \(15\,\mathrm{cm}\) in air
  • (D) At infinity
Answer: (D)

Solution: Using \(\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}\) with \(u = -30\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(R = +15\,\mathrm{cm}\): \(\frac{1.5}{v} - \frac{1.0}{-30} = \frac{1.5 - 1.0}{15} = \frac{1}{30} \implies \frac{1.5}{v} = \frac{1}{30} - \frac{1}{30} = 0 \implies v = \infty\).

Practice Question 6

Two thin lenses of powers \(+5.0\,\mathrm{D}\) and \(-3.0\,\mathrm{D}\) are placed coaxially in contact. The focal length and nature of the combination are:

  • (A) \(+50\,\mathrm{cm}\), converging
  • (B) \(-50\,\mathrm{cm}\), diverging
  • (C) \(+12.5\,\mathrm{cm}\), converging
  • (D) \(-12.5\,\mathrm{cm}\), diverging
Answer: (A)

Solution: Net power \(P = P_1 + P_2 = +5.0 + (-3.0) = +2.0\,\mathrm{D}\). Equivalent focal length \(f = \frac{1}{P} = \frac{1}{2.0}\,\mathrm{m} = +0.50\,\mathrm{m} = +50\,\mathrm{cm}\). Since \(f > 0\), the combination is converging.

Practice Question 7

A biconvex lens made of glass (\(n = 1.5\)) has a focal length of \(25\,\mathrm{cm}\) in air. When completely immersed in a transparent liquid of refractive index \(1.6\), its focal length becomes:

  • (A) \(+200\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (B) \(-200\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (C) \(+100\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (D) \(-100\,\mathrm{cm}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Let \(K = \left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)\). In air: \(\frac{1}{25} = (1.5 - 1)K = 0.5K \implies K = \frac{2}{25}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}\). In the liquid: \(\frac{1}{f_{\text{liq}}} = \left(\frac{1.5}{1.6} - 1\right)K = \left(-\frac{0.1}{1.6}\right)\left(\frac{2}{25}\right) = -\frac{1}{200}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-1}\). Hence \(f_{\text{liq}} = -200\,\mathrm{cm}\) (behaves as a diverging lens).

Practice Question 8

A thin glass prism has a refracting angle of \(4.0^\circ\) and refractive index \(1.5\). What is the angle of deviation produced by the prism?

  • (A) \(1.0^\circ\)
  • (B) \(2.0^\circ\)
  • (C) \(3.0^\circ\)
  • (D) \(6.0^\circ\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: For a thin prism, the deviation is \(\delta \approx (n - 1)A = (1.5 - 1) \times 4.0^\circ = 0.5 \times 4.0^\circ = 2.0^\circ\).

Practice Question 9

An astronomical refracting telescope in normal adjustment (final image at infinity) has an objective of focal length \(120\,\mathrm{cm}\) and an eyepiece of focal length \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\). Its magnifying power and tube length are respectively:

  • (A) \(30\) and \(124\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (B) \(30\) and \(116\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (C) \(480\) and \(124\,\mathrm{cm}\)
  • (D) \(15\) and \(120\,\mathrm{cm}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: In normal adjustment, \(m = \frac{f_o}{f_e} = \frac{120}{4.0} = 30\), and the separation (tube length) is \(L = f_o + f_e = 120 + 4.0 = 124\,\mathrm{cm}\).

Practice Question 10

Given below are two statements:
Statement I: A concave mirror can form a virtual, erect, and magnified image of a real object when the object is placed between its pole and principal focus.
Statement II: A convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image for any real object placed in front of it.
Choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)

Solution: For a concave mirror (\(f < 0\)), if \(-|f| < u < 0\), the mirror equation gives \(v > 0\) and \(m = -\frac{v}{u} > +1\) (virtual, erect, magnified). For a convex mirror (\(f > 0\)) and any real object (\(u < 0\)), \(\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} + \frac{1}{|u|} > \frac{1}{f}\), giving \(0 < v < f\) and \(0 < m < 1\) (virtual, erect, diminished). Both statements are correct.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Sign Convention Confusion: Mixing up the signs of \(\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}\) (mirrors) and \(\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}\) (lenses), or forgetting the minus sign in mirror magnification \(m = -\frac{v}{u}\).
  • Optical Density vs. Mass Density: Assuming a denser liquid (like water) always has a higher refractive index than a lighter liquid (like turpentine or oil). Always compare refractive indices directly.
  • Lens in a Liquid: Treating a convex lens as always converging. If \(n_{\text{surrounding}} > n_{\text{lens}}\), a convex lens becomes diverging and a concave lens becomes converging.
  • Microscope vs. Telescope Objectives: In a compound microscope, both \(f_o\) and \(f_e\) are small (with \(f_o < f_e\) typically). In a telescope, \(f_o\) is very large and \(f_e\) is small.
  • Unit Consistency in Lens Power: Using focal length in centimetres inside \(P = \frac{1}{f}\) without converting to metres (or using \(P = \frac{100}{f(\text{in cm})}\)).
