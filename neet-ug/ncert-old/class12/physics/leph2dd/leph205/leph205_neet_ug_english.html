Atomic Nuclei: Structure, Binding Energy, Nuclear Force, and Energy Release

Concept mastery, quantitative methods, and NEET UG-aligned practice on nuclear composition, mass-energy equivalence, stability, fission, and fusion.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Rutherford’s alpha-particle scattering experiments established that nearly all the mass (more than 99.9%) and the entire positive charge of an atom reside in a tiny central core called the nucleus. While the radius of an atom is roughly \(10^4\) times larger than that of its nucleus, the nuclear volume is only about \(10^{-12}\) times the atomic volume—meaning an atom is mostly empty space.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial
  • Representing nuclides using atomic number (\(Z\)), neutron number (\(N\)), and mass number (\(A\)), and distinguishing isotopes, isobars, and isotones.
  • Calculating average atomic mass from isotopic abundances and relating nuclear radius (\(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\)) to constant nuclear density.
  • Applying Einstein’s mass-energy equivalence (\(E = mc^2\)) to compute mass defect (\(\Delta M\)), binding energy (\(E_b\)), and binding energy per nucleon (\(E_{bn}\)).
  • Interpreting the binding energy per nucleon curve and the nucleon-nucleon potential energy curve to explain nuclear stability and the properties of the strong nuclear force.
  • Distinguishing \(\alpha\)-, \(\beta\)-, and \(\gamma\)-decays, calculating \(Q\)-values of nuclear reactions, and analyzing nuclear fission and thermonuclear fusion.

Nuclear Composition, Isotopes, and Atomic Mass Units

1. Protons, Neutrons, and Nuclide Notation

A nucleus consists of positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons, collectively termed nucleons. Electrons do not reside inside the nucleus; they occupy the space outside it. In a neutral atom, the number of orbital electrons equals the number of nuclear protons.

  • Atomic Number (\(Z\)): Total number of protons in the nucleus, giving a nuclear charge of \(+Ze\).
  • Neutron Number (\(N\)): Total number of neutrons in the nucleus.
  • Mass Number (\(A\)): Total number of nucleons, \(A = Z + N\).

A specific nuclear species (a nuclide) is denoted as \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\), where \(\mathrm{X}\) is the chemical symbol. For instance, \(^{197}_{79}\mathrm{Au}\) contains 79 protons and \(197 - 79 = 118\) neutrons.

2. Discovery and Stability of the Neutron

Because isotopes of hydrogen—protium (\(^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}\)), deuterium (\(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}\)), and tritium (\(^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}\))—all have one proton yet exhibit nuclear mass ratios close to \(1:2:3\), neutral nuclear matter was hypothesized. In 1932, James Chadwick bombarded beryllium nuclei with \(\alpha\)-particles (\(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\) nuclei) and detected penetrating neutral radiation capable of knocking protons out of light nuclei (such as helium, carbon, and nitrogen). Conservation of energy and momentum ruled out photons and confirmed a neutral particle of mass nearly equal to the proton: the neutron.

While a free proton is stable, a free neutron is unstable and decays with a mean life of about \(1000\,\mathrm{s}\) into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino. Inside a stable nucleus, however, neutrons remain stable.

3. Atomic Mass Unit (u) and Weighted Average Mass

Because the kilogram is inconveniently large for atomic masses (one \(^{12}\mathrm{C}\) atom has mass \(1.992647 \times 10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}\)), masses are expressed in the atomic mass unit (\(\mathrm{u}\)), defined as \(\frac{1}{12}\) of the mass of a single unbound \(^{12}\mathrm{C}\) atom:

Standard Masses and Energy Equivalent
\[ 1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.660539 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2 \] \[ m_p = 1.00727\,\mathrm{u} = 1.67262 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}, \quad m_n = 1.00866\,\mathrm{u} = 1.6749 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}, \quad m_e = 0.00055\,\mathrm{u} \]

Most elements occur naturally as mixtures of isotopes. The observed atomic mass of an element is the weighted average of the masses of its isotopes according to their percentage abundances. For example, chlorine has two major isotopes of masses \(34.98\,\mathrm{u}\) (75.4%) and \(36.98\,\mathrm{u}\) (24.6%), yielding an average atomic mass of \(35.47\,\mathrm{u}\).

4. Classification of Nuclides

Category Defining Condition Key Property Examples
Isotopes Same \(Z\), different \(N\) (and \(A\)) Identical electronic structure and chemical properties; same periodic table position \(^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H},\; ^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H},\; ^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}\)
Isobars Same \(A\), different \(Z\) and \(N\) Different elements with equal total nucleon count \(^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) and \(^{3}_{2}\mathrm{He}\)
Isotones Same \(N = A - Z\), different \(Z\) Different elements sharing the same neutron number \(^{198}_{80}\mathrm{Hg}\) and \(^{197}_{79}\mathrm{Au}\) (\(N = 118\))

Nuclear Size and Constant Nuclear Density

In Rutherford scattering with \(5.5\,\mathrm{MeV}\) \(\alpha\)-particles on gold foil, the distance of closest approach under pure Coulomb repulsion is about \(4.0 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m}\), placing an upper bound on nuclear size. Using higher-energy \(\alpha\)-particles or fast electron scattering (which probes the charge distribution accurately), nuclear radii are found to follow the empirical relation:

Nuclear Radius and Volume
\[ R = R_0 A^{1/3}, \quad \text{where } R_0 = 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m} = 1.2\,\mathrm{fm} \]

Since nuclear volume \(V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A\) is directly proportional to the mass number \(A\), and the nuclear mass is approximately \(A \times m_{\text{nucleon}}\), the mass number \(A\) cancels out when calculating density:

\[ \rho = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}} \approx \frac{A \, m_{\text{nucleon}}}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A} = \frac{3 \, m_{\text{nucleon}}}{4\pi R_0^3} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \]

Thus, nuclear matter density is constant and independent of \(A\), resembling an incompressible liquid drop. This extreme density—about \(10^{14}\) times the density of water (\(10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\))—is comparable to the density inside astrophysical neutron stars.

Mass-Energy Equivalence, Mass Defect, and Binding Energy

Einstein’s special theory of relativity unifies mass and energy through \(E = mc^2\) (with \(c \approx 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)). Converting just \(1\,\mathrm{g}\) (\(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) of matter yields \(9 \times 10^{13}\,\mathrm{J}\) of energy. In any nuclear process, it is the total mass-energy that is conserved.

Mass Defect (\(\Delta M\)) and Binding Energy (\(E_b\))

Precise mass spectrometry shows that the actual mass \(M\) of a bound nucleus is always less than the sum of the masses of its \(Z\) free protons and \((A - Z)\) free neutrons. This difference is the mass defect:

Mass Defect, Binding Energy, and Binding Energy per Nucleon
\[ \Delta M = \left[ Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n \right] - M \] \[ E_b = \Delta M c^2 = \Delta M(\text{in u}) \times 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV} \] \[ E_{bn} = \frac{E_b}{A} \]

The binding energy (\(E_b\)) is the energy released when \(Z\) protons and \((A - Z)\) neutrons assemble to form the nucleus—or equivalently, the minimum energy required to completely separate the nucleus into its constituent free nucleons. A more direct measure of nuclear stability is the binding energy per nucleon (\(E_{bn}\)).

Key Deductions from the Binding Energy Curve

  • Nearly Constant Plateau (\(30 < A < 170\)): \(E_{bn}\) stays close to \(8.0\,\mathrm{MeV}\), reaching a maximum of \(8.75\,\mathrm{MeV}\) at \(A = 56\) (\(^{56}\mathrm{Fe}\)). This constancy arises because the nuclear force is short-ranged and saturates—each interior nucleon interacts only with its immediate neighbors, so adding more nucleons to a large nucleus does not raise \(E_{bn}\).
  • Light Nuclei (\(A < 30\)) & Shell Peaks: \(E_{bn}\) is generally lower, though nuclides like \(^{4}\mathrm{He}\), \(^{12}\mathrm{C}\), and \(^{16}\mathrm{O}\) show distinct local peaks, indicating extra stability (shell-like nuclear structure). Combining very light nuclei (\(A \le 10\)) into a heavier nucleus increases \(E_{bn}\), releasing energy (nuclear fusion).
  • Heavy Nuclei (\(A > 170\)): \(E_{bn}\) gradually decreases (dropping to \(7.6\,\mathrm{MeV}\) at \(A = 238\)) due to increasing Coulomb repulsion among protons. Splitting a heavy nucleus into two intermediate-mass fragments raises \(E_{bn}\), releasing energy (nuclear fission).

The Strong Nuclear Force

To hold positively charged protons and neutral neutrons together within a femtometre-scale volume despite strong electrostatic repulsion, a distinct fundamental interaction—the strong nuclear force—must act inside the nucleus. Its experimentally established properties are:

  1. Extreme Strength: It is much stronger than both the Coulomb force and the gravitational force (which is negligible at nuclear scales).
  2. Short Range and Saturation: The force drops rapidly to zero beyond a few femtometres (\(2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{fm}\)). Consequently, nucleons only interact with nearest neighbors, causing force saturation and nearly constant \(E_{bn}\) in medium and heavy nuclei.
  3. Attractive and Repulsive Regimes: The potential energy \(V(r)\) between two nucleons reaches a minimum at \(r_0 \approx 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\). For \(r > 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\), the force is attractive; for \(r < 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\), it becomes strongly repulsive, preventing the nucleus from collapsing.
  4. Charge Independence: The nuclear force between \(n\text{–}n\), \(p\text{–}n\), and \(p\text{–}p\) pairs is approximately identical, independent of electric charge.

Radioactivity and Nuclear Energy (Fission & Fusion)

1. Nuclear Stability and Types of Radioactive Decay

Nuclear stability depends on the neutron-to-proton (\(N/Z\)) ratio: light stable nuclei have \(N/Z \approx 1:1\), whereas heavy stable nuclei require more neutrons to offset proton-proton repulsion, reaching \(N/Z \approx 3:2\). Only about 10% of known isotopes are stable; unstable nuclei undergo radioactive decay (discovered by A. H. Becquerel in 1896) in three natural modes:

  • \(\alpha\)-decay: Emission of a helium nucleus (\(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\)).
  • \(\beta\)-decay: Emission of high-speed electrons (\(e^-\)) or positrons (\(e^+\), the antiparticle of the electron with identical mass and opposite charge).
  • \(\gamma\)-decay: Emission of high-energy electromagnetic photons (hundreds of \(\mathrm{keV}\) or more) with wavelengths shorter than X-rays.

2. The \(Q\)-Value of a Nuclear Process

For any nuclear reaction or decay \(A + b \rightarrow C + d\), the disintegration energy or \(Q\)-value is the net kinetic energy released, which equals the mass difference converted into energy:

\(Q\)-Value Formula
\[ Q = K_{\text{final}} - K_{\text{initial}} = \left( \sum m_{\text{initial}} - \sum m_{\text{final}} \right) c^2 \]

If \(Q > 0\), the process is exothermic (energy is released). If \(Q < 0\), it is endothermic (energy must be supplied). In every nuclear reaction, both the total proton number (charge) and total nucleon number are conserved, even though elements may transmute.

3. Nuclear Fission

When a heavy nucleus such as \(^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) absorbs a neutron, it forms an unstable compound nucleus (\(^{236}_{92}\mathrm{U}\)) that splits into two intermediate-mass fragments and 2 to 4 neutrons, for example:

\[ ^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{236}_{92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{144}_{56}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{89}_{36}\mathrm{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \]

Because \(E_{bn}\) increases from \(\approx 7.6\,\mathrm{MeV}\) (for \(A \approx 240\)) to \(\approx 8.5\,\mathrm{MeV}\) (for \(A \approx 120\)), the gain of \(\approx 0.9\,\mathrm{MeV}\) per nucleon releases roughly \(200\,\mathrm{MeV}\) per fissioning uranium nucleus. Fission of \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) of uranium yields \(\sim 10^{14}\,\mathrm{J}\)—a million times more than burning \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) of coal (\(\sim 10^{7}\,\mathrm{J}\)). Controlled fission powers nuclear reactors, whereas uncontrolled fission drives nuclear weapons.

4. Thermonuclear Fusion

In nuclear fusion, two light nuclei combine to form a heavier, more tightly bound nucleus. Because both nuclei are positively charged, they must overcome a strong electrostatic Coulomb barrier (about \(400\,\mathrm{keV}\) for two protons, corresponding to an average thermal temperature \(T \sim 3 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{K}\)) to come within nuclear-force range. Achieving fusion via high temperature is called thermonuclear fusion.

Inside the Sun’s core (\(T \approx 1.5 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{K}\)), protons with energies well above average fuse via the multi-step proton-proton (\(p\text{–}p\)) cycle, whose net result is:

\[ 4\,{}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H} + 2e^{-} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + 2\nu + 6\gamma + 26.7\,\mathrm{MeV} \]

Once core hydrogen is exhausted (after roughly 5 billion more years for the Sun), gravitational contraction heats the core to \(\sim 10^8\,\mathrm{K}\), enabling helium fusion into carbon as the outer envelope expands into a red giant. On Earth, controlled thermonuclear fusion aims to confine high-temperature plasma (\(\sim 10^8\,\mathrm{K}\)) to generate clean, steady power.

Worked Examples

Calculating Average Atomic Mass from Isotopes

Problem: A hypothetical element \(\mathrm{M}\) has two naturally occurring isotopes: \(^{63}\mathrm{M}\) of atomic mass \(62.93\,\mathrm{u}\) with relative abundance \(70.0\%\), and \(^{65}\mathrm{M}\) of atomic mass \(64.93\,\mathrm{u}\) with relative abundance \(30.0\%\) (assumed values for illustration). Find the average atomic mass of element \(\mathrm{M}\).

Solution:

\[ \text{Average mass} = \frac{(70.0 \times 62.93) + (30.0 \times 64.93)}{100} = 44.051 + 19.479 = 63.53\,\mathrm{u} \]

Comparing Nuclear Radii and Density

Problem: Find the ratio of the nuclear radii and nuclear densities of \(^{27}_{13}\mathrm{Al}\) and \(^{125}_{52}\mathrm{Te}\).

Solution: Using \(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\):

\[ \frac{R_{\mathrm{Al}}}{R_{\mathrm{Te}}} = \left(\frac{27}{125}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.60 \]

Because nuclear density \(\rho = \frac{3 m_{\text{nucleon}}}{4\pi R_0^3}\) is independent of \(A\), the ratio of their nuclear densities is \(1 : 1\).

Determining Mass Defect and Binding Energy per Nucleon

Problem: A carbon-12 nucleus (\(^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}\)) has a nuclear mass of \(11.99670\,\mathrm{u}\). Given \(m_p = 1.00727\,\mathrm{u}\), \(m_n = 1.00866\,\mathrm{u}\), and \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\), calculate its mass defect, total binding energy, and binding energy per nucleon.

Solution: Here \(Z = 6\) and \(N = 12 - 6 = 6\).

\[ \sum m_{\text{nucleons}} = 6(1.00727) + 6(1.00866) = 6.04362 + 6.05196 = 12.09558\,\mathrm{u} \] \[ \Delta M = 12.09558 - 11.99670 = 0.09888\,\mathrm{u} \] \[ E_b = 0.09888 \times 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx 92.11\,\mathrm{MeV} \] \[ E_{bn} = \frac{E_b}{12} = \frac{92.11}{12} \approx 7.68\,\mathrm{MeV/nucleon} \]

Evaluating the \(Q\)-Value of a Nuclear Fusion Reaction

Problem: Calculate the \(Q\)-value for the fusion reaction \(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n}\) using the atomic masses \(m(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}) = 2.014102\,\mathrm{u}\), \(m(^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}) = 3.016049\,\mathrm{u}\), \(m(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}) = 4.002603\,\mathrm{u}\), and \(m_n = 1.008665\,\mathrm{u}\).

Solution: Since both sides have 2 electrons, atomic masses can be used directly:

\[ \sum m_{\text{initial}} = 2.014102 + 3.016049 = 5.030151\,\mathrm{u} \] \[ \sum m_{\text{final}} = 4.002603 + 1.008665 = 5.011268\,\mathrm{u} \] \[ \Delta m = 5.030151 - 5.011268 = 0.018883\,\mathrm{u} \] \[ Q = 0.018883 \times 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV} \approx +17.59\,\mathrm{MeV} \]

Since \(Q > 0\), the reaction is exothermic.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following pairs of nuclides represents a pair of isotones?

  • (A) \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(^{16}_{8}\mathrm{O}\) and \(^{18}_{8}\mathrm{O}\)
  • (C) \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{16}_{8}\mathrm{O}\)
  • (D) \(^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}\) and \(^{23}_{12}\mathrm{Mg}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Isotones have the same neutron number \(N = A - Z\). For \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\), \(N = 14 - 6 = 8\). For \(^{16}_{8}\mathrm{O}\), \(N = 16 - 8 = 8\). Pair (A) are isobars, (B) are isotopes, and (D) have \(N = 12\) and \(N = 11\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If the nuclear radius of \(^{64}_{30}\mathrm{Zn}\) is \(R\), the approximate nuclear radius of \(^{27}_{13}\mathrm{Al}\) is:

  • (A) \(0.42\,R\)
  • (B) \(0.60\,R\)
  • (C) \(0.75\,R\)
  • (D) \(1.33\,R\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Using \(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\), we get \(\frac{R_{\mathrm{Al}}}{R_{\mathrm{Zn}}} = \left(\frac{27}{64}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75\), so \(R_{\mathrm{Al}} = 0.75\,R\).

Practice Question 3 (Assertion-Reason)

Assertion (A): The density of nuclear matter is nearly the same for both \(^{16}_{8}\mathrm{O}\) and \(^{208}_{82}\mathrm{Pb}\).
Reason (R): The radius of a nucleus is directly proportional to the cube root of its mass number \(A\).

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Since \(R \propto A^{1/3}\), nuclear volume \(V \propto R^3 \propto A\). Because nuclear mass is also proportional to \(A\), the density \(\rho = M/V\) becomes independent of \(A\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If \(2\,\mathrm{mg}\) of matter is completely converted into energy, the total energy released is:

  • (A) \(1.8 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(9.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(1.8 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(6.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Using \(E = mc^2\) with \(m = 2 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(c = 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(E = (2 \times 10^{-6}) \times (3 \times 10^8)^2 = 1.8 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The binding energy per nucleon of \(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) is \(1.1\,\mathrm{MeV}\) and that of \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\) is \(7.0\,\mathrm{MeV}\). When two \(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) nuclei fuse to form one \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\) nucleus, the energy released is:

  • (A) \(5.9\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (B) \(11.8\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (C) \(23.6\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
  • (D) \(28.0\,\mathrm{MeV}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Initial total binding energy \(= 2 \times (2 \times 1.1) = 4.4\,\mathrm{MeV}\). Final total binding energy \(= 4 \times 7.0 = 28.0\,\mathrm{MeV}\). Energy released \(= 28.0 - 4.4 = 23.6\,\mathrm{MeV}\).

Practice Question 6 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Statement I: Inside a nucleus, the strong nuclear force between two protons is significantly weaker than that between two neutrons at the same separation.
Statement II: The nuclear force exhibits saturation because it is short-ranged and acts only over a few femtometres.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  • (C) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
Answer: (C)

Solution: The strong nuclear force is charge-independent (\(p\text{–}p\), \(p\text{–}n\), and \(n\text{–}n\) nuclear interactions are approximately equal), making Statement I false. Statement II is true and explains why \(E_{bn}\) is nearly constant for \(30 < A < 170\).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When two nucleons are separated by a distance of \(0.5\,\mathrm{fm}\) (less than \(r_0 \approx 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\)), the nuclear force between them is:

  • (A) Strongly attractive
  • (B) Strongly repulsive
  • (C) Zero
  • (D) Purely gravitational
Answer: (B)

Solution: The nucleon-nucleon potential energy curve has a minimum at \(r_0 \approx 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\). For separations \(r < r_0\), the potential rises steeply as \(r\) decreases, meaning the force is strongly repulsive.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A free neutron is unstable (mean life \(\approx 1000\,\mathrm{s}\)) and decays into:

  • (A) A proton, a positron, and a neutrino
  • (B) A proton, an electron, and an antineutrino
  • (C) A deuteron and a \(\gamma\)-photon
  • (D) Two \(\alpha\)-particles
Answer: (B)

Solution: A free neutron undergoes \(\beta^-\)-decay into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino, conserving charge and nucleon number.

Practice Question 9 (Numerical MCQ)

A fission reactor generates \(900\,\mathrm{MW}\) of power using \(^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\), where each fission releases \(200\,\mathrm{MeV}\). Approximately how many fissions occur per second? (Take \(1\,\mathrm{MeV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}\))

  • (A) \(2.8 \times 10^{19}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(1.4 \times 10^{18}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(5.6 \times 10^{20}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(9.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Energy per fission \(= 200 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-13} = 3.2 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{J}\). Power \(P = 900 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}\). Fission rate \(= \frac{9 \times 10^8}{3.2 \times 10^{-11}} \approx 2.81 \times 10^{19}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Thermonuclear fusion of light nuclei requires extremely high temperatures (\(10^7\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(10^9\,\mathrm{K}\)) primarily to:

  • (A) Ionize all neutrons inside the nuclei
  • (B) Provide enough kinetic energy to overcome the electrostatic Coulomb repulsion barrier
  • (C) Decrease the mass defect of the product nucleus
  • (D) Convert protons into neutrons before collision
Answer: (B)

Solution: Positively charged light nuclei repel each other via the Coulomb force. High thermal kinetic energy allows them to overcome this barrier and come within the femtometre range of the attractive nuclear force.

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

  • Using Atomic vs. Nuclear Mass in Mass Defect: If you use the atomic mass of a neutral atom in \(\Delta M\), remember to use \(Z m_{\mathrm{H}}\) (mass of \(Z\) hydrogen atoms) instead of \(Z m_p\), so that electron masses cancel out cleanly.
  • Confusing Total Binding Energy with \(E_{bn}\): In reaction energy calculations, always multiply \(E_{bn}\) by the mass number \(A\) to get total binding energy before subtracting initial from final values.
  • Assuming Nuclear Density Depends on \(A\): Nuclear density is \(\approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) for all nuclei; only nuclear radius scales as \(A^{1/3}\).
  • Sign of \(Q\)-Value: \(Q = (\sum m_{\text{initial}} - \sum m_{\text{final}})c^2\), which is positive for exothermic reactions (mass decreases, total binding energy increases).

Concise Final Recap

  • \(R = R_0 A^{1/3}\) (\(R_0 = 1.2\,\mathrm{fm}\)) ⇒ nuclear density is constant (\(\sim 2.3 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)).
  • \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 931.5\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2\).
  • \(E_b = \Delta M c^2\), peaking in \(E_{bn}\) at \(^{56}\mathrm{Fe}\) (\(8.75\,\mathrm{MeV}\)).
  • Nuclear force: strongest fundamental force, charge-independent, short-ranged, saturating, repulsive for \(r < 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\), attractive for \(r > 0.8\,\mathrm{fm}\).
  • Fission (heavy nuclei splitting, \(\sim 200\,\mathrm{MeV}\) per \(^{235}\mathrm{U}\)) and fusion (light nuclei combining, \(26.7\,\mathrm{MeV}\) per \(^{4}\mathrm{He}\) in the Sun’s \(p\text{–}p\) cycle) both move products toward higher \(E_{bn}\).
