Learning Objectives and Overview

Modern electronic technology relies on solid-state semiconductor devices rather than bulky, high-voltage vacuum tubes. Unlike vacuum valves that require a heated cathode and evacuated space, semiconductor devices control charge carrier density and direction directly inside a crystalline solid using small voltages, heat, or light.

What You Will Master

  • Distinguish metals, semiconductors, and insulators using electrical resistivity and energy band gaps (\(E_g\)).
  • Explain conduction in intrinsic semiconductors via thermally generated electrons and holes.
  • Analyze n-type and p-type extrinsic semiconductors formed by pentavalent and trivalent doping, using \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\).
  • Describe p-n junction formation, depletion region, barrier potential, and forward/reverse biasing characteristics.
  • Evaluate half-wave and full-wave diode rectifier circuits, output frequencies, and capacitor filtering.

Classification of Solids: Conductivity and Energy Band Theory

Solids are broadly grouped into metals, semiconductors, and insulators based on their electrical resistivity (\(\rho\)) or conductivity (\(\sigma = 1/\rho\)). However, the microscopic reason for this difference lies in how atomic orbitals merge into continuous energy bands inside a crystal.

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When \(N\) atoms pack closely in a crystal lattice, outer electron orbitals overlap, splitting discrete levels into bands of closely spaced states. In tetravalent crystals like Silicon (Si) or Germanium (Ge), the \(4N\) valence electrons completely fill the lower valence band (top energy \(E_V\)) at \(0\,\mathrm{K}\), while the upper conduction band (bottom energy \(E_C\)) with \(4N\) states remains completely empty. The forbidden region between them is the energy band gap \(E_g = E_C - E_V\).

Material Class Resistivity \(\rho\) (\(\mathrm{Ω\,m}\)) Conductivity \(\sigma\) (\(\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\)) Energy Band Structure
Metals \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^{-8}\) \(10^{2}\) to \(10^{8}\) Conduction & valence bands overlap (\(E_g \approx 0\)), or conduction band is partially filled.
Semiconductors \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{6}\) \(10^{5}\) to \(10^{-6}\) Small finite gap: \(0.2\,\mathrm{eV} < E_g < 3\,\mathrm{eV}\) (e.g., Ge: \(0.7\,\mathrm{eV}\), Si: \(1.1\,\mathrm{eV}\)).
Insulators \(10^{11}\) to \(10^{19}\) \(10^{-11}\) to \(10^{-19}\) Large band gap: \(E_g > 3\,\mathrm{eV}\) (e.g., Diamond C: \(5.4\,\mathrm{eV}\)). Thermal excitation fails at room temperature.

Semiconductor Categories: Elemental semiconductors include Silicon (Si) and Germanium (Ge). Compound semiconductors include inorganic compounds (GaAs, CdS, CdSe, InP), organic compounds (anthracene, doped phthalocyanines), and conducting polymers (polypyrrole, polyaniline, polythiophene). Notice also that Group IV elements show a clear trend: Carbon (diamond) is an insulator (\(E_g = 5.4\,\mathrm{eV}\)), Si (\(1.1\,\mathrm{eV}\)) and Ge (\(0.7\,\mathrm{eV}\)) are semiconductors, while Tin (Sn) is a metal (\(E_g = 0\,\mathrm{eV}\)).

Intrinsic Semiconductors and Charge Carrier Dynamics

Pure Si and Ge crystals (lattice constants \(5.43\,\mathrm{Å}\) and \(5.66\,\mathrm{Å}\), respectively) crystallize in a diamond-like tetrahedral structure where each atom shares one valence electron with each of its four nearest neighbours to form four covalent bonds. At \(T = 0\,\mathrm{K}\), all covalent bonds remain intact and an intrinsic semiconductor behaves as a perfect insulator.

For \(T > 0\,\mathrm{K}\), thermal energy breaks a small fraction of covalent bonds, freeing electrons into the conduction band. Each freed electron (charge \(-q\)) leaves behind an empty bond state with an effective positive charge (\(+q\)), called a hole. When an electron from an adjacent covalent bond jumps to fill a hole, the vacancy shifts to the neighbouring site—making the hole behave as a mobile positive charge carrier.

\[ n_e = n_h = n_i \qquad \text{and} \qquad I = I_e + I_h \]

Here \(n_i\) is the intrinsic carrier concentration, and total current \(I\) under an applied electric field is the sum of electron current \(I_e\) and hole current \(I_h\). Simultaneously, free electrons collide with holes in a process called recombination; at thermal equilibrium, the rate of generation equals the rate of recombination.

Extrinsic Semiconductors: Doping, n-Type and p-Type Materials

Because pure semiconductors have very low conductivity at room temperature, we deliberately add a tiny, controlled amount (a few parts per million, ppm) of a compatible impurity atom—a process called doping. Dopant atoms must have nearly the same atomic size as Si or Ge so they substitute into lattice sites without distorting the crystal.

  • n-type Semiconductor (Pentavalent Donor): Doping Si or Ge with Group 15 elements such as Phosphorus (P), Arsenic (As), or Antimony (Sb) provides 5 valence electrons. Four form covalent bonds, while the fifth electron is very weakly bound (ionization energy \(\approx 0.01\,\mathrm{eV}\) for Ge and \(\approx 0.05\,\mathrm{eV}\) for Si). The donor energy level \(E_D\) lies just below \(E_C\), so nearly all donors ionize at room temperature. Electrons become majority carriers and holes become minority carriers (\(n_e \gg n_h\)).
  • p-type Semiconductor (Trivalent Acceptor): Doping with Group 13 elements such as Boron (B), Aluminium (Al), or Indium (In) leaves one covalent bond incomplete, creating a hole. The acceptor energy level \(E_A\) lies just above \(E_V\). Electrons from the valence band easily jump to \(E_A\), leaving mobile holes in the valence band and negatively charged immobile acceptor cores. Holes become majority carriers and electrons become minority carriers (\(n_h \gg n_e\)).
\[ n_e n_h = n_i^2 \qquad \text{(Mass-Action Law at Thermal Equilibrium)} \]

Crucial Principle: Both n-type and p-type semiconductors remain electrically neutral overall because the charge of mobile majority carriers is exactly balanced by the opposite charge of the fixed ionized donor (\(+ \)) or acceptor (\(- \)) cores. Moreover, increasing majority carriers increases the recombination rate, which suppresses the minority carrier concentration far below \(n_i\).

The p-n Junction and Semiconductor Diode Characteristics

A p-n junction cannot be formed by physically pressing a p-type slab against an n-type slab because surface roughness far exceeds interatomic crystal spacing (\(\sim 2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{Å}\)). Instead, it is fabricated within a single crystal wafer by diffusing pentavalent impurities into part of a p-type wafer.

Diffusion, Drift, and Depletion Region: Because of the sharp concentration gradient across the metallurgical junction, holes diffuse from p to n (\(p \to n\)) and electrons diffuse from n to p (\(n \to p\)), creating a diffusion current. As carriers cross, they leave behind immobile ionized acceptor (\(- \)) cores on the p-side and immobile ionized donor (\(+ \)) cores on the n-side. This charge-depleted zone (thickness \(\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{µm}\)) is the depletion region. Its internal electric field (directed from n to p) sweeps minority carriers across the junction, producing an opposing drift current. At equilibrium, diffusion current equals drift current, net current is zero, and a built-in barrier potential \(V_0\) opposes further majority carrier flow.

Forward Bias vs. Reverse Bias

  • Forward Bias (p connected to \(+\), n to \(-\)): The external voltage \(V\) opposes \(V_0\), reducing the effective barrier height to \((V_0 - V)\) and narrowing the depletion width. Majority carriers cross the junction in a process called minority carrier injection, producing a large diffusion current (in \(\mathrm{mA}\)). Current remains negligible until \(V\) exceeds the threshold (cut-in) voltage (\(\sim 0.2\,\mathrm{V}\) for Ge, \(\sim 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) for Si), after which it rises exponentially.
  • Reverse Bias (p connected to \(-\), n to \(+\)): The external voltage aids \(V_0\), raising the effective barrier to \((V_0 + V)\) and widening the depletion layer. Diffusion current drops virtually to zero. Only a tiny, nearly voltage-independent reverse saturation drift current (in \(\mathrm{µA}\)) flows due to minority carriers. At a critical breakdown voltage (\(V_{br}\)), reverse current increases sharply.
\[ r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} \qquad \text{(Dynamic Resistance of a Diode)} \]

Junction Diode as a Rectifier and Filter Circuits

Because a diode conducts easily in forward bias and blocks current in reverse bias, it converts alternating current (ac) into unidirectional pulsating current—a process called rectification.

  • Half-Wave Rectifier: A single diode in series with a load resistor \(R_L\) conducts only during the positive half-cycles of the ac input. Thus, the output fundamental frequency equals the input frequency: \(f_{\text{out}} = f_{\text{in}}\).
  • Full-Wave Centre-Tap Rectifier: Two diodes (\(D_1\) and \(D_2\)) connect to opposite ends of a centre-tapped transformer secondary, with their n-sides joined to \(R_L\). During the positive half-cycle at one end, \(D_1\) conducts; during the negative half-cycle, the other end becomes positive and \(D_2\) conducts. Both half-cycles produce unidirectional pulses through \(R_L\), doubling the fundamental ripple frequency: \(f_{\text{out}} = 2 f_{\text{in}}\).
  • Capacitor Input Filter: To smooth pulsating dc into steady dc, a large capacitor \(C\) is connected in parallel with \(R_L\). As rectified voltage rises, \(C\) charges to the peak voltage. When rectified voltage falls, \(C\) discharges slowly through \(R_L\) with time constant \(\tau = R_L C\). A large \(R_L C\) keeps the output voltage close to the peak value.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Minority Carrier Concentration in Doped Silicon

Problem (Hypothetical Assumption): A pure Silicon crystal has intrinsic carrier concentration \(n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) at room temperature. It is uniformly doped with Boron (trivalent acceptor) at an assumed concentration of \(N_A = 3.0 \times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). Identify the semiconductor type and calculate the electron concentration \(n_e\).

Solution:

1. Boron is a Group 13 trivalent impurity, so the resulting material is a p-type semiconductor with holes as majority carriers.

2. Since \(N_A = 3.0 \times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \gg n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\), nearly all holes come from ionized acceptors: \[ n_h \approx N_A = 3.0 \times 10^{21}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \]

3. Applying the mass-action law \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\): \[ n_e = \frac{n_i^2}{n_h} = \frac{(1.5 \times 10^{16})^2}{3.0 \times 10^{21}} = \frac{2.25 \times 10^{32}}{3.0 \times 10^{21}} = 7.5 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \]

Example 2: Determining Diode Dynamic Resistance and Reverse Resistance

Problem (Hypothetical Assumption): For a Silicon junction diode, the forward current increases from \(12\,\mathrm{mA}\) at \(0.72\,\mathrm{V}\) to \(22\,\mathrm{mA}\) at \(0.80\,\mathrm{V}\). Under reverse bias of \(-8.0\,\mathrm{V}\), the reverse saturation current is \(2.0\,\mathrm{µA}\). Calculate the dynamic forward resistance and the static reverse resistance.

Solution:

1. Dynamic forward resistance: \[ r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} = \frac{0.80\,\mathrm{V} - 0.72\,\mathrm{V}}{(22 - 12)\,\mathrm{mA}} = \frac{0.08\,\mathrm{V}}{10 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A}} = 8\,\mathrm{Ω} \]

2. Static reverse resistance: \[ R_{rb} = \frac{|V|}{|I|} = \frac{8.0\,\mathrm{V}}{2.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{A}} = 4.0 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{Ω} \]

Example 3: Analyzing Rectifier Output Frequency

Problem (Hypothetical Assumption): An ac mains supply of assumed frequency \(60\,\mathrm{Hz}\) is fed to (a) a half-wave rectifier and (b) a full-wave centre-tap rectifier. Find the fundamental ripple frequency of the unfiltered output in each case.

Solution:

(a) In a half-wave rectifier, only one pulse appears per complete input cycle, so: \[ f_{\text{out}} = f_{\text{in}} = 60\,\mathrm{Hz} \]

(b) In a full-wave rectifier, two unidirectional pulses appear per input cycle (one from each diode), so: \[ f_{\text{out}} = 2 f_{\text{in}} = 2 \times 60\,\mathrm{Hz} = 120\,\mathrm{Hz} \]

  • (D) \(8.0 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)

उत्तर: (A)

हल: \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\) का उपयोग करने पर: \[ n_h = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^{16})^2}{4.0 \times 10^{22}} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{32}}{4.0 \times 10^{22}} = 1.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \]

Practice Question 3 (Assertion-Reason):

Assertion (A): When a p-n junction diode is reverse biased, the thickness of the depletion region increases.

Reason (R): Under reverse bias, the applied external voltage is in the same direction as the built-in barrier potential.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.

Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: In reverse bias (p to negative, n to positive), the external voltage aids the built-in barrier potential \(V_0\), raising the effective barrier to \((V_0 + V)\). This pulls majority carriers further away from the junction, widening the depletion region. Thus both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A.

Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ): Read the following two statements regarding Group 14 elements:

Statement I: The energy band gap order for Diamond (C), Silicon (Si), and Germanium (Ge) is \((E_g)_{\text{C}} > (E_g)_{\text{Si}} > (E_g)_{\text{Ge}}\).

Statement II: In an unbiased p-n junction at equilibrium, the diffusion current is zero and the drift current is zero separately.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.

Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Statement I is correct because \(E_g\) values are \(5.4\,\mathrm{eV}\) (C), \(1.1\,\mathrm{eV}\) (Si), and \(0.7\,\mathrm{eV}\) (Ge). Statement II is incorrect because at equilibrium, neither diffusion current nor drift current is individually zero; rather, they are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, making the net current zero.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ): A full-wave centre-tap rectifier is supplied with a \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\) ac voltage. If a capacitor filter is connected across the load resistor \(R_L\), what is the fundamental ripple frequency of the rectified waveform before filtering, and how does a larger capacitance \(C\) affect the output?

  • (A) \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\); larger \(C\) increases the ripple amplitude.
  • (B) \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\); larger \(C\) increases the time constant \(R_L C\) and produces smoother dc.
  • (C) \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\); larger \(C\) decreases the time constant \(R_L C\) and drops the output voltage to zero.
  • (D) \(25\,\mathrm{Hz}\); larger \(C\) keeps the output at the average ac value.

Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: A full-wave rectifier produces two output pulses per input cycle, so \(f_{\text{out}} = 2 \times 50\,\mathrm{Hz} = 100\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Increasing \(C\) increases the discharge time constant \(\tau = R_L C\), so the capacitor discharges more slowly between peaks, yielding smoother dc near the peak voltage.

हिन्दी

अभ्यास प्रश्न 5 (एकल-सही बहुविकल्पीय प्रश्न): एक पूर्ण-तरंग मध्य-निष्कासित दिष्टकारी को \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\) ac वोल्टता दी जाती है। यदि लोड प्रतिरोध \(R_L\) के समानांतर एक संधारित्र फिल्टर जोड़ा जाए, तो फिल्टर से पहले दिष्टीकृत तरंग की मूल ऊर्मिका आवृत्ति क्या होगी, और अधिक धारिता \(C\) का निर्गत पर क्या प्रभाव पड़ेगा?

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Avoid These Common Exam Mistakes

  • Charge Neutrality Myth: Do not assume an n-type semiconductor is negatively charged or a p-type is positively charged. Both are electrically neutral.
  • Equilibrium Currents: At zero bias, diffusion and drift currents are equal and opposite—they are not individually zero.
  • Cut-in Voltage Values: Remember \(\sim 0.2\,\mathrm{V}\) for Germanium and \(\sim 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) for Silicon.
  • Rectifier Frequency Trap: Half-wave output frequency is \(f\), whereas full-wave output frequency is \(2f\).

Quick Revision Checklist

  • \(E_g = 0\) (metals), \(0.2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{eV}\) (semiconductors), \(> 3\,\mathrm{eV}\) (insulators).
  • Intrinsic: \(n_e = n_h = n_i\); Extrinsic: \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\).
  • Forward bias: barrier \((V_0 - V)\), narrow depletion layer, \(\mathrm{mA}\) current.
  • Reverse bias: barrier \((V_0 + V)\), wide depletion layer, \(\mathrm{µA}\) current.

Concise Final Recap

Semiconductors bridge the gap between conductors and insulators via a small energy band gap (\(E_g < 3\,\mathrm{eV}\)). Controlled doping with pentavalent or trivalent impurities tailors majority carrier type while preserving overall neutrality and obeying \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\). Forming a p-n junction creates a depletion barrier that allows unidirectional conduction under forward bias and blocks current under reverse bias—the fundamental principle enabling half-wave and full-wave rectification with capacitor-filtered dc power supplies.
