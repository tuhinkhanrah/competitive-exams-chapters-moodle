Semiconductor Electronics: Materials, Devices, and Rectifier Circuits

Energy bands, intrinsic and extrinsic conduction, p-n junction physics, diode characteristics, and rectification

Learning Objectives and Overview

Modern electronic hardware relies on solid-state semiconductor devices rather than bulky vacuum tubes. For NEET UG Physics, this chapter tests your conceptual command of band theory, carrier statistics under doping, p-n junction electrostatics, current-voltage characteristics, and rectifier circuits.

  • Distinguish metals, semiconductors, and insulators using electrical resistivity, conductivity, and energy band gaps.
  • Explain thermal generation, recombination, and the dual-carrier mechanism (electrons and holes) in intrinsic semiconductors.
  • Analyze how pentavalent (donor) and trivalent (acceptor) doping alters carrier concentrations while preserving overall charge neutrality.
  • Describe diffusion, drift, depletion region formation, and barrier potential in an unbiased and biased p-n junction.
  • Interpret forward and reverse \(V\text{–}I\) characteristics of a junction diode and compute dynamic resistance.
  • Compare half-wave and full-wave rectifiers, determine output frequencies, and explain capacitor input filtering.

1. Solid-State Electronics and Material Classification

Before transistors emerged in 1948, electronic control depended on vacuum tubes (valves) such as vacuum diodes and triodes. Those devices required a heated cathode to emit electrons into an evacuated space, operated near \(100\,\mathrm{V}\), consumed substantial power, and permitted conduction only from cathode to anode. Solid-state semiconductor devices eliminate the need for an external vacuum or cathode heater: charge carriers are generated and controlled directly inside a crystal lattice at low voltages and low power, offering high reliability and compact size.

Classification by Electrical Conductivity and Resistivity

Solids are grouped into three broad electrical categories based on their room-temperature resistivity (\(\rho\)) and conductivity (\(\sigma = 1/\rho\)).

Material Class Resistivity \(\rho\) (\(\mathrm{Ω\cdot m}\)) Conductivity \(\sigma\) (\(\mathrm{S\cdot m^{-1}}\)) Typical Examples
Metals (Conductors) \(10^{-2}\text{ to }10^{-8}\) \(10^{2}\text{ to }10^{8}\) Copper, Silver, Aluminium, Tin
Semiconductors \(10^{-5}\text{ to }10^{6}\) \(10^{5}\text{ to }10^{-6}\) Si, Ge (elemental); GaAs, CdS, InP (compound)
Insulators \(10^{11}\text{ to }10^{19}\) \(10^{-11}\text{ to }10^{-19}\) Diamond (C), Glass, Quartz

Semiconductors themselves are divided into elemental semiconductors (Silicon and Germanium) and compound semiconductors, which include inorganic compounds (\(\mathrm{CdS}\), \(\mathrm{GaAs}\), \(\mathrm{CdSe}\), \(\mathrm{InP}\)), organic solids (anthracene, doped phthalocyanines), and conducting polymers (polypyrrole, polyaniline, polythiophene).

Classification by Energy Band Theory

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When \(N\) atoms pack closely into a crystal lattice, outer electron orbitals overlap and each electron experiences a slightly different surrounding charge distribution. Consequently, discrete atomic levels broaden into continuous energy bands:

  • Valence Band: The lower energy band containing the energy levels of valence electrons; its highest energy edge is denoted \(E_V\).
  • Conduction Band: The higher energy band above the valence band; its lowest energy edge is denoted \(E_C\).
  • Energy Band Gap (\(E_g\)): The forbidden energy interval between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band, \(E_g = E_C - E_V\).

In a tetravalent crystal such as \(\mathrm{Si}\) (\(n=3\) outer shell) or \(\mathrm{Ge}\) (\(n=4\) outer shell) containing \(N\) atoms, there are \(4N\) valence electrons and \(8N\) available outer states (\(2s + 6p\)). At the equilibrium interatomic spacing of the crystal, these \(8N\) states split into a completely filled valence band of \(4N\) states and a completely empty conduction band of \(4N\) states at \(T = 0\,\mathrm{K}\).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Energy band alignment in metals, semiconductors, and insulators.

Notice why Group 14 elements behave differently despite sharing the same diamond-like crystal structure: the four bonding electrons occupy the \(n=2\) shell in Carbon (diamond), \(n=3\) in Silicon, and \(n=4\) in Germanium. Electrons closer to the nucleus are more tightly bound, so the band gap decreases down the group:

  • Diamond (C): \(E_g = 5.4\,\mathrm{eV}\) (lattice spacing \(a = 3.56\,\mathrm{Å}\)) — Insulator
  • Silicon (Si): \(E_g = 1.1\,\mathrm{eV}\) (lattice spacing \(a = 5.43\,\mathrm{Å}\)) — Semiconductor
  • Germanium (Ge): \(E_g = 0.7\,\mathrm{eV}\) (lattice spacing \(a = 5.66\,\mathrm{Å}\)) — Semiconductor
  • Tin (Sn): \(E_g = 0\,\mathrm{eV}\) — Metal

2. Intrinsic Semiconductors: Electrons, Holes, and Equilibrium

A chemically pure semiconductor crystal without significant lattice defects is called an intrinsic semiconductor. Each \(\mathrm{Si}\) or \(\mathrm{Ge}\) atom forms four covalent bonds with its four nearest neighbours. At absolute zero (\(T = 0\,\mathrm{K}\)), all covalent bonds remain intact, the valence band is completely full, the conduction band is completely empty, and the crystal behaves as a perfect insulator.

At temperatures above \(0\,\mathrm{K}\), thermal energy breaks a small fraction of covalent bonds, exciting electrons from the valence band into the conduction band. Every freed electron leaves behind an electron vacancy in a covalent bond, which behaves like a mobile particle of effective positive charge \(+q\) called a hole. A hole moves apparently through the lattice when a bound valence electron from a neighbouring bond jumps into the vacant site, shifting the vacancy in the opposite direction.

\[ n_e = n_h = n_i \qquad \text{and} \qquad I = I_e + I_h \]

Here \(n_e\) is the free electron concentration, \(n_h\) is the hole concentration, and \(n_i\) is the intrinsic carrier concentration. Under an external electric field, conduction electrons drift toward the positive potential (giving electron current \(I_e\)) while holes drift toward the negative potential (giving hole current \(I_h\)). Both currents add in the same conventional direction. Simultaneously, free electrons collide with holes and recombine; in thermal equilibrium, the rate of thermal generation equals the rate of recombination.

3. Doping and Extrinsic Semiconductors

Intrinsic semiconductors have very low conductivity at room temperature because \(n_i\) is small. Adding a controlled trace amount—typically a few parts per million (ppm)—of a specific impurity, called doping, boosts conductivity by orders of magnitude. To avoid distorting the host crystal lattice, the dopant atom must have nearly the same atomic size as \(\mathrm{Si}\) or \(\mathrm{Ge}\). Such doped materials are extrinsic semiconductors.

n-Type Semiconductors (Pentavalent Donors)

When a Group 15 pentavalent atom such as Phosphorus (\(\mathrm{P}\)), Arsenic (\(\mathrm{As}\)), or Antimony (\(\mathrm{Sb}\)) substitutes a \(\mathrm{Si}\) or \(\mathrm{Ge}\) atom, four valence electrons form covalent bonds with neighbours while the fifth electron remains weakly bound to the donor core. Only about \(0.01\,\mathrm{eV}\) (in \(\mathrm{Ge}\)) or \(0.05\,\mathrm{eV}\) (in \(\mathrm{Si}\)) is needed to ionize this fifth electron into the conduction band—far less than the intrinsic band gap. Thus, pentavalent impurities act as donors, creating a discrete donor energy level \(E_D\) just below \(E_C\).

Electrons become the majority carriers and holes the minority carriers (\(n_e \gg n_h\)). Because the abundance of electrons increases the probability of electron-hole recombination, the thermally generated hole concentration drops well below \(n_i\).

p-Type Semiconductors (Trivalent Acceptors)

When a Group 13 trivalent atom such as Boron (\(\mathrm{B}\)), Aluminium (\(\mathrm{Al}\)), or Indium (\(\mathrm{In}\)) substitutes a host atom, it forms three covalent bonds and leaves one bond incomplete—creating a hole. A nearby valence electron can jump into this incomplete bond with very little energy (\(0.01\text{–}0.05\,\mathrm{eV}\)), leaving behind a mobile hole in the valence band and turning the impurity into an immobile negatively charged acceptor core. This introduces an acceptor energy level \(E_A\) just above \(E_V\).

Holes become the majority carriers and electrons the minority carriers (\(n_h \gg n_e\)).

Tutorial Diagram 2: Impurity energy levels (ED and EA) in n-type and p-type semiconductors (filled dots = electrons, open circles = holes).
Key Law: Mass-Action Relation & Electrical Neutrality

In thermal equilibrium, the product of electron and hole concentrations in any semiconductor (intrinsic or extrinsic) equals the square of the intrinsic carrier concentration at that temperature:

\[ n_e n_h = n_i^2 \]

Furthermore, both n-type and p-type semiconductors remain electrically neutral overall because the charge of mobile majority carriers is exactly balanced by the opposite charge of the fixed ionized donor (\(+e\)) or acceptor (\(-e\)) cores in the lattice.

Property Intrinsic n-Type Extrinsic p-Type Extrinsic
Dopant Valency & Examples None (pure \(\mathrm{Si}\) or \(\mathrm{Ge}\)) Pentavalent (\(\mathrm{P, As, Sb}\)) Trivalent (\(\mathrm{B, Al, In}\))
Majority Carriers Equal (\(n_e = n_h = n_i\)) Electrons (\(n_e \approx N_D\)) Holes (\(n_h \approx N_A\))
Minority Carriers None (equal numbers) Holes (\(n_h = n_i^2 / n_e\)) Electrons (\(n_e = n_i^2 / n_h\))
Impurity Energy Level None Donor level \(E_D\) just below \(E_C\) Acceptor level \(E_A\) just above \(E_V\)
Net Crystal Charge Zero (neutral) Zero (neutral) Zero (neutral)

4. The p-n Junction and Semiconductor Diode

A p-n junction cannot be made by simply pressing a p-type slab against an n-type slab because even polished surfaces have microscopic roughness much larger than interatomic crystal spacing (\(2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{Å}\)), creating a discontinuity for charge carriers. Instead, a single crystal wafer (for example, p-type \(\mathrm{Si}\)) is doped selectively with pentavalent impurities on one side to form a continuous metallurgical junction.

Formation of the Depletion Region and Barrier Potential

Two opposing transport processes establish equilibrium across a newly formed p-n junction:

  1. Diffusion: Due to the steep concentration gradient across the junction, holes diffuse from the p-side to the n-side (\(p \rightarrow n\)) and electrons diffuse from the n-side to the p-side (\(n \rightarrow p\)), creating a diffusion current directed from p to n.
  2. Space-Charge Layer (Depletion Region): As electrons leave the n-side, they expose immobile positively charged donor ions near the junction; as holes leave the p-side, they expose immobile negatively charged acceptor ions. This region on both sides of the junction—depleted of mobile carriers—is the depletion region, typically \(\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{µm}\) thick.
  3. Drift and Equilibrium: The exposed positive and negative space charges establish an internal electric field \(\vec{E}\) pointing from the n-side toward the p-side. This field sweeps minority carriers across the junction (electrons from \(p \rightarrow n\), holes from \(n \rightarrow p\)), producing a drift current opposite to the diffusion current. Equilibrium is reached when the diffusion current equals the drift current, leaving zero net current and a built-in barrier potential \(V_0\) (n-side at higher potential than p-side).

Forward Bias vs. Reverse Bias

A semiconductor diode is a p-n junction with metallic contacts at both ends. In circuit symbols, the triangle points from the p-side (anode) to the n-side (cathode), showing the direction of conventional current under forward bias. Because the depletion layer has virtually no free carriers, almost the entire external voltage \(V\) drops across it.

Feature Forward Bias (\(p\) to \(+\), \(n\) to \(-\)) Reverse Bias (\(p\) to \(-\), \(n\) to \(+\))
Effective Barrier Height Decreases to \((V_0 - V)\) Increases to \((V_0 + V)\)
Depletion Layer Width Decreases (narrows) Increases (widens)
Dominant Mechanism Minority carrier injection & diffusion Drift of minority carriers
Current Magnitude Large, in milliamperes (\(\mathrm{mA}\)) Very small, in microamperes (\(\mathrm{µA}\))
Effective Resistance Low (typically tens of \(\mathrm{Ω}\)) Very high (typically \(\mathrm{MΩ}\) range)

V–I Characteristics and Dynamic Resistance

In forward bias, current remains negligible until the applied voltage exceeds the threshold (cut-in) voltage—approximately \(0.2\,\mathrm{V}\) for \(\mathrm{Ge}\) and \(0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) for \(\mathrm{Si}\)—after which current rises exponentially. In reverse bias, a tiny, nearly voltage-independent reverse saturation current (\(\sim \mathrm{µA}\)) flows due to minority carriers swept across the junction. If the reverse voltage reaches the breakdown voltage (\(V_{\text{br}}\)), current increases abruptly and can destroy an ordinary diode by overheating unless limited by an external resistor.

\[ r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} \qquad (\text{Dynamic resistance of a diode}) \]

5. Junction Diode as a Rectifier and Filtering

Because a p-n junction diode conducts readily in forward bias and blocks current in reverse bias, it converts bidirectional alternating current (AC) into unidirectional pulsating direct current (DC)—a process called rectification.

Half-Wave vs. Full-Wave Rectifier

  • Half-Wave Rectifier: A single diode in series with a load resistor \(R_L\) across a transformer secondary conducts only during the positive half-cycles when its anode is positive. The output consists of half-sinusoid pulses separated by zero-voltage gaps, so the fundamental output ripple frequency equals the input AC frequency (\(f_{\text{out}} = f_{\text{in}}\)).
  • Centre-Tap Full-Wave Rectifier: Two diodes \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) connect to opposite ends \(A\) and \(B\) of a centre-tapped transformer secondary, with their cathodes joined together to feed \(R_L\) relative to the centre tap. During one half-cycle, \(A\) is positive and \(B\) is negative, so \(D_1\) conducts while \(D_2\) is reverse-biased. During the next half-cycle, \(B\) becomes positive and \(D_2\) conducts while \(D_1\) is reverse-biased. Current flows through \(R_L\) in the same direction during both half-cycles, doubling the pulse frequency (\(f_{\text{out}} = 2f_{\text{in}}\)).
Tutorial Diagram 5: Centre-tap full-wave rectifier with parallel capacitor filter and corresponding waveforms.

Capacitor Input Filtering

To smooth pulsating rectified voltage into steady DC, a large capacitor \(C\) is connected in parallel across the load \(R_L\) (or an inductor in series with \(R_L\)). As the rectified voltage rises, the capacitor charges rapidly to the peak voltage. When the rectifier pulse falls, the diode becomes reverse-biased and the capacitor discharges slowly through \(R_L\) until the next pulse recharges it. The decay rate depends inversely on the time constant:

\[ \tau = R_L C \]

Choosing a large capacitance \(C\) makes \(\tau\) much larger than the period between pulses, keeping the DC output voltage nearly constant and close to the peak rectified voltage.

Worked Examples

Calculating Minority Carrier Concentration in Doped Silicon

Problem: Assume a pure silicon crystal has an atomic density of \(5.0 \times 10^{28}\,\mathrm{atoms\,m^{-3}}\) and an intrinsic carrier concentration of \(n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\) at room temperature. If it is doped with \(2\,\mathrm{ppm}\) (parts per million) of trivalent Indium, calculate the resulting majority and minority carrier concentrations.

Solution:

A doping level of \(2\,\mathrm{ppm}\) means \(2\) Indium atoms per \(10^6\) Silicon atoms. Thus, the acceptor concentration \(N_A\) is:

\[ N_A = \frac{2}{10^6} \times 5.0 \times 10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} = 1.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \]

Since \(N_A \gg n_i\), thermally generated holes are negligible compared to acceptor-generated holes, so the majority hole concentration is:

\[ n_h \approx N_A = 1.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \]

Using the mass-action law \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\) in thermal equilibrium, the minority electron concentration is:

\[ n_e = \frac{n_i^2}{n_h} = \frac{(1.5 \times 10^{16})^2}{1.0 \times 10^{23}} = \frac{2.25 \times 10^{32}}{1.0 \times 10^{23}} = 2.25 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \]

Determining Dynamic Resistance from Diode Characteristics

Problem: In a laboratory test on a hypothetical silicon p-n junction diode, the forward current increases linearly from \(12\,\mathrm{mA}\) at \(0.72\,\mathrm{V}\) to \(28\,\mathrm{mA}\) at \(0.80\,\mathrm{V}\). In reverse bias at \(-15\,\mathrm{V}\), the steady reverse current is \(-1.5\,\mathrm{µA}\). Calculate the forward dynamic resistance and the reverse DC resistance at \(-15\,\mathrm{V}\).

Solution:

For the forward bias region, the dynamic resistance is:

\[ r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} = \frac{0.80\,\mathrm{V} - 0.72\,\mathrm{V}}{(28 - 12) \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A}} = \frac{0.08}{16 \times 10^{-3}} = 5.0\,\mathrm{Ω} \]

For the reverse bias operating point at \(-15\,\mathrm{V}\):

\[ R_{\text{rev}} = \frac{|V|}{|I|} = \frac{15\,\mathrm{V}}{1.5 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{A}} = 1.0 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{Ω} = 10\,\mathrm{MΩ} \]

Analyzing Biasing and Barrier Potential in a p-n Junction

Problem: Suppose a silicon diode with a threshold (cut-in) voltage of \(0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) is connected in series with a \(430\,\mathrm{Ω}\) resistor across a \(5.0\,\mathrm{V}\) DC supply such that the p-side is connected to \(+5.0\,\mathrm{V}\) and the n-side (through the resistor) is connected to \(0\,\mathrm{V}\). Determine the state of bias and the current flowing through the circuit.

Solution:

Since the p-side is at a higher potential (\(+5.0\,\mathrm{V}\)) than the n-side (\(0\,\mathrm{V}\)), the diode is forward biased, and its effective potential barrier is reduced. Because the supply voltage exceeds the \(0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) cut-in voltage, the diode conducts strongly. Taking the forward voltage drop across the silicon diode as \(V_D = 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\), the voltage across the series resistor is:

\[ V_R = 5.0\,\mathrm{V} - 0.7\,\mathrm{V} = 4.3\,\mathrm{V} \]

The circuit current is:

\[ I = \frac{V_R}{R} = \frac{4.3\,\mathrm{V}}{430\,\mathrm{Ω}} = 0.010\,\mathrm{A} = 10\,\mathrm{mA} \]

Evaluating Output Frequency and Time Constant of a Rectifier Filter

Problem: An AC mains supply of frequency \(60\,\mathrm{Hz}\) (assumed for this example) feeds a centre-tapped full-wave rectifier connected to a load resistor \(R_L = 2.0\,\mathrm{kΩ}\) and a parallel filter capacitor \(C = 500\,\mathrm{µF}\). Find (a) the fundamental ripple frequency of the unfiltered rectified output and (b) the discharge time constant \(\tau\) of the filter.

Solution:

(a) In a full-wave rectifier, each half-cycle of the AC input produces one unidirectional output pulse. Therefore, the output ripple frequency is twice the input frequency:

\[ f_{\text{out}} = 2 f_{\text{in}} = 2 \times 60\,\mathrm{Hz} = 120\,\mathrm{Hz} \]

(b) The discharge time constant of the capacitor-resistor filter is:

\[ \tau = R_L C = (2.0 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{Ω}) \times (500 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{F}) = 1.0\,\mathrm{s} \]

Since \(\tau = 1.0\,\mathrm{s}\) is much larger than the pulse period \(T_{\text{out}} = 1/120\,\mathrm{s} \approx 8.33\,\mathrm{ms}\), the capacitor discharges very little between peaks, yielding a smooth DC output.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

For a pure semiconductor at room temperature, the band gap \(E_g\) and the relation between electron concentration \(n_e\) and hole concentration \(n_h\) satisfy:

  1. (1) \(E_g > 3\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(n_e = n_h\)
  2. (2) \(E_g < 3\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(n_e = n_h\)
  3. (3) \(E_g < 3\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(n_e \gg n_h\)
  4. (4) \(E_g \approx 0\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(n_e = n_h\)
Answer: (2)

Worked Solution: Semiconductors have a small finite forbidden energy gap \(E_g < 3\,\mathrm{eV}\) (typically \(0.2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{eV}\)). In a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor, every thermally excited electron leaves behind one hole in the valence band, so \(n_e = n_h = n_i\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following impurities, when doped into pure Germanium, will convert it into a p-type semiconductor?

  1. (1) Arsenic
  2. (2) Antimony
  3. (3) Indium
  4. (4) Phosphorus
Answer: (3)

Worked Solution: A p-type semiconductor is formed by doping a tetravalent host (\(\mathrm{Si}\) or \(\mathrm{Ge}\)) with a trivalent (Group 13) acceptor impurity such as Boron, Aluminium, or Indium. Arsenic, Antimony, and Phosphorus are pentavalent donors that produce n-type semiconductors.

Practice Question 3 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): An n-type semiconductor crystal carries a net negative electric charge because electrons are the majority carriers.
Reason (R): In an n-type semiconductor, each ionized pentavalent donor core carries an effective positive charge \(+e\) fixed in the crystal lattice.

  1. (1) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (2) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (3) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  4. (4) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Answer: (4)

Worked Solution: Assertion (A) is false because an n-type semiconductor is electrically neutral overall; each donor atom contributes one conduction electron (\(-e\)) and leaves behind one immobile positively charged donor ion (\(+e\)) in the lattice. Reason (R) is true.

Practice Question 4 (Numerical MCQ)

An intrinsic semiconductor has \(n_i = 2.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). When doped with a pentavalent impurity, the electron concentration becomes \(n_e = 4.0 \times 10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). What is the minority hole concentration at the same temperature?

  1. (1) \(1.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)
  2. (2) \(2.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)
  3. (3) \(8.0 \times 10^{38}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)
  4. (4) \(5.0 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\)
Answer: (1)

Worked Solution: By the mass-action law \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\):

\[ n_h = \frac{(2.0 \times 10^{16})^2}{4.0 \times 10^{22}} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{32}}{4.0 \times 10^{22}} = 1.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}} \]
Practice Question 5 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Statement I: When a p-n junction is unbiased, the diffusion current is zero because no carriers cross the junction.
Statement II: In an unbiased p-n junction at equilibrium, the diffusion current and drift current are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

  1. (1) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (2) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (3) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (4) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (4)

Worked Solution: Statement I is incorrect because carriers do cross the junction in an unbiased diode—it is only the net current that is zero. Statement II is correct: at equilibrium, the diffusion current (majority carriers) exactly balances the opposite drift current (minority carriers).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When a p-n junction diode is reverse biased, which of the following changes occur?

  1. (1) Barrier height decreases and depletion width decreases.
  2. (2) Barrier height increases and depletion width decreases.
  3. (3) Barrier height increases and depletion width increases.
  4. (4) Barrier height decreases and depletion width increases.
Answer: (3)

Worked Solution: Under reverse bias (p-side negative, n-side positive), the applied voltage aids the built-in barrier potential \(V_0\), increasing the effective barrier height to \((V_0 + V)\) and widening the depletion layer.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

For a silicon p-n junction diode, the forward voltage is changed from \(0.75\,\mathrm{V}\) to \(0.85\,\mathrm{V}\), causing the forward current to rise from \(10\,\mathrm{mA}\) to \(30\,\mathrm{mA}\). The dynamic resistance of the diode in this region is:

  1. (1) \(2.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  2. (2) \(5.0\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  3. (3) \(10\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  4. (4) \(20\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
Answer: (2)

Worked Solution: Dynamic resistance is \(r_d = \Delta V / \Delta I\):

\[ r_d = \frac{0.85\,\mathrm{V} - 0.75\,\mathrm{V}}{(30 - 10) \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{A}} = \frac{0.10}{0.020} = 5.0\,\mathrm{Ω} \]
Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If the energy band gaps of three Group 14 elements Diamond (\(\mathrm{C}\)), Silicon (\(\mathrm{Si}\)), and Germanium (\(\mathrm{Ge}\)) are \((E_g)_{\mathrm{C}}\), \((E_g)_{\mathrm{Si}}\), and \((E_g)_{\mathrm{Ge}}\) respectively, which ordering is correct?

  1. (1) \((E_g)_{\mathrm{Ge}} > (E_g)_{\mathrm{Si}} > (E_g)_{\mathrm{C}}\)
  2. (2) \((E_g)_{\mathrm{C}} > (E_g)_{\mathrm{Ge}} > (E_g)_{\mathrm{Si}}\)
  3. (3) \((E_g)_{\mathrm{C}} > (E_g)_{\mathrm{Si}} > (E_g)_{\mathrm{Ge}}\)
  4. (4) \((E_g)_{\mathrm{C}} = (E_g)_{\mathrm{Si}} = (E_g)_{\mathrm{Ge}}\)
Answer: (3)

Worked Solution: Outer bonding electrons lie in the \(n=2, 3, 4\) shells for \(\mathrm{C}, \mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{Ge}\) respectively. Tighter nuclear binding in smaller atoms gives larger band gaps: \((E_g)_{\mathrm{C}} = 5.4\,\mathrm{eV} > (E_g)_{\mathrm{Si}} = 1.1\,\mathrm{eV} > (E_g)_{\mathrm{Ge}} = 0.7\,\mathrm{eV}\).

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

An AC voltage of frequency \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\) is applied to a half-wave rectifier and separately to a full-wave rectifier. The fundamental output ripple frequencies in the two cases are respectively:

  1. (1) \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  2. (2) \(25\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  3. (3) \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  4. (4) \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
Answer: (3)

Worked Solution: A half-wave rectifier produces one pulse per full AC cycle, so \(f_{\text{out}} = f_{\text{in}} = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}\). A full-wave rectifier produces two pulses per full AC cycle (one from each half-cycle), so \(f_{\text{out}} = 2f_{\text{in}} = 100\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In a full-wave rectifier circuit with a capacitor input filter connected across a load resistor \(R_L\), what is the primary effect of increasing the capacitance \(C\)?

  1. (1) The time constant \(R_L C\) decreases, increasing the ripple in the output.
  2. (2) The time constant \(R_L C\) increases, keeping the output voltage steadier and closer to the peak value.
  3. (3) The output ripple frequency doubles from \(2f\) to \(4f\).
  4. (4) The diode conducts continuously throughout the entire cycle.
Answer: (2)

Worked Solution: Increasing \(C\) increases the discharge time constant \(\tau = R_L C\), so the capacitor loses very little charge through \(R_L\) between successive rectified peaks. This reduces AC ripple and keeps the DC output close to the peak rectified voltage.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Conceptual Traps
  • Trap 1 — Net charge of doped crystals: Neither n-type nor p-type semiconductors are charged; both are electrically neutral overall.
  • Trap 2 — Minority carrier reduction: Adding donor atoms increases \(n_e\) and decreases \(n_h\) below \(n_i\) because \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\) must hold in thermal equilibrium.
  • Trap 3 — Cut-in voltages: Remember \(\sim 0.2\,\mathrm{V}\) for Germanium and \(\sim 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) for Silicon diodes.
  • Trap 4 — Reverse current scale: Forward current is in \(\mathrm{mA}\) (majority carrier diffusion), whereas reverse saturation current is in \(\mathrm{µA}\) (minority carrier drift).
  • Trap 5 — Physical contact myth: Joining two separate p and n slabs does not create a p-n junction because surface roughness exceeds crystal lattice spacing (\(2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{Å}\)).

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Band gap thresholds: Metals (\(E_g \approx 0\) or overlapping bands), Semiconductors (\(E_g < 3\,\mathrm{eV}\)), Insulators (\(E_g > 3\,\mathrm{eV}\)).
  • Intrinsic relation: \(n_e = n_h = n_i\); total current \(I = I_e + I_h\).
  • Extrinsic relation: \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\); n-type uses Group 15 donors (\(E_D\) near \(E_C\)), p-type uses Group 13 acceptors (\(E_A\) near \(E_V\)).
  • Unbiased p-n junction: Diffusion current equals drift current; depletion width \(\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{µm}\); barrier potential \(V_0\).
  • Biasing: Forward bias lowers barrier to \((V_0 - V)\) and narrows depletion layer; reverse bias raises barrier to \((V_0 + V)\) and widens depletion layer.
  • Rectifiers: Half-wave \(f_{\text{out}} = f_{\text{in}}\); Full-wave \(f_{\text{out}} = 2f_{\text{in}}\); Capacitor filter time constant \(\tau = R_L C\).

Concise Final Recap

Semiconductor devices control charge flow inside a crystal lattice through energy band engineering and selective doping. Pure crystals carry equal densities of electrons and holes (\(n_i\)), while pentavalent or trivalent doping creates n-type or p-type materials governed by \(n_e n_h = n_i^2\). Combining p-type and n-type regions in a single crystal produces a p-n junction whose depletion barrier narrows under forward bias (allowing \(\mathrm{mA}\) conduction above the cut-in voltage) and widens under reverse bias (limiting flow to \(\mathrm{µA}\) drift until breakdown). This asymmetric conduction enables half-wave and full-wave rectification, which, paired with a large parallel capacitor filter (\(\tau = R_L C\)), delivers steady DC power.
