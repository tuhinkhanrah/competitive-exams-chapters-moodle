Atoms: Nuclear Model, Bohr Theory, and Hydrogen Spectra

A complete, self-contained study guide covering α-particle scattering, classical orbit mechanics, Bohr's quantization postulates, atomic spectra, and matter-wave standing orbits.

Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

Understanding how positive and negative charges are arranged inside an electrically neutral atom was one of the central triumphs of early twentieth-century physics. For NEET UG, this topic bridges electrostatics, circular mechanics, and quantum physics, regularly contributing direct numerical and conceptual questions.

What You Will Master
  • Why J. J. Thomson's plum-pudding model failed to explain large-angle α-particle scattering.
  • How the Geiger–Marsden experiment established the tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus and the concept of impact parameter and distance of closest approach.
  • Why Rutherford's classical planetary model predicts an unstable atom and a continuous spectrum, contradicting experimental reality.
  • How Bohr's three postulates explain discrete energy levels, emission and absorption line spectra, and scaling laws for hydrogenic (single-electron) atoms.

From Thomson's Model to Rutherford's Nuclear Atom

By the late nineteenth century, cathode-ray experiments by J. J. Thomson had shown that all atoms contain identical, negatively charged electrons. Because bulk matter is electrically neutral, an atom must carry an equal amount of positive charge. In 1898, Thomson proposed the plum-pudding model, picturing the atom as a sphere of uniformly distributed positive charge (radius \(\approx 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)) with electrons embedded inside it like seeds in a watermelon. In this picture, the atom has a nearly uniform mass distribution and electrons sit in stable electrostatic equilibrium.

The Geiger–Marsden α-Particle Scattering Experiment

To probe the internal distribution of charge and mass, Hans Geiger and Ernst Marsden (at Ernest Rutherford's suggestion) directed a collimated beam of \(5.5\,\mathrm{MeV}\) α-particles from a radioactive bismuth source (\(^{214}_{83}\mathrm{Bi}\)) onto an ultra-thin gold foil (thickness \(\approx 2.1 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}\)) inside an evacuated chamber. A rotatable zinc sulphide (ZnS) screen viewed through a microscope recorded brief flashes (scintillations) at various scattering angles \(\theta\).

  • Most α-particles passed straight through with little or no deflection, showing that an atom is overwhelmingly empty space.
  • Only about \(0.14\%\) of incident particles scattered through angles greater than \(1^\circ\).
  • About \(1\) in \(8000\) deflected backward through angles greater than \(90^\circ\) (up to \(180^\circ\)).

Thomson's diffuse positive sphere could never exert an electric field intense enough to turn back a heavy, energetic α-particle. Rutherford concluded that virtually all the positive charge (\(+Ze\)) and nearly the entire mass of the atom are concentrated in a tiny central core—the nucleus—with a radius of roughly \(10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m}\), whereas the atomic radius is about \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\) (roughly \(10^{4}\) to \(10^{5}\) times larger than the nucleus). Because a gold nucleus (\(Z = 79\)) is about \(50\) times heavier than an α-particle, the nucleus remains essentially stationary during a single scattering encounter, and light atomic electrons cause negligible deflection of the incoming α-particle.

Impact Parameter and Distance of Closest Approach

An α-particle carries charge \(+2e\). At separation \(r\) from a target nucleus of charge \(+Ze\), the repulsive Coulomb force is:

\[F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(2e)(Ze)}{r^2}\]

The hyperbolic trajectory of an α-particle depends on its impact parameter \(b\), defined as the perpendicular distance between the initial velocity vector of the α-particle and a parallel line passing through the centre of the nucleus:

  • Large \(b\): The particle stays far from the nucleus, feels a weak force, and suffers almost no deflection (\(\theta \approx 0^\circ\)).
  • Small \(b\): The particle passes close to the nucleus and undergoes a large-angle deflection.
  • Head-on collision (\(b = 0\)): The particle travels directly toward the nucleus, slows down under Coulomb repulsion, stops momentarily at a minimum separation \(d\)—called the distance of closest approach—and rebounds straight back (\(\theta = 180^\circ\)).
Diagram 1: Dependence of α-particle deflection angle θ on impact parameter b, and distance of closest approach d for a head-on encounter.

At the turning point of a head-on collision, the entire initial kinetic energy \(K\) of the α-particle converts into electrostatic potential energy \(U\):

\[K = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(2e)(Ze)}{d} \implies d = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 K}\]

Note that \(d\) gives only an upper bound on the nuclear radius if the α-particle reverses before physically touching the nuclear surface.

Classical Electron Orbits and Failure of Rutherford's Model

In Rutherford's planetary model, an electron of mass \(m\) and charge \(-e\) revolves around a hydrogen nucleus (\(+e\)) in a circular orbit of radius \(r\) with speed \(v\). Equating the inward electrostatic attraction to the required centripetal force gives:

\[\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{e^2}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \implies r = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 m v^2}\]

From this relation, the kinetic energy \(K\), electrostatic potential energy \(U\), and total mechanical energy \(E\) of the electron are:

\[K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}, \qquad U = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}, \qquad E = K + U = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\]
High-Yield NEET Relationship: Bound-Orbit Energy Ratios

For any electron moving in a circular Coulomb orbit, the magnitudes and signs of \(K\), \(U\), and \(E\) obey the exact identity:

\[E = -K = \frac{U}{2} \qquad \text{and} \qquad |U| = 2K = 2|E|\]

The negative sign of \(E\) confirms that the electron is bound to the nucleus.

Why Classical Physics Fails for Atoms

Despite its mechanical elegance, Rutherford's classical model suffers from two fatal contradictions with observation:

  1. Atomic Instability: An electron in circular motion undergoes continuous centripetal acceleration. According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charge must radiate electromagnetic waves, continuously losing energy and spiraling into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second. Matter would collapse!
  2. Continuous vs. Line Spectra: Classically, the frequency of emitted radiation equals the electron's orbital frequency. As the electron spirals inward, its orbital frequency increases continuously, so it should emit a continuous spectrum. In reality, rarefied gases emit sharp, discrete line spectra.

Atomic Emission and Absorption Spectra

Dense solids and liquids emit a continuous spectrum of wavelengths due to strong interatomic interactions. By contrast, when a low-pressure atomic gas is excited electrically or thermally, individual atoms emit light at specific, characteristic wavelengths—an emission line spectrum (bright lines on a dark background). Conversely, when white light passes through the same cool gas, the transmitted spectrum shows dark lines—an absorption spectrum—at the exact same wavelengths that the gas emits. Thus, atomic line spectra act as unique fingerprints of each element.

Bohr Model of the Hydrogen Atom and Hydrogenic Species

In 1913, Niels Bohr resolved the paradox of atomic stability and discrete spectra by combining classical mechanics with early quantum ideas for hydrogenic atoms—single-electron systems consisting of a nucleus of charge \(+Ze\) and one electron (such as \(\mathrm{H}\), \(\mathrm{He}^+\), \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\)).

Bohr's Three Postulates

  1. Stationary States: An electron can revolve in certain special, stable circular orbits without radiating electromagnetic energy. Each allowed orbit has a definite total energy.
  2. Quantization of Angular Momentum: Only those circular orbits are permitted for which the orbital angular momentum \(L\) of the electron is an integral multiple of \(\frac{h}{2\pi}\): \[L = mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\] where \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\) is Planck's constant and \(n\) is the principal quantum number.
  3. Frequency Condition for Transitions: An atom emits or absorbs a photon only when the electron jumps from one stationary state to another. If an electron transitions from a higher energy state \(E_i\) to a lower energy state \(E_f\), a photon of frequency \(\nu\) is emitted such that: \[h\nu = E_i - E_f\]

Key Equations and Scaling Laws (Hydrogen & Hydrogenic Ions)

Combining the centripetal force condition \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Ze^2}{r_n^2} = \frac{mv_n^2}{r_n}\) with angular momentum quantization \(mv_n r_n = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\) yields all orbital properties for a hydrogenic atom of atomic number \(Z\):

Physical Quantity General Formula (Hydrogenic, \(Z\)) Hydrogen (\(Z = 1\)) Value Scaling with \(n\) and \(Z\)
Orbital Radius (\(r_n\)) \(r_n = \frac{\varepsilon_0 h^2}{\pi m e^2}\frac{n^2}{Z}\) \(r_n = 0.529\,n^2\,\mathrm{Å}\) \(r_n \propto \frac{n^2}{Z}\)
Orbital Speed (\(v_n\)) \(v_n = \frac{e^2}{2\varepsilon_0 h}\frac{Z}{n}\) \(v_n \approx \frac{2.19 \times 10^6}{n}\,\mathrm{m/s}\) \(v_n \propto \frac{Z}{n}\)
Time Period (\(T_n\)) \(T_n = \frac{2\pi r_n}{v_n}\) \(T_n \propto n^3\) \(T_n \propto \frac{n^3}{Z^2}\)
Orbital Frequency (\(f_n\)) \(f_n = \frac{1}{T_n}\) \(f_n \propto \frac{1}{n^3}\) \(f_n \propto \frac{Z^2}{n^3}\)
Total Energy (\(E_n\)) \(E_n = -\frac{m e^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2 h^2}\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\) \(E_n = -\frac{13.6}{n^2}\,\mathrm{eV}\) \(E_n \propto -\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\)

Energy Levels and the Line Spectra of Hydrogen

For hydrogen (\(Z = 1\)), the lowest energy state (\(n = 1\)) is the ground state, with radius \(a_0 = 0.529\,\mathrm{Å}\) (the Bohr radius) and energy \(E_1 = -13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\). States with \(n = 2, 3, 4, \dots\) are excited states: \(n = 2\) is the first excited state (\(E_2 = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\)), \(n = 3\) is the second excited state (\(E_3 = -1.51\,\mathrm{eV}\)), \(n = 4\) is the third excited state (\(E_4 = -0.85\,\mathrm{eV}\)), and so on. As \(n \to \infty\), \(E_\infty = 0\,\mathrm{eV}\), representing a free, ionised electron at rest. Any energy above \(0\,\mathrm{eV}\) forms a continuum of unbound states.

  • Ionisation Energy: The minimum energy required to completely remove an electron from the ground state to \(n = \infty\) is \(0 - (-13.6\,\mathrm{eV}) = 13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\) for hydrogen (or \(13.6\,Z^2\,\mathrm{eV}\) for a hydrogenic ion).
  • Excitation Energy: The energy required to raise an electron from \(n = 1\) to a specific excited state \(n\); for example, first excitation energy is \(E_2 - E_1 = 10.2\,\mathrm{eV}\), and second excitation energy is \(E_3 - E_1 = 12.09\,\mathrm{eV}\).
Diagram 3: Quantized energy levels of the hydrogen atom and the primary emission series (Lyman, Balmer, and Paschen).

Counting Spectral Lines and Identifying Series

When a collection of hydrogen atoms is excited to level \(n\), electrons can cascade down through all intermediate levels to \(n = 1\). The maximum number of distinct emission wavelengths produced is:

\[N = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}\]

Within any series ending at lower level \(n_f\):

  • Longest wavelength (series first line): Transition from \(n_i = n_f + 1 \to n_f\) (smallest \(\Delta E\)).
  • Shortest wavelength (series limit): Transition from \(n_i = \infty \to n_f\) (largest \(\Delta E\)).

de Broglie's Explanation of Quantized Orbits & Model Limitations

In 1923, Louis de Broglie explained why angular momentum is quantized. Treating the orbiting electron as a matter wave of wavelength \(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv_n}\), only those circular orbits persist in which the electron wave forms a closed standing wave without destructive interference. This requires the orbit's circumference to equal an integer number of de Broglie wavelengths:

\[2\pi r_n = n\lambda = n\left(\frac{h}{mv_n}\right) \implies mv_n r_n = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\]
Diagram 4: de Broglie matter-wave standing pattern for the n = 4 Bohr orbit, fitting four complete wavelengths along the circular path.

Limitations of the Bohr Model

  • Restricted to Single-Electron Atoms: It applies only to hydrogen and hydrogenic ions (\(\mathrm{He}^+\), \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\)). It fails even for two-electron helium because it ignores mutual electron–electron electrostatic repulsion, which is comparable in strength to the electron–nucleus attraction.
  • Cannot Explain Spectral Intensities: While Bohr's model predicts spectral frequencies accurately, it cannot explain why some emission lines are bright and others are faint.
  • Fixed Orbits vs. Uncertainty: Assigning both a precise radius and momentum violates Heisenberg's uncertainty principle; modern quantum mechanics replaces fixed orbits with probability clouds.

Worked Examples

Calculating the Distance of Closest Approach

Problem: An α-particle with an assumed kinetic energy of \(5.0\,\mathrm{MeV}\) is fired head-on toward a stationary silver nucleus (\(Z = 47\)). Estimate the distance of closest approach \(d\). (Take \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9.0 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C^2}\) and \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)).

Solution:

Convert the initial kinetic energy into joules:

\[K = 5.0 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{eV} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J/eV} = 8.0 \times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}\]

At the turning point, total mechanical energy conservation gives \(K = U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(2e)(Ze)}{d}\):

\[d = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 K} = \frac{2 \times (9.0 \times 10^9) \times 47 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}}\] \[d = \frac{2.166 \times 10^{-26}}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}} \approx 2.71 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m} = 27.1\,\mathrm{fm}\]

Energy Ratios in a Bound Electron Orbit

Problem: In a hydrogen atom, an electron occupies the \(n = 2\) stationary state. Determine its total energy, kinetic energy, electrostatic potential energy, and orbital radius.

Solution:

Using \(E_n = -\frac{13.6}{n^2}\,\mathrm{eV}\) for \(n = 2\):

\[E_2 = -\frac{13.6}{2^2}\,\mathrm{eV} = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\]

Using the bound-orbit identities \(K = -E\) and \(U = 2E\):

\[K_2 = +3.40\,\mathrm{eV}, \qquad U_2 = 2(-3.40\,\mathrm{eV}) = -6.80\,\mathrm{eV}\]

The orbital radius is \(r_n = 0.529\,n^2\,\mathrm{Å}\):

\[r_2 = 0.529 \times 2^2\,\mathrm{Å} = 2.116\,\mathrm{Å} \approx 2.12 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\]

Spectral Transitions and Photon Wavelength in a Hydrogenic Ion

Problem: A singly ionised helium ion (\(\mathrm{He}^+\), \(Z = 2\)) undergoes a transition from the \(n_i = 4\) state to the \(n_f = 2\) state. Calculate the energy and wavelength of the emitted photon. (Take \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\), \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}\)).

Solution:

For \(\mathrm{He}^+\) (\(Z = 2\)), the energy levels are \(E_n = -\frac{13.6 \times 2^2}{n^2} = -\frac{54.4}{n^2}\,\mathrm{eV}\):

\[E_4 = -\frac{54.4}{16} = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}, \qquad E_2 = -\frac{54.4}{4} = -13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\]

The emitted photon energy is:

\[\Delta E = E_4 - E_2 = -3.40 - (-13.6) = 10.2\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.632 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J}\]

The photon wavelength is:

\[\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{1.632 \times 10^{-18}} \approx 1.219 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 121.9\,\mathrm{nm}\]

Standing Matter Waves in a Bohr Orbit

Problem: Assuming the first Bohr radius of hydrogen is \(r_1 = 0.53\,\mathrm{Å}\), calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron revolving in the \(n = 3\) orbit.

Solution:

The radius of the \(n = 3\) orbit is \(r_3 = n^2 r_1 = 9 r_1 = 9 \times 0.53\,\mathrm{Å} = 4.77\,\mathrm{Å}\). By de Broglie's standing-wave condition \(2\pi r_n = n\lambda_n\):

\[\lambda_3 = \frac{2\pi r_3}{3} = \frac{2\pi (9 r_1)}{3} = 6\pi r_1 = 6 \times 3.1416 \times 0.53\,\mathrm{Å} \approx 9.99\,\mathrm{Å}\]

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

An α-particle of kinetic energy \(K\) approaches a stationary nucleus of atomic number \(Z\) head-on, reaching a distance of closest approach \(d\). If the kinetic energy is doubled to \(2K\) and the target nucleus is replaced by one of atomic number \(2Z\), the new distance of closest approach will be:

  • (A) \(d\)
  • (B) \(2d\)
  • (C) \(4d\)
  • (D) \(d/2\)

Correct Answer: (A) \(d\)

Worked Solution: The distance of closest approach is \(d = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 K}\), so \(d \propto \frac{Z}{K}\). When \(Z \to 2Z\) and \(K \to 2K\), the new distance is \(d' = \frac{2Z}{2K} \cdot \left(\frac{2e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\right) = d\).

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

For an electron revolving in a stable circular orbit around a proton in a hydrogen atom, what is the ratio of its kinetic energy \(K\) to its electrostatic potential energy \(U\)?

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(-2 : 1\)
  • (C) \(-1 : 2\)
  • (D) \(-1 : 1\)

Correct Answer: (C) \(-1 : 2\)

Worked Solution: In a circular Coulomb orbit, \(K = +\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\) and \(U = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\). Therefore, \(\frac{K}{U} = -\frac{1}{2}\), which is \(-1 : 2\).

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): According to classical electromagnetic theory, Rutherford's planetary model of the atom cannot remain stable.
Reason (R): An electron moving in a circular orbit experiences centripetal acceleration and must continuously radiate electromagnetic energy, causing it to spiral into the nucleus.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) Both A and R are false.

Correct Answer: (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Worked Solution: Classical electrodynamics predicts that any accelerating charge emits electromagnetic waves. Since an orbiting electron has centripetal acceleration, it loses mechanical energy continuously and spirals inward, making Rutherford's classical atom unstable.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

According to the Bohr model, what is the ratio of the orbital time period of an electron in the \(n = 2\) state of \(\mathrm{He}^+\) (\(Z = 2\)) to that in the \(n = 1\) state of hydrogen (\(Z = 1\))?

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(2 : 1\)
  • (C) \(4 : 1\)
  • (D) \(8 : 1\)

Correct Answer: (B) \(2 : 1\)

Worked Solution: Since \(T_n = \frac{2\pi r_n}{v_n}\), with \(r_n \propto \frac{n^2}{Z}\) and \(v_n \propto \frac{Z}{n}\), we get \(T_n \propto \frac{n^3}{Z^2}\). Thus, \(\frac{T(\mathrm{He}^+, n=2)}{T(\mathrm{H}, n=1)} = \frac{2^3 / 2^2}{1^3 / 1^2} = \frac{8/4}{1} = 2 : 1\).

Practice Question 5 Numerical MCQ

The minimum energy required to excite a hydrogen atom from its ground state to its second excited state is:

  • (A) \(1.51\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (B) \(10.2\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (C) \(12.09\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (D) \(13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\)

Correct Answer: (C) \(12.09\,\mathrm{eV}\)

Worked Solution: Ground state is \(n = 1\) (\(E_1 = -13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\)), and the second excited state is \(n = 3\) (\(E_3 = -\frac{13.6}{3^2} = -1.51\,\mathrm{eV}\)). Required excitation energy \(= E_3 - E_1 = -1.51 - (-13.6) = 12.09\,\mathrm{eV}\).

Practice Question 6 Statement-Based MCQ

Statement I: The emission line spectrum of a low-pressure atomic gas consists of bright lines of discrete wavelengths on a dark background.
Statement II: When white light passes through the same gas, the dark absorption lines appear at wavelengths completely different from those in its emission spectrum.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Correct Answer: (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.

Worked Solution: An atom absorbs photons of the exact same energy differences (and hence the exact same wavelengths) that it emits when electrons transition between the same pair of stationary states.

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

A sample of hydrogen atoms is excited to the \(n = 4\) energy level. How many distinct emission spectral lines can be observed as the atoms return to the ground state, and which transition produces the longest wavelength?

  • (A) \(4\) lines; \(n = 4 \to n = 1\)
  • (B) \(6\) lines; \(n = 4 \to n = 1\)
  • (C) \(6\) lines; \(n = 2 \to n = 1\)
  • (D) \(6\) lines; \(n = 4 \to n = 3\)

Correct Answer: (D) \(6\) lines; \(n = 4 \to n = 3\)

Worked Solution: Total possible emission lines \(= \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{4 \times 3}{2} = 6\). Since \(\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E}\), the longest wavelength corresponds to the smallest energy gap \(\Delta E\), which occurs between \(n = 4\) and \(n = 3\).

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

If \(r_1\) denotes the radius of the innermost Bohr orbit of a hydrogen atom, what is the de Broglie wavelength of an electron revolving in the \(n = 4\) orbit?

  • (A) \(4\pi r_1\)
  • (B) \(8\pi r_1\)
  • (C) \(16\pi r_1\)
  • (D) \(32\pi r_1\)

Correct Answer: (B) \(8\pi r_1\)

Worked Solution: From de Broglie's condition \(2\pi r_n = n\lambda_n\) and \(r_n = n^2 r_1\), we obtain \(\lambda_n = \frac{2\pi n^2 r_1}{n} = 2\pi n r_1\). Substituting \(n = 4\) gives \(\lambda_4 = 8\pi r_1\).

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

A beam of \(12.3\,\mathrm{eV}\) electrons bombards gaseous hydrogen at room temperature (all atoms initially in the ground state). Which spectral series will be observed in the emitted radiation?

  • (A) Only Lyman series
  • (B) Both Lyman and Balmer series
  • (C) Lyman, Balmer, and Paschen series
  • (D) No spectral lines will be emitted

Correct Answer: (B) Both Lyman and Balmer series

Worked Solution: Excitation energies from \(n = 1\) are \(E_2 - E_1 = 10.2\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(E_3 - E_1 = 12.09\,\mathrm{eV}\), and \(E_4 - E_1 = 12.75\,\mathrm{eV}\). Since \(12.3\,\mathrm{eV}\) lies between \(12.09\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(12.75\,\mathrm{eV}\), electrons can be excited at most to \(n = 3\). Subsequent downward transitions \(3 \to 2\) (Balmer), \(3 \to 1\) (Lyman), and \(2 \to 1\) (Lyman) emit both Lyman and Balmer lines.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Excited State Numbering: The first excited state is \(n = 2\) (not \(n = 1\)), and the second excited state is \(n = 3\).
  • Forgetting \(Z\) in Hydrogenic Ions: Always include \(Z^2\) in \(E_n = -\frac{13.6 Z^2}{n^2}\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(1/Z\) in \(r_n = 0.529\,\frac{n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{Å}\) for \(\mathrm{He}^+\) (\(Z = 2\)) or \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\) (\(Z = 3\)).
  • Sign Errors in Energy: Kinetic energy \(K\) is always positive, while potential energy \(U\) and total energy \(E\) are negative for bound orbits (\(U = 2E = -2K\)).
  • Electron Bombardment vs. Photon Absorption: An electron with \(12.3\,\mathrm{eV}\) can transfer \(12.09\,\mathrm{eV}\) and keep \(0.21\,\mathrm{eV}\), whereas a photon of \(12.3\,\mathrm{eV}\) cannot be absorbed at all by ground-state hydrogen because its energy does not match any exact level difference.
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Nuclear size: \(10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m}\); atomic size: \(\approx 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\).
  • Distance of closest approach: \(d = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 K}\).
  • Bohr quantization: \(mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\) and standing wave condition \(2\pi r_n = n\lambda\).
  • Hydrogen levels: \(E_1 = -13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(E_2 = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(E_3 = -1.51\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(E_4 = -0.85\,\mathrm{eV}\).
  • Max emission lines from level \(n\): \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\).

Concise Final Recap

Rutherford's α-scattering experiment proved that an atom's mass and positive charge reside in a tiny central nucleus, leaving most of the atom empty. Bohr's quantum model stabilized this nuclear atom by restricting electrons to non-radiating stationary orbits with quantized angular momentum \(L = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\)—later explained by de Broglie as circular standing matter waves—and accounted for the discrete emission and absorption line spectra of hydrogenic atoms via photon transitions \(h\nu = E_i - E_f\).
