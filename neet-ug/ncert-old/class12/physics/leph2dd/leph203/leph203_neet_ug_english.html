Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter

A Complete Conceptual, Analytical, and Problem-Solving Tutorial for NEET UG Preparation

Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

At the close of the nineteenth century, classical electromagnetism had firmly established light as a continuous wave, while cathode-ray experiments had revealed the electron as a universal constituent of matter. Yet the interaction of light with metal surfaces exposed a fundamental puzzle that classical wave physics could not resolve. Resolving that puzzle gave birth to quantum physics: radiation carries discrete energy packets (photons), and moving material particles exhibit wave-like behavior (matter waves).

What You Will Master

  • Define work function (\(\phi_0\)) and distinguish between thermionic, field, and photoelectric emission.
  • Analyze the experimental laws of the photoelectric effect, including saturation current, stopping potential (\(V_0\)), and threshold frequency (\(\nu_0\)).
  • Explain why classical wave theory fails to account for photoelectric observations and apply Einstein's photoelectric equation.
  • Use the photon model of radiation to compute photon energy, momentum, and photon flux.
  • Calculate the de Broglie wavelength (\(\lambda\)) of moving particles in terms of momentum, kinetic energy, and accelerating potential.
  • Solve NEET UG numerical, graphical, assertion-reason, and statement-based questions with speed and accuracy.

Foundations: Electrons and Surface Emission

Investigations of low-pressure electrical discharges led J. J. Thomson (1897) to measure the specific charge (\(e/m\)) of cathode-ray particles using crossed electric and magnetic fields. The ratio \(e/m \approx 1.76 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\) proved identical regardless of the cathode material or gas used, establishing the electron as a universal building block of matter. Later, R. A. Millikan's oil-drop experiment (1913) established charge quantization and measured the elementary charge \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), enabling determination of the electron mass (\(m_e \approx 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)).

Inside a metal, conduction electrons move relatively freely through the lattice of positive ions. However, if an electron attempts to leave the boundary of the metal, the surface acquires a net positive charge that pulls the electron back. To escape completely, an electron must receive at least a characteristic minimum energy.

Work Function (\(\phi_0\)) and the Electron-Volt (\(\mathrm{eV}\))

The work function (\(\phi_0\)) of a metal is the minimum energy required by an electron to escape from the metal surface. It depends on the metal and the condition of its surface, and is commonly expressed in electron-volts (\(\mathrm{eV}\)).

\[1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\]

Depending on how the required escape energy is supplied, electron emission from a metal surface is classified into three primary mechanisms:

Emission Mechanism Energy Source Key Condition / Example
Thermionic Emission Thermal energy (heating the metal) Metal is heated to high temperature so free electrons gain kinetic energy exceeding \(\phi_0\).
Field Emission Strong external electric field Electric fields of order \(10^8\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) pull electrons out of the surface (e.g., spark plugs).
Photoelectric Emission Electromagnetic radiation (light) Incident light of frequency \(\nu \ge \nu_0\) ejects electrons (called photoelectrons); alkali metals respond even to visible light, whereas Zn, Cd, and Mg require ultraviolet light.

Experimental Laws of the Photoelectric Effect

First noticed by Heinrich Hertz (1887) and systematically explored by Wilhelm Hallwachs and Philipp Lenard (1886–1902), the photoelectric effect can be studied using an evacuated glass tube fitted with a transparent quartz window (allowing ultraviolet light to pass) and two electrodes: a photosensitive emitter plate C and a collector plate A.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Experimental setup for studying the photoelectric effect. A variable DC supply with a polarity-reversing commutator adjusts the potential of collector A relative to emitter C, while a microammeter (µA) measures photocurrent and a voltmeter (V) measures the potential difference.

Systematic measurements with this apparatus reveal four foundational experimental laws:

  1. Effect of Intensity on Photocurrent: For a fixed frequency (\(\nu > \nu_0\)) and fixed accelerating potential, the photoelectric current increases linearly with the intensity of incident light. Because photocurrent is proportional to the number of photoelectrons emitted per second, higher intensity means more electrons are ejected per second.
  2. Effect of Potential (Saturation Current & Stopping Potential):
    • As the positive potential on collector A increases, more emitted electrons reach A until all emitted electrons are collected. Beyond this point, the current levels off at a maximum value called the saturation current.
    • When collector A is made negative (retarding potential) relative to C, electrons are repelled. Only the most energetic electrons reach A. At a sharp critical negative potential \(V_0\), called the stopping potential (or cut-off potential), the photocurrent drops to zero.
    • At stopping potential \(V_0\), even the fastest photoelectrons (with maximum kinetic energy \(K_{\max}\)) are just stopped before reaching A: \[K_{\max} = \frac{1}{2} m_e v_{\max}^2 = e V_0\]
    • Increasing light intensity increases the saturation current, but does not change the stopping potential \(V_0\). Thus, \(K_{\max}\) is independent of light intensity.
  3. Effect of Frequency & Threshold Frequency (\(\nu_0\)):
    • When light of the same intensity but higher frequency is used, the saturation current remains the same, while the stopping potential becomes more negative (\(\nu_3 > \nu_2 > \nu_1 \implies V_{03} > V_{02} > V_{01}\)).
    • Plotting \(V_0\) against \(\nu\) yields a straight line. Below a characteristic minimum frequency called the threshold frequency (\(\nu_0\)), no photoelectric emission occurs, regardless of how intense the incident beam is.
  4. Instantaneous Emission: Whenever \(\nu > \nu_0\), photoelectrons are emitted instantaneously (within \(\sim 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}\) or less), even if the incident light is extremely dim.

Right: Linear dependence of stopping potential \(V_0\) on frequency \(\nu\) for two different metals, yielding parallel lines of slope \(h/e\).

Failure of Wave Theory and Einstein's Photon Equation

Classical electromagnetic wave theory treats light as a continuous wave whose energy is spread uniformly across the wavefront and depends on the square of the field amplitudes (intensity). Every major prediction of this wave picture contradicts the photoelectric observations:

Feature Classical Wave Theory Prediction Experimental Reality
Effect of Intensity on \(K_{\max}\) Higher intensity delivers larger field amplitudes, so \(K_{\max}\) should increase with intensity. \(K_{\max}\) (and \(V_0\)) is completely independent of intensity.
Threshold Frequency Any frequency, if sufficiently intense and applied long enough, should eject electrons. Below \(\nu_0\), zero emission occurs regardless of intensity.
Time Lag Energy is shared across billions of surface atoms; an electron should take hours to accumulate \(\phi_0\). Emission is instantaneous (\(\sim 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}\) or less) even at very low intensity.

Einstein's Quantum Explanation (1905)

Building on Planck's quantum hypothesis, Albert Einstein proposed that electromagnetic radiation interacts with matter in discrete energy packets called quanta (later named photons). Each photon of frequency \(\nu\) carries an energy \(E = h\nu\), where \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\) is Planck's constant.

In a single elementary event, one electron absorbs one photon completely. If \(h\nu > \phi_0\), the excess energy becomes the kinetic energy of the escaping electron. Electrons near the surface that suffer no internal energy loss emerge with the maximum kinetic energy \(K_{\max}\):

Einstein's Photoelectric Equation
\[K_{\max} = h\nu - \phi_0 = \frac{hc}{\lambda} - \phi_0\] \[eV_0 = h\nu - \phi_0 \implies V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \frac{\phi_0}{e}\]

At threshold (\(\nu = \nu_0\), \(\lambda = \lambda_0\)), \(K_{\max} = 0\), so: \[\phi_0 = h\nu_0 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_0}\]

Notice how neatly this linear relation explains the \(V_0\) versus \(\nu\) graph: the slope is \(h/e\) (a universal constant independent of the emitter metal), and the horizontal intercept is \(\nu_0 = \phi_0/h\). Robert Millikan experimentally verified this linear relation for alkali metals in 1916 and obtained an accurate value of \(h\).

NEET UG Calculation Shortcut

When wavelength \(\lambda\) is given in nanometers (\(\mathrm{nm}\)) or ångströms (\(\mathrm{Å}\)) and energy is needed in \(\mathrm{eV}\): \[hc \approx 1240\,\mathrm{eV\cdot nm} = 12400\,\mathrm{eV\cdot Å}\] (Depending on whether \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\) or \(6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\) is used, \(hc\) lies between \(1240\) and \(1243\,\mathrm{eV\cdot nm}\). Always check the constants specified in a problem.)

Particle Nature of Light: Properties of a Photon

  • Energy and Momentum: A photon of frequency \(\nu\) and wavelength \(\lambda\) travels at speed \(c\) in vacuum with energy \(E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}\) and momentum \(p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h\nu}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}\).
  • Independence from Intensity: Increasing the intensity of monochromatic light increases only the number of photons crossing unit area per second (photon flux); the energy and momentum of each individual photon remain unchanged.
  • Electrical Neutrality: Photons carry zero electric charge and are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.
  • Conservation in Collisions: In photon-particle interactions (such as photoelectric absorption or Compton scattering), total energy and total momentum are conserved, though photon number need not be conserved (photons can be absorbed or created).

Wave Nature of Matter: de Broglie Hypothesis

Light exhibits dual behavior: wave properties in interference, diffraction, and polarization, and particle properties in the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering. In 1924, Louis Victor de Broglie proposed that nature is symmetrical—moving material particles must also possess wave-like character.

de Broglie Wavelength (\(\lambda\))

For any particle of mass \(m\) moving with speed \(v\) and momentum \(p = mv\):

\[\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}\]

In terms of kinetic energy \(K = \frac{p^2}{2m}\):

\[\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}}\]

For a particle of charge \(q\) accelerated from rest through a potential difference \(V\) (\(K = qV\)):

\[\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mqV}}\]

Special Formula for an Accelerated Electron (High-Yield NEET UG)

Substituting \(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), and \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\) for an electron accelerated through potential difference \(V\) (in volts): \[\lambda_e \approx \frac{1.227}{\sqrt{V}}\,\mathrm{nm} = \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}}\,\mathrm{Å}\]

Notice that \(\lambda\) is independent of the charge or nature of the particle once \(p\) (or \(m\) and \(K\)) is fixed. Why do we not observe wave effects for a thrown cricket ball? Because \(h \sim 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\) is extraordinarily small; for macroscopic masses, \(\lambda \sim 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{m}\), which is far smaller than a proton (\(\sim 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\)) and impossible to diffract. For sub-atomic particles like electrons, however, \(\lambda\) is comparable to inter-atomic spacings in crystals (\(\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{nm}\)), making wave behavior directly measurable.

Physical Quantity Symbol Dimensions SI Unit Key Relation
Planck's constant \(h\) \([\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}]\) \(\mathrm{J\cdot s}\) \(E = h\nu\)
Work function \(\phi_0\) \([\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}]\) \(\mathrm{J}\) (also \(\mathrm{eV}\)) \(\phi_0 = h\nu_0\)
Stopping potential \(V_0\) \([\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-3}\,A^{-1}}]\) \(\mathrm{V}\) \(eV_0 = K_{\max}\)
Threshold frequency \(\nu_0\) \([\mathrm{T^{-1}}]\) \(\mathrm{Hz}\) \(\nu_0 = \phi_0 / h\)
de Broglie wavelength \(\lambda\) \([\mathrm{L}]\) \(\mathrm{m}\) \(\lambda = h/p\)

Worked Examples

1. Calculating Photon Energy, Momentum, and Flux from a Laser Beam

Problem: Suppose a monochromatic laser emits light of wavelength \(\lambda = 500\,\mathrm{nm}\) at a steady power of \(P = 5.0\,\mathrm{mW}\) (assumed values). Calculate (a) the energy of each photon in \(\mathrm{J}\) and \(\mathrm{eV}\), (b) the momentum of each photon, and (c) the average number of photons emitted per second. (Assume \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\), \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\).)

Solution:

(a) Energy of one photon: \[E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s} \times 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{500 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}} = 3.978 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 3.98 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\] Converting to \(\mathrm{eV}\): \[E = \frac{3.978 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 2.49\,\mathrm{eV}\]

(b) Momentum of one photon: \[p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}}{5.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}} = 1.326 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m\,s^{-1}} \approx 1.33 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\cdot m\,s^{-1}}\]

(c) Since power \(P = N E\), where \(N\) is the number of photons emitted per second: \[N = \frac{P}{E} = \frac{5.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{W}}{3.978 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}} \approx 1.26 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{photons\,s^{-1}}\]

2. Applying Einstein's Photoelectric Equation for Stopping Potential and Maximum Speed

Problem: Ultraviolet radiation of wavelength \(\lambda = 300\,\mathrm{nm}\) falls on a metal plate whose assumed work function is \(\phi_0 = 2.50\,\mathrm{eV}\). Find (a) the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, (b) the stopping potential, and (c) the maximum speed of the emitted photoelectrons. (Assume \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\), \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), and \(m_e = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\).)

Solution:

(a) Energy of each incident photon: \[E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{300 \times 10^{-9}}\,\mathrm{J} = 6.63 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\] In electron-volts: \[E = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 4.14\,\mathrm{eV}\] Using Einstein's photoelectric equation: \[K_{\max} = E - \phi_0 = 4.14\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.50\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.64\,\mathrm{eV}\] In joules: \(K_{\max} = 1.64 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} = 2.624 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\).

(b) Since \(eV_0 = K_{\max}\), the stopping potential is: \[V_0 = \frac{1.64\,\mathrm{eV}}{e} = 1.64\,\mathrm{V}\]

(c) Maximum speed \(v_{\max}\): \[v_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2 K_{\max}}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 2.624 \times 10^{-19}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{5.767 \times 10^{11}} \approx 7.59 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

3. Determining Planck's Constant and Work Function from Stopping Potential Data

Problem: In a hypothetical photoelectric experiment, light of frequency \(\nu_1 = 6.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\) produces a stopping potential of \(V_1 = 0.80\,\mathrm{V}\), while light of frequency \(\nu_2 = 9.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\) produces a stopping potential of \(V_2 = 2.04\,\mathrm{V}\). Determine the experimental value of Planck's constant \(h\) and the work function \(\phi_0\) of the metal. (Use \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\).)

Solution:

From \(eV_0 = h\nu - \phi_0\), subtracting the two measurements eliminates \(\phi_0\): \[e(V_2 - V_1) = h(\nu_2 - \nu_1)\] \[h = \frac{e(V_2 - V_1)}{\nu_2 - \nu_1} = \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C} \times (2.04 - 0.80)\,\mathrm{V}}{(9.0 - 6.0) \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}} = \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.24}{3.0 \times 10^{14}} \approx 6.61 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\]

To find the work function in \(\mathrm{eV}\), note that \(\frac{h}{e} = \frac{1.24}{3.0 \times 10^{14}} = 4.133 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{V\cdot s}\). Then: \[\frac{\phi_0}{e} = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu_1 - V_1 = (4.133 \times 10^{-15} \times 6.0 \times 10^{14}) - 0.80 = 2.48 - 0.80 = 1.68\,\mathrm{V}\] Hence, \(\phi_0 = 1.68\,\mathrm{eV}\).

4. Comparing de Broglie Wavelengths of an Electron and a Proton

Problem: An electron (\(m_e = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) and a proton (\(m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) are both accelerated from rest through an assumed potential difference of \(V = 100\,\mathrm{V}\). Find the de Broglie wavelength of the electron and the ratio \(\lambda_e / \lambda_p\).

Solution:

Using the electron shortcut formula for \(V = 100\,\mathrm{V}\): \[\lambda_e \approx \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{100}}\,\mathrm{Å} = 1.227\,\mathrm{Å} = 0.1227\,\mathrm{nm}\] Since both particles carry the same magnitude of charge \(e\) and are accelerated through the same potential difference \(V\), \(\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\). Therefore: \[\frac{\lambda_e}{\lambda_p} = \sqrt{\frac{m_p}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.67 \times 10^{-27}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{1835.16} \approx 42.84\] Thus, the electron's de Broglie wavelength is nearly \(43\) times larger than the proton's (\(\lambda_p \approx 0.0286\,\mathrm{Å}\)).

5. Finding the Minimum Wavelength of X-Rays from an Accelerating Potential

Problem: In an X-ray tube, electrons are accelerated from rest through a potential difference of \(V = 40\,\mathrm{kV}\) (assumed value) before striking a metal target. Calculate the maximum frequency and minimum wavelength of the X-rays produced. (Assume \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\), \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\).)

Solution:

The maximum energy of an emitted X-ray photon equals the entire kinetic energy \(eV\) of the incident electron: \[h\nu_{\max} = eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C} \times 4.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{V} = 6.4 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{J}\] \[\nu_{\max} = \frac{6.4 \times 10^{-15}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} \approx 9.65 \times 10^{18}\,\mathrm{Hz}\] The corresponding minimum wavelength is: \[\lambda_{\min} = \frac{c}{\nu_{\max}} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{9.65 \times 10^{18}} \approx 3.11 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.311\,\mathrm{Å}\]

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The threshold wavelength for photoelectric emission from a certain metal surface is \(600\,\mathrm{nm}\). Taking \(hc = 1242\,\mathrm{eV\cdot nm}\), the work function of this metal is:

  • (A) \(1.84\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (B) \(2.07\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (C) \(2.48\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (D) \(4.14\,\mathrm{eV}\)
Answer: (B) \(2.07\,\mathrm{eV}\)

Worked Solution: The work function is related to the threshold wavelength \(\lambda_0\) by \(\phi_0 = \frac{hc}{\lambda_0}\). Substituting \(hc = 1242\,\mathrm{eV\cdot nm}\) and \(\lambda_0 = 600\,\mathrm{nm}\):

\[\phi_0 = \frac{1242\,\mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{600\,\mathrm{nm}} = 2.07\,\mathrm{eV}\]
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Monochromatic light of frequency \(\nu\) (where \(\nu > \nu_0\)) is incident on the emitter plate of a photocell. If the intensity of the incident light is doubled while its frequency is kept constant, which of the following occurs?

  • (A) Both the saturation current and stopping potential double.
  • (B) The saturation current remains unchanged, and the stopping potential doubles.
  • (C) The saturation current doubles, and the stopping potential remains unchanged.
  • (D) Both the saturation current and stopping potential remain unchanged.
Answer: (C) The saturation current doubles, and the stopping potential remains unchanged.

Worked Solution: Doubling the intensity at constant frequency doubles the number of photons arriving per second, which doubles the number of photoelectrons emitted per second and thus doubles the saturation current. Because the energy of each photon (\(h\nu\)) is unchanged, \(K_{\max} = h\nu - \phi_0\) and hence the stopping potential \(V_0 = K_{\max}/e\) remain unchanged.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Light of wavelength \(400\,\mathrm{nm}\) is incident on a photosensitive surface of work function \(2.10\,\mathrm{eV}\). Taking \(hc = 1240\,\mathrm{eV\cdot nm}\), the stopping potential required to halt the fastest photoelectrons is:

  • (A) \(0.80\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(1.00\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(1.55\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(2.10\,\mathrm{V}\)
Answer: (B) \(1.00\,\mathrm{V}\)

Worked Solution: The photon energy is \(E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{1240\,\mathrm{eV\cdot nm}}{400\,\mathrm{nm}} = 3.10\,\mathrm{eV}\). By Einstein's photoelectric equation:

\[eV_0 = E - \phi_0 = 3.10\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.10\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.00\,\mathrm{eV} \implies V_0 = 1.00\,\mathrm{V}\]
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A proton and an alpha particle have the same kinetic energy \(K\). Taking the mass of the alpha particle to be \(4\) times the mass of the proton (\(m_\alpha = 4m_p\)), the ratio of their de Broglie wavelengths \(\lambda_p : \lambda_\alpha\) is:

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(2 : 1\)
  • (C) \(1 : 4\)
  • (D) \(4 : 1\)
Answer: (B) \(2 : 1\)

Worked Solution: Using \(\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}}\), for identical kinetic energy \(K\), we have \(\lambda \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\). Therefore:

\[\frac{\lambda_p}{\lambda_\alpha} = \sqrt{\frac{m_\alpha}{m_p}} = \sqrt{\frac{4m_p}{m_p}} = \frac{2}{1}\]

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A monochromatic source of power \(60\,\mathrm{W}\) emits light of wavelength \(663\,\mathrm{nm}\). Taking \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\) and \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the number of photons emitted by the source per second is:

  • (A) \(1.0 \times 10^{19}\)
  • (B) \(2.0 \times 10^{19}\)
  • (C) \(1.0 \times 10^{20}\)
  • (D) \(2.0 \times 10^{20}\)
Answer: (D) \(2.0 \times 10^{20}\)

\[E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{663 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\]

Number of photons emitted per second:

\[N = \frac{P}{E} = \frac{60\,\mathrm{W}}{3.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}} = 2.0 \times 10^{20}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\]
Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When light of frequency \(2\nu_0\) (where \(\nu_0\) is the threshold frequency) is incident on a metal surface, the maximum speed of the emitted photoelectrons is \(v_1\). When the frequency of the incident light is increased to \(5\nu_0\), the maximum speed becomes \(v_2\). The ratio \(v_1 : v_2\) is:

  • (A) \(1 : 2\)
  • (B) \(1 : 4\)
  • (C) \(2 : 5\)
  • (D) \(1 : \sqrt{5}\)
Answer: (A) \(1 : 2\)

Worked Solution: Applying \(\frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\max}^2 = h(\nu - \nu_0)\) for both frequencies:

\[\frac{1}{2}m_e v_1^2 = h(2\nu_0 - \nu_0) = h\nu_0, \qquad \frac{1}{2}m_e v_2^2 = h(5\nu_0 - \nu_0) = 4h\nu_0\]

Dividing the two equations gives \(\frac{v_1^2}{v_2^2} = \frac{1}{4}\), so \(\frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{1}{2}\).

Practice Question 8 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Given below are two statements: one labelled as Assertion (A) and the other labelled as Reason (R).

Assertion (A): Photoelectric emission from a metal surface does not occur if the frequency of incident light is less than the threshold frequency, no matter how high the intensity is.
Reason (R): In the photon picture, photoelectric emission takes place when a single electron absorbs a single photon whose energy \(h\nu\) exceeds the work function \(\phi_0\).

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

Worked Solution: Because an electron absorbs one photon at a time, increasing intensity only increases the number of photons, not the energy \(h\nu\) of each photon. If \(h\nu < \phi_0\) (i.e., \(\nu < \nu_0\)), no single photon can supply the minimum escape energy \(\phi_0\), so emission cannot occur.

Practice Question 9 (Two-Statement MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: Photons are deflected by strong magnetic fields because they carry both energy and momentum.
Statement II: The de Broglie wavelength associated with a macroscopic moving object, such as a cricket ball, is too small to produce observable wave-like diffraction effects.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Worked Solution: Photons are electrically neutral, so they experience no Lorentz force and are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields (Statement I is incorrect). For macroscopic objects, large mass \(m\) makes \(\lambda = h/(mv) \sim 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{m}\), far beyond experimental detection (Statement II is correct).

  • (D) Metal X and metal Y have the same threshold frequency, but different Planck's constants.
Answer: (B) Metal Y has a higher work function than metal X, and both lines have slope \(h/e\).

Worked Solution: From \(V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \frac{\phi_0}{e}\), the slope of the \(V_0\)–\(\nu\) graph is always \(h/e\) for every metal. The horizontal intercept (\(V_0 = 0\)) equals the threshold frequency \(\nu_0 = \phi_0 / h\). A larger intercept for metal Y means \(\nu_{0Y} > \nu_{0X}\), so \(\phi_{0Y} > \phi_{0X}\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Pitfall 1 — Confusing \(\mathrm{eV}\) and \(\mathrm{J}\): Always convert work function \(\phi_0\) from \(\mathrm{eV}\) to \(\mathrm{J}\) (by multiplying by \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\)) before substituting into \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{2K_{\max}/m_e}\) or \(\nu_0 = \phi_0/h\).
  • Pitfall 2 — Assuming all photoelectrons have kinetic energy \(K_{\max}\): Emitted photoelectrons have a distribution of kinetic energies ranging from \(0\) up to \(K_{\max}\). Only the fastest surface electrons have \(K = K_{\max} = eV_0\).
  • Pitfall 3 — Mixing up slope of \(V_0\)–\(\nu\) vs. \(K_{\max}\)–\(\nu\) graphs: The \(V_0\) vs. \(\nu\) graph has slope \(h/e\), whereas the \(K_{\max}\) vs. \(\nu\) graph has slope \(h\).
  • Pitfall 4 — Wrong scaling for de Broglie wavelength: For a fixed accelerating potential \(V\), \(\lambda \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}\) (not \(1/V\)). Quadrupling \(V\) halves \(\lambda\).
  • Pitfall 5 — Intensity vs. Frequency: Intensity controls the rate of photon arrival (hence photocurrent); frequency controls the energy per photon (hence \(K_{\max}\) and \(V_0\)).

Concise Final Recap

  • Work Function & Threshold: \(\phi_0 = h\nu_0 = hc/\lambda_0\). Photoemission occurs only when \(\nu \ge \nu_0\) (or \(\lambda \le \lambda_0\)).
  • Einstein's Equation: \(K_{\max} = eV_0 = \frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\max}^2 = h\nu - \phi_0\).
  • Photon Properties: \(E = h\nu = hc/\lambda\), \(p = h/\lambda\), neutral, speed \(c\), photon flux \(N = P/E\).
  • Matter Waves: \(\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mqV}}\); for an electron, \(\lambda_e \approx \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}}\,\mathrm{Å}\).
