Chemical Kinetics

Reaction Rates, Rate Laws, Integrated Equations, Temperature Dependence, and Collision Theory

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Scope

While thermodynamics predicts whether a reaction is feasible (\(\Delta G < 0\) at constant temperature and pressure) and chemical equilibrium indicates the maximum extent to which reactants convert into products, neither tells us how fast the transformation occurs. Chemical kinetics bridges this gap by examining reaction speeds, the experimental factors that control them, and the molecular pathways through which reactants become products.

After mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between average and instantaneous reaction rates and relate them to stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Write experimental rate laws, determine overall and partial reaction orders, and derive units of the rate constant \(k\) for any order.
  • Differentiate between elementary and complex reactions, and contrast order with molecularity.
  • Derive and apply integrated rate equations and half-life expressions for zero-order and first-order reactions, including gas-phase pressure systems and pseudo-first-order processes.
  • Quantify the effect of temperature and catalysts using the Arrhenius equation, activation energy profiles, and Maxwell–Boltzmann energy distributions.
  • Explain reaction rates at the molecular level using collision theory, threshold energy, and steric orientation.

2. Rate of a Chemical Reaction

The rate of a chemical reaction measures how rapidly the molar concentration (or partial pressure, for gases) of a reactant decreases or that of a product increases per unit time. Consider a simple constant-volume transformation \(\mathrm{R} \rightarrow \mathrm{P}\):

  • Average Rate (\(r_{\mathrm{av}}\)): Measured over a finite time interval \(\Delta t = t_2 - t_1\): \[ r_{\mathrm{av}} = -\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{R}]}{\Delta t} = +\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{P}]}{\Delta t} \] The minus sign before \(\Delta[\mathrm{R}]\) ensures the rate is always reported as a positive quantity, because \([\mathrm{R}]_2 < [\mathrm{R}]_1\).
  • Instantaneous Rate (\(r_{\mathrm{inst}}\)): Obtained as the time interval approaches zero (\(\Delta t \rightarrow 0\)), representing the exact rate at a specific moment \(t\): \[ r_{\mathrm{inst}} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{P}]}{\mathrm{d}t} \] Graphically, \(r_{\mathrm{inst}}\) equals the magnitude of the slope of the tangent drawn to the concentration–time curve at time \(t\).

Stoichiometric Normalization and Units

When stoichiometric coefficients differ from unity, dividing each species' rate of change by its stoichiometric coefficient yields a single, unambiguous reaction rate. For a general balanced equation:

\[ a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightarrow c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D} \]
\[ \text{Rate} = -\frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{b}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{B}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{c}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{d}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{D}]}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

The standard SI-aligned unit of reaction rate is \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) (or \(\mathrm{M\,s^{-1}}\)). For gas-phase reactions monitored by partial pressure at constant temperature, concentration is proportional to pressure, so rate is commonly expressed in \(\mathrm{atm\,s^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{bar\,min^{-1}}\).

3. Rate Law, Rate Constant, and Reaction Order

At a fixed temperature, reaction rate generally depends on reactant concentrations. The mathematical equation connecting rate to molar concentrations of reactants is called the rate law (or differential rate equation):

\[ \text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^x[\mathrm{B}]^y \]

Here, \(k\) is the rate constant (specific reaction rate, equal to the reaction rate when all reactant concentrations are unity), and \(x\) and \(y\) are the partial orders with respect to \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\).

  • Overall Order (\(n\)): Defined as \(n = x + y\). It can be \(0, 1, 2, 3\), or even fractional/negative.
  • Experimental Nature: The exponents \(x\) and \(y\) cannot be deduced from stoichiometric coefficients of an overall balanced equation; they must be determined experimentally.

General Units of Rate Constant (\(k\))

For an \(n\)-th order reaction, \(\text{Rate} = k(\text{concentration})^n\), which gives:

\[ \text{Units of } k = \left(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\right)^{1-n}\mathrm{s^{-1}} = \mathrm{mol^{1-n}\,L^{n-1}\,s^{-1}} \]
Reaction Order (\(n\)) Differential Rate Law Units of \(k\) (Concentration in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) Units of \(k\) (Pressure in \(\mathrm{atm}\))
\(0\) (Zero order) \(\text{Rate} = k\) \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{atm\,s^{-1}}\)
\(1\) (First order) \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{R}]\) \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
\(2\) (Second order) \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{R}]^2\) \(\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{atm^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
\(3/2\) (Half-integer) \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{R}]^{3/2}\) \(\mathrm{L^{1/2}\,mol^{-1/2}\,s^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{atm^{-1/2}\,s^{-1}}\)

4. Elementary vs. Complex Reactions and Molecularity

An elementary reaction occurs in a single concerted step, whereas a complex reaction proceeds through a sequence of elementary steps (the reaction mechanism) involving short-lived intermediates that do not appear in the overall balanced equation.

Molecularity is the number of reacting species (atoms, ions, or molecules) that must collide simultaneously in an elementary step: unimolecular (\(1\)), bimolecular (\(2\)), or termolecular (\(3\)). Simultaneous collision of more than three species has negligible probability, so molecularity greater than \(3\) is practically unknown.

In a complex reaction, the slowest elementary step acts as the bottleneck and is called the rate-determining step (RDS). The overall rate law and order are governed by the RDS.

Property Order of Reaction Molecularity of Reaction
Basis Determined experimentally from the rate law. Theoretical concept based on the mechanism of an elementary step.
Allowed Values Can be zero, positive integer, negative, or fractional. Always a positive integer (\(1, 2,\) or \(3\)); never zero or fractional.
Applicability Applicable to both elementary and complex reactions. Defined only for elementary reactions; meaningless for overall complex reactions.

5. Integrated Rate Equations, Half-Life, and Pseudo-First-Order Kinetics

Integrating differential rate laws provides direct algebraic relationships between reactant concentration and elapsed time, avoiding the need to measure tangent slopes.

5.1 Zero-Order Reactions

For \(\mathrm{R} \rightarrow \mathrm{P}\) with \(\text{Rate} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = k[\mathrm{R}]^0 = k\), integrating from \([\mathrm{R}]_0\) at \(t = 0\) to \([\mathrm{R}]\) at time \(t\) gives:

\[ [\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_0 - kt \quad \text{and} \quad k = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0 - [\mathrm{R}]}{t} \]

The half-life (\(t_{1/2}\))—the time required for \([\mathrm{R}]\) to fall to \(\frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{R}]_0\)—is:

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{2k} \]

Thus, \(t_{1/2}\) for a zero-order reaction is directly proportional to initial concentration \([\mathrm{R}]_0\). Common examples include enzyme-catalysed reactions at substrate saturation and heterogeneous gas decompositions on metal surfaces at high pressure (such as \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) decomposition on hot platinum or \(\mathrm{HI}\) decomposition on gold).

5.2 First-Order Reactions

For \(\mathrm{R} \rightarrow \mathrm{P}\) with \(-\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = k[\mathrm{R}]\), separating variables and integrating yields:

\[ \ln[\mathrm{R}] = \ln[\mathrm{R}]_0 - kt \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad [\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_0 e^{-kt} \] \[ k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\left(\frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{[\mathrm{R}]}\right) \]

Substituting \([\mathrm{R}] = \frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{R}]_0\) at \(t = t_{1/2}\) gives:

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k} \]

For a first-order reaction, \(t_{1/2}\) is independent of initial concentration. All natural and artificial radioactive decays follow first-order kinetics. Useful NEET benchmarks for first-order reactions include \(t_{75\%} = 2\,t_{1/2}\), \(t_{87.5\%} = 3\,t_{1/2}\), \(t_{99\%} = 2\,t_{90\%}\), and \(t_{99.9\%} \approx 10\,t_{1/2}\).

Tutorial Diagram 2: Diagnostic straight-line plots for zero-order and first-order kinetics.

5.3 Gas-Phase First-Order Kinetics

For a gaseous reaction \(\mathrm{A(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{B(g)} + \mathrm{C(g)}\) at constant temperature and volume, if \(p_i\) is the initial pressure of \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(p_t\) is the total pressure at time \(t\):

\[ p_t = (p_i - x) + x + x = p_i + x \implies x = p_t - p_i \]

Hence \(p_{\mathrm{A}} = p_i - x = 2p_i - p_t\), and the rate constant becomes:

\[ k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\left(\frac{p_i}{2p_i - p_t}\right) \]

5.4 Pseudo-First-Order Reactions

A bimolecular reaction can behave as first-order when one reactant is present in large excess so that its concentration remains virtually constant throughout the reaction. Classic examples are acid-catalysed hydrolysis of ethyl acetate in excess water and inversion of cane sugar (sucrose) in aqueous acid.

6. Temperature Dependence, Activation Energy, and Catalysis

Reaction rates increase markedly with temperature; for many reactions near room temperature, a \(10\,\mathrm{K}\) (or \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)) rise roughly doubles the rate constant. Svante Arrhenius expressed this exponential sensitivity through the Arrhenius equation:

\[ k = A e^{-E_a / RT} \]

Here, \(A\) is the pre-exponential (frequency) factor, \(E_a\) is the activation energy (\(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\)), \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) is the universal gas constant, and \(T\) is absolute temperature in kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\)).

  • Linear Form: \(\ln k = -\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + \ln A\). Plotting \(\ln k\) versus \(1/T\) gives a straight line with slope \(= -E_a/R\) and \(y\)-intercept \(= \ln A\).
  • Two-Temperature Form: Comparing rate constants \(k_1\) at \(T_1\) and \(k_2\) at \(T_2\): \[ \log_{10}\left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{E_a}{2.303 R}\left(\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right) \]

Tutorial Diagram 3: Left — A catalyst lowers \(E_a\) without changing \(\Delta H\). Right — Raising temperature by \(10\,\mathrm{K}\) broadens the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, nearly doubling the shaded fraction of molecules with \(E \ge E_a\).

Role of a Catalyst

A catalyst accelerates a reaction without being permanently consumed by providing an alternate mechanism with a lower activation energy. Crucially, a catalyst:

  • Lowers \(E_a\) for both forward and backward reactions by the same amount.
  • Does not alter the enthalpy change (\(\Delta H\)), Gibbs energy change (\(\Delta G\)), or equilibrium constant (\(K_{\mathrm{eq}}\))—it simply helps the system reach equilibrium faster.

7. Collision Theory of Chemical Reactions

Developed by Max Trautz and William Lewis, collision theory models reactant molecules as hard spheres. For a bimolecular elementary reaction \(\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{B} \rightarrow \text{Products}\), a collision is effective (produces products) only when two criteria are satisfied simultaneously:

  1. Energy Criterion: Colliding molecules must possess kinetic energy equal to or greater than the threshold energy (where \(\text{Threshold Energy} = E_a + \text{Average Energy of Reactants}\)).
  2. Orientation Criterion: Molecules must collide with proper spatial orientation so that existing bonds can break and new bonds can form.

Introducing the steric or probability factor \(P\) alongside collision frequency \(Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\) gives the modified rate expression:

\[ \text{Rate} = P Z_{\mathrm{AB}} e^{-E_a / RT} \]

Worked Examples

Example 1: Relating Stoichiometric Rates of Disappearance and Appearance

Hypothetical assumption: In a constant-volume vessel, ammonia synthesis \(\mathrm{N_2(g)} + 3\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NH_3(g)}\) proceeds such that \(\mathrm{H_2}\) is consumed at \(6.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) over a short interval.

Problem: Calculate (a) the overall rate of reaction, (b) the rate of disappearance of \(\mathrm{N_2}\), and (c) the rate of appearance of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\).

Solution:

From stoichiometry:

\[ \text{Rate} = -\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{N_2}]}{\Delta t} = -\frac{1}{3}\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{H_2}]}{\Delta t} = +\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{NH_3}]}{\Delta t} \]

(a) \(\text{Rate} = \frac{1}{3}(6.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}) = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\).

(b) Rate of disappearance of \(\mathrm{N_2} = -\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{N_2}]}{\Delta t} = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\).

(c) Rate of appearance of \(\mathrm{NH_3} = +\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{NH_3}]}{\Delta t} = 2 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\).

Example 2: Determining Rate Law and Rate Constant from Initial Rates

Hypothetical assumption: Initial rate measurements for \(2\mathrm{P} + \mathrm{Q} \rightarrow \mathrm{R}\) yield the following dataset.

Run \([\mathrm{P}]_0\) (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) \([\mathrm{Q}]_0\) (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) Initial Rate (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\))
1\(0.20\)\(0.10\)\(1.2 \times 10^{-3}\)
2\(0.40\)\(0.10\)\(4.8 \times 10^{-3}\)
3\(0.20\)\(0.30\)\(3.6 \times 10^{-3}\)

Problem: Determine the rate law, overall order, and rate constant \(k\).

Solution:

Let \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{P}]^x[\mathrm{Q}]^y\).

Comparing Runs 1 and 2 (where \([\mathrm{Q}]\) is constant): doubling \([\mathrm{P}]\) quadruples the rate (\(4.8 \times 10^{-3} / 1.2 \times 10^{-3} = 4 = 2^x\)), so \(x = 2\).

Comparing Runs 1 and 3 (where \([\mathrm{P}]\) is constant): tripling \([\mathrm{Q}]\) triples the rate (\(3.6 \times 10^{-3} / 1.2 \times 10^{-3} = 3 = 3^y\)), so \(y = 1\).

Thus, \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{P}]^2[\mathrm{Q}]\), and overall order \(= 2 + 1 = 3\).

Using Run 1 to find \(k\):

\[ k = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-3}}{(0.20)^2(0.10)} = \frac{1.2 \times 10^{-3}}{4.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 0.30\,\mathrm{L^2\,mol^{-2}\,s^{-1}} \]
Example 3: Applying the First-Order Integrated Rate Law and Half-Life

Hypothetical assumption: A compound \(\mathrm{M}\) undergoes first-order decomposition with initial concentration \([\mathrm{M}]_0 = 0.80\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\), falling to \(0.20\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) in \(40\,\mathrm{min}\).

Problem: Find the rate constant \(k\), half-life \(t_{1/2}\), and time required for \(87.5\%\) completion.

Solution:

\[ k = \frac{2.303}{40\,\mathrm{min}}\log_{10}\left(\frac{0.80}{0.20}\right) = \frac{2.303 \times \log_{10} 4}{40} = \frac{2.303 \times 0.6021}{40} = 0.03467\,\mathrm{min^{-1}} \]

Half-life: \(t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{0.03467\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}} = 20\,\mathrm{min}\) (consistent with \(0.80 \rightarrow 0.40 \rightarrow 0.20\) taking two half-lives).

For \(87.5\%\) completion, \(12.5\%\) (\(= 1/8 = (1/2)^3\)) remains, which corresponds to \(3\) half-lives: \(t_{87.5\%} = 3 \times 20\,\mathrm{min} = 60\,\mathrm{min}\).

Example 4: Gas-Phase First-Order Kinetics Using Total Pressure

Hypothetical assumption: For \(\mathrm{A(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{B(g)} + \mathrm{C(g)}\) at constant temperature and volume, initial pressure \(p_i = 0.40\,\mathrm{atm}\), and total pressure at \(t = 200\,\mathrm{s}\) is \(p_t = 0.50\,\mathrm{atm}\).

Problem: Calculate the first-order rate constant \(k\).

Solution:

The partial pressure of \(\mathrm{A}\) at \(200\,\mathrm{s}\) is \(p_{\mathrm{A}} = 2p_i - p_t = 2(0.40) - 0.50 = 0.30\,\mathrm{atm}\).

\[ k = \frac{2.303}{200\,\mathrm{s}}\log_{10}\left(\frac{0.40}{0.30}\right) = \frac{2.303 \times 0.1249}{200\,\mathrm{s}} = 1.44 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \]
Example 5: Calculating Activation Energy from Two Temperatures

Hypothetical assumption: The rate constant of a reaction doubles when the temperature is raised from \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(310\,\mathrm{K}\). Take \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\).

Problem: Determine the activation energy \(E_a\) in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Solution:

\[ \log_{10}\left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{E_a}{2.303 R}\left(\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right) \] \[ \log_{10}(2) = \frac{E_a}{2.303 \times 8.314}\left(\frac{310 - 300}{300 \times 310}\right) \] \[ 0.3010 = \frac{E_a}{19.147} \times \frac{10}{93000} \implies E_a = 0.3010 \times 19.147 \times 9300 \approx 53598\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} = 53.6\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

Practice Question 1

For the reaction \(2\mathrm{A(g)} + \mathrm{B(g)} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{C(g)}\), if the rate of appearance of \(\mathrm{C}\) at a given instant is \(1.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\), what is the rate of disappearance of \(\mathrm{A}\) at the same instant?

  • (A) \(5.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(1.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(2.25 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Using stoichiometric normalization, \(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{3}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\). Therefore, \(-\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{2}{3}(1.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 2

A chemical reaction obeys the experimental rate law \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{X}]^{1/2}[\mathrm{Y}]^{3/2}\). What are the units of the rate constant \(k\) when concentration is measured in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) and time in \(\mathrm{s}\)?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{L^2\,mol^{-2}\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Overall order \(n = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{3}{2} = 2\). Substituting \(n = 2\) into \(\mathrm{mol^{1-n}\,L^{n-1}\,s^{-1}}\) gives \(\mathrm{mol^{-1}\,L\,s^{-1}}\) (i.e., \(\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)).

Practice Question 3

Which of the following statements regarding order and molecularity of a reaction is incorrect?

  • (A) Order is an experimental quantity, whereas molecularity is a theoretical property of an elementary step.
  • (B) Order of a reaction can be zero or fractional, whereas molecularity cannot be zero or fractional.
  • (C) For a complex reaction, overall order is determined by the slowest elementary step.
  • (D) Molecularity of a complex reaction is equal to the sum of stoichiometric coefficients of all reactants in the overall balanced equation.
Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: Molecularity is defined only for elementary reactions and has no meaning for an overall complex reaction. Each elementary step has its own molecularity (\(1, 2,\) or \(3\)).

Practice Question 4

A zero-order reaction starts with an initial reactant concentration of \(0.60\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) and has a rate constant \(k = 1.5 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\). Calculate the half-life of the reaction.

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (B) \(20\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (C) \(40\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (D) \(46.2\,\mathrm{s}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: For a zero-order reaction, \(t_{1/2} = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{2k} = \frac{0.60}{2 \times 1.5 \times 10^{-2}} = \frac{0.60}{0.030} = 20\,\mathrm{s}\).

Practice Question 5

For a first-order reaction, let \(t_{99\%}\) be the time required for \(99\%\) completion and \(t_{90\%}\) be the time required for \(90\%\) completion. Which relation is correct?

  • (A) \(t_{99\%} = 1.5\,t_{90\%}\)
  • (B) \(t_{99\%} = 2\,t_{90\%}\)
  • (C) \(t_{99\%} = 3\,t_{90\%}\)
  • (D) \(t_{99\%} = 10\,t_{90\%}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: \(t_{99\%} = \frac{2.303}{k}\log_{10}\left(\frac{100}{1}\right) = \frac{2.303 \times 2}{k}\), while \(t_{90\%} = \frac{2.303}{k}\log_{10}\left(\frac{100}{10}\right) = \frac{2.303 \times 1}{k}\). Thus, \(t_{99\%} = 2\,t_{90\%}\).

Practice Question 6

A plot of \(\log_{10}\left(\frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{[\mathrm{R}]}\right)\) versus time \(t\) for a first-order reaction yields a straight line passing through the origin with slope \(m\). The rate constant \(k\) is given by:

  • (A) \(k = m\)
  • (B) \(k = -m\)
  • (C) \(k = 2.303\,m\)
  • (D) \(k = \frac{m}{2.303}\)
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Rearranging the first-order integrated equation gives \(\log_{10}\left(\frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{[\mathrm{R}]}\right) = \left(\frac{k}{2.303}\right)t\). Hence, the slope is \(m = \frac{k}{2.303}\), so \(k = 2.303\,m\).

Practice Question 7

The acid-catalysed hydrolysis of ethyl acetate in a large excess of water follows pseudo-first-order kinetics because:

  • (A) The reaction is actually an elementary unimolecular process.
  • (B) Water acts as a catalyst and is regenerated in equal amounts.
  • (C) Water is present in large excess, so its concentration remains practically constant during the reaction.
  • (D) The activation energy with respect to water is zero.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Although the true rate law is \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5}][\mathrm{H_2O}]\), taking water in large excess keeps \([\mathrm{H_2O}]\) essentially constant, folding it into an apparent first-order rate constant \(k' = k[\mathrm{H_2O}]\).

Practice Question 8

For a reaction, a plot of \(\ln k\) versus \(\frac{1}{T}\) is a straight line with a slope of \(-2.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{K}\). What is the activation energy \(E_a\) of the reaction? (\(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\))

  • (A) \(166.3\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(83.14\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(382.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(20.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: From \(\ln k = -\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + \ln A\), the slope equals \(-\frac{E_a}{R}\). Thus, \(E_a = (2.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{K}) \times (8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}) = 166280\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx 166.3\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 9

Read the following two statements carefully and choose the correct option:

Statement I: A positive catalyst increases the rate of a reaction by providing an alternate pathway with lower activation energy.

Statement II: Adding a positive catalyst increases the equilibrium constant \(K_c\) of a reversible reaction.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: A catalyst lowers the activation energy barrier for both forward and reverse reactions equally. It helps attain equilibrium faster without altering \(\Delta G\) or the equilibrium constant \(K_c\).

Practice Question 10

Given below are two statements labelled as Assertion (A) and Reason (R):

Assertion (A): Not every collision between reactant molecules possessing energy equal to or greater than the threshold energy results in product formation.

Reason (R): To form products, colliding molecules must also have proper spatial orientation at the moment of impact.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: According to collision theory (\(\text{Rate} = P Z_{\mathrm{AB}} e^{-E_a/RT}\)), both sufficient energy (\(E \ge E_{\text{threshold}}\)) and proper steric orientation (accounted for by \(P\)) are necessary for an effective collision.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Forgetting Stoichiometric Divisors: Always divide \(-\Delta[\mathrm{R}]/\Delta t\) or \(+\Delta[\mathrm{P}]/\Delta t\) by the stoichiometric coefficient when reporting the overall rate of reaction.
  • Confusing Order with Stoichiometry: Never assume the exponents in the rate law equal the coefficients in a balanced equation unless the step is explicitly stated to be elementary.
  • Unit Mismatches in Arrhenius Calculations: \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) is in joules, whereas \(E_a\) is often given in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Always convert \(E_a\) to \(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\) (multiply by \(1000\)) and temperatures to kelvin before substituting.
  • Half-Life Dependence Trap: Zero-order \(t_{1/2}\) depends on \([\mathrm{R}]_0\) (\(t_{1/2} \propto [\mathrm{R}]_0\)), whereas first-order \(t_{1/2}\) is strictly independent of \([\mathrm{R}]_0\).
  • Catalyst Misconception: A catalyst never makes a non-spontaneous reaction spontaneous (\(\Delta G\) is unchanged) and never shifts equilibrium composition.

Concise Final Recap

  • Rate: \(-\frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{c}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\); units of \(k\) for order \(n\): \(\mathrm{mol^{1-n}\,L^{n-1}\,s^{-1}}\).
  • Zero-Order: \([\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_0 - kt\); \(t_{1/2} = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{2k}\); plot of \([\mathrm{R}]\) vs \(t\) has slope \(-k\).
  • First-Order: \(k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{[\mathrm{R}]}\); \(t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}\); plot of \(\log_{10}([\mathrm{R}]_0/[\mathrm{R}])\) vs \(t\) has slope \(k/2.303\).
  • Arrhenius & Collision Theory: \(k = A e^{-E_a / RT} = P Z_{\mathrm{AB}} e^{-E_a / RT}\); effective collisions require both \(E \ge E_{\text{threshold}}\) and proper orientation.
