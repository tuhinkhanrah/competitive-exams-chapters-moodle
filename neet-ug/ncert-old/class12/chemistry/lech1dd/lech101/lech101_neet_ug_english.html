The Solid State

Crystal Structure, Packing Efficiency, Unit Cell Calculations, Imperfections, and Electronic & Magnetic Properties

Learning Objectives and Overview

Unlike liquids and gases—collectively termed fluids because their constituent particles move freely—solids possess fixed particle positions and exhibit rigidity. Whether a substance exists as a solid under given temperature and pressure depends on the balance between intermolecular attractive forces (which draw particles together) and thermal energy (which drives particles apart). At sufficiently low thermal energy, particles cling to fixed mean positions and only oscillate about them.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish crystalline solids from amorphous solids using structural order, melting behavior, cleavage, and anisotropy.
  • Classify crystalline solids into molecular, ionic, metallic, and covalent (network) types based on binding forces.
  • Relate crystal lattices, motifs, unit cell parameters, and the 14 Bravais lattices across the 7 crystal systems.
  • Compute effective atoms per unit cell, void occupancy, packing efficiency, and crystal density.
  • Analyze stoichiometric, impurity, and non-stoichiometric point defects and their impact on density and colour.
  • Explain electrical conduction (band theory and doping) and magnetic behaviour in solids.

1. General Characteristics and Classification of Solids

1.1 Core Properties of the Solid State

  • Definite mass, volume, and shape arising from tightly packed particles at short interparticle distances.
  • Strong interparticle forces that restrict translational motion; particles only vibrate about fixed lattice sites.
  • Rigidity and incompressibility because negligible free space exists between adjacent particles.

1.2 Crystalline vs. Amorphous Solids

Solids are divided into crystalline and amorphous forms according to the spatial range of structural order:

Property Crystalline Solids (True Solids) Amorphous Solids (Pseudo Solids)
Particle Order Long-range and short-range order; periodic 3D repetition. Short-range order only; disordered over long distances.
Melting Point Sharp, characteristic melting temperature and definite enthalpy of fusion. Soften gradually over a temperature range; no definite enthalpy of fusion.
Cleavage Split with a sharp tool to yield plain, smooth newly generated surfaces. Break into pieces with irregular, curved surfaces.
Directional Behaviour Anisotropic: physical properties (refractive index, electrical resistance, shear strength) vary with measurement direction. Isotropic: physical properties have the same value in all directions due to random average arrangement.
Examples NaCl, quartz, diamond, iron, copper, benzoic acid, naphthalene. Quartz glass, rubber, plastics (PVC, Teflon, polyurethane), fibreglass, amorphous silicon.
Exam Nuance — Supercooled Liquids & Polycrystalline Solids: Amorphous solids resemble extremely viscous liquids and flow imperceptibly over long periods (hence called supercooled liquids; ancient glass panes can thicken slightly at the bottom or turn milky due to slow partial crystallization). Conversely, polycrystalline solids (such as most commercial metal samples) consist of many randomly oriented microscopic single crystals; although each individual crystallite is anisotropic, the bulk sample appears isotropic.

1.3 Classification of Crystalline Solids by Binding Forces

Category Constituent Particles Binding Forces Physical Nature & Conductivity Examples
Molecular (Non-polar) Atoms or non-polar molecules London / dispersion forces Soft, very low m.p., electrical insulators Ar, \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\), \(\mathrm{I_2}\), \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\), dry ice (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\))
Molecular (Polar) Polar molecules Dipole–dipole interactions Soft, low m.p., electrical insulators Solid \(\mathrm{HCl}\), solid \(\mathrm{SO_2}\), solid \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)
Molecular (H-bonded) Molecules with H attached to F, O, or N Hydrogen bonding Hard (at low T), low m.p., insulators \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (ice)
Ionic Cations and anions Coulombic (electrostatic) forces Hard but brittle, high m.p.; insulators in solid state, conductors when molten or aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{MgO}\), \(\mathrm{ZnS}\), \(\mathrm{CaF_2}\), \(\mathrm{LiBr}\)
Metallic Positive metal ions in a sea of delocalized electrons Metallic bonding Hard, malleable, ductile, lustrous; conductors in solid & molten states \(\mathrm{Fe}\), \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Ag}\), \(\mathrm{Mg}\), \(\mathrm{Rb}\), brass
Covalent (Network) Non-metal atoms Directional covalent bonds Very hard, extremely high m.p., insulators (Exception: Graphite is soft & conducts) Diamond, \(\mathrm{SiC}\), \(\mathrm{SiO_2}\) (quartz), \(\mathrm{AlN}\), Graphite

Why Graphite is an Exception: In diamond, each carbon is \(sp^3\)-hybridized and bonded tetrahedrally to four neighbours (\(\mathrm{C-C} = 154\,\mathrm{pm}\)), making it rigid and insulating. In graphite, each carbon is \(sp^2\)-hybridized and bonded to three neighbours in hexagonal sheets (\(\mathrm{C-C} = 141.5\,\mathrm{pm}\)). The fourth valence electron is delocalized between layers (interlayer spacing \(340\,\mathrm{pm}\)), enabling electrical conduction, while weak interlayer forces allow sheets to slide easily, making graphite a solid lubricant.

2. Crystal Lattices, Unit Cells, and Bravais Lattices

A crystal lattice (space lattice) is an infinite 3D array of abstract points representing identical environments. Associating a structural unit (atom, ion, or molecule)—called a basis or motif—with every lattice point generates the actual crystal structure.

The smallest repeating 3D building block whose translational repetition constructs the entire lattice is the unit cell. A unit cell is fully specified by six parameters: three edge lengths (\(a, b, c\)) and three interaxial angles (\(\alpha\) between \(b\) and \(c\); \(\beta\) between \(a\) and \(c\); \(\gamma\) between \(a\) and \(b\)).

2.1 Seven Crystal Systems and 14 Bravais Lattices

Unit cells are either primitive (P)—particles only at the 8 corners—or centred: body-centred (I) (additional particle at body centre), face-centred (F) (additional particles at all 6 face centres), or end-centred (C) (additional particles at any two opposite face centres). Bravais proved that only 14 distinct 3D lattices exist across 7 crystal systems:

Crystal System Bravais Variations Edge Lengths Axial Angles Representative Examples
Cubic P, I, F (3) \(a = b = c\) \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90°\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{ZnS}\), \(\mathrm{Cu}\)
Tetragonal P, I (2) \(a = b \neq c\) \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90°\) White tin, \(\mathrm{SnO_2}\), \(\mathrm{TiO_2}\), \(\mathrm{CaSO_4}\)
Orthorhombic P, I, F, C (4) \(a \neq b \neq c\) \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90°\) Rhombic sulphur, \(\mathrm{KNO_3}\), \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\)
Hexagonal P (1) \(a = b \neq c\) \(\alpha = \beta = 90°, \gamma = 120°\) Graphite, \(\mathrm{ZnO}\), \(\mathrm{CdS}\)
Rhombohedral (Trigonal) P (1) \(a = b = c\) \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma \neq 90°\) Calcite (\(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\)), cinnabar (\(\mathrm{HgS}\))
Monoclinic P, C (2) \(a \neq b \neq c\) \(\alpha = \gamma = 90°, \beta \neq 90°\) Monoclinic sulphur, \(\mathrm{Na_2SO_4\cdot 10H_2O}\)
Triclinic P (1) \(a \neq b \neq c\) \(\alpha \neq \beta \neq \gamma \neq 90°\) \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\), \(\mathrm{CuSO_4\cdot 5H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{H_3BO_3}\)

Memory Tip for NEET: Cubic is the most symmetrical system; Triclinic is the most unsymmetrical system; Orthorhombic has all four Bravais variations (P, I, F, C).

2.2 Effective Number of Atoms per Cubic Unit Cell (\(z\))

Because particles on boundaries are shared among neighbouring cells, their fractional contributions to a single cubic unit cell are: Corner = \(1/8\) (shared by 8 cubes), Face-centre = \(1/2\) (shared by 2 cubes), Edge-centre = \(1/4\) (shared by 4 cubes), and Body-centre = \(1\) (unshared).

  • Simple Cubic (SC / Primitive): \(z = 8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1\) atom.
  • Body-Centred Cubic (BCC): \(z = \left(8 \times \frac{1}{8}\right) + (1 \times 1) = 2\) atoms.
  • Face-Centred Cubic (FCC): \(z = \left(8 \times \frac{1}{8}\right) + \left(6 \times \frac{1}{2}\right) = 4\) atoms.

3. Close Packing, Voids, and Packing Efficiency

3.1 Stacking of Identical Hard Spheres

  • 1D Close Packing: Spheres aligned in a single row; coordination number (CN) = 2.
  • 2D Close Packing:
    • Square close packing (AAA...): Rows aligned vertically and horizontally; CN = 4; packing efficiency \(\approx 52.4\%\) (in 3D AAA... stacking gives Simple Cubic, CN = 6).
    • Hexagonal close packing (ABAB...): Second row sits in depressions of the first; CN = 6; leaves triangular voids.
  • 3D Close Packing from 2D Hexagonal Layers: Placing a second hexagonal layer B into the depressions of layer A creates two types of interstitial voids:
    • Tetrahedral void (T): Surrounded by 4 spheres (3 in one layer, 1 above/below).
    • Octahedral void (O): Surrounded by 6 spheres (3 in layer A with apex up, 3 in layer B with apex down).
    Covering tetrahedral voids with the third layer reproduces layer A (ABAB... pattern \(\rightarrow\) Hexagonal Close Packing, HCP, found in \(\mathrm{Mg}, \mathrm{Zn}\)). Covering octahedral voids places the third layer at new positions C (ABCABC... pattern \(\rightarrow\) Cubic Close Packing, CCP / FCC, found in \(\mathrm{Cu}, \mathrm{Ag}\)). Both HCP and CCP have CN = 12 and 74% packing efficiency.
Void Count Rule: For \(N\) close-packed spheres in HCP or CCP:
\(\text{Number of Octahedral Voids} = N\)   |   \(\text{Number of Tetrahedral Voids} = 2N\)

Locations of Voids in an FCC (CCP) Unit Cell (\(z = 4\)):

  • 8 Tetrahedral Voids: One at the centre of each of the 8 mini-cubes (octants) dividing the unit cell, located at \(1/4\) of the body diagonal from each corner (total \(= 8\)).
  • 4 Octahedral Voids: One at the body centre (\(1 \times 1 = 1\)) and twelve at the edge centres (\(12 \times \frac{1}{4} = 3\)), giving \(1 + 3 = 4\).

3.2 Packing Efficiency and Density Formula

Packing efficiency is the percentage of unit cell volume occupied by constituent spheres:

\[ \text{Packing Efficiency} = \frac{z \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3}{a^3} \times 100\% \]
Cubic Lattice \(z\) Edge–Radius Relation Coordination Number Packing Efficiency Empty Space (Voids)
Simple Cubic (SC) 1 \(a = 2r\) 6 \(52.4\%\) \(47.6\%\)
Body-Centred (BCC) 2 \(\sqrt{3}\,a = 4r \implies r = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a\) 8 \(68.0\%\) \(32.0\%\)
Face-Centred (FCC / CCP) 4 \(\sqrt{2}\,a = 4r \implies r = \frac{a}{2\sqrt{2}}\) 12 \(74.0\%\) \(26.0\%\)
Unit Cell Density Equation:
\[ d = \frac{z \cdot M}{a^3 \cdot N_A} \] where \(d\) is density (\(\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)), \(z\) is atoms per unit cell, \(M\) is molar mass (\(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)), \(a\) is edge length (\(\mathrm{cm}\), note \(1\,\mathrm{pm} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{cm}\)), and \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\).

4. Imperfections in Solids (Point Defects)

Real crystals are never ideal; irregularities around a point or atom are called point defects. They fall into three major classes:

4.1 Stoichiometric (Intrinsic / Thermodynamic) Defects

These defects preserve the overall chemical formula (stoichiometry) of the solid.

  • Vacancy Defect (Non-ionic solids): Some lattice sites are empty (often induced by heating). Decreases density.
  • Interstitial Defect (Non-ionic solids): Extra atoms/molecules occupy interstitial spaces. Increases density.
  • Frenkel Defect (Dislocation Defect in Ionic Solids): A smaller ion (typically the cation) leaves its normal lattice site and occupies an interstitial site. Electrical neutrality is maintained and density remains unchanged. Shown by ionic crystals with a large size difference between cation and anion (low CN), e.g., \(\mathrm{ZnS}\), \(\mathrm{AgCl}\), \(\mathrm{AgBr}\), \(\mathrm{AgI}\).
  • Schottky Defect (Pair Vacancy in Ionic Solids): Equal numbers of cations and anions are missing from their lattice sites to preserve electrical neutrality. Density decreases. Shown by ionic solids where cation and anion have nearly similar sizes (high CN), e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{KCl}\), \(\mathrm{CsCl}\), \(\mathrm{AgBr}\).

Key NEET Fact: \(\mathrm{AgBr}\) exhibits both Frenkel and Schottky defects.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Comparison of Schottky (paired vacancy) and Frenkel (dislocation) defects.

4.2 Impurity Defects

Crystallizing molten \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) with a small amount of \(\mathrm{SrCl_2}\) causes \(\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}\) ions to substitute for \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) ions. To maintain electrical neutrality, each \(\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}\) ion replaces two \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) ions—occupying one site and leaving the other as a cation vacancy. Thus, the number of cation vacancies equals the number of \(\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}\) ions introduced. A solid solution of \(\mathrm{CdCl_2}\) in \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) behaves identically.

4.3 Non-Stoichiometric Defects

  • Metal Excess via Anionic Vacancies (F-Centres): Heating alkali halides in their own metal vapour (e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) in \(\mathrm{Na}\) vapour) causes metal atoms to deposit on the surface, ionize to \(\mathrm{Na^+}\), and release electrons that diffuse into anionic vacancies. Unpaired electrons trapped in anionic vacancies are called F-centres (Farbzentren). They absorb visible light and impart colour:
    • \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) with excess \(\mathrm{Na}\) \(\rightarrow\) Yellow
    • \(\mathrm{LiCl}\) with excess \(\mathrm{Li}\) \(\rightarrow\) Pink
    • \(\mathrm{KCl}\) with excess \(\mathrm{K}\) \(\rightarrow\) Violet (lilac)
  • Metal Excess via Extra Interstitial Cations: White \(\mathrm{ZnO}\) loses oxygen on heating and turns yellow: \[ \mathrm{ZnO} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{Zn}^{2+} + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{O}_2 + 2e^- \] The excess \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\) ions and electrons occupy neighbouring interstitial sites, forming \(\mathrm{Zn}_{1+x}\mathrm{O}\).
  • Metal Deficiency Defect: Transition metal oxides such as \(\mathrm{FeO}\) (typically \(\mathrm{Fe}_{0.95}\mathrm{O}\), ranging from \(\mathrm{Fe}_{0.93}\mathrm{O}\) to \(\mathrm{Fe}_{0.96}\mathrm{O}\)) or \(\mathrm{Ni}_{0.98}\mathrm{O}\) have missing \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) ions whose positive charge deficit is balanced by \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) ions.

5. Electrical and Magnetic Properties of Solids

5.1 Band Theory and Classification by Conductivity

  • Conductors (\(10^4\text{ to }10^7\,\Omega^{-1}\mathrm{m}^{-1}\)): Valence band is partially filled or overlaps with the unoccupied conduction band, allowing free electron flow.
  • Insulators (\(10^{-20}\text{ to }10^{-10}\,\Omega^{-1}\mathrm{m}^{-1}\)): Large forbidden energy gap between the filled valence band and empty conduction band.
  • Semiconductors (\(10^{-6}\text{ to }10^4\,\Omega^{-1}\mathrm{m}^{-1}\)): Small energy gap; conductivity increases with temperature as more electrons jump into the conduction band (intrinsic semiconductors: \(\mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{Ge}\)).

5.2 Doping and Compound Semiconductors

  • n-Type Semiconductors: Group 14 element (\(\mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{Ge}\)) doped with Group 15 electron-rich impurity (\(\mathrm{P}, \mathrm{As}\)). Four valence electrons form covalent bonds; the fifth electron is delocalized and carries negative charge.
  • p-Type Semiconductors: Group 14 element doped with Group 13 electron-deficient impurity (\(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{Al}, \mathrm{Ga}, \mathrm{In}\)). Missing fourth electron creates a positive electron hole; adjacent electrons jump to fill it, causing apparent positive-hole migration.
  • Compound Semiconductors: Group 13–15 compounds (\(\mathrm{InSb}, \mathrm{AlP}, \mathrm{GaAs}\)) and Group 12–16 compounds (\(\mathrm{ZnS}, \mathrm{CdS}, \mathrm{CdSe}, \mathrm{HgTe}\)) simulate an average valence of 4. Transition metal oxides like \(\mathrm{TiO}, \mathrm{CrO_2}, \mathrm{ReO_3}\) show metallic conductivity (\(\mathrm{ReO_3}\) resembles copper).

5.3 Magnetic Properties

Electron orbital and spin motions generate tiny magnetic moments measured in Bohr magnetons (\(\mu_B = 9.27 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}\)).

Type Electronic / Domain Origin Field Behaviour Examples
Diamagnetic All electrons paired; net moment = 0 Weakly repelled by magnetic field \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{C_6H_6}\)
Paramagnetic One or more unpaired electrons Weakly attracted; loses magnetism when field removed \(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\), \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\), \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\)
Ferromagnetic Domains align parallel in same direction Strongly attracted; forms permanent magnets \(\mathrm{Fe}\), \(\mathrm{Co}\), \(\mathrm{Ni}\), \(\mathrm{Gd}\), \(\mathrm{CrO_2}\)
Antiferromagnetic Equal domains aligned antiparallel Net magnetic moment = 0 \(\mathrm{MnO}\)
Ferrimagnetic Unequal parallel & antiparallel domains Weakly attracted; becomes paramagnetic on heating \(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\), \(\mathrm{MgFe_2O_4}\), \(\mathrm{ZnFe_2O_4}\)

Worked Examples

Example 1: Determining Empirical Formula from Void Occupancy

Problem (Assumed Scenario): In an ionic crystal, anions \(Y\) form a cubic close-packed (CCP) lattice. Cations \(X\) occupy one-fourth of the tetrahedral voids and one-half of the octahedral voids. Determine the empirical formula of the compound.

Solution:

  1. Let the number of \(Y\) ions in the CCP lattice be \(N\).
  2. Number of octahedral voids \(= N\); number of tetrahedral voids \(= 2N\).
  3. Number of \(X\) ions occupying voids: \[ N_X = \frac{1}{4}(2N) + \frac{1}{2}(N) = 0.5N + 0.5N = N \]
  4. The ratio \(N_X : N_Y = N : N = 1 : 1\). Hence, the empirical formula is \(XY\).
Example 2: Identifying Unit Cell Type from Density

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A hypothetical metal \(M\) (molar mass \(M = 60.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) crystallizes in a cubic unit cell with edge length \(a = 400\,\mathrm{pm}\) and measured density \(d = 6.23\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\). Identify the type of cubic unit cell. (Take \(N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\).)

Solution:

  1. Convert edge length to centimetres: \(a = 400\,\mathrm{pm} = 4.00 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{cm}\), so \(a^3 = 64.0 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{cm^3}\).
  2. Rearrange the density equation for \(z\): \[ z = \frac{d \cdot a^3 \cdot N_A}{M} = \frac{6.23 \times 64.0 \times 10^{-24} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}}{60.0} = \frac{240.03}{60.0} \approx 4 \]
  3. Since \(z = 4\), the metal crystallizes in a face-centred cubic (FCC / CCP) unit cell.
Example 3: Calculating Atomic Radius in a BCC Lattice

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A hypothetical element \(E\) (molar mass \(M = 90.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) crystallizes in a body-centred cubic (BCC) lattice with a density of \(11.11\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\). Calculate its atomic radius. (Take \(N_A = 6.00 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\).)

Solution:

  1. For a BCC unit cell, \(z = 2\). Solve for \(a^3\): \[ a^3 = \frac{z \cdot M}{d \cdot N_A} = \frac{2 \times 90.0}{11.11 \times 6.00 \times 10^{23}} \approx 27.0 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{cm^3} \]
  2. Taking the cube root gives \(a = 3.00 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{cm} = 300\,\mathrm{pm}\).
  3. In a BCC lattice, \(r = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a\): \[ r = \frac{1.732 \times 300\,\mathrm{pm}}{4} = 129.9\,\mathrm{pm} \]
Example 4: Quantifying Cation Vacancies from Aliovalent Doping

Problem (Assumed Scenario): Pure \(\mathrm{KCl}\) is doped with \(2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\%}\) of \(\mathrm{CaCl_2}\). Calculate the concentration of cation vacancies per mole of \(\mathrm{KCl}\).

Solution:

  1. \(2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\%}\) means \(100\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{KCl}\) contains \(2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\), so \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{KCl}\) contains \(2.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\).
  2. Each \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) ion replaces two \(\mathrm{K^+}\) ions, creating exactly one cation vacancy.
  3. Number of cation vacancies per mole of \(\mathrm{KCl}\): \[ 2.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 1.2044 \times 10^{18}\,\mathrm{vacancies\,mol^{-1}} \]
Example 5: Oxidation State Fractions in Non-Stoichiometric Iron Oxide

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A sample of non-stoichiometric wüstite has the composition \(\mathrm{Fe}_{0.92}\mathrm{O}_{1.00}\). What percentage of total iron exists as \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) ions?

Solution:

  1. Per \(1.00\,\mathrm{O^{2-}}\) ion (total negative charge \(= -2\)), there are \(0.92\) iron ions. Let \(x\) be the number of \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) ions and \((0.92 - x)\) be the number of \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) ions.
  2. By charge neutrality: \[ 3x + 2(0.92 - x) = 2 \implies x + 1.84 = 2 \implies x = 0.16 \]
  3. Percentage of iron as \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\): \[ \frac{0.16}{0.92} \times 100\% \approx 17.39\% \]

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements regarding crystalline and amorphous solids is incorrect?

  • (A) Crystalline solids are anisotropic because particle arrangement differs along different directions.
  • (B) Amorphous solids are often called supercooled liquids because they possess short-range order and flow very slowly.
  • (C) Polycrystalline metal samples appear isotropic even though individual crystallites are anisotropic.
  • (D) Amorphous solids have a sharp melting point and a definite enthalpy of fusion.
Answer: (D)

Explanation: Crystalline solids have a sharp melting point and definite enthalpy of fusion, whereas amorphous solids soften gradually over a range of temperature and lack a definite enthalpy of fusion.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which crystal system exhibits all four Bravais lattice variations (primitive, body-centred, face-centred, and end-centred)?

  • (A) Cubic
  • (B) Tetragonal
  • (C) Orthorhombic
  • (D) Monoclinic
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Orthorhombic (\(a \neq b \neq c\), \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90°\)) is the only crystal system that has all four Bravais lattices (P, I, F, C). Cubic has 3 (P, I, F), Tetragonal has 2 (P, I), and Monoclinic has 2 (P, C).

Practice Question 3 (Numerical MCQ)

In a hypothetical cubic solid made of elements \(P\) and \(Q\), atoms of \(Q\) occupy all corners and face centres of the cube, while atoms of \(P\) occupy all edge centres and the body centre. What is the simplest formula of the compound?

  • (A) \(PQ\)
  • (B) \(P_2Q\)
  • (C) \(PQ_2\)
  • (D) \(P_3Q_4\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Number of \(Q\) atoms per unit cell \(= 8(\text{corners}) \times \frac{1}{8} + 6(\text{faces}) \times \frac{1}{2} = 1 + 3 = 4\). Number of \(P\) atoms per unit cell \(= 12(\text{edges}) \times \frac{1}{4} + 1(\text{body centre}) \times 1 = 3 + 1 = 4\). Thus \(P:Q = 4:4 = 1:1\), giving \(PQ\).

Practice Question 4 (Numerical MCQ)

A metal crystallizes in a face-centred cubic (FCC) lattice with a unit cell edge length of \(353.5\,\mathrm{pm}\). What is the atomic radius of the metal? (Take \(\sqrt{2} = 1.414\).)

  • (A) \(125.0\,\mathrm{pm}\)
  • (B) \(153.1\,\mathrm{pm}\)
  • (C) \(176.8\,\mathrm{pm}\)
  • (D) \(250.0\,\mathrm{pm}\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: For an FCC unit cell, \(r = \frac{a}{2\sqrt{2}} = \frac{353.5\,\mathrm{pm}}{2 \times 1.414} = \frac{353.5}{2.828} = 125.0\,\mathrm{pm}\).

Practice Question 5 (Numerical MCQ)

An ionic compound is formed by cations \(A\) and anions \(B\). Anions \(B\) form a hexagonal close-packed (HCP) lattice, and cations \(A\) occupy \(75\%\) of the octahedral voids. What is the formula of the compound?

  • (A) \(A_3B_4\)
  • (B) \(A_4B_3\)
  • (C) \(A_2B_3\)
  • (D) \(A_3B_2\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: For \(N\) anions of \(B\), there are \(N\) octahedral voids. Cations \(A\) occupy \(0.75N = \frac{3}{4}N\) octahedral voids. Ratio \(A : B = \frac{3}{4} : 1 = 3 : 4\), so the formula is \(A_3B_4\).

Practice Question 6 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Statement I: \(\mathrm{AgBr}\) exhibits both Frenkel and Schottky defects.
Statement II: Schottky defect decreases the density of an ionic crystal, whereas Frenkel defect leaves the density unchanged.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
  • (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  • (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: \(\mathrm{AgBr}\) is a classic example showing both Frenkel and Schottky defects. Schottky defect removes equal numbers of cations and anions from the crystal, lowering density, whereas Frenkel defect merely relocates a cation to an interstitial site, preserving density.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When \(\mathrm{KCl}\) crystals are heated in an atmosphere of potassium vapour, they acquire a violet (lilac) colour. This colouration is caused by:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{K^+}\) ions occupying interstitial sites
  • (B) Schottky pair vacancies
  • (C) Electrons trapped in anionic vacancies (F-centres)
  • (D) Cation vacancies formed by \(\mathrm{K}\) oxidation
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Heating \(\mathrm{KCl}\) in potassium vapour creates metal excess defect due to anionic vacancies. Unpaired electrons diffuse into these anionic vacancies to form F-centres, which absorb visible light and impart a violet/lilac colour.

  • (D) (i)–(s), (ii)–(r), (iii)–(q), (iv)–(p)

Explanation: \(\mathrm{GaAs}\) is a 13–15 semiconductor; \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) is a 12–16 compound; \(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\) (magnetite) is ferrimagnetic; \(\mathrm{MnO}\) is antiferromagnetic.

Practice Question 9 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): Doping pure silicon with indium produces a p-type semiconductor.
Reason (R): Indium belongs to Group 13 and has three valence electrons, creating an electron vacancy (hole) in the silicon lattice.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Silicon (Group 14, 4 valence electrons) doped with a Group 13 element like indium (3 valence electrons) lacks one electron for the fourth covalent bond, creating a positive hole and yielding a p-type semiconductor.

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

What is the total empty (void) space percentage in a body-centred cubic (BCC) unit cell?

  • (A) \(26\%\)
  • (B) \(32\%\)
  • (C) \(47.6\%\)
  • (D) \(68\%\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: The packing efficiency of a BCC lattice is \(68\%\). Therefore, the unoccupied void space is \(100\% - 68\% = 32\%\).

Common Pitfalls and NEET Revision Checklist

Avoid These Frequent Exam Mistakes:
  • Unit Conversion in Density: Always convert edge length \(a\) from \(\mathrm{pm}\) to \(\mathrm{cm}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{pm} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{cm}\)) when density is in \(\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\). Forgetting the factor of \(10^{-30}\) in \(a^3\) is a classic error.
  • Packing Efficiency vs. Void Percentage: Read carefully whether the question asks for occupied space (SC: 52.4%, BCC: 68%, CCP/HCP: 74%) or empty space (SC: 47.6%, BCC: 32%, CCP/HCP: 26%).
  • Graphite & \(\mathrm{ReO_3}\) Exceptions: Graphite is a covalent network solid that is soft and conducts electricity; \(\mathrm{ReO_3}\) is a transition metal oxide that behaves like metallic copper.
  • Frenkel vs. Schottky Density: Frenkel defect does not change density; Schottky defect decreases density.
  • Aliovalent Doping Count: One \(\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}\) or \(\mathrm{Cd^{2+}}\) ion creates one cation vacancy (not two), even though it replaces two univalent cations.

Concise Final Recap

  • Order & Bonding: Crystalline solids have long-range order, sharp melting points, and anisotropy; amorphous solids have short-range order and isotropy.
  • Lattices: 7 crystal systems give 14 Bravais lattices. Cubic \(z\): SC \(= 1\), BCC \(= 2\), FCC \(= 4\).
  • Close Packing: HCP (ABAB...) and CCP/FCC (ABCABC...) both have CN = 12 and 74% efficiency. For \(N\) spheres, octahedral voids \(= N\) and tetrahedral voids \(= 2N\).
  • Defects: Stoichiometric (Schottky \(\downarrow d\), Frenkel \(d\) unchanged), Impurity (cation vacancies), Non-stoichiometric (F-centres, \(\mathrm{Zn}_{1+x}\mathrm{O}\), \(\mathrm{Fe}_{0.95}\mathrm{O}\)).
  • Electronic & Magnetic: Semiconductors conduct better on heating; Group 15 doping \(\rightarrow\) n-type, Group 13 doping \(\rightarrow\) p-type. Ferromagnetic domains align parallel; antiferromagnetic cancel; ferrimagnetic have unequal antiparallel domains.
