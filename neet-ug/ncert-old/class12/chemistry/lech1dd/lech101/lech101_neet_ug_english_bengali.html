Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Solids owe their rigidity and definite shape to strong interparticle attractions that lock constituent particles (atoms, ions, or molecules) into fixed mean positions, permitting only thermal vibrations around those sites. Whether a substance adopts a solid, liquid, or gaseous state at a given temperature and pressure depends on the competition between cohesive intermolecular forces and disruptive thermal energy.

  • Distinguish crystalline solids from amorphous solids using particle order, melting behaviour, cleavage, and directional properties.
  • Classify crystalline solids into molecular, ionic, metallic, and covalent (network) types based on binding forces.
  • Relate unit cell geometry, Bravais lattices, and effective particle count (\(Z\)) to packing efficiency and crystal density.
  • Determine chemical formulas from tetrahedral and octahedral void occupancy.
  • Analyze stoichiometric, impurity, and non-stoichiometric point defects and their impact on density and colour.
  • Explain electrical conduction (band theory, doping) and magnetic classes (diamagnetic, paramagnetic, ferromagnetic, antiferromagnetic, ferrimagnetic).

Crystalline vs. Amorphous Solids

Solids are divided into two broad structural classes according to the spatial range over which their constituent particles repeat regularly:

  • Crystalline Solids (True Solids): Possess long-range order—a three-dimensional periodic pattern extending across the entire crystal. They melt at a sharp, characteristic temperature, possess a definite enthalpy of fusion, and split along smooth, flat planes when cleaved with a sharp blade. Examples: \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), quartz (\(\mathrm{SiO_2}\)), diamond, copper.
  • Amorphous Solids (Pseudo Solids / Supercooled Liquids): Possess only short-range order—small, scattered pockets of local regularity without global periodicity. They soften gradually over a temperature range, lack a fixed enthalpy of fusion, and fracture into irregular surfaces. Over long timescales, they can flow very slowly (e.g., ancient glass panes becoming thicker at the bottom or turning milky due to partial crystallization). Examples: glass, rubber, plastics, amorphous silicon (used in photovoltaics).
Anisotropy vs. Isotropy (Key NEET Concept): Crystalline solids are anisotropic because physical properties such as refractive index, electrical resistance, and shear strength vary with measurement direction—different directions intercept different atomic arrangements. Amorphous solids are isotropic because their disordered, liquid-like averaging makes every direction statistically equivalent. Note: Polycrystalline metals appear isotropic macroscopically only because countless tiny anisotropic crystallites are randomly oriented.

Classification of Crystalline Solids

Based on the dominant interparticle binding forces, crystalline solids fall into four major categories:

Category Particles & Forces Physical & Electrical Traits Key Examples
Molecular
(Non-polar, Polar, H-bonded)		 Molecules held by London dispersion, dipole–dipole, or hydrogen bonds Soft (H-bonded ice is hard), low melting point, electrical insulators \(\mathrm{Ar}\), \(\mathrm{I_2}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{HCl}\), \(\mathrm{SO_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (ice)
Ionic Cations & anions bound by strong electrostatic (Coulombic) forces Hard and brittle, high melting point; insulators as solids, conductors when molten or aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{MgO}\), \(\mathrm{ZnS}\), \(\mathrm{CaF_2}\)
Metallic Positive metal kernels in a sea of delocalised mobile electrons Malleable, ductile, lustrous, high thermal & electrical conductivity (solid & molten) \(\mathrm{Fe}\), \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Ag}\), \(\mathrm{Mg}\)
Covalent / Network Atoms linked by directional covalent bonds into giant 3D or 2D networks Very hard, extremely high melting point, insulators (Exception: Graphite) Diamond, \(\mathrm{SiC}\), \(\mathrm{SiO_2}\) (quartz), \(\mathrm{AlN}\), Graphite

Graphite vs. Diamond Exception: In diamond, each carbon is \(sp^3\)-hybridised and tetrahedrally bonded to four neighbours, making it extremely hard and an electrical insulator. In graphite, each carbon is \(sp^2\)-hybridised and bonded to three neighbours in hexagonal sheets; the fourth valence electron is delocalised between layers, making graphite a good electrical conductor, while weak interlayer forces allow sheets to slide (solid lubricant).

Crystal Lattices, Unit Cells, and Effective Atom Count

A crystal lattice (space lattice) is an infinite 3D array of points representing identical environments around structural motifs (atoms, ions, or molecules). The smallest repeating 3D building block that generates the entire lattice by pure translation is the unit cell, defined by six parameters: three edge lengths (\(a, b, c\)) and three interaxial angles (\(\alpha\) between \(b, c\); \(\beta\) between \(a, c\); \(\gamma\) between \(a, b\)).

Based on symmetry, crystals belong to 7 crystal systems, which yield 14 Bravais lattices when primitive (P), body-centred (I), face-centred (F), and end-centred (C) variations are included:

Crystal System Variations Edge Lengths Axial Angles
Cubic Primitive, Body-centred, Face-centred (3) \(a = b = c\) \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90°\)
Tetragonal Primitive, Body-centred (2) \(a = b \neq c\) \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90°\)
Orthorhombic Primitive, Body-centred, Face-centred, End-centred (4) \(a \neq b \neq c\) \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma = 90°\)
Hexagonal Primitive (1) \(a = b \neq c\) \(\alpha = \beta = 90°,\; \gamma = 120°\)
Rhombohedral (Trigonal) Primitive (1) \(a = b = c\) \(\alpha = \beta = \gamma \neq 90°\)
Monoclinic Primitive, End-centred (2) \(a \neq b \neq c\) \(\alpha = \gamma = 90°,\; \beta \neq 90°\)
Triclinic Primitive (1) \(a \neq b \neq c\) \(\alpha \neq \beta \neq \gamma \neq 90°\)

Contribution of Lattice Sites to a Cubic Unit Cell

  • Corner: Shared by 8 adjacent cubes \(\rightarrow\) contribution \(=\frac{1}{8}\).
  • Face-centre: Shared by 2 adjacent cubes \(\rightarrow\) contribution \(=\frac{1}{2}\).
  • Edge-centre: Shared by 4 adjacent cubes \(\rightarrow\) contribution \(=\frac{1}{4}\).
  • Body-centre: Wholly inside 1 cube \(\rightarrow\) contribution \(=1\).

Close Packing, Interstitial Voids, and Packing Efficiency

Treating constituent particles as identical hard spheres of radius \(r\), maximum packing density is achieved through close packing:

  • 1D Close Packing: Spheres touch in a line; coordination number (CN) \(= 2\).
  • 2D Close Packing:
    • Square close packing (AAA...): Rows align directly; CN \(= 4\).
    • Hexagonal close packing (ABAB...): Spheres sit in depressions of the neighbouring row; CN \(= 6\), leaving triangular voids.
  • 3D Close Packing from 2D Hexagonal Layers: Placing layer B in the depressions of layer A creates two types of interstitial voids:
    • Tetrahedral Void (T): Surrounded by 4 spheres (a sphere of layer B over a triangular void of layer A, or vice versa).
    • Octahedral Void (O): Surrounded by 6 spheres (two non-overlapping triangular voids facing opposite directions in layers A and B).
For \(N\) close-packed spheres: \(\quad \text{Number of Octahedral Voids} = N, \qquad \text{Number of Tetrahedral Voids} = 2N\)

Stacking Layer 3: Covering tetrahedral voids aligns layer 3 with layer 1, giving the ABAB... (hcp) lattice (e.g., \(\mathrm{Mg}\), \(\mathrm{Zn}\)). Covering octahedral voids produces a new layer C, giving the ABCABC... (ccp or fcc) lattice (e.g., \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Ag}\)). Both hcp and ccp have CN = 12 and 74% packing efficiency.

Locating Voids in an FCC Unit Cell (\(Z = 4\)): Octahedral voids lie at the 1 body-centre (\(1\)) and 12 edge-centres (\(12 \times \frac{1}{4} = 3\)), totalling \(4\). Tetrahedral voids lie one inside each of the 8 mini-cubes (octants) along the body diagonals at a distance \(\frac{\sqrt{3}a}{4}\) from each corner, totalling \(8\).

Crystal Density & Unit Cell Dimensions

Since the density of a single unit cell equals the macroscopic density (\(d\)) of the pure crystal:

\[d = \frac{\text{Mass of unit cell}}{\text{Volume of unit cell}} = \frac{Z \times M}{a^3 \times N_A}\]

where \(Z\) is the number of formula units/atoms per unit cell, \(M\) is molar mass (in \(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\) for \(d\) in \(\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)), \(a\) is the edge length in \(\mathrm{cm}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{pm} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{cm}\), \(1\,\mathrm{Å} = 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{cm}\)), and \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\).

Imperfections in Solids (Point Defects)

Real crystals always contain deviations from ideal periodicity. Point defects are irregularities around a single lattice site and fall into three classes:

  • 1. Stoichiometric Defects (Intrinsic / Thermodynamic): Preserve the chemical formula.
    • Vacancy Defect (non-ionic): Missing particles create empty sites \(\rightarrow\) decreases density (often induced by heating).
    • Interstitial Defect (non-ionic): Extra particles occupy interstitial spaces \(\rightarrow\) increases density.
    • Frenkel Defect (Dislocation Defect, ionic): A smaller ion (usually cation) leaves its normal site for an interstitial site. Density remains unchanged. Seen when cation and anion sizes differ greatly (e.g., \(\mathrm{ZnS}\), \(\mathrm{AgCl}\), \(\mathrm{AgBr}\), \(\mathrm{AgI}\)).
    • Schottky Defect (ionic): Equal numbers of cations and anions are missing to maintain electrical neutrality. Decreases density. Seen when cation and anion sizes are comparable (e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{KCl}\), \(\mathrm{CsCl}\), \(\mathrm{AgBr}\)). Important: \(\mathrm{AgBr}\) exhibits both Frenkel and Schottky defects.
  • 2. Impurity Defects: Adding a higher-valence cation (e.g., \(\mathrm{SrCl_2}\) in molten \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), or \(\mathrm{CdCl_2}\) in \(\mathrm{AgCl}\)) introduces cation vacancies to preserve charge neutrality. Each \(\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}\) replaces two \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) ions, leaving one cation vacancy per \(\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}\) ion added.
  • 3. Non-Stoichiometric Defects:
    • Metal Excess via Anionic Vacancies (F-centres): Heating alkali halides in alkali metal vapour (e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) in \(\mathrm{Na}\) vapour) traps electrons at anionic vacancies called F-centres (Farbenzentren), imparting colour by visible-light excitation (\(\mathrm{NaCl} \rightarrow\) yellow, \(\mathrm{LiCl} \rightarrow\) pink, \(\mathrm{KCl} \rightarrow\) violet/lilac).
    • Metal Excess via Interstitial Cations: White \(\mathrm{ZnO}\) loses oxygen on heating (\(\mathrm{ZnO} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn^{2+}} + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{O_2} + 2e^-\)) and turns yellow as excess \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\) and electrons occupy interstitial sites (\(\mathrm{Zn}_{1+x}\mathrm{O}\)).
    • Metal Deficiency Defect: Seen in transition metal compounds like \(\mathrm{Fe}_{0.95}\mathrm{O}\) (range \(\mathrm{Fe}_{0.93}\mathrm{O}\) to \(\mathrm{Fe}_{0.96}\mathrm{O}\)), where missing \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) ions are charge-compensated by \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) ions.

Electrical & Magnetic Properties of Solids

Electrical Conduction & Band Theory

Solids span conductivities from \(10^{-20}\) to \(10^{7}\,\mathrm{Ω^{-1}\,m^{-1}}\):

  • Conductors (\(10^4\) to \(10^7\,\mathrm{Ω^{-1}\,m^{-1}}\)): Filled valence band overlaps with the empty conduction band (or is partially filled), allowing free electron flow.
  • Insulators (\(10^{-20}\) to \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{Ω^{-1}\,m^{-1}}\)): Large forbidden energy gap prevents thermal excitation of electrons to the conduction band.
  • Semiconductors (\(10^{-6}\) to \(10^4\,\mathrm{Ω^{-1}\,m^{-1}}\)): Small energy gap; conductivity increases with temperature. Pure \(\mathrm{Si}\) and \(\mathrm{Ge}\) are intrinsic semiconductors.

Doping (Extrinsic Semiconductors): Doping Group 14 (\(\mathrm{Si}, \mathrm{Ge}\)) with Group 15 (\(\mathrm{P}, \mathrm{As}\)) adds a delocalised extra electron \(\rightarrow\) n-type semiconductor. Doping with Group 13 (\(\mathrm{B}, \mathrm{Al}, \mathrm{Ga}\)) creates a positive electron vacancy (hole) \(\rightarrow\) p-type semiconductor. Average valence of 4 is also simulated in 13–15 (\(\mathrm{InSb}, \mathrm{AlP}, \mathrm{GaAs}\)) and 12–16 (\(\mathrm{ZnS}, \mathrm{CdS}, \mathrm{CdSe}, \mathrm{HgTe}\)) compounds.

Magnetic Properties

Electron orbital and spin motions generate magnetic moments measured in Bohr magnetons (\(1\,\mathrm{µ_B} = 9.27 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}\)).

  • Paramagnetic: Weakly attracted by magnetic fields due to unpaired electrons; loses magnetism when the field is removed (e.g., \(\mathrm{O_2}, \mathrm{Cu^{2+}}, \mathrm{Fe^{3+}}, \mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\)).
  • Diamagnetic: Weakly repelled by magnetic fields; all electrons are paired (e.g., \(\mathrm{H_2O}, \mathrm{NaCl}, \mathrm{C_6H_6}\)).
  • Ferromagnetic: Strongly attracted and permanently magnetisable; magnetic domains align parallel (e.g., \(\mathrm{Fe}, \mathrm{Co}, \mathrm{Ni}, \mathrm{Gd}, \mathrm{CrO_2}\)).
  • Antiferromagnetic: Equal domains align antiparallel, cancelling net moment (e.g., \(\mathrm{MnO}\)).
  • Ferrimagnetic: Unequal parallel and antiparallel domains give a weak net attraction; becomes paramagnetic upon heating (e.g., \(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\), ferrites \(\mathrm{MgFe_2O_4}, \mathrm{ZnFe_2O_4}\)).

Worked Examples

1. Determining Formula from Void Occupancy

Problem: In a hypothetical ionic crystal, anions \(Y\) form a cubic close-packed (ccp) lattice. Cations \(M\) occupy one-half of the octahedral voids, and cations \(X\) occupy one-fourth of the tetrahedral voids. Determine the simplest formula of the compound.

Solution:

  1. For a ccp (fcc) unit cell, the effective number of close-packed \(Y\) ions is \(N = 4\).
  2. Number of octahedral voids \(= N = 4\). Since \(M\) occupies \(\frac{1}{2}\) of these, number of \(M\) ions \(= 4 \times \frac{1}{2} = 2\).
  3. Number of tetrahedral voids \(= 2N = 8\). Since \(X\) occupies \(\frac{1}{4}\) of these, number of \(X\) ions \(= 8 \times \frac{1}{4} = 2\).
  4. Thus, \(M : X : Y = 2 : 2 : 4 = 1 : 1 : 2\). The simplest formula is \(\mathrm{MXY_2}\).

2. Calculating Crystal Density from Unit Cell Parameters

Problem: A hypothetical metal \(Q\) (assumed molar mass \(M = 60\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) crystallises in a body-centred cubic (bcc) lattice with a unit cell edge length of \(300\,\mathrm{pm}\). Calculate its density in \(\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\). (Assume \(N_A = 6.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\).)

Solution:

  1. For bcc, \(Z = 2\). Edge length \(a = 300\,\mathrm{pm} = 300 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{cm} = 3.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{cm}\).
  2. Volume of unit cell, \(a^3 = (3.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{cm})^3 = 27 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{cm^3}\).
  3. Using \(d = \frac{Z \cdot M}{a^3 \cdot N_A}\): \[d = \frac{2 \times 60}{27 \times 10^{-24} \times 6.0 \times 10^{23}} = \frac{120}{16.2} \approx 7.41\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\]

4. Analyzing Non-Stoichiometric Metal Deficiency

Problem: Analysis of a hypothetical transition-metal oxide gives the formula \(\mathrm{M_{0.96}O_{1.00}}\), where \(M\) exists as \(\mathrm{M^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{M^{3+}}\) ions. Calculate the percentage of \(M\) ions present in the \(\mathrm{M^{3+}}\) state.

Solution:

  1. Consider \(100\) \(\mathrm{O^{2-}}\) ions (total negative charge \(= -200\)) paired with \(96\) \(M\) ions.
  2. Let the number of \(\mathrm{M^{3+}}\) ions be \(x\); then the number of \(\mathrm{M^{2+}}\) ions is \((96 - x)\).
  3. By electrical neutrality: \(3x + 2(96 - x) = 200 \implies x + 192 = 200 \implies x = 8\).
  4. Percentage of \(\mathrm{M^{3+}}\) ions \(= \frac{8}{96} \times 100\% = \mathbf{8.33\%}\).

Practice Question 3 (Stoichiometry MCQ)

In a hypothetical cubic crystal composed of elements \(A\) and \(B\), atoms of \(A\) occupy all 8 corners of the unit cell and atoms of \(B\) occupy the face-centres. If one atom of \(B\) is missing from one of the face-centres, what is the simplest formula of the compound?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{AB_3}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{A_2B_5}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{A_2B_3}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{AB_2}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Effective number of \(A\) atoms per unit cell \(= 8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1\). Since 1 of the 6 face-centred \(B\) atoms is missing, 5 face-centred \(B\) atoms remain: effective \(B\) atoms \(= 5 \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{5}{2}\). Ratio \(A : B = 1 : \frac{5}{2} = 2 : 5 \implies \mathrm{A_2B_5}\).

Practice Question 5 (Numerical MCQ)

A hypothetical metal \(X\) (assumed molar mass \(= 100\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) crystallises in an fcc lattice with unit cell edge length \(400\,\mathrm{pm}\). What is the density of metal \(X\)? (Use \(N_A = 6.0 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\).)

  • (A) \(5.21\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)
  • (B) \(10.42\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)
  • (C) \(2.60\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)

Explanation: For fcc, \(Z = 4\). Practice Question 7 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Common NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid:
  • Confusing anisotropy (crystalline) with isotropy (amorphous).
  • Forgetting to convert edge length from \(\mathrm{pm}\) to \(\mathrm{cm}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{pm} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{cm}\)) in density problems—a factor of \(10^{-30}\) error in \(a^3\)!
  • Mixing up Frenkel (density unchanged) and Schottky (density decreases) defects, or forgetting that \(\mathrm{AgBr}\) shows both.
  • Assuming \(\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}\) doping in \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) creates two vacancies—it creates only one cation vacancy per \(\mathrm{Sr^{2+}}\) ion.
  • [ ] Know \(Z\) values: SC \(= 1\), BCC \(= 2\), FCC/CCP \(= 4\).
  • [ ] Know \(a\)–\(r\) relations: SC (\(a = 2r\)), BCC (\(\sqrt{3}a = 4r\)), FCC (\(\sqrt{2}a = 4r\)).
  • [ ] Know packing efficiencies: SC (\(52.4\%\)), BCC (\(68\%\)), FCC/HCP (\(74\%\)).
  • [ ] Memorise \(N\) octahedral voids and \(2N\) tetrahedral voids.

Concise Final Recap

Crystalline solids feature long-range 3D order, sharp melting points, and anisotropy across 7 crystal systems and 14 Bravais lattices. Close-packed hcp and ccp (fcc) lattices achieve the maximum \(74\%\) packing efficiency (CN \(= 12\)) and generate \(N\) octahedral and \(2N\) tetrahedral voids. Crystal density follows \(d = \frac{ZM}{a^3 N_A}\), while point defects (Schottky, Frenkel, impurity, and F-centres), doping (n-type/p-type), and electron-domain alignment govern density, colour, conductivity, and magnetism.

কেলাসাকার কঠিন পদার্থে দীর্ঘ পাল্লার ত্রিমাত্রিক সজ্জা, নির্দিষ্ট গলনাঙ্ক ও অসমদৈশিকতা থাকে এবং এরা ৭টি কেলাস শ্রেণি ও ১৪টি ব্রাভে ল্যাটিসে বিভক্ত। hcp ও ccp (fcc) প্যাকিংয়ে সর্বোচ্চ \(74\%\) দক্ষতা (CN \(= 12\)) এবং \(N\)-টি অক্টাহেড্রাল ও \(2N\)-টি টেট্রাহেড্রাল ভয়েড গঠিত হয়। কেলাসের ঘনত্ব \(d = \frac{ZM}{a^3 N_A}\) সূত্র মেনে চলে এবং বিন্দু ত্রুটি, ডোপিং ও ডোমেইন বিন্যাস কঠিন পদার্থের ঘনত্ব, বর্ণ, পরিবাহিতা ও চুম্বকত্ব নিয়ন্ত্রণ করে।
