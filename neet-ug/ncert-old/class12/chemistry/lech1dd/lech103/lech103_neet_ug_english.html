NEET UG Physical Chemistry

Electrochemistry: Principles, Conductance, and Quantitative Analysis

A complete, exam-oriented tutorial covering galvanic and electrolytic cells, the Nernst equation, electrolytic conductance, Kohlrausch's law, Faraday's laws, batteries, fuel cells, and corrosion.

Learning Objectives and Overview

Electrochemistry examines the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. Spontaneous redox reactions can generate electric current in galvanic cells, while an external electrical source can drive non-spontaneous chemical transformations in electrolytic cells. For NEET UG, this topic bridges thermodynamics, ionic equilibrium, and stoichiometry.

After studying this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between galvanic and electrolytic cells, including electrode polarity and electron flow.
  • Use standard electrode potentials and the Nernst equation to calculate cell EMF, standard Gibbs energy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\ominus\)), and equilibrium constants (\(K_{\mathrm{c}}\)).
  • Relate resistance, resistivity (\(\rho\)), conductivity (\(\kappa\)), cell constant (\(G^*\)), and molar conductivity (\(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\)), and explain their variation with dilution.
  • Apply Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions to determine \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus\), degree of dissociation (\(\alpha\)), and dissociation constants (\(K_{\mathrm{a}}\)) of weak electrolytes.
  • Solve quantitative electrolysis problems using Faraday's laws and predict electrolysis products in molten and aqueous systems.
  • Explain the chemistry of primary batteries, secondary batteries, hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells, and electrochemical corrosion.

1. Electrochemical Cells: Galvanic vs. Electrolytic Operation

An electrochemical cell consists of two metallic (or inert) electrodes immersed in electrolyte solutions. Depending on whether the redox reaction occurs spontaneously or is forced by an external power supply, the cell operates in one of two modes:

  • Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell: Converts the Gibbs energy released by a spontaneous redox reaction (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G < 0\), \(E_{\mathrm{cell}} > 0\)) into electrical work.
  • Electrolytic Cell: Uses electrical energy from an external DC source to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G > 0\)).

Anatomy of a Galvanic Cell (Daniell Cell)

In a Daniell cell, a zinc strip dips into an aqueous \(\mathrm{ZnSO_4}\) solution and a copper strip dips into an aqueous \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) solution. The two half-cells are connected externally by a metallic wire and internally by a salt bridge (an inverted U-tube filled with an inert electrolyte such as \(\mathrm{KCl}\) or \(\mathrm{KNO_3}\) in agar-agar gel).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Schematic of a Daniell galvanic cell. Oxidation occurs at the negative zinc anode, and reduction occurs at the positive copper cathode.

The overall spontaneous reaction in the Daniell cell is:

\[\mathrm{Zn(s)} + \mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)} + \mathrm{Cu(s)} \quad (E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\text{ at } 298\,\mathrm{K})\]

Role of the Salt Bridge: It completes the internal circuit by allowing ions to migrate between half-cells and maintains electrical neutrality in both solutions without letting the two electrolytes mix directly.

Effect of an Opposing External Potential (\(E_{\mathrm{ext}}\))

When an external opposing voltage \(E_{\mathrm{ext}}\) is applied across a Daniell cell (whose standard EMF is \(1.10\,\mathrm{V}\)), three distinct regimes arise:

Condition Cell Behavior Electron & Current Flow Chemical Change
\(E_{\mathrm{ext}} < 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\) Acts as a Galvanic cell Electrons: Zn → Cu; Current: Cu → Zn Zn dissolves at anode; Cu deposits at cathode
\(E_{\mathrm{ext}} = 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\) Equilibrium (no net cell action) No flow of electrons or current (\(I = 0\)) No net chemical reaction occurs
\(E_{\mathrm{ext}} > 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\) Acts as an Electrolytic cell Electrons: Cu → Zn; Current: Zn → Cu Cu dissolves at anode; Zn deposits at cathode

IUPAC Cell Notation

By IUPAC convention, the oxidation half-cell (anode) is written on the left and the reduction half-cell (cathode) on the right. A single vertical line (\(\mid\)) represents a phase boundary (electrode–electrolyte interface), and a double vertical line (\(\parallel\)) represents the salt bridge:

\[\mathrm{Zn(s)} \mid \mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)} \parallel \mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)} \mid \mathrm{Cu(s)}\]

Under this convention, the cell potential is always:

\[E_{\mathrm{cell}} = E_{\mathrm{right}} - E_{\mathrm{left}} = E_{\mathrm{cathode}} - E_{\mathrm{anode}}\]

2. Electrode Potential, Standard Hydrogen Electrode, and the Electrochemical Series

At any metal–electrolyte interface, metal ions in solution tend to deposit on the electrode (making it positive), while metal atoms tend to enter the solution as ions, leaving electrons behind (making it negative). At equilibrium, this charge separation establishes an electrode potential. When all species are at unit concentration (\(1\,\mathrm{M}\)), gases are at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure, and temperature is \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), it is called the standard electrode potential (\(E^\ominus\)). According to IUPAC convention, standard electrode potentials are always tabulated as standard reduction potentials.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

An isolated half-cell potential cannot be measured directly; only the potential difference between two half-cells can be measured. Therefore, the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) is chosen as the universal reference electrode and assigned a standard potential of exactly \(0.00\,\mathrm{V}\) at all temperatures:

\[\mathrm{Pt(s)} \mid \mathrm{H_2(g,\,1\,bar)} \mid \mathrm{H^+(aq,\,1\,M)} \qquad \mathrm{H^+(aq)} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightleftharpoons \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{H_2(g)} \quad (E^\ominus = 0.00\,\mathrm{V})\]
Tutorial Diagram 2: Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE), assigned \(E^\ominus = 0.00\,\mathrm{V}\) at all temperatures.

Interpreting the Electrochemical Series

Arranging half-cell reduction reactions in decreasing order of standard reduction potential (\(E^\ominus\)) gives the electrochemical series. Key trends for NEET UG:

  • Higher (more positive) \(E^\ominus\): Greater tendency to get reduced; the oxidized form is a stronger oxidizing agent (e.g., \(\mathrm{F_2}\) with \(E^\ominus = +2.87\,\mathrm{V}\) is the strongest oxidizing agent).
  • Lower (more negative) \(E^\ominus\): Greater tendency for the reverse (oxidation) reaction; the reduced form is a stronger reducing agent (e.g., \(\mathrm{Li}\) with \(E^\ominus = -3.05\,\mathrm{V}\) is the strongest reducing agent in aqueous solution).
  • Reaction with Acids: Metals with negative \(E^\ominus\) (above hydrogen in reducing power, such as \(\mathrm{Zn}\), \(\mathrm{Fe}\), \(\mathrm{Mg}\)) liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) gas from dilute acids (\(\mathrm{HCl}\), dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)). Metals with positive \(E^\ominus\) (such as \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Ag}\), \(\mathrm{Au}\)) cannot displace \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from \(\mathrm{H^+}\).
  • Displacement Feasibility: A metal with lower \(E^\ominus\) displaces a metal with higher \(E^\ominus\) from its aqueous salt solution. For example, \(\mathrm{Zn}\) (\(E^\ominus = -0.76\,\mathrm{V}\)) displaces \(\mathrm{Cu}\) (\(E^\ominus = +0.34\,\mathrm{V}\)) from \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\), so \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) cannot be stored in a zinc vessel.
Reduction Half-Reaction \(E^\ominus / \mathrm{V}\) (at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)) Relative Strength

\(\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 8\mathrm{H^+} + 5\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + 4\mathrm{H_2O}\) \(+1.51\) Strong oxidizer in acidic medium
\(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)}\) \(+1.36\) Oxidizes \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) and \(\mathrm{I^-}\)

\(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq)} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe^{2+}(aq)}\) \(+0.77\) Oxidizes \(\mathrm{I^-}\) (\(E^\ominus = +0.54\,\mathrm{V}\)), not \(\mathrm{Br^-}\)

3. Nernst Equation, Equilibrium Constant, and Gibbs Energy

Standard potentials apply only at unit concentration (\(1\,\mathrm{M}\)) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) gas pressure. The Nernst equation gives the electrode or cell potential at any concentration and temperature.

Nernst Equation for a Half-Cell and Full Cell

For a reduction half-reaction \(\mathrm{M}^{n+}(\mathrm{aq}) + n\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{M(s)}\), taking the activity of pure solid \(\mathrm{M}\) as unity:

\[E_{\mathrm{M}^{n+}/\mathrm{M}} = E_{\mathrm{M}^{n+}/\mathrm{M}}^\ominus - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln\frac{1}{[\mathrm{M}^{n+}]}\]

For a general balanced cell reaction \(a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \xrightarrow{n\mathrm{e^-}} c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}\) with reaction quotient \(Q = \dfrac{[\mathrm{C}]^c[\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a[\mathrm{B}]^b}\):

\[E_{\mathrm{cell}} = E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q\]

At \(T = 298\,\mathrm{K}\), substituting \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\), \(F = 96487\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\), and converting \(\ln Q = 2.303\log_{10} Q\) gives the standard NEET working form:

\[E_{\mathrm{cell}} = E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus - \frac{0.059}{n}\log_{10} Q \qquad (\text{at } 298\,\mathrm{K})\]

For a hydrogen electrode (\(\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{H_2}\)) at \(p_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 1\,\mathrm{bar}\) and \(298\,\mathrm{K}\):

\[E_{\mathrm{H^+/\mathrm{H_2}}} = 0 - \frac{0.059}{1}\log_{10}\frac{1}{[\mathrm{H^+}]} = -0.059\,\mathrm{pH}\]

Equilibrium Constant (\(K_{\mathrm{c}}\)) and Gibbs Energy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G\))

As a galvanic cell discharges, reactant concentrations fall, product concentrations rise, and \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}\) decreases until chemical equilibrium is reached. At equilibrium, \(E_{\mathrm{cell}} = 0\) and \(Q = K_{\mathrm{c}}\):

\[E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = \frac{2.303RT}{nF}\log_{10} K_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{0.059}{n}\log_{10} K_{\mathrm{c}} \quad (\text{at } 298\,\mathrm{K})\]

The maximum electrical work obtainable from a galvanic cell equals the decrease in Gibbs energy:

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G = -nFE_{\mathrm{cell}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\ominus = -nFE_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = -RT\ln K_{\mathrm{c}}\]
Crucial Distinction: Intensive vs. Extensive Properties

\(E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus\) is an intensive property (independent of stoichiometric scaling), whereas \(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\ominus\) is an extensive property (scales with \(n\)). Therefore, when combining two half-reactions to find the \(E^\ominus\) of a third half-reaction (where electrons do not cancel), you must add \(\Delta G^\ominus\) values—never add \(E^\ominus\) values directly! Specifically, \(E_3^\ominus = \dfrac{n_1 E_1^\ominus + n_2 E_2^\ominus}{n_3}\).

4. Conductance of Electrolytic Solutions and Kohlrausch's Law

Electrical conduction occurs through two mechanisms: electronic (metallic) conductance via mobile electrons (no chemical change; decreases as temperature rises due to increased lattice vibrations) and electrolytic (ionic) conductance via mobile ions in molten salts or solutions (increases as temperature rises due to lower viscosity and higher ionic mobility/dissociation).

Key Conductance Quantities and Units

Quantity Symbol & Formula SI Unit Common CGS Unit & Conversion
Resistance \(R = \rho \dfrac{l}{A}\) \(\mathrm{\Omega}\) (ohm) \(\mathrm{\Omega}\)
Resistivity \(\rho = R\dfrac{A}{l}\) \(\mathrm{\Omega\,m}\) \(\mathrm{\Omega\,cm}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{\Omega\,m} = 100\,\mathrm{\Omega\,cm}\))
Conductance \(G = \dfrac{1}{R}\) \(\mathrm{S}\) (siemens, \(\mathrm{\Omega^{-1}}\)) \(\mathrm{S}\) or \(\mathrm{\Omega^{-1}}\)
Conductivity \(\kappa = \dfrac{1}{\rho} = G \cdot G^*\) \(\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}} = 100\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\))
Cell Constant \(G^* = \dfrac{l}{A} = R\kappa\) \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}} = 100\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\))
Molar Conductivity \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = \dfrac{\kappa}{c}\) \(\mathrm{S\,m^2\,mol^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{S\,m^2\,mol^{-1}} = 10^4\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\))

In practical calculations with molarity \(M\) in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) and \(\kappa\) in \(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\):

\[\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\;(\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}) = \frac{\kappa\;(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}) \times 1000}{M\;(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})}\]

Measurement Note: Resistance of an ionic solution is measured using a conductivity cell (with platinized platinum electrodes) connected to a Wheatstone bridge powered by an AC source (audio frequency) rather than DC, because DC would cause electrolysis and alter the solution composition.

Variation of \(\kappa\) and \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) with Concentration

  • Conductivity (\(\kappa\)) always decreases on dilution for both strong and weak electrolytes, because the number of current-carrying ions per unit volume decreases.
  • Molar conductivity (\(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\)) always increases on dilution, because the total volume \(V\) containing \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of electrolyte increases (\(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = \kappa V\)), and the increase in \(V\) more than compensates for the decrease in \(\kappa\).
Tutorial Diagram 3: Variation of molar conductivity (\(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\)) with \(c^{1/2}\) for a strong electrolyte (linear) and a weak electrolyte (steep curve).

For strong electrolytes (completely dissociated), \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) increases slowly with dilution due to reduced inter-ionic attractions and follows the Debye–Hückel–Onsager equation:

\[\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = \Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus - A\sqrt{c}\]

Here \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus\) is the limiting molar conductivity (at infinite dilution, \(c \to 0\)), and \(A\) depends on the solvent, temperature, and electrolyte charge type (e.g., 1–1 like \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), 2–1 like \(\mathrm{CaCl_2}\), 2–2 like \(\mathrm{MgSO_4}\)).

For weak electrolytes (such as \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\)), \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) rises steeply at low concentrations because the degree of dissociation (\(\alpha\)) increases rapidly. Consequently, \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus\) cannot be obtained by linear extrapolation.

Kohlrausch's Law of Independent Migration of Ions

At infinite dilution, each ion migrates independently of its co-ion. The limiting molar conductivity of an electrolyte equals the sum of the individual limiting molar conductivities of its constituent cations and anions:

\[\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus = \nu_+\lambda_+^\ominus + \nu_-\lambda_-^\ominus\]

where \(\nu_+\) and \(\nu_-\) are the numbers of cations and anions per formula unit. For a weak electrolyte at concentration \(c\):

\[\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus} \qquad \text{and} \qquad K_{\mathrm{a}} = \frac{c\alpha^2}{1 - \alpha} = \frac{c\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^2}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus - \Lambda_{\mathrm{m}})}\]

5. Electrolysis: Faraday's Laws and Product Prediction

In an electrolytic cell, an external DC voltage forces reduction at the cathode (negative electrode) and oxidation at the anode (positive electrode).

Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

  • First Law: The mass (\(m\)) of substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the total electric charge (\(Q = I \times t\)) passed through the electrolyte: \[m = ZIt = \frac{M_{\mathrm{molar}}}{nF}It\] where \(Z\) is the electrochemical equivalent, \(M_{\mathrm{molar}}\) is the molar mass, \(n\) is the number of electrons per mole of species in the half-reaction, and \(1\,\mathrm{F} = 96487\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}} \approx 96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\).
  • Second Law: When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes connected in series, the masses of substances liberated are proportional to their chemical equivalent weights (\(E_{\mathrm{eq}} = M_{\mathrm{molar}} / n\)): \[\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{E_{\mathrm{eq},1}}{E_{\mathrm{eq},2}}\]

Predicting Products of Electrolysis

Products depend on the nature of the electrolyte (molten vs. aqueous), electrode material (inert Pt/graphite vs. reactive metal), standard electrode potentials, concentration, and kinetic overpotential:

  • At the Cathode (Reduction): The species with the higher \(E^\ominus\) (more positive reduction potential) is reduced preferentially.
  • At the Anode (Oxidation): The species with the lower \(E^\ominus\) (easier to oxidize) is oxidized preferentially, unless kinetic overpotential alters the preference.

6. Batteries, Fuel Cells, and Corrosion

Primary Batteries (Non-Rechargeable)

  • Dry Cell (Leclanché Cell, ~\(1.5\,\mathrm{V}\)): Zinc container (anode) and graphite rod surrounded by \(\mathrm{MnO_2}\) + carbon powder (cathode), with moist \(\mathrm{NH_4Cl} + \mathrm{ZnCl_2}\) paste.
    Anode: \(\mathrm{Zn(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Zn^{2+}} + 2\mathrm{e^-}\); Cathode: \(\mathrm{MnO_2} + \mathrm{NH_4^+} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{MnO(OH)} + \mathrm{NH_3}\) (Mn goes from \(+4\) to \(+3\); \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) forms \([\mathrm{Zn(NH_3)_4}]^{2+}\)).
  • Mercury Cell (~\(1.35\,\mathrm{V}\)): \(\mathrm{Zn–Hg}\) amalgam (anode) and \(\mathrm{HgO}\) + carbon paste (cathode) with \(\mathrm{KOH–ZnO}\) electrolyte. Overall reaction: \(\mathrm{Zn(Hg)} + \mathrm{HgO(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnO(s)} + \mathrm{Hg(l)}\). Its voltage remains constant throughout its life because the overall reaction involves no dissolved ions whose concentration can change.

Secondary Batteries (Rechargeable)

  • Lead Storage Battery: Lead grid anode, \(\mathrm{PbO_2}\)-packed lead grid cathode, and \(38\%\) \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) electrolyte.
    Overall discharge reaction: \(\mathrm{Pb(s)} + \mathrm{PbO_2(s)} + 2\mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{PbSO_4(s)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\).
    During recharging, external current reverses this reaction, regenerating \(\mathrm{Pb}\) at the cathode and \(\mathrm{PbO_2}\) at the anode, and restoring \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) concentration.
  • Nickel–Cadmium (Ni–Cd) Cell: Longer life than lead storage battery, though costlier. Discharge: \(\mathrm{Cd(s)} + 2\mathrm{Ni(OH)_3(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CdO(s)} + 2\mathrm{Ni(OH)_2(s)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)}\).

Hydrogen–Oxygen Fuel Cell

Fuel cells continuously convert the combustion energy of fuels (\(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\)) directly into electricity with ~\(70\%\) efficiency (compared to ~\(40\%\) for thermal power plants) and zero pollution. In an alkaline \(\mathrm{H_2–O_2}\) fuel cell, gases bubble through porous carbon electrodes impregnated with \(\mathrm{Pt/Pd}\) catalysts into concentrated aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\):

\[\text{Anode: } 2\mathrm{H_2(g)} + 4\mathrm{OH^-(aq)} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{H_2O(l)} + 4\mathrm{e^-} \qquad \text{Cathode: } \mathrm{O_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} + 4\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\] \[\text{Overall: } 2\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\]
Tutorial Diagram 4: Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell using porous carbon electrodes and alkaline electrolyte.

Corrosion as an Electrochemical Process

Rusting of iron in moist air acts as a microscopic galvanic cell on the metal surface:

  • Anodic spot: \(2\mathrm{Fe(s)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Fe^{2+}(aq)} + 4\mathrm{e^-}\) (\(E^\ominus = -0.44\,\mathrm{V}\))
  • Cathodic spot: \(\mathrm{O_2(g)} + 4\mathrm{H^+(aq)} + 4\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\) (\(E^\ominus = +1.23\,\mathrm{V}\)), where \(\mathrm{H^+}\) comes from dissolved \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (\(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\)) or acidic atmospheric oxides.
  • Overall cell reaction: \(2\mathrm{Fe(s)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} + 4\mathrm{H^+(aq)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Fe^{2+}(aq)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\) (\(E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = +1.67\,\mathrm{V}\)).
  • Rust formation: \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) is further oxidized by atmospheric \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to hydrated ferric oxide, \(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3\cdot xH_2O}\) (rust), regenerating \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ions.

Prevention: Barrier protection (paint, grease, bisphenol), coating with inert or protective metals (\(\mathrm{Sn}\), \(\mathrm{Zn}\)), and cathodic (sacrificial) protection using a more reactive metal such as \(\mathrm{Mg}\) or \(\mathrm{Zn}\) that corrodes preferentially to save iron.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Cell EMF Using the Nernst Equation

Consider a hypothetical galvanic cell at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) represented as: \(\mathrm{Zn(s)} \mid \mathrm{Zn^{2+}}(0.10\,\mathrm{M}) \parallel \mathrm{Ag^+}(0.010\,\mathrm{M}) \mid \mathrm{Ag(s)}\). Given \(E_{\mathrm{Zn^{2+}/Zn}}^\ominus = -0.76\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{Ag^+/Ag}}^\ominus = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\), calculate \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}\). (Concentrations are assumed values for illustration.)

Solution:

1. Balanced cell reaction: \(\mathrm{Zn(s)} + 2\mathrm{Ag^+(aq)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Zn^{2+}(aq)} + 2\mathrm{Ag(s)}\), so \(n = 2\).

2. Standard cell potential: \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = E_{\mathrm{cathode}}^\ominus - E_{\mathrm{anode}}^\ominus = 0.80 - (-0.76) = 1.56\,\mathrm{V}\).

3. Apply the Nernst equation at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\):

\[E_{\mathrm{cell}} = E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus - \frac{0.059}{2}\log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}]}{[\mathrm{Ag^+}]^2} = 1.56 - 0.0295\log_{10}\frac{0.10}{(0.010)^2}\] \[E_{\mathrm{cell}} = 1.56 - 0.0295\log_{10}(10^3) = 1.56 - 0.0295(3) = 1.56 - 0.0885 \approx 1.47\,\mathrm{V}\]

Example 2: Determining Standard Gibbs Energy and Equilibrium Constant

A hypothetical redox cell with \(n = 2\) has \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = 0.295\,\mathrm{V}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). Calculate \(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\ominus\) (using \(F = 96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\)) and \(K_{\mathrm{c}}\). (Cell potential is an assumed value.)

Solution:

1. Standard Gibbs energy:

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\ominus = -nFE_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = -2 \times 96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}} \times 0.295\,\mathrm{V} = -56935\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} = -56.94\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

2. Equilibrium constant at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\):

\[\log_{10} K_{\mathrm{c}} = \frac{nE_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus}{0.059} = \frac{2 \times 0.295}{0.059} = 10 \implies K_{\mathrm{c}} = 10^{10}\]

Example 3: Computing Cell Constant, Conductivity, and Molar Conductivity

A conductivity cell filled with a reference solution of conductivity \(1.40\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\) has a resistance of \(80\,\Omega\). When filled with a \(0.025\,\mathrm{M}\) solution of a hypothetical electrolyte \(\mathrm{MX}\), its resistance is \(400\,\Omega\). Calculate the cell constant, conductivity, and molar conductivity of the \(\mathrm{MX}\) solution. (Values are assumed.)

Solution:

1. Cell constant: \(G^* = \kappa_{\mathrm{ref}} \times R_{\mathrm{ref}} = 1.40\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}} \times 80\,\Omega = 112\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} = 1.12\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\).

2. Conductivity of \(\mathrm{MX}\) solution:

\[\kappa = \frac{G^*}{R} = \frac{112\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}}{400\,\Omega} = 0.28\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}} = 0.0028\,\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\]

3. Molar conductivity:

\[\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = \frac{\kappa\,(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}) \times 1000}{M\,(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})} = \frac{0.0028 \times 1000}{0.025} = 112\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\]

Example 4: Applying Kohlrausch's Law to Find Dissociation Constant

At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the limiting molar conductivities of \(\mathrm{NaX}\) (sodium salt of a weak monoprotic acid \(\mathrm{HX}\)), \(\mathrm{HCl}\), and \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) are \(95.0\), \(426.0\), and \(126.0\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\), respectively. If a \(0.010\,\mathrm{M}\) solution of \(\mathrm{HX}\) has \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = 19.75\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\), calculate \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus(\mathrm{HX})\), \(\alpha\), and \(K_{\mathrm{a}}\). (Conductivity values for \(\mathrm{NaX}\) and \(\mathrm{HX}\) are assumed.)

Solution:

1. By Kohlrausch's law:

\[\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus(\mathrm{HX}) = \Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus(\mathrm{HCl}) + \Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus(\mathrm{NaX}) - \Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus(\mathrm{NaCl}) = 426.0 + 95.0 - 126.0 = 395.0\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\]

2. Degree of dissociation:

\[\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus} = \frac{19.75}{395.0} = 0.050\]

3. Acid dissociation constant:

\[K_{\mathrm{a}} = \frac{c\alpha^2}{1 - \alpha} = \frac{0.010 \times (0.050)^2}{1 - 0.050} = \frac{2.5 \times 10^{-5}}{0.95} \approx 2.63 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\]

Example 5: Quantitative Electrolysis Using Faraday's Laws

A steady current of \(2.0\,\mathrm{A}\) is passed through an aqueous \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) solution for \(965\,\mathrm{s}\) using platinum electrodes. Calculate the mass of copper deposited at the cathode (Atomic mass of \(\mathrm{Cu} = 63.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), \(F = 96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\)). (Current and time are assumed.)

Solution:

1. Total charge passed: \(Q = I \times t = 2.0\,\mathrm{A} \times 965\,\mathrm{s} = 1930\,\mathrm{C}\).

2. Number of Faradays: \(\dfrac{1930}{96500} = 0.020\,\mathrm{F}\).

3. Cathode reaction: \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)} + 2\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu(s)}\) (\(n = 2\)). Thus, \(2\,\mathrm{F}\) deposits \(63.5\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{Cu}\).

4. Mass deposited: \(m = \dfrac{63.5}{2} \times 0.020 = 0.635\,\mathrm{g}\).

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

For a Daniell cell with \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\), an external opposing potential \(E_{\mathrm{ext}} = 1.35\,\mathrm{V}\) is applied. Which statement is correct?

  • (A) Electrons flow from the zinc electrode to the copper electrode.
  • (B) Zinc dissolves at the zinc electrode and copper deposits at the copper electrode.
  • (C) Copper dissolves at the copper electrode and zinc deposits at the zinc electrode.
  • (D) No chemical reaction occurs because \(E_{\mathrm{ext}} > E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus\).
Answer: (C)

Solution: Since \(E_{\mathrm{ext}} = 1.35\,\mathrm{V} > 1.10\,\mathrm{V}\), the cell functions as an electrolytic cell. Electrons are forced from Cu to Zn, so Cu is oxidized (dissolves at the anode) and \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\) is reduced (deposits at the cathode).

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Given the standard reduction potentials: \(E_{\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}}^\ominus = +0.34\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{Cu^{+}/Cu}}^\ominus = +0.52\,\mathrm{V}\). What is the standard reduction potential \(E_{\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu^+}}^\ominus\)?

  • (A) \(+0.16\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(+0.18\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(+0.43\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(+0.86\,\mathrm{V}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Combine \(\Delta G^\ominus\) values (since \(E^\ominus\) is intensive):
(1) \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + 2\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu}\), \(\Delta G_1^\ominus = -2F(0.34) = -0.68F\)
(2) \(\mathrm{Cu} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu^+} + \mathrm{e^-}\), \(\Delta G_2^\ominus = +1F(0.52) = +0.52F\)
Adding gives \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu^+}\), \(\Delta G_3^\ominus = -0.68F + 0.52F = -0.16F\). Since \(\Delta G_3^\ominus = -1F E_3^\ominus\), we get \(E_{\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu^+}}^\ominus = +0.16\,\mathrm{V}\).

At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the potential of a hydrogen electrode (\(p_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 1\,\mathrm{bar}\)) dipped in an aqueous buffer solution of \(\mathrm{pH} = 4\) is:

  • (B) \(-0.236\,\mathrm{V}\)

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

For a cell reaction with \(n = 2\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the equilibrium constant is \(K_{\mathrm{c}} = 10^{12}\). What is \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus\)?

  • (A) \(0.354\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(0.708\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(0.177\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(1.180\,\mathrm{V}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = \dfrac{0.059}{n}\log_{10} K_{\mathrm{c}} = \dfrac{0.059}{2} \times 12 = 0.059 \times 6 = 0.354\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 5 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: On diluting an aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\), its conductivity (\(\kappa\)) decreases while its molar conductivity (\(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\)) increases.
Statement II: The limiting molar conductivity of \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\) can be determined accurately by extrapolating the linear plot of \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) versus \(c^{1/2}\) to \(c = 0\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Solution: Statement I is true for all electrolytes. Statement II is false because \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\) is a weak electrolyte; its \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) vs \(c^{1/2}\) curve rises steeply near \(c \to 0\) and is non-linear, so \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus\) must be found via Kohlrausch's law.

Practice Question 6 Numerical MCQ

Given \(\lambda^\ominus(\mathrm{Al^{3+}}) = 189\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(\lambda^\ominus(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}) = 160\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the limiting molar conductivity \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus\) of \(\mathrm{Al_2(SO_4)_3}\) is:

  • (A) \(349\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(509\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(698\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(858\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\)
Answer: (D)

Solution: \(\mathrm{Al_2(SO_4)_3}\) dissociates into \(2\,\mathrm{Al^{3+}}\) and \(3\,\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\). By Kohlrausch's law: \[\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus = 2(189) + 3(160) = 378 + 480 = 858\,\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 7 Numerical MCQ

How many Faradays of electricity are required to reduce \(0.5\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) to \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) in acidic medium?

  • (A) \(1.0\,\mathrm{F}\)
  • (B) \(2.5\,\mathrm{F}\)
  • (C) \(3.0\,\mathrm{F}\)
  • (D) \(5.0\,\mathrm{F}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: The half-reaction is \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 8\mathrm{H^+} + 5\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + 4\mathrm{H_2O}\). Reducing \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) requires \(5\,\mathrm{F}\), so \(0.5\,\mathrm{mol}\) requires \(0.5 \times 5 = 2.5\,\mathrm{F}\).

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

During the electrolysis of concentrated aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) solution using platinum electrodes, the products obtained at the cathode and anode, respectively, are:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Na}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Na}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: At the cathode, \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (\(E^\ominus = -0.83\,\mathrm{V}\) in neutral water / \(\mathrm{H^+}\) at \(0.00\,\mathrm{V}\)) is reduced in preference to \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) (\(E^\ominus = -2.71\,\mathrm{V}\)), giving \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\). At the anode, \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) is oxidized to \(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)}\) rather than \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) to \(\mathrm{O_2}\) because of the high overpotential of oxygen on platinum.

Practice Question 9 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): A mercury button cell maintains a nearly constant potential of \(1.35\,\mathrm{V}\) throughout its operating life.
Reason (R): The overall cell reaction in a mercury cell does not involve any ion in solution whose concentration can change during discharge.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Solution: The overall reaction \(\mathrm{Zn(Hg)} + \mathrm{HgO(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{ZnO(s)} + \mathrm{Hg(l)}\) involves only solids and pure liquid mercury, so the reaction quotient \(Q\) remains constant and the cell potential stays steady at ~\(1.35\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 10 Numerical MCQ

Two electrolytic cells containing aqueous \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\) and \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) solutions are connected in series. If \(1.08\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{Ag}\) is deposited in the first cell, what mass of \(\mathrm{Cu}\) is deposited in the second cell? (Atomic masses: \(\mathrm{Ag} = 108\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm{Cu} = 63.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\))

  • (A) \(0.3175\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (B) \(0.635\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (C) \(1.270\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (D) \(0.1588\,\mathrm{g}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: By Faraday's Second Law, equivalents deposited are equal: \[\frac{m_{\mathrm{Ag}}}{E_{\mathrm{eq,Ag}}} = \frac{m_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{E_{\mathrm{eq,Cu}}} \implies \frac{1.08}{108/1} = \frac{m_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{63.5/2} \implies 0.01 = \frac{m_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{31.75} \implies m_{\mathrm{Cu}} = 0.3175\,\mathrm{g}\]

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Electrode Polarity Swap: Oxidation always occurs at the anode and reduction always occurs at the cathode—but the anode is negative in a galvanic cell and positive in an electrolytic cell.
  • Adding \(E^\ominus\) Directly: Never add or subtract \(E^\ominus\) values directly when combining two half-reactions into a third half-reaction; always convert to \(\Delta G^\ominus = -nFE^\ominus\) first.
  • Unit Mismatch in \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\): When using \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}} = \dfrac{\kappa \times 1000}{M}\), \(\kappa\) must be in \(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\) and \(M\) in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\). If \(\kappa\) is in \(\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\), divide by \(100\) first to get \(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}\), or use \(c = 1000\,M\) in \(\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}\).
  • Dilution Trap: Dilution decreases \(\kappa\) (fewer ions per unit volume) but increases \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\) (larger volume per mole).

Concise Final Recap

Essential Formula Summary (at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\))
  • \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = E_{\mathrm{cathode}}^\ominus - E_{\mathrm{anode}}^\ominus\)
  • \(E_{\mathrm{cell}} = E_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus - \dfrac{0.059}{n}\log_{10} Q\)
  • \(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}G^\ominus = -nFE_{\mathrm{cell}}^\ominus = -2.303RT\log_{10} K_{\mathrm{c}}\)
  • \(G^* = \dfrac{l}{A} = R\kappa\), \(\quad \Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}\,(\mathrm{S\,cm^2\,mol^{-1}}) = \dfrac{\kappa\,(\mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}}) \times 1000}{M}\)
  • \(\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus = \nu_+\lambda_+^\ominus + \nu_-\lambda_-^\ominus\), \(\quad \alpha = \dfrac{\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}}{\Lambda_{\mathrm{m}}^\ominus}\), \(\quad K_{\mathrm{a}} = \dfrac{c\alpha^2}{1 - \alpha}\)
  • \(m = \dfrac{M_{\mathrm{molar}}}{nF}It\), \(\quad 1\,\mathrm{F} \approx 96500\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}\)
