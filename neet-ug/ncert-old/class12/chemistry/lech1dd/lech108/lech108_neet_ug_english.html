NEET UG Chemistry

The d- and f-Block Elements

Transition Metals, Inner Transition Series, Periodic Trends, Oxoanions, and Exam-Focused Problem Solving

Learning Objectives and Chapter Scope

The central region of the periodic table (Groups 3 to 12) houses the d-block elements, while the two rows placed separately at the bottom comprise the f-block elements. For NEET UG, this chapter tests conceptual reasoning on electronic configurations, anomalous periodic trends, standard electrode potentials, magnetic moments, colour, and the redox chemistry of \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) and \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\).

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish true transition metals from Group 12 elements using the IUPAC definition.
  • Write ground-state and ionic electronic configurations for \(3d\), \(4d\), \(5d\), \(4f\), and \(5f\) elements, including key exceptions.
  • Explain trends in melting points, enthalpies of atomisation, ionisation enthalpies, and atomic/ionic radii (including lanthanoid and actinoid contractions).
  • Predict relative stabilities of oxidation states using \(E^\circ\) values, hydration enthalpies, and \(d^0, d^5, d^{10}, t_{2g}^3\) configurations.
  • Calculate spin-only magnetic moments and relate \(d\)-\(d\) transitions to the colour of aquated ions.
  • Write balanced redox equations, preparations, and structures for \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) and \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\).
  • Compare lanthanoids and actinoids across electronic structure, oxidation states, and reactivity.

1. Position, Classification, and Electronic Configurations

IUPAC Definition of Transition Elements

According to IUPAC, a transition metal is an element that possesses an incompletely filled \(d\) subshell either in its neutral ground-state atom or in any of its common oxidation states.

  • Scandium (\(Z = 21\)) has a \(3d^1 4s^2\) ground state (incomplete \(3d\)) and is a transition element, even though \(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}\) is \(3d^0\).
  • Copper (\(Z = 29\)), Silver (\(Z = 47\)), and Gold (\(Z = 79\)) have \(d^{10} s^1\) ground states, yet they are transition elements because their common ions—such as \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) (\(3d^9\)), \(\mathrm{Ag^{2+}}\) (\(4d^9\)), and \(\mathrm{Au^{3+}}\) (\(5d^8\))—contain partially filled \(d\) orbitals.
  • Group 12 Elements (\(\mathrm{Zn, Cd, Hg, Cn}\)) have \((n-1)d^{10}ns^2\) configurations in the neutral atom and retain the full \(d^{10}\) subshell in their stable \(+2\) oxidation state. Consequently, they are d-block elements but are not classified as transition elements.

The Four Transition Series

  • \(3d\) series (First transition series): \(\mathrm{Sc}\) (\(Z=21\)) to \(\mathrm{Zn}\) (\(Z=30\)).
  • \(4d\) series (Second transition series): \(\mathrm{Y}\) (\(Z=39\)) to \(\mathrm{Cd}\) (\(Z=48\)).
  • \(5d\) series (Third transition series): \(\mathrm{La}\) (\(Z=57\)) and \(\mathrm{Hf}\) (\(Z=72\)) to \(\mathrm{Hg}\) (\(Z=80\)).
  • \(6d\) series (Fourth transition series): \(\mathrm{Ac}\) (\(Z=89\)) and \(\mathrm{Rf}\) (\(Z=104\)) to \(\mathrm{Cn}\) (\(Z=112\)).

General Configuration and Anomalies

The outer electronic configuration of \(d\)-block elements generally follows \((n-1)d^{1\text{–}10}\,ns^{1\text{–}2}\). Because the energy difference between the \((n-1)d\) and \(ns\) orbitals is very small, extra stability associated with half-filled (\(d^5\)) and completely filled (\(d^{10}\)) subshells (driven by exchange energy and symmetrical distribution) causes notable deviations:

  • Chromium (\(Z=24\)): \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1\) (not \(3d^4 4s^2\)).
  • Copper (\(Z=29\)): \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1\) (not \(3d^9 4s^2\)).
  • Palladium (\(Z=46\)): \([\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10} 5s^0\) (unique zero \(s\)-electron ground state in the \(4d\) series).
  • Ionisation rule: When transition metals form cations, electrons are always removed from the outermost \(ns\) orbital first, before any \((n-1)d\) electrons are lost. For example, \(\mathrm{Fe}\) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6 4s^2\), so \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6\) and \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5\).

2. Physical Properties, Atomic Sizes, and Lanthanoid Contraction

Melting Points and Enthalpies of Atomisation

Transition metals (except \(\mathrm{Zn, Cd, Hg}\)) are hard, dense, and possess high melting points and high enthalpies of atomisation (\(\Delta_a H^\circ\)). This strength arises because both \(ns\) and unpaired \((n-1)d\) electrons participate in interatomic metallic bonding.

  • Across each series, melting points and \(\Delta_a H^\circ\) rise to a maximum near the middle (\(d^5\) configuration, e.g., \(\mathrm{Cr, Mo, W}\)), where one unpaired electron per \(d\) orbital maximises metallic bonding.
  • Anomalous dips at \(\mathrm{Mn}\) and \(\mathrm{Tc}\): Although \(\mathrm{Mn}\) has five unpaired \(3d\) electrons, its half-filled \(3d^5\) subshell is tightly held and less available for delocalised metallic bonding, giving \(\mathrm{Mn}\) a lower melting point and \(\Delta_a H^\circ\) (\(281\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) than its neighbours.
  • Lowest \(\Delta_a H^\circ\) at \(\mathrm{Zn}\): Zinc (\(3d^{10} 4s^2\)) has no unpaired \(d\) electrons for metallic bonding, so its enthalpy of atomisation (\(126\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is the lowest in the \(3d\) series.
  • Metals of the \(4d\) and \(5d\) series have significantly higher \(\Delta_a H^\circ\) than \(3d\) metals, which promotes frequent metal–metal bonding in heavier transition metal compounds.

Atomic and Ionic Radii

Across a given series, atomic and ionic radii decrease gradually from left to right because each added electron enters an inner \((n-1)d\) orbital while nuclear charge increases by \(+1\). Since \(d\) electrons shield the outer \(ns\) electrons imperfectly, effective nuclear charge rises. Near the end of the series (\(\mathrm{Cu, Zn}\)), inter-electronic repulsions among paired \(d\) electrons cause a slight increase in radius.

Down a group, radii increase from \(3d\) to \(4d\) as a new shell is added. However, the radii of the \(4d\) and \(5d\) series are nearly identical (for example, \(\mathrm{Zr} = 160\,\mathrm{pm}\) and \(\mathrm{Hf} = 159\,\mathrm{pm}\)). This occurs because the \(14\) lanthanoid elements (\(4f\) filling) intervene before \(\mathrm{Hf}\). Poor shielding by \(4f\) electrons causes a steady shrinkage—the lanthanoid contraction—that cancels the expected size increase from shell addition. Consequently, \(4d\) and \(5d\) congeners have very similar chemical properties and are difficult to separate.

3. Ionisation Enthalpies, Oxidation States, and Electrode Potentials

Ionisation Enthalpy Trends

Ionisation enthalpies increase gradually across a transition series, though much less steeply than in main-group periods because inner \((n-1)d\) electrons partially screen the \(ns\) electrons. Important breaks occur due to \(d^5\) and \(d^{10}\) stability:

  • High \(IE_2\) for \(\mathrm{Cr}\) and \(\mathrm{Cu}\): Removing the second electron disrupts stable \(3d^5\) (\(\mathrm{Cr^+}\)) and \(3d^{10}\) (\(\mathrm{Cu^+}\)) configurations.
  • Low \(IE_2\) for \(\mathrm{Zn}\): Removing the second \(4s\) electron leaves a stable \(3d^{10}\) ion.
  • \(IE_3\) of \(\mathrm{Fe}\) vs. \(\mathrm{Mn}\): \(IE_3(\mathrm{Fe}) < IE_3(\mathrm{Mn})\) because \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) yields a stable \(3d^5\) ion, whereas \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) destroys a \(3d^5\) configuration.

Variable Oxidation States

  • Transition elements show multiple oxidation states differing by \(1\) unit (e.g., \(\mathrm{V^{II}, V^{III}, V^{IV}, V^{V}}\)), unlike \(p\)-block elements where states typically differ by \(2\) units (inert pair effect).
  • The widest range appears in the middle of the \(3d\) series: Manganese exhibits every state from \(+2\) to \(+7\).
  • Heavier \(d\)-block contrast: In \(p\)-block groups, lower oxidation states become more stable down a group; in \(d\)-block groups, higher oxidation states become more stable down a group. Thus, \(\mathrm{Mo(VI)}\) and \(\mathrm{W(VI)}\) in \(\mathrm{MoO_3}\) and \(\mathrm{WO_3}\) are stable, whereas \(\mathrm{Cr(VI)}\) in \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) is a strong oxidising agent.
  • Stabilising highest states: Only \(\mathrm{O}\) and \(\mathrm{F}\) stabilise maximum oxidation states (\(\mathrm{CrF_6, VF_5, Mn_2O_7}\)). Oxygen surpasses fluorine (\(\mathrm{Mn_2O_7}\) vs. \(\mathrm{MnF_4}\)) because oxygen can form \(p\pi\text{–}d\pi\) multiple bonds with the metal.
  • Zero/low oxidation states: Stabilised by \(\pi\)-acceptor ligands such as \(\mathrm{CO}\) in \(\mathrm{Ni(CO)_4}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe(CO)_5}\).

Standard Electrode Potentials (\(E^\circ\))

The standard reduction potential \(E^\circ(\mathrm{M^{2+}/M})\) depends on the overall enthalpy change for converting solid metal into hydrated ions:

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{total}}H^\circ = \Delta_a H^\circ(\mathrm{M}) + IE_1 + IE_2 + \Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}}H^\circ(\mathrm{M^{2+}})\]
  • Why \(\mathrm{Cu}\) has positive \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}) = +0.34\,\mathrm{V}\): The high energy needed to atomise \(\mathrm{Cu}\) and ionise it to \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) is not compensated by its hydration enthalpy. Hence \(\mathrm{Cu}\) cannot liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from dilute non-oxidising acids; it reacts only with oxidising acids (\(\mathrm{HNO_3}\), hot conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)).
  • Disproportionation of \(\mathrm{Cu^+}\): In aqueous solution, \(\mathrm{Cu^+}\) is unstable and disproportionates: \(2\mathrm{Cu^+(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)} + \mathrm{Cu(s)}\). Even though \(IE_2\) of \(\mathrm{Cu}\) is high, the much more negative hydration enthalpy of \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) makes the overall process favourable.
  • Anomalously negative \(E^\circ(\mathrm{M^{2+}/M})\) for \(\mathrm{Mn, Ni, Zn}\): Extra stability of \(3d^5\) (\(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\)), \(3d^{10}\) (\(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\)), and very high negative \(\Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}}H^\circ\) (\(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}\)).
  • \(E^\circ(\mathrm{M^{3+}/M^{2+}})\) trends:
    • \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) is a strong reducing agent (\(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}/Cr^{2+}}) = -0.41\,\mathrm{V}\)) because \(d^4 \rightarrow d^3\) gives a half-filled \(t_{2g}^3\) configuration in octahedral aqueous complexes.
    • \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) (\(E^\circ = +1.57\,\mathrm{V}\)) and \(\mathrm{Co^{3+}}\) (\(E^\circ = +1.97\,\mathrm{V}\)) are strong oxidising agents; \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) restores the stable \(d^5\) subshell.
    • \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}\) has a moderate \(E^\circ = +0.77\,\mathrm{V}\) due to the stability of \(d^5\) \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\).

4. Magnetic Moment, Colour, Catalysis, and Alloys

Spin-Only Magnetic Moment

For first-row (\(3d\)) transition metal ions, orbital angular momentum is effectively quenched by the surrounding ligands, so the magnetic moment depends only on the number of unpaired electrons (\(n\)):

\[\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mathrm{BM}\]

where \(\mathrm{BM}\) stands for Bohr magneton. Values range from \(0\,\mathrm{BM}\) (\(n=0\), diamagnetic, e.g., \(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}, Ti^{4+}, Cu^+, Zn^{2+}}\)) to \(5.92\,\mathrm{BM}\) (\(n=5\), e.g., \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}, Fe^{3+}}\)).

Colour of Aquated Ions

Partially filled \(d\) orbitals split in energy under the influence of ligands (such as \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)). Absorption of visible light promotes an electron from a lower-energy \(d\) orbital to a higher-energy \(d\) orbital (\(d\)-\(d\) transition), and the complementary colour is observed. Ions with \(d^0\) (\(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}, Ti^{4+}}\)) or \(d^{10}\) (\(\mathrm{Cu^+, Zn^{2+}}\)) configurations have no \(d\)-\(d\) transitions and are colourless.

High-Yield NEET Fact: Colour Without \(d\) Electrons \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) (purple), \(\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}\) (yellow), and \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) (orange) have \(d^0\) metal centres (\(\mathrm{Mn^{VII}}\), \(\mathrm{Cr^{VI}}\)). Their intense colours arise from ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT), not \(d\)-\(d\) transitions.

Complexes, Catalysis, Interstitial Compounds, and Alloys

  • Complex formation: Favoured by small cation size, high ionic charge, and availability of vacant \(d\) orbitals.
  • Catalytic activity: Due to variable oxidation states and ability to adsorb reactants on surfaces using \(3d\) and \(4s\) electrons, lowering activation energy. Examples: \(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\) (Contact process for \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)), finely divided \(\mathrm{Fe}\) (Haber process for \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)), \(\mathrm{Ni}\) (hydrogenation), \(\mathrm{TiCl_4 + Al(CH_3)_3}\) (Ziegler–Natta catalyst for polythene), \(\mathrm{PdCl_2}\) (Wacker process). In homogeneous catalysis, \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) catalyses \(2\mathrm{I^-} + \mathrm{S_2O_8^{2-}} \rightarrow \mathrm{I_2} + 2\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) by cycling between \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\).
  • Interstitial compounds: Formed when small atoms (\(\mathrm{H, C, N, B}\)) occupy interstitial voids in metal lattices (e.g., \(\mathrm{TiC, Mn_4N, Fe_3H, VH_{0.56}}\)). They are non-stoichiometric, extremely hard, chemically inert, retain metallic conductivity, and have higher melting points than the pure metals.
  • Alloys: Transition metals readily form substitutional alloys because their metallic radii lie within \(\approx 15\%\) of one another (e.g., steels with \(\mathrm{Cr, V, W, Mo, Mn}\); brass \(\mathrm{Cu\text{–}Zn}\); bronze \(\mathrm{Cu\text{–}Sn}\)).

5. Oxides, Potassium Dichromate, and Potassium Permanganate

Acid–Base Character of Oxides

As the oxidation state of a transition metal increases, ionic character decreases and covalent/acidic character increases:

  • Vanadium: \(\mathrm{V_2O_3}\) (basic) \(\rightarrow\) \(\mathrm{V_2O_4}\) (less basic) \(\rightarrow\) \(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\) (amphoteric, predominantly acidic).
  • Chromium: \(\mathrm{CrO}\) (basic) \(\rightarrow\) \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}\) (amphoteric) \(\rightarrow\) \(\mathrm{CrO_3}\) (acidic).
  • Manganese: \(\mathrm{MnO}\) (basic) \(\rightarrow\) \(\mathrm{Mn_2O_7}\) (covalent green oil, strongly acidic, forming \(\mathrm{HMnO_4}\)).

Potassium Dichromate (\(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\))

Preparation from chromite ore (\(\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4}\)):

  1. Fusion with \(\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}\) in excess air gives yellow sodium chromate: \[4\mathrm{FeCr_2O_4} + 8\mathrm{Na_2CO_3} + 7\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 8\mathrm{Na_2CrO_4} + 2\mathrm{Fe_2O_3} + 8\mathrm{CO_2}\]
  2. Acidification with \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) converts chromate to orange sodium dichromate: \[2\mathrm{Na_2CrO_4} + 2\mathrm{H^+} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na_2Cr_2O_7} + 2\mathrm{Na^+} + \mathrm{H_2O}\]
  3. Treatment with \(\mathrm{KCl}\) precipitates less soluble orange crystals of \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\): \[\mathrm{Na_2Cr_2O_7} + 2\mathrm{KCl} \rightarrow \mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7} + 2\mathrm{NaCl}\]

pH-dependent equilibrium: Chromate (yellow, \(\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}\)) and dichromate (orange, \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\)) both have \(\mathrm{Cr}\) in the \(+6\) state and interconvert reversibly:

\[2\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}} \text{ (yellow)} + 2\mathrm{H^+} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}} \text{ (orange)} + \mathrm{H_2O}\]

Oxidising action in acidic medium (\(E^\circ = +1.33\,\mathrm{V}\)):

\[\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}} + 14\mathrm{H^+} + 6\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Cr^{3+}} + 7\mathrm{H_2O}\]

It oxidises \(\mathrm{I^- \rightarrow I_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2S \rightarrow S}\), \(\mathrm{Sn^{2+} \rightarrow Sn^{4+}}\), and \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+} \rightarrow Fe^{3+}}\).

Potassium Permanganate (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\))

Preparation from pyrolusite (\(\mathrm{MnO_2}\)):

  1. Alkaline oxidative fusion with \(\mathrm{KOH}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) (or \(\mathrm{KNO_3}\)) yields dark green potassium manganate: \[2\mathrm{MnO_2} + 4\mathrm{KOH} + \mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{K_2MnO_4} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\]
  2. Disproportionation in neutral/acidic solution (or electrolytic oxidation in alkaline solution commercially) gives \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\): \[3\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}} + 4\mathrm{H^+} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + \mathrm{MnO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\]
  3. Laboratory preparation: Oxidation of \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) by peroxodisulphate: \[2\mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + 5\mathrm{S_2O_8^{2-}} + 8\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 10\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}} + 16\mathrm{H^+}\]

Properties and thermal decomposition: \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) is isostructural with \(\mathrm{KClO_4}\). On heating to \(513\,\mathrm{K}\), it decomposes: \(2\mathrm{KMnO_4} \rightarrow \mathrm{K_2MnO_4} + \mathrm{MnO_2} + \mathrm{O_2}\). Green \(\mathrm{MnO_4^{2-}}\) (\(3d^1\)) is paramagnetic, whereas purple \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) (\(3d^0\)) is diamagnetic (with weak temperature-independent paramagnetism).

6. Inner Transition Elements (f-Block): Lanthanoids and Actinoids

Lanthanoids (\(4f\) Series: \(\mathrm{Ce}\) to \(\mathrm{Lu}\), \(Z = 58\text{–}71\))

  • Electronic configuration: General atomic configuration \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{1\text{–}14} 5d^{0\text{–}1} 6s^2\). Notably, \(\mathrm{La}\) (\(5d^1 6s^2\)), \(\mathrm{Ce}\) (\(4f^1 5d^1 6s^2\)), \(\mathrm{Gd}\) (\(4f^7 5d^1 6s^2\)), and \(\mathrm{Lu}\) (\(4f^{14} 5d^1 6s^2\)) have one \(5d\) electron. All \(\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}\) ions have \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^n\) (\(n=1\text{–}14\)).
  • Oxidation states: Predominantly \(+3\). Deviations to \(+4\) or \(+2\) occur to achieve \(f^0\), \(f^7\), or \(f^{14}\) stability:
    • \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\) (\(4f^0\), strong oxidant, \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}/Ce^{3+}}) = +1.74\,\mathrm{V}\)) and \(\mathrm{Tb^{4+}}\) (\(4f^7\), oxidant).
    • \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\) (\(4f^7\), strong reductant) and \(\mathrm{Yb^{2+}}\) (\(4f^{14}\), strong reductant).
  • Colour and magnetism: Most \(\mathrm{Ln^{3+}}\) ions are coloured (sharp \(f\)-\(f\) bands) and paramagnetic, except \(f^0\) (\(\mathrm{La^{3+}, Ce^{4+}}\)) and \(f^{14}\) (\(\mathrm{Yb^{2+}, Lu^{3+}}\)) which are colourless and diamagnetic.
  • Reactivity and alloys: Silvery-white soft metals; earlier members react like \(\mathrm{Ca}\), later like \(\mathrm{Al}\). They form \(\mathrm{Ln_2O_3}\), \(\mathrm{Ln(OH)_3}\) (basic), carbides (\(\mathrm{LnC_2}\)), and liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) with acids. Mischmetall (\(\approx 95\%\) lanthanoid metal + \(\approx 5\%\) \(\mathrm{Fe}\) + traces of \(\mathrm{S, C, Ca, Al}\)) is used in \(\mathrm{Mg}\)-based alloys for lighter flints and bullets.

Actinoids (\(5f\) Series: \(\mathrm{Th}\) to \(\mathrm{Lr}\), \(Z = 90\text{–}103\))

All actinoids are radioactive. Because \(5f\), \(6d\), and \(7s\) orbitals have comparable energies, actinoids exhibit a much wider range of oxidation states (up to \(+7\) in \(\mathrm{Np}\) and \(\mathrm{Pu}\)), and \(5f\) electrons participate more extensively in bonding than \(4f\) electrons.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Calculating Spin-Only Magnetic Moment and Identifying Unpaired Electrons

Problem: Consider two hypothetical aqueous solutions containing divalent and trivalent metal ions: Solution X contains \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) (\(Z=26\)) and Solution Y contains \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) (\(Z=24\)). Calculate the spin-only magnetic moment of each ion and determine which solution shows stronger paramagnetism.

Solution:

  1. For \(\mathrm{Fe}\) (\(Z=26\)), the ground-state configuration is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6 4s^2\). Removing two \(4s\) electrons gives \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) with \(3d^6\). By Hund's rule, five \(3d\) orbitals hold \(1\) paired set and \(4\) unpaired electrons (\(n = 4\)). \[\mu(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}) = \sqrt{4(4+2)} = \sqrt{24} \approx 4.90\,\mathrm{BM}\]
  2. For \(\mathrm{Cr}\) (\(Z=24\)), the ground-state configuration is \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1\). Removing one \(4s\) and two \(3d\) electrons gives \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) with \(3d^3\), which has \(3\) unpaired electrons (\(n = 3\)). \[\mu(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}) = \sqrt{3(3+2)} = \sqrt{15} \approx 3.87\,\mathrm{BM}\]
  3. Because \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) has more unpaired electrons (\(4 > 3\)), Solution X exhibits stronger paramagnetism.

Example 2: Predicting Redox Behaviour from Standard Electrode Potentials

Problem: Using standard reduction potentials \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}/Cr^{2+}}) = -0.41\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}/Mn^{2+}}) = +1.57\,\mathrm{V}\), predict whether (a) \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) can reduce \(\mathrm{H^+}\) to \(\mathrm{H_2}\) in acid, and (b) \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) can oxidise \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) to \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) given \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}) = +0.77\,\mathrm{V}\).

Solution:

  • (a) For \(2\mathrm{Cr^{2+}(aq)} + 2\mathrm{H^+(aq)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Cr^{3+}(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)}\): \[E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ(\mathrm{H^+/H_2}) - E^\circ(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}/Cr^{2+}}) = 0.00 - (-0.41) = +0.41\,\mathrm{V}\] Since \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} > 0\), the reaction is thermodynamically spontaneous; \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) liberates \(\mathrm{H_2}\) from dilute acid.
  • (b) For \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}(aq)} + \mathrm{Fe^{2+}(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Mn^{2+}(aq)} + \mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq)}\): \[E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} = E^\circ(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}/Mn^{2+}}) - E^\circ(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}) = 1.57 - 0.77 = +0.80\,\mathrm{V}\] Since \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{cell}} > 0\), \(\mathrm{Mn^{3+}}\) readily oxidises \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) to \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\).

Example 3: Stoichiometry of Permanganate Titrations in Acidic vs. Neutral Media

Problem: Suppose \(25.0\,\mathrm{mL}\) of a \(0.040\,\mathrm{M}\) potassium permanganate (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)) solution is used in two separate hypothetical experiments: (a) to oxidise oxalate ions (\(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\)) in acidic medium, and (b) to oxidise iodide ions (\(\mathrm{I^-}\)) in neutral medium. Calculate the moles of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) produced in (a) and moles of \(\mathrm{IO_3^-}\) produced in (b).

Solution:

  1. Moles of \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) used: \[n(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}) = 0.040\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} \times 0.0250\,\mathrm{L} = 1.00 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol}\]
  2. (a) Acidic medium: \(2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + 5\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}} + 16\mathrm{H^+} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Mn^{2+}} + 10\mathrm{CO_2} + 8\mathrm{H_2O}\).
    Mole ratio \(\mathrm{MnO_4^- : CO_2} = 2 : 10 = 1 : 5\). \[n(\mathrm{CO_2}) = 5 \times 1.00 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol} = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol}\]
  3. (b) Neutral medium: \(2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + \mathrm{I^-} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{MnO_2} + \mathrm{IO_3^-} + 2\mathrm{OH^-}\).
    Mole ratio \(\mathrm{MnO_4^- : IO_3^-} = 2 : 1\). \[n(\mathrm{IO_3^-}) = \frac{1}{2} \times 1.00 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol} = 5.00 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol}\]

Example 4: Determining Lanthanoid Ion Stability and Magnetic Character

Problem: Predict the magnetic behaviour (paramagnetic or diamagnetic) and redox tendency of \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\) (\(Z=58\)), \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\) (\(Z=63\)), and \(\mathrm{Lu^{3+}}\) (\(Z=71\)).

Solution:

  • \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\): \(\mathrm{Ce}\) is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^1 5d^1 6s^2\), so \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\) is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^0\) (no unpaired electrons \(\rightarrow\) diamagnetic). Because \(+3\) is the most stable lanthanoid state, \(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}}\) acts as a strong oxidising agent (\(\mathrm{Ce^{4+} \rightarrow Ce^{3+}}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\): \(\mathrm{Eu}\) is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^7 6s^2\), so \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\) is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^7\) (\(7\) unpaired electrons \(\rightarrow\) strongly paramagnetic). Though \(4f^7\) stabilises its formation, \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\) readily oxidises to \(\mathrm{Eu^{3+}}\) and is a strong reducing agent.
  • \(\mathrm{Lu^{3+}}\): \(\mathrm{Lu}\) is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14} 5d^1 6s^2\), so \(\mathrm{Lu^{3+}}\) is \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14}\) (all orbitals filled \(\rightarrow\) diamagnetic and colourless, stable in the \(+3\) state).

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following elements is a \(d\)-block element but is not classified as a transition element under the IUPAC definition?

  • (A) Silver (\(\mathrm{Ag}\), \(Z=47\))
  • (B) Cadmium (\(\mathrm{Cd}\), \(Z=48\))
  • (C) Copper (\(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(Z=29\))
  • (D) Scandium (\(\mathrm{Sc}\), \(Z=21\))
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Cadmium (\(\mathrm{Cd}\)) has a \([\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10} 5s^2\) ground state and retains a completely filled \(4d^{10}\) subshell in its only common oxidation state (\(\mathrm{Cd^{2+}}\)). Silver and copper have \(d^9\) configurations in their \(+2\) ions, and scandium has an incomplete \(3d^1\) subshell in its neutral atom.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which pair of elements has almost identical atomic radii due to lanthanoid contraction?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Ti}\) and \(\mathrm{Zr}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Nb}\) and \(\mathrm{Ta}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Sc}\) and \(\mathrm{Y}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Mn}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: \(\mathrm{Nb}\) (\(4d\) series, Group 5) and \(\mathrm{Ta}\) (\(5d\) series, Group 5) lie immediately after the lanthanoids. Poor shielding by the \(4f\) electrons causes the \(5d\) radius of \(\mathrm{Ta}\) to match that of \(\mathrm{Nb}\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Among the following aquated ions, which one is colourless and diamagnetic?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Ti^{3+}}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{V^{3+}}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}\)
Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: \(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}\) has a \(3d^0\) configuration with zero unpaired electrons, so it is diamagnetic and cannot undergo \(d\)-\(d\) transitions, making it colourless. \(\mathrm{Ti^{3+}}\) (\(3d^1\)), \(\mathrm{V^{3+}}\) (\(3d^2\)), and \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) (\(3d^9\)) all have unpaired \(d\) electrons.

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The spin-only magnetic moment of a trivalent ion of a \(3d\) metal is \(5.92\,\mathrm{BM}\). The atomic number of the metal is:

  • (A) 24
  • (B) 25
  • (C) 26
  • (D) 27
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: \(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = 5.92\,\mathrm{BM} \Rightarrow n(n+2) \approx 35 \Rightarrow n = 5\). A trivalent \(3d\) ion with \(5\) unpaired electrons has the \(3d^5\) configuration. Adding back \(3\) electrons gives \(3d^6 4s^2\), which is iron (\(\mathrm{Fe}, Z = 26\)).

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Copper has a positive standard electrode potential \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}) = +0.34\,\mathrm{V}\).
Reason (R): The high enthalpy of atomisation and sum of first and second ionisation enthalpies of copper are not balanced by its hydration enthalpy.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Converting \(\mathrm{Cu(s)}\) to \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)}\) requires \(\Delta_a H^\circ + IE_1 + IE_2\), which is very large and not compensated by the negative \(\Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}}H^\circ(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}})\). Therefore, \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu})\) is positive.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) oxidises iodide ion (\(\mathrm{I^-}\)) in a neutral or faintly alkaline medium, the iodine-containing product formed is:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{I_2}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{IO^-}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{IO_3^-}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{IO_4^-}\)
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: In neutral or faintly alkaline solution, permanganate oxidises iodide to iodate: \(2\mathrm{MnO_4^-} + \mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{I^-} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{MnO_2} + 2\mathrm{OH^-} + \mathrm{IO_3^-}\). (In acidic medium, \(\mathrm{I_2}\) is formed instead.)

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which statement regarding chromate (\(\mathrm{CrO_4^{2-}}\)) and dichromate (\(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\)) ions is incorrect?

  • (A) Both ions contain chromium in the \(+6\) oxidation state.
  • (B) Dichromate converts into chromate upon adding alkali.
  • (C) In \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\), all eight \(\mathrm{Cr\text{–}O}\) bonds are of equal length.
  • (D) The \(\mathrm{Cr\text{–}O\text{–}Cr}\) bond angle in \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) is \(126^\circ\).
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: In \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\), the six terminal \(\mathrm{Cr\text{–}O}\) bonds are equivalent (\(163\,\mathrm{pm}\)), whereas the two bridging \(\mathrm{Cr\text{–}O}\) bonds are longer (\(179\,\mathrm{pm}\)). Hence, not all \(\mathrm{Cr\text{–}O}\) bonds are equal.

Practice Question 8 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Consider the following two statements:
Statement I: In the \(3d\) series, \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) acts as a stronger reducing agent than \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) in aqueous solution.
Statement II: \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) converts a \(d^4\) ion into a \(d^3\) ion having a half-filled \(t_{2g}\) level.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: In aqueous octahedral complexes, \(d^3\) has a stable half-filled \(t_{2g}^3\) configuration, making \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) more stable than \(\mathrm{Cr^{2+}}\) (\(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}/Cr^{2+}}) = -0.41\,\mathrm{V}\)), whereas \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}) = +0.77\,\mathrm{V}\). Both statements are correct.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following lanthanoid ions exhibits a \(+2\) oxidation state stabilised by a completely filled \(4f^{14}\) subshell?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Yb^{2+}}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Ce^{2+}}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Gd^{2+}}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Ytterbium (\(\mathrm{Yb}, Z=70\)) has the neutral configuration \([\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14} 6s^2\). Losing two \(6s\) electrons gives \(\mathrm{Yb^{2+}}\) with a completely filled \(4f^{14}\) subshell. (\(\mathrm{Eu^{2+}}\) has \(4f^7\).)

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Why is actinoid contraction greater from element to element than lanthanoid contraction?

  • (A) \(5f\) electrons provide poorer shielding than \(4f\) electrons.
  • (B) Actinoids have smaller nuclear charges than lanthanoids.
  • (C) \(6d\) orbitals are filled before \(5f\) orbitals in all actinoids.
  • (D) Actinoids do not exhibit variable oxidation states.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: \(5f\) orbitals are more spatially diffuse than \(4f\) orbitals and shield the increasing nuclear charge even less effectively from element to element, resulting in a larger contraction across the actinoid series.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent NEET Traps
  • Removing \(d\) electrons before \(s\) electrons: Always remove \(ns\) electrons first when forming transition metal cations.
  • Assuming \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) and \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) are coloured by \(d\)-\(d\) transitions: Both are \(d^0\) and coloured by ligand-to-metal charge transfer.
  • Confusing acidic and neutral \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) products with \(\mathrm{I^-}\): Acidic gives \(\mathrm{I_2}\) (\(n=5\) for \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\)); neutral/faintly alkaline gives \(\mathrm{IO_3^-}\) (\(n=3\) for \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\)).
  • Forgetting \(\mathrm{Cr}\) and \(\mathrm{Mn}\) dips/peaks: \(\mathrm{Mn}\) has a lower melting point and \(\Delta_a H^\circ\) than \(\mathrm{Cr}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe}\); \(\mathrm{Zn}\) has the lowest \(\Delta_a H^\circ\) in the \(3d\) series.
  • [ ] Verify IUPAC transition-metal status (\(d^1\text{–}d^9\) in atom or common ion).
  • [ ] Memorise anomalies: \(\mathrm{Cr}\) (\(3d^5 4s^1\)), \(\mathrm{Cu}\) (\(3d^{10} 4s^1\)), \(\mathrm{Pd}\) (\(4d^{10} 5s^0\)), \(\mathrm{Gd}\) (\(4f^7 5d^1 6s^2\)).
  • [ ] Recall \(E^\circ(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}) = +0.34\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(2\mathrm{Cu^+ \rightarrow Cu^{2+} + Cu}\).
  • [ ] Practice \(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mathrm{BM}\) for \(3d\) ions and \(4f\) ions (\(\mathrm{Ce^{4+}, Eu^{2+}, Lu^{3+}}\)).
  • [ ] Review \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\) pH equilibrium and \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) \(n\)-factors (\(5, 3, 1\)).

Concise Final Recap

The chemistry of the \(d\)-block is governed by the close energy of \((n-1)d\) and \(ns\) orbitals, yielding variable oxidation states differing by \(1\), paramagnetic and coloured ions, catalytic activity, complexes, interstitial compounds, and alloys. Poor shielding by \(4f\) and \(5f\) electrons drives the lanthanoid and actinoid contractions, making \(4d\) and \(5d\) metals nearly identical in size. Mastery of electronic configurations, \(E^\circ\) trends, and the redox reactions of \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_7^{2-}}\) and \(\mathrm{MnO_4^-}\) covers the highest-weightage NEET UG concepts in this chapter.
