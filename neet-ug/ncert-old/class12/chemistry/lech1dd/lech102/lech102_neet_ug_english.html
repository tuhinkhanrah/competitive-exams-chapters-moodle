Solutions: Concentration, Vapour Pressure, Colligative Properties, and Abnormal Molar Masses

A comprehensive, exam-focused tutorial covering homogeneous binary mixtures, quantitative concentration scales, gas and liquid solubility laws, ideal and non-ideal behaviour, colligative properties, and the van't Hoff factor.

Learning Objectives

After completing this tutorial, you should be able to:

  • Classify binary solutions by the physical states of solute and solvent and convert effortlessly between temperature-dependent and temperature-independent concentration units.
  • Apply Henry's law to gas-in-liquid equilibria and explain pressure, temperature, and physiological effects.
  • Use Raoult's law to calculate total vapour pressure and vapour-phase mole fractions for ideal volatile mixtures, and distinguish positive from negative deviations and azeotropes.
  • Compute molar masses and solution properties using the four colligative properties: relative lowering of vapour pressure, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression, and osmotic pressure.
  • Account for electrolyte dissociation and solute association using the van't Hoff factor \(i\).

1. Classification of Binary Solutions

A solution is a single-phase, homogeneous mixture of two or more substances whose composition and intensive properties are uniform throughout. In a binary solution (two components), the component present in the larger amount—or the one that determines the final physical state of the mixture—is the solvent, while the dissolved component is the solute.

Solution Phase Solute State Solvent State Representative Example
Gaseous Gas Gas Oxygen and nitrogen mixture (air)
Gaseous Liquid Gas Chloroform vapour in nitrogen gas
Gaseous Solid Gas Camphor sublimate in nitrogen gas
Liquid Gas Liquid Dissolved oxygen in water
Liquid Liquid Liquid Ethanol mixed with water
Liquid Solid Liquid Glucose or NaCl dissolved in water
Solid Gas Solid Hydrogen occluded in palladium
Solid Liquid Solid Mercury amalgam with sodium
Solid Solid Solid Copper alloyed with gold

2. Quantitative Concentration Scales

Qualitative labels such as "dilute" or "concentrated" are insufficient for stoichiometric or thermodynamic calculations. For NEET UG problems, pay close attention to whether a concentration unit involves solution volume (which expands or contracts with temperature) or purely mass/moles (which remain invariant with temperature).

Concentration Unit Mathematical Definition Temperature Effect
Mass Percentage (% w/w) \(\dfrac{\text{Mass of component}}{\text{Total mass of solution}} \times 100\) Independent
Volume Percentage (% V/V) \(\dfrac{\text{Volume of component}}{\text{Total volume of solution}} \times 100\) Dependent
Mass by Volume (% w/V) \(\dfrac{\text{Mass of solute (g)}}{\text{Volume of solution (mL)}} \times 100\) Dependent
Parts per Million (ppm) \(\dfrac{\text{Parts of component}}{\text{Total parts of solution}} \times 10^6\) Independent (if w/w)
Mole Fraction (\(x_i\)) \(x_i = \dfrac{n_i}{\sum n_j}, \quad \sum x_i = 1\) Independent
Molarity (\(M\)) \(M = \dfrac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution in L}}\) Dependent
Molality (\(m\)) \(m = \dfrac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}}\) Independent
NEET Shortcut: Linking Molarity (\(M\)), Molality (\(m\)), and Density (\(\rho\))

If a solution has density \(\rho\) (in \(\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\)) and solute molar mass \(M_2\) (in \(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)), molarity and molality are directly connected by:

\[m = \frac{1000 M}{1000\rho - M M_2}\]

Similarly, for a binary solution with mole fraction of solute \(x_2\) and solvent molar mass \(M_1\) (in \(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)):

\[m = \frac{1000 x_2}{(1 - x_2) M_1}\]

3. Solubility and Henry's Law

Solubility is the maximum quantity of solute that can dissolve in a fixed amount of solvent at a specified temperature and pressure, forming a saturated solution in dynamic equilibrium with undissolved solute:

\[\text{Solute} + \text{Solvent} \rightleftharpoons \text{Solution}\]

Solids in Liquids

  • Intermolecular Similarity ("Like Dissolves Like"): Polar or ionic solids (e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), sucrose) dissolve readily in polar solvents such as water via ion-dipole or hydrogen-bonding interactions. Non-polar solids (e.g., naphthalene, anthracene) dissolve in non-polar solvents such as benzene via dispersion forces.
  • Temperature Effect: Governed by Le Chatelier's principle. Endothermic dissolution (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}}H > 0\)) shows increased solubility with rising temperature; exothermic dissolution (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}}H < 0\)) shows decreased solubility with rising temperature.
  • Pressure Effect: Negligible, because solids and liquids are virtually incompressible.

Gases in Liquids and Henry's Law

Unlike solids, gas solubility in liquids is strongly sensitive to both pressure and temperature:

  • Henry's Law: At constant temperature, the partial pressure \(p\) of a gas in the vapour phase above a solution is directly proportional to its mole fraction \(x\) in the solution:
\[p = K_{\mathrm{H}} x\]

Here \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) is the Henry's law constant, which depends on the nature of the gas, the solvent, and temperature. At a given partial pressure \(p\), a higher \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) implies lower gas solubility.

  • Temperature Effect: Gas dissolution in a liquid resembles condensation and is exothermic (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}}H < 0\)). Raising the temperature shifts equilibrium toward the gas phase, increasing \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) and decreasing solubility. Consequently, aquatic organisms thrive better in cold water, which holds more dissolved \(\mathrm{O}_2\) than warm water.
  • Practical & Biological Applications:
    1. Carbonated beverages: Sealed under high \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) pressure to increase dissolved gas concentration.
    2. Scuba diving ("bends"): Breathing compressed air underwater increases \(\mathrm{N}_2\) solubility in blood. Rapid ascent lowers pressure, releasing \(\mathrm{N}_2\) bubbles that block capillaries. Divers use \(\mathrm{He}\)-diluted air (\(\approx 11.7\%\,\mathrm{He},\,56.2\%\,\mathrm{N}_2,\,32.1\%\,\mathrm{O}_2\)) because helium has very low blood solubility.
    3. High-altitude anoxia: Lower atmospheric partial pressure of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) at high altitudes reduces blood oxygen concentration, causing weakness and impaired cognition.

4. Vapour Pressure, Raoult's Law, and Ideal vs. Non-Ideal Solutions

Binary Solutions of Volatile Liquids

In a closed vessel containing two volatile liquids (components 1 and 2), Raoult's law states that at a constant temperature, the partial vapour pressure of each component is directly proportional to its mole fraction in the liquid phase:

\[p_1 = x_1 p_1^0, \qquad p_2 = x_2 p_2^0\] \[p_{\mathrm{total}} = p_1 + p_2 = x_1 p_1^0 + x_2 p_2^0 = p_1^0 + (p_2^0 - p_1^0)x_2\]

where \(p_1^0\) and \(p_2^0\) are the vapour pressures of pure components 1 and 2 at the same temperature. Using Dalton's law of partial pressures, the mole fraction \(y_i\) of component \(i\) in the vapour phase is:

\[p_i = y_i p_{\mathrm{total}} \implies y_1 = \frac{p_1}{p_{\mathrm{total}}}, \qquad y_2 = \frac{p_2}{p_{\mathrm{total}}}\]

Key principle: At equilibrium, the vapour phase is always enriched in the more volatile component (the one with the higher pure vapour pressure \(p^0\)).

Comparing \(p_1 = x_1 p_1^0\) with \(p = K_{\mathrm{H}}x\) shows that Raoult's law is a special case of Henry's law where \(K_{\mathrm{H}} = p_1^0\).

Ideal vs. Non-Ideal Solutions and Azeotropes

Solutions that obey Raoult's law over the entire composition range are ideal solutions. Microscopically, A–B intermolecular attractions match A–A and B–B attractions. Real mixtures often deviate positively or negatively:

Property Ideal Solution Positive Deviation Negative Deviation
Intermolecular Forces \(F_{\mathrm{A-B}} \approx F_{\mathrm{A-A}}, F_{\mathrm{B-B}}\) \(F_{\mathrm{A-B}} < F_{\mathrm{A-A}}, F_{\mathrm{B-B}}\) \(F_{\mathrm{A-B}} > F_{\mathrm{A-A}}, F_{\mathrm{B-B}}\)
Vapour Pressure \(p_{\mathrm{obs}} = p_{\mathrm{Raoult}}\) \(p_{\mathrm{obs}} > p_{\mathrm{Raoult}}\) \(p_{\mathrm{obs}} < p_{\mathrm{Raoult}}\)
\(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}H\) & \(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}V\) \(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}H = 0,\; \Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}V = 0\) \(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}H > 0,\; \Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}V > 0\) \(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}H < 0,\; \Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}V < 0\)
Azeotrope Type (if large) None Minimum-boiling azeotrope (e.g., 95% ethanol + water) Maximum-boiling azeotrope (e.g., 68% \(\mathrm{HNO}_3\) + water)
Examples n-hexane + n-heptane; benzene + toluene; bromoethane + chloroethane Ethanol + acetone; \(\mathrm{CS}_2\) + acetone; ethanol + water Chloroform + acetone (H-bonding); phenol + aniline; \(\mathrm{HNO}_3\) + water

Azeotropes are constant-boiling binary mixtures that have the exact same composition in both liquid and vapour phases, making complete separation by fractional distillation impossible.

5. Colligative Properties of Dilute Solutions

When a non-volatile solute is added to a volatile solvent, solute particles occupy part of the liquid surface, reducing the escaping tendency of solvent molecules and lowering the vapour pressure. Properties that depend strictly on the number of solute particles relative to solvent particles—and not on the chemical identity of the solute—are called colligative properties.

5.1 Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure

For a non-electrolyte, non-volatile solute (component 2) in solvent (component 1):

\[\frac{p_1^0 - p_1}{p_1^0} = x_2 = \frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\]

For very dilute solutions (\(n_2 \ll n_1\)), this simplifies to \(\dfrac{\Delta p_1}{p_1^0} \approx \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} = \dfrac{w_2 M_1}{M_2 w_1}\).

5.2 Elevation of Boiling Point (\(\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}\))

Because the solution has a lower vapour pressure than the pure solvent at any given temperature, a higher temperature is needed for its vapour pressure to reach atmospheric pressure (\(1.013\,\mathrm{bar}\)).

\[\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}} = T_{\mathrm{b}} - T_{\mathrm{b}}^0 = K_{\mathrm{b}} m = K_{\mathrm{b}} \left(\frac{1000\,w_2}{M_2\,w_1}\right)\]

Here \(K_{\mathrm{b}}\) (in \(\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}\)) is the ebullioscopic constant (molal elevation constant), which depends solely on the solvent: \(K_{\mathrm{b}} = \dfrac{R M_1 (T_{\mathrm{b}}^0)^2}{1000\,\Delta_{\mathrm{vap}}H}\).

5.3 Depression of Freezing Point (\(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\))

At the freezing point, liquid and solid solvent have equal vapour pressures. Lowering the liquid's vapour pressure shifts the intersection with the solid solvent's vapour pressure curve to a lower temperature.

\[\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} = T_{\mathrm{f}}^0 - T_{\mathrm{f}} = K_{\mathrm{f}} m = K_{\mathrm{f}} \left(\frac{1000\,w_2}{M_2\,w_1}\right)\]

Here \(K_{\mathrm{f}}\) (in \(\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}\)) is the cryoscopic constant (molal depression constant): \(K_{\mathrm{f}} = \dfrac{R M_1 (T_{\mathrm{f}}^0)^2}{1000\,\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}}H}\).

5.4 Osmosis, Osmotic Pressure (\(\Pi\)), and Reverse Osmosis

Osmosis is the spontaneous net flow of solvent molecules across a semipermeable membrane (SPM) from a pure solvent (or dilute solution) into a more concentrated solution. Osmotic pressure (\(\Pi\)) is the excess hydrostatic pressure that must be applied to the solution side just to stop osmosis.

\[\Pi = C R T = \left(\frac{n_2}{V}\right) R T = \frac{w_2 R T}{M_2 V}\]

where \(C\) is molarity (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)), \(T\) is absolute temperature (\(\mathrm{K}\)), and \(R = 0.0821\,\mathrm{L\,atm\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) or \(0.0831\,\mathrm{L\,bar\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\).

  • Why \(\Pi\) is ideal for biomolecules & polymers: Measurements are performed near room temperature (preventing thermal denaturation of proteins) and yield large, accurately measurable pressures even at very low molarities.
  • Tonicity:
    • Isotonic: Same osmotic pressure at a given temperature (e.g., \(0.9\%\,\text{w/V}\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) normal saline is isotonic with human red blood cells).
    • Hypertonic: Higher \(\Pi\) than cell fluid; cells lose water and shrink (crenation/plasmolysis).
    • Hypotonic: Lower \(\Pi\) than cell fluid; cells absorb water and swell.
  • Reverse Osmosis (RO): Applying an external pressure greater than \(\Pi\) (\(P > \Pi\)) on the solution side forces pure solvent out through the SPM (e.g., cellulose acetate membrane) into the fresh-water compartment, enabling seawater desalination.
Tutorial Diagram 5: Reverse osmosis. External pressure \(P > \Pi\) drives solvent molecules from the concentrated solution into the pure solvent side.

6. Abnormal Molar Masses and the van't Hoff Factor (\(i\))

Electrolytes dissociate into ions in polar solvents (increasing particle count), whereas some solutes such as carboxylic acids dimerise via hydrogen bonding in low-dielectric solvents like benzene (decreasing particle count). Consequently, experimentally measured molar masses deviate from formula masses—called abnormal molar masses.

\[i = \frac{\text{Normal molar mass}}{\text{Abnormal (observed) molar mass}} = \frac{\text{Observed colligative property}}{\text{Calculated colligative property}} = \frac{\text{Total moles of particles after dissociation/association}}{\text{Moles of particles before dissociation/association}}\]
  • Dissociation (1 mole of solute gives \(n\) ions with degree of dissociation \(\alpha\)): \[i = 1 + (n - 1)\alpha \implies \alpha = \frac{i - 1}{n - 1} \qquad (i > 1)\]
  • Association (\(n\) molecules combine to form 1 associated species with degree of association \(\alpha\)): \[i = 1 - \alpha\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) \implies \alpha = \frac{1 - i}{1 - \frac{1}{n}} \qquad (i < 1)\]

All colligative property equations are corrected by multiplying the solute term by \(i\):

\[\frac{p_1^0 - p_1}{p_1^0} = i\,x_2 \;\text{(dilute: } i\frac{n_2}{n_1}\text{)}, \qquad \Delta T_{\mathrm{b}} = i\,K_{\mathrm{b}}\,m, \qquad \Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} = i\,K_{\mathrm{f}}\,m, \qquad \Pi = i\,C R T\]

Worked Examples

Converting Mass Percentage and Density to Molality and Molarity

Problem: Assume an aqueous solution of urea (\(\mathrm{NH}_2\mathrm{CONH}_2\), molar mass \(60\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) is \(15\%\) by mass and has a density of \(1.04\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\). Calculate its molality and molarity.

Solution:

Assume \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of solution. Mass of urea \(= 15\,\mathrm{g}\); mass of water \(= 100 - 15 = 85\,\mathrm{g} = 0.085\,\mathrm{kg}\).

\[\text{Moles of urea} = \frac{15\,\mathrm{g}}{60\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 0.25\,\mathrm{mol}\] \[\text{Molality } (m) = \frac{0.25\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.085\,\mathrm{kg}} = 2.94\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\]

Volume of \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of solution:

\[V = \frac{100\,\mathrm{g}}{1.04\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}} = 96.154\,\mathrm{mL} = 0.096154\,\mathrm{L}\] \[\text{Molarity } (M) = \frac{0.25\,\mathrm{mol}}{0.096154\,\mathrm{L}} = 2.60\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\]

Applying Henry's Law to Gas Dissolution

Problem: Assume at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), Henry's law constant for \(\mathrm{O}_2\) in water is \(K_{\mathrm{H}} = 4.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{bar}\). How many millimoles of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) dissolve in \(500\,\mathrm{mL}\) of water (assume density \(= 1.0\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\)) when the partial pressure of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) above water is \(0.20\,\mathrm{bar}\)?

Solution:

By Henry's law, the mole fraction of dissolved \(\mathrm{O}_2\) is:

\[x_{\mathrm{O}_2} = \frac{p}{K_{\mathrm{H}}} = \frac{0.20\,\mathrm{bar}}{4.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{bar}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-6}\]

Moles of water in \(500\,\mathrm{g}\):

\[n_{\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}} = \frac{500\,\mathrm{g}}{18\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 27.78\,\mathrm{mol}\]

Since \(n_{\mathrm{O}_2} \ll n_{\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}}\):

\[n_{\mathrm{O}_2} \approx x_{\mathrm{O}_2} \times n_{\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 27.78\,\mathrm{mol} = 1.39 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol} = 0.139\,\mathrm{mmol}\]

Calculating Vapour Pressure and Vapour-Phase Composition

Problem: Assume two volatile liquids A (molar mass \(80\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), \(p_{\mathrm{A}}^0 = 300\,\mathrm{mmHg}\)) and B (molar mass \(100\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), \(p_{\mathrm{B}}^0 = 500\,\mathrm{mmHg}\)) form an ideal solution at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\). If \(160\,\mathrm{g}\) of A is mixed with \(300\,\mathrm{g}\) of B, find the total vapour pressure and the mole fraction of each component in the vapour phase.

Solution:

\[n_{\mathrm{A}} = \frac{160}{80} = 2.0\,\mathrm{mol}, \qquad n_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{300}{100} = 3.0\,\mathrm{mol}\] \[x_{\mathrm{A}} = \frac{2.0}{2.0 + 3.0} = 0.40, \qquad x_{\mathrm{B}} = 1 - 0.40 = 0.60\]

Partial vapour pressures:

\[p_{\mathrm{A}} = x_{\mathrm{A}} p_{\mathrm{A}}^0 = 0.40 \times 300 = 120\,\mathrm{mmHg}\] \[p_{\mathrm{B}} = x_{\mathrm{B}} p_{\mathrm{B}}^0 = 0.60 \times 500 = 300\,\mathrm{mmHg}\] \[p_{\mathrm{total}} = 120 + 300 = 420\,\mathrm{mmHg}\]

Vapour-phase mole fractions:

\[y_{\mathrm{A}} = \frac{120}{420} = 0.286, \qquad y_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{300}{420} = 0.714\]

Notice that component B (more volatile) is enriched in the vapour phase (\(y_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.714 > x_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.60\)).

Determining Molar Mass from Freezing Point Depression

Problem: Assume \(3.6\,\mathrm{g}\) of a non-volatile, non-electrolyte solid is dissolved in \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of benzene (\(K_{\mathrm{f}} = 5.12\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}\), pure freezing point \(278.6\,\mathrm{K}\)). The solution freezes at \(277.576\,\mathrm{K}\). Calculate the molar mass of the solute.

Solution:

\[\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} = 278.6\,\mathrm{K} - 277.576\,\mathrm{K} = 1.024\,\mathrm{K}\] \[M_2 = \frac{K_{\mathrm{f}} \times w_2 \times 1000}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} \times w_1} = \frac{5.12 \times 3.6 \times 1000}{1.024 \times 100} = 5 \times 36 = 180\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\]

Calculating the van't Hoff Factor and Degree of Dissociation

Problem: Assume a \(0.20\,\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\) aqueous solution of a weak monobasic acid \(\mathrm{HA}\) exhibits a boiling point elevation of \(0.130\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(K_{\mathrm{b}}\) for water \(= 0.52\,\mathrm{K\,kg\,mol^{-1}}\)). Find the van't Hoff factor \(i\) and percentage dissociation of \(\mathrm{HA}\).

Solution:

Calculated elevation (assuming no dissociation):

\[\Delta T_{\mathrm{b,calc}} = K_{\mathrm{b}} m = 0.52 \times 0.20 = 0.104\,\mathrm{K}\] \[i = \frac{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b,obs}}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b,calc}}} = \frac{0.130}{0.104} = 1.25\]

For \(\mathrm{HA} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H}^+ + \mathrm{A}^-\), each molecule produces \(n = 2\) ions:

\[i = 1 + (n - 1)\alpha = 1 + \alpha \implies \alpha = 1.25 - 1 = 0.25\text{ (or } 25\%\text{)}\]

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following concentration terms is dependent on temperature?

  • (A) Molality
  • (B) Mole fraction
  • (C) Mass percentage
  • (D) Molarity
Answer: (D)

Solution: Molarity is defined as moles of solute per litre of solution. Because liquid volume expands or contracts with temperature, molarity varies with temperature. Molality, mole fraction, and mass percentage depend only on masses and moles, which are temperature-independent.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the Henry's law constants (\(K_{\mathrm{H}}\)) in water for three gases X, Y, and Z are \(1.2\,\mathrm{kbar}\), \(38\,\mathrm{kbar}\), and \(75\,\mathrm{kbar}\), respectively. At the same partial pressure, the correct order of their solubilities in water is:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{Z > Y > X}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{X > Y > Z}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{Y > X > Z}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{X > Z > Y}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: From Henry's law, \(x = p / K_{\mathrm{H}}\). At constant partial pressure \(p\), solubility (mole fraction \(x\)) is inversely proportional to \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\). Since \(1.2 < 38 < 75\), the solubility order is \(\mathrm{X > Y > Z}\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two volatile liquids P and Q form an ideal solution at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\). Their pure vapour pressures are \(p_{\mathrm{P}}^0 = 200\,\mathrm{mmHg}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{Q}}^0 = 600\,\mathrm{mmHg}\). In an equimolar liquid mixture of P and Q, the mole fraction of Q in the vapour phase is:

  • (A) \(0.25\)
  • (B) \(0.50\)
  • (C) \(0.75\)
  • (D) \(0.67\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: For an equimolar mixture, \(x_{\mathrm{P}} = 0.50\) and \(x_{\mathrm{Q}} = 0.50\). Thus, \(p_{\mathrm{P}} = 0.50 \times 200 = 100\,\mathrm{mmHg}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{Q}} = 0.50 \times 600 = 300\,\mathrm{mmHg}\). Total pressure \(p_{\mathrm{total}} = 100 + 300 = 400\,\mathrm{mmHg}\). Therefore, \(y_{\mathrm{Q}} = p_{\mathrm{Q}} / p_{\mathrm{total}} = 300 / 400 = 0.75\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which binary liquid mixture exhibits negative deviation from Raoult's law with \(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}H < 0\)?

  • (A) Ethanol and acetone
  • (B) Carbon disulphide and acetone
  • (C) Chloroform and acetone
  • (D) n-Hexane and n-heptane
Answer: (C)

Solution: Chloroform (\(\mathrm{CHCl}_3\)) forms an intermolecular hydrogen bond with the carbonyl oxygen of acetone (\((\mathrm{CH}_3)_2\mathrm{C=O}\)), making A–B attractions stronger than A–A and B–B attractions. This lowers escaping tendency (negative deviation) and releases heat (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}H < 0\)).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When \(18\,\mathrm{g}\) of glucose (molar mass \(180\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) is dissolved in \(178.2\,\mathrm{g}\) of water (molar mass \(18\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) at a temperature where pure water has a vapour pressure of \(24.0\,\mathrm{mmHg}\), the vapour pressure of the solution is:

  • (A) \(23.76\,\mathrm{mmHg}\)
  • (B) \(23.52\,\mathrm{mmHg}\)
  • (C) \(23.90\,\mathrm{mmHg}\)
  • (D) \(23.20\,\mathrm{mmHg}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: Moles of glucose \(n_2 = 18 / 180 = 0.1\,\mathrm{mol}\). Moles of water \(n_1 = 178.2 / 18 = 9.9\,\mathrm{mol}\). Mole fraction of glucose \(x_2 = 0.1 / (9.9 + 0.1) = 0.01\). Lowering of vapour pressure \(\Delta p = x_2 p_1^0 = 0.01 \times 24.0 = 0.24\,\mathrm{mmHg}\). Thus, \(p_1 = 24.0 - 0.24 = 23.76\,\mathrm{mmHg}\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

At the same temperature, a \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (assuming complete dissociation) is isotonic with:

  • (A) \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous glucose
  • (B) \(0.2\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous urea
  • (C) \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous \(\mathrm{BaCl}_2\)
  • (D) \(0.2\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous \(\mathrm{K}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Osmotic pressure \(\Pi = i C R T\). For completely dissociated \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(i = 2\), so effective particle molarity \(iC = 2 \times 0.1 = 0.2\,\mathrm{M}\). Urea is a non-electrolyte (\(i = 1\)), so \(0.2\,\mathrm{M}\) urea also has \(iC = 1 \times 0.2 = 0.2\,\mathrm{M}\), making it isotonic.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Assuming complete dissociation for electrolytes, which \(0.1\,\mathrm{m}\) aqueous solution will have the lowest freezing point?

  • (A) Glucose
  • (B) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{CaCl}_2\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Al}_2(\mathrm{SO}_4)_3\)
Answer: (D)

Solution: \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}} = i K_{\mathrm{f}} m\). For the same molality \(m = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}\), the solute with the highest van't Hoff factor \(i\) produces the largest freezing point depression \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\) and therefore the lowest freezing point \(T_{\mathrm{f}} = T_{\mathrm{f}}^0 - \Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\). Here, \(i = 1\) for glucose, \(2\) for \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(3\) for \(\mathrm{CaCl}_2\), and \(5\) for \(\mathrm{Al}_2(\mathrm{SO}_4)_3\).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A carboxylic acid \(\mathrm{RCOOH}\) dimerises in benzene to the extent of \(80\%\). The van't Hoff factor \(i\) for this solution is:

  • (A) \(0.40\)
  • (B) \(0.60\)
  • (C) \(0.80\)
  • (D) \(1.20\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: For dimerisation, \(n = 2\) and \(\alpha = 0.80\). Using \(i = 1 - \alpha(1 - 1/n) = 1 - 0.80(1 - 1/2) = 1 - 0.40 = 0.60\).

Practice Question 9 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): Aquatic species are more comfortable in cold water than in warm water.

Reason (R): Dissolution of a gas in a liquid is exothermic, so Henry's law constant \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) decreases with increasing temperature.

  • (A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true but R is false.
  • (D) A is false but R is true.
Answer: (C)

Solution: Assertion (A) is true because cold water holds more dissolved \(\mathrm{O}_2\). Reason (R) is false: while gas dissolution is indeed exothermic, increasing temperature lowers gas solubility, which means \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) increases (not decreases) with rising temperature.

Statement II: Osmotic pressure measurements are carried out near room temperature and yield measurable values even for very dilute solutions of macromolecules.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Biomolecules like proteins denature at elevated temperatures (ruling out ebullioscopy) and have very small molalities due to high molar masses (making \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}\) or \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\) too tiny to measure precisely). Osmotic pressure operates at room temperature and produces appreciable pressures even for dilute macromolecular solutions.

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Using \(M\) instead of \(m\) for \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}\) and \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\): Boiling point elevation and freezing point depression use molality (\(m\)), whereas osmotic pressure uses molarity (\(C\)).
  • Confusing \(T_{\mathrm{f}}\) with \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\): Adding more solute increases \(\Delta T_{\mathrm{f}}\), which lowers the actual freezing point \(T_{\mathrm{f}}\).
  • Forgetting the van't Hoff factor \(i\): Always check whether the solute is an electrolyte (e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{K}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\)) or associates in a non-polar solvent (e.g., benzoic acid in benzene) before computing any colligative property.
  • Misreading \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) trends: Larger \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) means lower gas solubility, not higher.
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Can you convert % w/w and density \(\rho\) into both \(M\) and \(m\)?
  • Do you remember \(p = K_{\mathrm{H}}x\) and why \(K_{\mathrm{H}}\) rises with temperature?
  • Can you calculate \(y_i = p_i / p_{\mathrm{total}}\) for an ideal binary mixture?
  • Can you distinguish positive vs. negative deviations and minimum- vs. maximum-boiling azeotropes?
  • Can you compute \(i\) from \(\alpha\) for both dissociation and association?

Concise Final Recap

Solutions are homogeneous mixtures whose composition is described by temperature-independent mass/mole units (% w/w, ppm, \(x\), \(m\)) or temperature-dependent volume units (% V/V, \(M\)). Gas solubility follows Henry's law (\(p = K_{\mathrm{H}}x\)) and decreases with rising temperature. Ideal liquid mixtures obey Raoult's law (\(p_{\mathrm{total}} = x_1 p_1^0 + x_2 p_2^0\)) with \(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}H = 0\) and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{mix}}V = 0\), whereas non-ideal mixtures show positive or negative deviations and can form constant-boiling azeotropes. Colligative properties—relative lowering of vapour pressure, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression, and osmotic pressure—depend strictly on the effective number of solute particles, requiring the van't Hoff factor \(i\) whenever dissociation (\(i > 1\)) or association (\(i < 1\)) occurs.
