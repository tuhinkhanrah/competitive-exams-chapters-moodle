NEET UG Chemistry

The p-Block Elements: Groups 15, 16, 17, and 18

A complete, exam-oriented conceptual tutorial, structural guide, and practice assessment covering the nitrogen, oxygen, halogen, and noble gas families.

Overview and Learning Objectives

The \(p\)-block spans Groups 13 to 18 of the periodic table, unified by a valence shell configuration of \(ns^2 np^{1\text{–}6}\) (with helium as the \(1s^2\) exception). Groups 15 through 18 display a remarkable chemical diversity—ranging from non-metals and metalloids to typical metals—driven by variations in atomic size, ionisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, electronegativity, and the availability of vacant \(d\)-orbitals from the third period onward.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Predict periodic trends, oxidation-state stabilities (including the inert pair effect), and second-period anomalies across Groups 15 to 18.
  • Explain the trends in thermal stability, acidic/basic character, reducing strength, and boiling points of hydrides of Groups 15, 16, and 17.
  • Recall the preparation, key reactions, and industrial conditions for \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\), \(\mathrm{O_3}\), \(\mathrm{SO_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\), and \(\mathrm{HCl}\).
  • Determine the structures, basicity, and redox behaviour of oxoacids of phosphorus, sulphur, and chlorine, as well as interhalogen and xenon compounds using VSEPR theory.

Foundational Periodic Principles Across Groups 15–18

Before studying individual groups, three overarching principles explain nearly every apparent exception tested in NEET UG:

  • Second-Period Anomaly (N, O, F): The lightest member of each group has an exceptionally small atomic radius, high electronegativity, high ionisation enthalpy, and no vacant \(d\)-orbitals in its valence shell (\(n = 2\)). Consequently, N, O, and F are restricted to a maximum covalency of 4 (often lower in practice), form strong \(p\pi\text{–}p\pi\) multiple bonds with themselves or small electronegative atoms, and form hydrogen bonds in their hydrides (\(\mathrm{NH_3, H_2O, HF}\)).
  • Inert Pair Effect: Down Groups 15 and 16, the \(ns^2\) valence electrons experience poor shielding by intervening \(d\) and \(f\) electrons and become reluctant to participate in bonding. Thus, the lower oxidation state (\(+3\) in Group 15; \(+4\) in Group 16) becomes progressively more stable than the group oxidation state (\(+5\) or \(+6\)).
  • Lone-Pair Repulsion in Small Single Bonds: Compact atoms with lone pairs experience strong interelectronic repulsion along short single bonds. This explains why the \(\mathrm{N\text{–}N}\) single bond is weaker than \(\mathrm{P\text{–}P}\), why \(\mathrm{O\text{–}O}\) is weaker than \(\mathrm{S\text{–}S}\), and why \(\mathrm{F\text{–}F}\) has a lower bond dissociation enthalpy than \(\mathrm{Cl\text{–}Cl}\).

Group 15: The Nitrogen Family

Atomic, Physical, and Oxidation-State Trends

Group 15 comprises nitrogen and phosphorus (non-metals), arsenic and antimony (metalloids), and bismuth and synthetic radioactive moscovium (metals). Because of the extra-stable half-filled \(np^3\) subshell and smaller atomic size, Group 15 elements have significantly higher first ionisation enthalpies than corresponding Group 14 elements.

  • Radii and Melting/Boiling Points: Covalent and ionic radii increase down the group, though the jump from As to Bi is modest due to poor shielding by filled \(d\)- and \(f\)-orbitals. Boiling points generally increase down the group, whereas melting points rise up to arsenic and then fall to bismuth.
  • Oxidation States: The stability of the \(-3\) and \(+5\) states decreases down the group, while the \(+3\) state gains stability due to the inert pair effect. The only well-characterised \(\mathrm{Bi(V)}\) compound is \(\mathrm{BiF_5}\). Intermediate oxidation states of nitrogen (\(+1\) to \(+4\)) disproportionate in acid, e.g., \(3\mathrm{HNO_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{HNO_3} + \mathrm{H_2O} + 2\mathrm{NO}\).

Reactivity Trends: Hydrides, Oxides, and Halides

  • Hydrides (\(\mathrm{EH_3}\)): As the \(\mathrm{E\text{–}H}\) bond dissociation enthalpy decreases from \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) to \(\mathrm{BiH_3}\), thermal stability decreases and reducing character increases (\(\mathrm{BiH_3}\) is the strongest reducing agent). Basicity decreases down the group (\(\mathrm{NH_3 > PH_3 > AsH_3 > SbH_3 \ge BiH_3}\)) as the lone pair becomes more diffuse. The \(\mathrm{H\text{–}E\text{–}H}\) bond angle drops sharply from \(107.8^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) (\(sp^3\) hybridised) to \(93.6^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{PH_3}\), \(91.8^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{AsH_3}\), and \(91.3^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{SbH_3}\). Intermolecular hydrogen bonding gives \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) a higher boiling point than \(\mathrm{PH_3}\).
  • Oxides (\(\mathrm{E_2O_3, E_2O_5}\)): Higher-oxidation-state oxides are more acidic than lower ones, and acidity decreases down the group: \(\mathrm{N_2O_3, P_4O_6}\) are acidic; \(\mathrm{As_2O_3, Sb_2O_3}\) are amphoteric; \(\mathrm{Bi_2O_3}\) is predominantly basic.
  • Halides (\(\mathrm{EX_3, EX_5}\)): Pentahalides are more covalent than trihalides because \(\mathrm{E^{5+}}\) has higher polarising power than \(\mathrm{E^{3+}}\). Among nitrogen trihalides, only \(\mathrm{NF_3}\) is stable.

Important Compounds of Nitrogen

Dinitrogen (\(\mathrm{N_2}\)): Inert at room temperature due to its high triple-bond enthalpy (\(941.4\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)). Prepared in the laboratory by warming aqueous \(\mathrm{NH_4Cl}\) with \(\mathrm{NaNO_2}\), or by thermal decomposition of \((\mathrm{NH_4})_2\mathrm{Cr_2O_7}\). Very pure \(\mathrm{N_2}\) is obtained by heating barium or sodium azide: \(\mathrm{Ba(N_3)_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{Ba} + 3\mathrm{N_2}\).

Ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)): Manufactured industrially via Haber's process:

\[\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)} \qquad \Delta_f H^\circ = -46.1\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\]

Optimum conditions are ~\(700\text{ K}\), \(200\text{ atm}\) (\(200 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}\)), and an iron oxide catalyst promoted with \(\mathrm{K_2O}\) and \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\). Trigonal pyramidal \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) acts as a weak Brønsted base in water and a Lewis base toward transition-metal ions, forming deep-blue \([\mathrm{Cu(NH_3)_4}]^{2+}\) and colourless \([\mathrm{Ag(NH_3)_2}]^+\).

Oxides of Nitrogen: \(\mathrm{N_2O}\) (\(+1\)) and \(\mathrm{NO}\) (\(+2\)) are neutral gases, whereas \(\mathrm{N_2O_3}\) (\(+3\), blue solid), \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) (\(+4\), brown paramagnetic gas), \(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\) (\(+4\), colourless dimer of \(\mathrm{NO_2}\)), and \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\) (\(+5\), colourless solid) are acidic. Because \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) has an odd electron, it dimerises readily to even-electron \(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\).

Nitric Acid (\(\mathrm{HNO_3}\)): Manufactured by Ostwald's process via catalytic oxidation of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) over \(\mathrm{Pt/Rh}\) gauze at \(500\text{ K}\) and \(9\text{ bar}\) to \(\mathrm{NO}\), followed by oxidation to \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) and absorption in water. Aqueous \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) is a strong monobasic acid and a powerful oxidising agent whose reduction product depends on acid concentration and metal reactivity:

  • \(3\mathrm{Cu} + 8\mathrm{HNO_3(dil.)} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{Cu(NO_3)_2} + 2\mathrm{NO} + 4\mathrm{H_2O}\)
  • \(\mathrm{Cu} + 4\mathrm{HNO_3(conc.)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu(NO_3)_2} + 2\mathrm{NO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\)
  • \(4\mathrm{Zn} + 10\mathrm{HNO_3(dil.)} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{Zn(NO_3)_2} + \mathrm{N_2O} + 5\mathrm{H_2O}\)
  • Metals such as \(\mathrm{Cr}\) and \(\mathrm{Al}\) become passive in concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) due to a protective surface oxide film.
  • Brown Ring Test for \(\mathrm{NO_3^-}\): Reduction of nitrate by \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) in concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) yields \(\mathrm{NO}\), which coordinates to \([\mathrm{Fe(H_2O)_6}]^{2+}\) to form the brown complex \([\mathrm{Fe(H_2O)_5(NO)}]^{2+}\) at the acid–solution interface.

Phosphorus: Allotropes, Halides, and Oxoacids

Phosphorus exists mainly as white, red, and black allotropes. White phosphorus consists of discrete tetrahedral \(\mathrm{P_4}\) molecules with \(60^\circ\) \(\mathrm{P\text{–}P\text{–}P}\) bond angles, causing severe angular strain that makes it highly reactive, waxy, poisonous, soluble in \(\mathrm{CS_2}\), and chemiluminescent in the dark. Boiling white \(\mathrm{P_4}\) with concentrated \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) in an inert \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) atmosphere yields phosphine (\(\mathrm{PH_3}\)) and sodium hypophosphite (\(\mathrm{NaH_2PO_2}\)). Red phosphorus (obtained by heating white phosphorus at \(573\text{ K}\) in an inert atmosphere) is a polymeric chain of linked \(\mathrm{P_4}\) tetrahedra; it is non-poisonous, insoluble in \(\mathrm{CS_2}\), and far less reactive. Black phosphorus (\(\alpha\)- and \(\beta\)-forms) is the most thermodynamically stable.

Among halides, pyramidal \(\mathrm{PCl_3}\) hydrolyses to \(\mathrm{H_3PO_3} + 3\mathrm{HCl}\), while \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) has a trigonal bipyramidal geometry in gas and liquid phases with three shorter equatorial bonds (\(202\text{ pm}\)) and two longer axial bonds (\(240\text{ pm}\)) due to greater axial bond-pair–bond-pair repulsion. Moist air hydrolyses \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) first to \(\mathrm{POCl_3}\) and then to \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\).

Group 16: The Oxygen Family (Chalcogens)

Periodic Trends and Hydrides

Group 16 elements have \(ns^2 np^4\) configurations. Their first ionisation enthalpies are lower than those of Group 15 in the same period because Group 15 possesses extra-stable half-filled \(np^3\) orbitals. Due to the compact size of the \(2p\) subshell in oxygen, interelectronic repulsion makes oxygen's electron gain enthalpy (\(-141\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) less negative than sulphur's (\(-200\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)).

  • Atomicity & Physical State: Oxygen is a diatomic gas (\(\mathrm{O_2}\), paramagnetic with two unpaired \(\pi^*\) electrons), whereas sulphur is a polyatomic solid (\(\mathrm{S_8}\)), accounting for the sharp jump in melting and boiling points from O to S.
  • Hydrides (\(\mathrm{H_2E}\)): Down the group from \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) to \(\mathrm{H_2Te}\), the \(\mathrm{H\text{–}E}\) bond dissociation enthalpy decreases. Consequently, acidic strength and reducing character increase (\(\mathrm{H_2O < H_2S < H_2Se < H_2Te}\)), while thermal stability decreases. Water is a liquid due to extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding, whereas \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) is a gas.
  • Halides: Hexafluorides (\(\mathrm{SF_6}\)) are octahedral and \(\mathrm{SF_6}\) is kinetically inert for steric reasons. Tetrafluorides (\(\mathrm{SF_4}\)) are \(sp^3d\) hybridised with a see-saw geometry (one equatorial lone pair).

Simple Oxides, Dioxygen, and Ozone

Simple oxides are classified as acidic (non-metal oxides like \(\mathrm{SO_2, Cl_2O_7}\) and high-oxidation-state metal oxides like \(\mathrm{Mn_2O_7, CrO_3, V_2O_5}\)), basic (\(\mathrm{Na_2O, CaO}\)), amphoteric (\(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\), reacting with both acids and alkalies), or neutral (\(\mathrm{CO, NO, N_2O}\)).

Ozone (\(\mathrm{O_3}\)): Prepared by passing dry \(\mathrm{O_2}\) through a silent electrical discharge (\(3\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{O_3}\), \(\Delta H^\circ = +142\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) to prevent thermal decomposition. \(\mathrm{O_3}\) is an angular resonance hybrid (bond angle ~\(117^\circ\), equal \(\mathrm{O\text{–}O}\) bond lengths of \(128\text{ pm}\)). Its decomposition to \(\mathrm{O_2}\) has \(\Delta H < 0\) and \(\Delta S > 0\), making \(\Delta G\) strongly negative and pure concentrated \(\mathrm{O_3}\) dangerously explosive. It acts as a powerful oxidising agent (e.g., \(\mathrm{PbS} \rightarrow \mathrm{PbSO_4}\)) and is estimated quantitatively by reacting with excess \(\mathrm{KI}\) buffered at pH 9.2 (borate buffer) and titrating liberated \(\mathrm{I_2}\) with \(\mathrm{Na_2S_2O_3}\). Stratospheric \(\mathrm{O_3}\) is depleted by \(\mathrm{NO}\) from supersonic jets (\(\mathrm{NO + O_3 \rightarrow NO_2 + O_2}\)) and freons.

Sulphur Allotropes, \(\mathrm{SO_2}\), Oxoacids, and \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)

  • Allotropes: Yellow rhombic (\(\alpha\)-) sulphur (stable below \(369\text{ K}\), soluble in \(\mathrm{CS_2}\)) transforms into needle-shaped monoclinic (\(\beta\)-) sulphur above the transition temperature of \(369\text{ K}\). Both contain puckered crown-shaped \(\mathrm{S_8}\) rings. At ~\(1000\text{ K}\), sulphur vapour exists predominantly as \(\mathrm{S_2}\), which is paramagnetic like \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  • Sulphur Dioxide (\(\mathrm{SO_2}\)): Angular resonance hybrid with two equal \(\mathrm{S\text{–}O}\) bonds. Moist \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) acts as a reducing agent, reducing \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) to \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) and decolourising acidified \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) (diagnostic test).
  • Contact Process for \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\): Catalytic oxidation \(2\mathrm{SO_2(g) + O_2(g) \xrightarrow{V_2O_5} 2SO_3(g)}\) (\(\Delta_r H^\circ = -196.6\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) is operated at \(2\text{ bar}\) and \(720\text{ K}\). \(\mathrm{SO_3}\) is absorbed in concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) to give oleum (\(\mathrm{H_2S_2O_7}\)), which is diluted to 96–98% \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\). Concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) is a strong dibasic acid (\(K_{a_1} \gg K_{a_2}\)), a dehydrating agent (chars carbohydrates), and a hot oxidising agent (\(\mathrm{Cu + 2H_2SO_4 \rightarrow CuSO_4 + SO_2 + 2H_2O}\)).

Group 17: The Halogens

Key Anomalies and Oxidising Power

Halogens (\(ns^2 np^5\)) have the smallest atomic radii and highest negative electron gain enthalpies in their respective periods. Two classic NEET UG anomalies arise from fluorine's compact \(2p\) orbitals:

  • Electron Gain Enthalpy Magnitude: \(\mathrm{Cl}\ (-349\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}) > \mathrm{F}\ (-333\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}) > \mathrm{Br}\ (-325\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}) > \mathrm{I}\ (-296\text{ kJ mol}^{-1})\). Incoming electrons face strong repulsion in fluorine's small \(2p\) shell.
  • Bond Dissociation Enthalpy: \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\ (242.6) > \mathrm{Br_2}\ (192.8) > \mathrm{F_2}\ (158.8) > \mathrm{I_2}\ (151.1)\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\), because lone-pair–lone-pair repulsion is severe across the short \(\mathrm{F\text{–}F}\) bond.
  • Oxidising Power (\(\mathrm{F_2 > Cl_2 > Br_2 > I_2}\)): Despite fluorine's lower electron gain enthalpy, \(\mathrm{F_2}\) is the strongest oxidising agent in aqueous solution (\(E^\circ = +2.87\text{ V}\)) owing to its low \(\mathrm{F\text{–}F}\) bond dissociation enthalpy and very high hydration enthalpy of \(\mathrm{F^-}\). Consequently, \(\mathrm{F_2}\) oxidises water to \(\mathrm{O_2}\), whereas \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) and \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) form \(\mathrm{HX + HOX}\), and \(\mathrm{I^-}\) is oxidised by \(\mathrm{O_2}\) in acid.

Hydrogen Halides, Chlorine, and Hydrochloric Acid

  • Hydrogen Halides (\(\mathrm{HX}\)): Acidic strength increases down the group (\(\mathrm{HF < HCl < HBr < HI}\)) as \(\mathrm{H\text{–}X}\) bond dissociation enthalpy drops (\(574 \rightarrow 295\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)). \(\mathrm{HF}\) is a liquid (b.p. \(293\text{ K}\)) due to hydrogen bonding.
  • Chlorine (\(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)): Manufactured by Deacon's process (\(4\mathrm{HCl + O_2 \xrightarrow{CuCl_2,\,723\,K} 2Cl_2 + 2H_2O}\)) or brine electrolysis. With cold, dilute \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) it gives \(\mathrm{NaCl + NaOCl}\); with hot, concentrated \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) it disproportionates to \(5\mathrm{NaCl + NaClO_3}\). With dry slaked lime it forms bleaching powder (\(\mathrm{Ca(OCl)_2 \cdot CaCl_2 \cdot Ca(OH)_2 \cdot 2H_2O}\)). Chlorine bleaches permanently by oxidation via nascent oxygen from \(\mathrm{HOCl}\).
  • Aqua Regia: A \(3:1\) mixture of concentrated \(\mathrm{HCl}\) and concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) dissolves noble metals like \(\mathrm{Au}\) (forming \(\mathrm{AuCl_4^-}\)) and \(\mathrm{Pt}\) (forming \(\mathrm{PtCl_6^{2-}}\)).

Oxoacids of Halogens and Interhalogen Compounds

Fluorine forms only \(\mathrm{HOF}\). Chlorine forms four oxoacids: \(\mathrm{HOCl}\) (\(+1\)), \(\mathrm{HOClO}\) (\(+3\)), \(\mathrm{HOClO_2}\) (\(+5\)), and \(\mathrm{HOClO_3}\) (\(+7\)), in all of which chlorine is \(sp^3\) hybridised and acidic strength increases with the oxidation state of chlorine.

Interhalogens (\(\mathrm{XX', XX'_3, XX'_5, XX'_7}\)): Formed between a larger, more electropositive halogen \(\mathrm{X}\) and a smaller halogen \(\mathrm{X'}\). All are covalent, diamagnetic, and more reactive than pure halogens (except \(\mathrm{F_2}\)) because the \(\mathrm{X\text{–}X'}\) bond is weaker than \(\mathrm{X\text{–}X}\). On hydrolysis, the smaller halogen always forms the halide ion (\(\mathrm{HX'}\)) while the larger halogen forms an oxoacid/oxoanion (\(\mathrm{HOX}, \mathrm{HXO_2}, \mathrm{HXO_3}, \mathrm{HXO_4}\)).

Group 18: The Noble Gases

Noble gases are monoatomic, sparingly soluble in water, and possess very low boiling points (He: \(4.2\text{ K}\)) because only weak London dispersion forces act between atoms. Their closed-shell \(ns^2 np^6\) configurations give them very high ionisation enthalpies and positive electron gain enthalpies.

In 1962, Neil Bartlett noted that the first ionisation enthalpy of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) (\(1175\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) nearly matches that of \(\mathrm{Xe}\) (\(1170\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)). Since \(\mathrm{PtF_6}\) oxidised \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to red \(\mathrm{O_2^+[PtF_6]^-}\), Bartlett reacted \(\mathrm{Xe}\) with \(\mathrm{PtF_6}\) to obtain \(\mathrm{Xe^+[PtF_6]^-}\), opening noble-gas chemistry.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Predicting Basicity and Reducing Strength of Phosphorus Oxoacids

Problem: A student prepares an aqueous solution containing \(0.10\text{ mol}\) of hypophosphorous acid (\(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\)) (assumed quantity) and titrates it completely with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\). How many moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) are required, and why does \(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\) reduce \(\mathrm{Ag^+}\) to metallic \(\mathrm{Ag}\) while \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\) does not?

Solution:

In phosphorus oxoacids, only hydrogen atoms bonded to oxygen (\(\mathrm{P\text{–}OH}\)) are ionisable; hydrogen atoms bonded directly to phosphorus (\(\mathrm{P\text{–}H}\)) are non-ionisable and impart reducing character.

  • \(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\) contains one \(\mathrm{P\text{–}OH}\) bond, two \(\mathrm{P\text{–}H}\) bonds, and one \(\mathrm{P=O}\) bond. Thus it is monobasic, so \(0.10\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\) requires exactly \(0.10\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\).
  • Because \(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\) has two \(\mathrm{P\text{–}H}\) bonds, it acts as a strong reducing agent (\(4\mathrm{AgNO_3} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{H_3PO_2} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{Ag} + 4\mathrm{HNO_3} + \mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)). \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\) has zero \(\mathrm{P\text{–}H}\) bonds and phosphorus is already in its maximum \(+5\) oxidation state, so it cannot act as a reducing agent.
Example 2: Explaining Oxidising Power vs Electron Gain Enthalpy in Halogens

Problem: Although chlorine has a more negative electron gain enthalpy (\(-349\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) than fluorine (\(-333\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)), fluorine is a much stronger oxidising agent in aqueous solution. Account for this using thermodynamic steps.

Solution:

The standard reduction potential of a halogen \(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{X_2(g)} + e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{X^-(aq)}\) depends on three enthalpy terms:

\[ \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{X_2(g)} \xrightarrow{\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{diss}}H^\circ} \mathrm{X(g)} \xrightarrow{\Delta_{\text{eg}}H^\circ} \mathrm{X^-(g)} \xrightarrow{\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ} \mathrm{X^-(aq)} \]

Fluorine compensates for its slightly less negative \(\Delta_{\text{eg}}H^\circ\) in two ways: (1) it has a much lower \(\mathrm{F\text{–}F}\) bond dissociation enthalpy (\(158.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\) vs \(242.6\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\) for \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)), and (2) the tiny \(\mathrm{F^-}\) ion has a substantially higher (more negative) hydration enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\circ = -515\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\) vs \(-381\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\) for \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\)). Hence \(\mathrm{F_2}\) has a higher \(E^\circ\) (\(+2.87\text{ V}\) vs \(+1.36\text{ V}\)) and is the stronger oxidising agent.

Example 3: Determining VSEPR Geometry and Redox Nature of Xenon Hydrolysis

Problem: Predict the geometry of \(\mathrm{XeO_2F_2}\) formed during the partial hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{XeF_6}\), and determine whether this reaction is a redox process.

Solution:

The partial hydrolysis equation is:

\[\mathrm{XeF_6 + 2H_2O \rightarrow XeO_2F_2 + 4HF}\]
  • Geometry: In \(\mathrm{XeO_2F_2}\), the central \(\mathrm{Xe}\) atom has 8 valence electrons. Two \(\mathrm{O}\) atoms form two double bonds (4 electrons) and two \(\mathrm{F}\) atoms form two single bonds (2 electrons), leaving 1 lone pair (2 electrons). With 4 \(\sigma\)-bond pairs + 1 lone pair = 5 steric domains (\(sp^3d\)), the lone pair and the two \(\mathrm{O}\) atoms occupy equatorial positions while the two \(\mathrm{F}\) atoms occupy axial positions, giving a see-saw molecular shape.
  • Redox check: In \(\mathrm{XeF_6}\), \(\mathrm{Xe}\) is in the \(+6\) oxidation state and \(\mathrm{F}\) is \(-1\). In \(\mathrm{XeO_2F_2}\), \(\mathrm{Xe}\) remains \(+6\) (\(x + 2(-2) + 2(-1) = 0 \implies x = +6\)). Since no element changes oxidation state, this is a non-redox reaction.
Example 4: Stoichiometry of Chlorine Disproportionation in Hot Alkali

Problem: Suppose \(0.60\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) gas (assumed quantity) is passed completely into hot, concentrated aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\). Calculate the moles of \(\mathrm{NaClO_3}\) formed and state the oxidation states of chlorine in the products.

Solution:

In hot, concentrated \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), chlorine undergoes disproportionation:

\[3\mathrm{Cl_2} + 6\mathrm{NaOH} \rightarrow 5\mathrm{NaCl} + \mathrm{NaClO_3} + 3\mathrm{H_2O}\]
  • Oxidation state of \(\mathrm{Cl}\) changes from \(0\) in \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) to \(-1\) in \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) and \(+5\) in \(\mathrm{NaClO_3}\).
  • From the stoichiometry, \(3\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) yields \(1\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaClO_3}\). Therefore, \(0.60\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) produces \(\frac{0.60}{3} = 0.20\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaClO_3}\).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements regarding the hydrides of Group 15 elements (\(\mathrm{EH_3}\)) is incorrect?

  1. (A) Thermal stability decreases from \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) to \(\mathrm{BiH_3}\).
  2. (B) \(\mathrm{BiH_3}\) is the strongest reducing agent among all Group 15 hydrides.
  3. (C) Basicity increases in the order \(\mathrm{NH_3 < PH_3 < AsH_3 < SbH_3}\).
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) has a higher boiling point than \(\mathrm{PH_3}\) due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Basicity of Group 15 hydrides decreases down the group (\(\mathrm{NH_3 > PH_3 > AsH_3 > SbH_3 \ge BiH_3}\)) because increasing atomic size makes the lone pair more diffuse and less available for protonation. Statements (A), (B), and (D) are all correct.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When copper metal is treated separately with dilute \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) and concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\), the nitrogen-containing gases evolved are, respectively:

  1. (A) \(\mathrm{N_2O}\) and \(\mathrm{NO_2}\)
  2. (B) \(\mathrm{NO}\) and \(\mathrm{NO_2}\)
  3. (C) \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{NO}\)
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{N_2O}\) and \(\mathrm{NO}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Copper reacts with dilute \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) to evolve \(\mathrm{NO}\) (\(3\mathrm{Cu} + 8\mathrm{HNO_3(dil.)} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{Cu(NO_3)_2} + 2\mathrm{NO} + 4\mathrm{H_2O}\)) and with concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) to evolve \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) (\(\mathrm{Cu} + 4\mathrm{HNO_3(conc.)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu(NO_3)_2} + 2\mathrm{NO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\)). (Note: \(\mathrm{Zn}\) with dilute \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) gives \(\mathrm{N_2O}\)).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The number of \(\mathrm{P\text{–}OH}\), \(\mathrm{P\text{–}H}\), and \(\mathrm{P\text{–}O\text{–}P}\) bonds present in pyrophosphoric acid (\(\mathrm{H_4P_2O_7}\)) are, respectively:

  1. (A) 4, 0, 1
  2. (B) 2, 2, 1
  3. (C) 4, 2, 0
  4. (D) 3, 1, 1
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Pyrophosphoric acid (\(\mathrm{H_4P_2O_7}\)) has phosphorus in the \(+5\) oxidation state and is formed by dehydrating two molecules of \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\). It contains four \(\mathrm{P\text{–}OH}\) bonds (tetrabasic), two \(\mathrm{P=O}\) bonds, one \(\mathrm{P\text{–}O\text{–}P}\) linkage, and zero \(\mathrm{P\text{–}H}\) bonds.

Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Statement I: The first ionisation enthalpy of oxygen is lower than that of nitrogen.
Statement II: The electron gain enthalpy of oxygen is more negative than that of sulphur.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Statement I is correct because nitrogen has an extra-stable half-filled \(2p^3\) configuration, making its first ionisation enthalpy (\(1402\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) higher than oxygen's (\(1314\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)). Statement II is incorrect because oxygen's compact \(2p\) orbitals cause strong interelectronic repulsion, making its electron gain enthalpy (\(-141\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) less negative than sulphur's (\(-200\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements about allotropes of sulphur is correct?

  1. (A) Monoclinic (\(\beta\)-) sulphur is stable below \(369\text{ K}\).
  2. (B) Both rhombic and monoclinic sulphur exist as \(\mathrm{S_6}\) rings in the chair conformation.
  3. (C) At around \(1000\text{ K}\), sulphur vapour exists predominantly as \(\mathrm{S_2}\), which is paramagnetic.
  4. (D) Rhombic sulphur is readily soluble in water.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: At elevated temperatures (~\(1000\text{ K}\)), \(\mathrm{S_2}\) is the dominant species and, like \(\mathrm{O_2}\), has two unpaired electrons in antibonding \(\pi^*\) orbitals, making it paramagnetic. Rhombic (\(\alpha\)-) sulphur is stable below \(369\text{ K}\), both \(\alpha\)- and \(\beta\)-forms contain crown \(\mathrm{S_8}\) rings, and rhombic sulphur is insoluble in water (soluble in \(\mathrm{CS_2}\)).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid:
  • Basicity of \(\mathrm{H_3PO_3}\) and \(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\): Never count all H atoms! \(\mathrm{H_3PO_3}\) is dibasic (2 \(\mathrm{P\text{–}OH}\)), and \(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\) is monobasic (1 \(\mathrm{P\text{–}OH}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{Cu}\) vs \(\mathrm{Zn}\) with dilute \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\): \(\mathrm{Cu}\) gives \(\mathrm{NO}\), whereas \(\mathrm{Zn}\) gives \(\mathrm{N_2O}\). Both give \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) with concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\).
  • Fluorine vs Chlorine Order: Electron gain enthalpy magnitude is \(\mathrm{Cl > F}\); bond dissociation enthalpy is \(\mathrm{Cl_2 > Br_2 > F_2 > I_2}\); oxidising power is \(\mathrm{F_2 > Cl_2 > Br_2 > I_2}\).
  • \(K_{a_1}\) vs \(K_{a_2}\) for \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\): \(K_{a_1} \gg K_{a_2}\) because removing \(\mathrm{H^+}\) from negatively charged \(\mathrm{HSO_4^-}\) is much harder than from neutral \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\).
  • Hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{XeF_6}\) vs \(\mathrm{XeF_2 / XeF_4}\): Complete or partial hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{XeF_6}\) is non-redox; hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{XeF_2}\) and \(\mathrm{XeF_4}\) is redox.
Concise Final Recap:
  • Second-period elements (N, O, F) lack \(d\)-orbitals, cap covalency at 4, form \(p\pi\text{–}p\pi\) bonds, and exhibit hydrogen bonding in their hydrides.
  • Inert pair effect stabilises lower oxidation states (\(+3\) in Bi, \(+4\) in Po/Te) down the group.
  • Hydride acidic strength increases down Group 16 (\(\mathrm{H_2O \rightarrow H_2Te}\)) and Group 17 (\(\mathrm{HF \rightarrow HI}\)), while Group 15 hydride basicity decreases (\(\mathrm{NH_3 \rightarrow BiH_3}\)).
  • VSEPR geometries of interhalogens and xenon compounds depend strictly on total steric domains (bond pairs + lone pairs).
