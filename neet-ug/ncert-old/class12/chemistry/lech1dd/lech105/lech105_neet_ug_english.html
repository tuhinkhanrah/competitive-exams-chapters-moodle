Surface Chemistry: Adsorption, Catalysis, and Colloidal Systems

A self-contained, concept-first tutorial with thermodynamics, mechanisms, visual models, worked examples, and NEET UG practice assessment.

Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Surface chemistry examines chemical and physical phenomena occurring at the boundary (interface) between two distinct bulk phases. Because atoms or molecules at a surface experience unbalanced forces compared to those inside the bulk, interfaces exhibit unique reactivity, binding capacity, and electrical properties.

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between adsorption, absorption, and sorption, and explain the thermodynamic driving forces (\(\Delta H\), \(\Delta S\), and \(\Delta G\)) behind spontaneous adsorption.
  • Compare physisorption and chemisorption across bond nature, enthalpy, reversibility, specificity, and temperature dependence.
  • Interpret the Freundlich adsorption isotherm for gases and solutions, including its linear logarithmic form and high-pressure limitations.
  • Explain homogeneous, heterogeneous, shape-selective (zeolite), and enzyme catalysis using surface-intermediate and lock-and-key models.
  • Classify colloidal dispersions by physical state, phase interaction, and particle structure, including micelle formation above the Kraft temperature and critical micelle concentration (CMC).
  • Analyze colloidal preparation, purification, optical/kinetic properties (Tyndall effect, Brownian motion), electrical double-layer stability, and coagulation via the Hardy–Schulze rule.

1. Interfacial Phenomena & Adsorption

1.1 Interfaces, Adsorbate, and Adsorbent

A phase boundary is denoted by separating the two bulk phases with a hyphen or slash (for example, solid–gas or solid/liquid). Note that two gases never form an interface because they are completely miscible. Studying pure solid surfaces requires ultra-high vacuum (\(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{-9}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)) so that atmospheric \(\mathrm{O}_2\) and \(\mathrm{N}_2\) do not immediately coat the surface.

  • Adsorption: The accumulation of molecular species strictly at the surface of a solid or liquid rather than penetrating into its bulk.
  • Adsorbate: The substance (gas or solute) that concentrates on the surface.
  • Adsorbent: The solid or liquid material providing the surface (e.g., activated charcoal, silica gel, alumina gel, clay, finely divided metals).
  • Desorption: The reverse process of removing an adsorbed substance from the surface.
Feature Adsorption Absorption Sorption
Location of uptake Concentrated only at the surface; bulk remains unaffected. Distributed uniformly throughout the entire bulk volume. Both surface adsorption and bulk absorption occur together.
Rate profile Rapid initially, slows down as surface sites saturate. Proceeds at a steady, uniform rate throughout the bulk. Combined surface-rapid and bulk-diffusion kinetics.
Classic examples Water vapour on silica gel; methylene blue dye on animal charcoal; ink colour on the outer skin of a chalk stick. Water vapour taken up by anhydrous \(\mathrm{CaCl}_2\); solvent of ink soaking into the interior of a chalk stick. Dyes taken up by textile fibres; \(\mathrm{H}_2\) uptake by palladium metal.

1.2 Mechanism & Thermodynamics of Adsorption

Inside a solid, every particle is surrounded symmetrically by neighbours, so attractive forces cancel out. At the surface, particles lack neighbours on the outer side and therefore possess unbalanced (residual) attractive forces. These residual forces pull adsorbate molecules onto the surface. Consequently, finely divided or porous solids have a much larger surface area per unit mass and act as far stronger adsorbents than smooth crystals.

When adsorption occurs, residual surface forces decrease, releasing energy as heat—making adsorption invariably exothermic (\(\Delta H < 0\)). Simultaneously, gas or solute molecules lose translational freedom upon binding to the surface, which decreases randomness (\(\Delta S < 0\)).

\[ \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S \]

For spontaneous adsorption at constant \(T\) and \(P\), \(\Delta G < 0\). Since \(-T\Delta S > 0\), \(\Delta H\) must be sufficiently negative (\(|\Delta H| > |T\Delta S|\)). As surface coverage grows, \(\Delta H\) becomes less negative until \(\Delta H = T\Delta S\) and \(\Delta G = 0\) (adsorption equilibrium).

1.3 Physisorption vs. Chemisorption

Depending on the nature of the forces holding the adsorbate to the surface, gas adsorption on solids is classified into two distinct regimes. Notably, physisorption at low temperature can transition into chemisorption as temperature rises (for instance, \(\mathrm{H}_2\) initially physisorbs on nickel via van der Waals forces, then dissociates into \(\mathrm{H}\) atoms that form chemical bonds with nickel).

Property Physical Adsorption (Physisorption) Chemical Adsorption (Chemisorption)
Binding force Weak universal van der Waals forces. Strong covalent or ionic chemical bonds.
Specificity Non-specific; any gas adsorbs to some degree. Highly specific; requires chemical affinity between adsorbate and adsorbent.
Reversibility Reversible (\(\text{Solid} + \text{Gas} \rightleftharpoons \text{Gas/Solid} + \text{Heat}\)). Usually irreversible due to surface compound formation.
Enthalpy (\(\Delta H_{\text{ads}}\)) Low: \(20\text{–}40\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). High: \(80\text{–}240\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).
Activation energy (\(E_a\)) Negligible; occurs rapidly even at low \(T\). High; often called activated adsorption.
Temperature effect Decreases steadily with rising temperature (Le Chatelier's principle). Initially increases with \(T\) (to overcome \(E_a\)), then decreases at very high \(T\).
Layer thickness Forms multimolecular layers at high pressure. Forms strictly a unimolecular layer (monolayer).
NEET Key Rule — Ease of Liquefaction: In physisorption, gases with higher critical temperatures (\(T_c\)) liquefy more easily because their intermolecular van der Waals forces are stronger, leading to greater adsorption on a given mass of charcoal. For example: \(\mathrm{SO}_2\) (\(T_c = 630\,\mathrm{K}\)) > \(\mathrm{CH}_4\) (\(T_c = 190\,\mathrm{K}\)) > \(\mathrm{H}_2\) (\(T_c = 33\,\mathrm{K}\)).

1.4 Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm

An adsorption isotherm plots the mass of gas adsorbed per unit mass of adsorbent (\(x/m\)) against equilibrium pressure (\(p\)) at constant temperature. In 1909, Freundlich proposed an empirical power-law relation valid over an intermediate pressure range:

\[ \frac{x}{m} = k\,p^{1/n} \quad (n > 1) \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \log\left(\frac{x}{m}\right) = \log k + \frac{1}{n}\log p \]
  • Here \(k\) and \(n\) are empirical constants depending on the adsorbent, gas, and temperature.
  • The exponent \(1/n\) lies between \(0\) and \(1\) (typically \(0.1\) to \(0.5\)).
  • Low pressure limit (\(1/n = 1\)): \(x/m = kp\), so adsorption is directly proportional to pressure.
  • High pressure limit (\(1/n = 0\)): \(x/m = k\) (constant), meaning the surface is saturated and independent of pressure. Because experimental curves level off completely at very high pressures while the power-law equation predicts continued growth, the Freundlich isotherm fails at high pressure.
  • Solution phase: Replacing pressure \(p\) with equilibrium concentration \(C\) yields \(\frac{x}{m} = k\,C^{1/n}\), applicable to solutes such as aqueous acetic acid adsorbed on charcoal.

1.5 Practical Applications of Adsorption

Adsorption underpins high-vacuum generation (charcoal traps residual air), gas masks in coal mines (activated charcoal adsorbs toxic gases), humidity control (silica and alumina gels), sugar decolourization (animal charcoal), noble gas separation on coconut charcoal at graded temperatures, froth floatation of sulphide ores, adsorption indicators (eosin/fluorescein on \(\mathrm{AgX}\) precipitates), and chromatographic analysis.

2. Catalysis & Enzyme Action

2.1 Catalysts, Promoters, and Poisons

A catalyst alters the rate of a chemical reaction without undergoing permanent chemical or quantitative change itself (for example, \(\mathrm{MnO}_2\) lowers the decomposition temperature of \(\mathrm{KClO}_3\) from \(653\text{–}873\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(473\text{–}633\,\mathrm{K}\)).

  • Promoters: Substances that enhance catalyst activity (e.g., molybdenum \(\mathrm{Mo}\) promotes iron \(\mathrm{Fe}\) in Haber's ammonia synthesis).
  • Poisons: Substances that reduce or destroy catalyst activity (e.g., \(\mathrm{CO}\) or arsenic impurities poisoning industrial metal catalysts).

(2) Acid-catalysed ester hydrolysis: \(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOCH}_3(\mathrm{l}) + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{HCl}(\mathrm{aq})} \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH} + \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{OH}\)

(4) Hydrogenation of vegetable oils (liquid) with \(\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g})\) over finely divided \(\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{s})\).

2.3 Modern Adsorption Theory of Heterogeneous Catalysis

Combining classical adsorption with intermediate compound formation, heterogeneous catalysis proceeds through five sequential surface steps: (1) diffusion of reactants to the catalyst surface, (2) chemisorption of reactants at free surface valencies, (3) surface reaction forming an activated intermediate, (4) desorption of products to regenerate active sites, and (5) diffusion of products away from the surface.

2.4 Key Features of Solid Catalysts & Zeolites

  • Activity: Depends on moderate chemisorption strength—strong enough to activate reactants, yet not so strong as to immobilize them. For hydrogenation, catalytic activity peaks at Groups 7–9 transition metals.
  • Selectivity: The ability of a catalyst to direct the same reactants toward different products:
    • \(\mathrm{CO}(\mathrm{g}) + 3\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g}) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Ni}} \mathrm{CH}_4(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}(\mathrm{g})\)
    • \(\mathrm{CO}(\mathrm{g}) + 2\mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g}) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Cu/ZnO\text{-}Cr_2O_3}} \mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{OH}(\mathrm{g})\)
    • \(\mathrm{CO}(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{H}_2(\mathrm{g}) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Cu}} \mathrm{HCHO}(\mathrm{g})\)
  • Shape-Selective Catalysis (Zeolites): Zeolites are microporous aluminosilicates with honeycomb-like 3D \(\mathrm{Al–O–Si}\) frameworks. Catalytic selectivity depends on pore geometry matching reactant and product molecular sizes. Notably, ZSM-5 dehydrates alcohols directly into gasoline (petrol).

2.5 Enzyme (Biochemical) Catalysis

Enzymes are high-molecular-mass protein molecules forming colloidal solutions in water that catalyse biological reactions with extraordinary efficiency (up to \(10^6\) molecules per minute per enzyme molecule) and strict specificity. They operate best at an optimum temperature of \(298\text{–}310\,\mathrm{K}\) (human body temperature \(310\,\mathrm{K}\)) and an optimum pH of \(5\text{–}7\).

Their activity increases with non-protein vitamin co-enzymes or metal-ion activators (e.g., \(\mathrm{Na}^+\) for amylase, \(\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}\), \(\mathrm{Co}^{2+}\), \(\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}\)) and is suppressed by inhibitors/poisons blocking active surface groups (\(-\mathrm{NH}_2\), \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), \(-\mathrm{SH}\), \(-\mathrm{OH}\)). According to the lock-and-key mechanism, a complementary substrate \(\mathrm{S}\) binds the enzyme cavity \(\mathrm{E}\) to form an activated complex \(\mathrm{ES}^*\), which decomposes into product \(\mathrm{P}\) and free enzyme \(\mathrm{E}\).

3. The Colloidal State & Classification

A colloid is a heterogeneous system in which one substance (the dispersed phase) is distributed as very fine particles—with diameters between \(1\,\mathrm{nm}\) and \(1000\,\mathrm{nm}\) (\(10^{-9}\) to \(10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}\))—throughout a continuous dispersion medium. Subdividing a \(1\,\mathrm{cm}\) cube (surface area \(6\,\mathrm{cm}^2\)) into \(10^{12}\) colloidal-sized cubes expands the total surface area to \(6\,\mathrm{m}^2\), explaining why surface phenomena dominate colloidal behaviour.

3.1 Classification by Physical State

Eight colloidal combinations exist (gas–gas mixtures are always homogeneous true solutions, never colloids). When water is the dispersion medium, a sol is called a hydrosol or aquasol; when alcohol is the medium, it is an alcosol.

3.2 Classification by Phase Interaction & Particle Type

  • Lyophilic (solvent-loving) vs. Lyophobic (solvent-hating) sols: Lyophilic sols (gum, gelatin, starch, rubber) form directly on mixing, are heavily solvated, stable against electrolytes, and reversible upon re-mixing after evaporation. Lyophobic sols (metals, metallic sulphides) require special preparation methods, need stabilizing agents, coagulate easily on adding small amounts of electrolytes, and are irreversible.
  • Multimolecular colloids: Aggregates of many small atoms or molecules held by van der Waals forces within \(1\text{–}1000\,\mathrm{nm}\) (e.g., gold sol, \(\mathrm{S}_8\) sulphur sol).
  • Macromolecular colloids: Single giant molecules whose dimensions naturally fall in the colloidal range (e.g., starch, cellulose, proteins, enzymes, polythene, nylon).
  • Associated colloids (Micelles): Substances that act as normal strong electrolytes at low concentration but aggregate into micelles (containing \(\ge 100\) ions) above the Kraft temperature (\(T_k\)) and Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC). For soaps, CMC is \(10^{-4}\text{–}10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\).

4. Preparation, Purification & Properties of Colloids

4.1 Preparation & Purification

  • Chemical methods:
    • Double decomposition: \(\mathrm{As}_2\mathrm{O}_3 + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{S} \rightarrow \mathrm{As}_2\mathrm{S}_3(\text{sol}) + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)
    • Oxidation: \(\mathrm{SO}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{S} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{S}(\text{sol}) + 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)
    • Reduction: \(2\mathrm{AuCl}_3 + 3\mathrm{HCHO} + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Au}(\text{sol}) + 3\mathrm{HCOOH} + 6\mathrm{HCl}\)
    • Hydrolysis: \(\mathrm{FeCl}_3 + 3\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe(OH)}_3(\text{sol}) + 3\mathrm{HCl}\)
  • Bredig's Arc method: An electric arc struck between metal electrodes (\(\mathrm{Au}\), \(\mathrm{Ag}\), \(\mathrm{Pt}\)) submerged in an ice-cooled dispersion medium vaporizes the metal, which condenses into colloidal particles (dispersion + condensation).
  • Peptization: Converting a freshly prepared precipitate into a colloidal sol by shaking it with a dispersion medium and a small amount of electrolyte (peptizing agent), which imparts like charges to the particles.
  • Purification: Excess electrolytes destabilize sols and are removed by dialysis (diffusion of ions through parchment/cellophane membrane), electrodialysis (accelerated by an electric field for ionic impurities), or ultrafiltration (using filter paper impregnated with collodion—a \(4\%\) solution of nitrocellulose in alcohol–ether—and hardened with formaldehyde).

4.2 Optical, Kinetic, and Electrical Properties

  • Colligative properties: Because colloidal particles are large aggregates, their molar particle count is much smaller than in true solutions of equal mass concentration, yielding very small osmotic pressure, boiling-point elevation, and freezing-point depression.
  • Tyndall effect: Scattering of light by colloidal particles, illuminating the beam path as a bright Tyndall cone when viewed at right angles. Requires two conditions: (1) dispersed particle diameter is not much smaller than the light wavelength, and (2) refractive indices of dispersed phase and medium differ markedly. Used in Zsigmondy's ultramicroscope.
  • Brownian movement: Continuous zig-zag motion caused by unbalanced bombardment from dispersion medium molecules. Faster for smaller particles and lower viscosity; it counteracts gravity and stabilizes sols.

4.3 Preferential Ion Adsorption, Double Layer & Zeta Potential

Sol particles acquire charge primarily by preferentially adsorbing an ion common to their own lattice from the medium:

  • Adding dilute \(\mathrm{AgNO}_3\) to excess \(\mathrm{KI}\) yields negatively charged \(\mathrm{AgI}/\mathrm{I}^-\); adding \(\mathrm{KI}\) to excess \(\mathrm{AgNO}_3\) yields positively charged \(\mathrm{AgI}/\mathrm{Ag}^+\).
  • Adding \(\mathrm{FeCl}_3\) to excess hot water yields positively charged \(\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3\cdot x\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}/\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}\); adding \(\mathrm{FeCl}_3\) to \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) yields negatively charged \(\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{O}_3\cdot x\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}/\mathrm{OH}^-\).

The firmly bound primary ion layer (fixed layer) attracts oppositely charged counter-ions into a mobile diffused layer, forming the Helmholtz electrical double layer. The potential difference between the fixed and diffused layers is called the electrokinetic potential or zeta potential.

4.4 Electrophoresis, Coagulation & Hardy–Schulze Rule

Under an applied electric field, charged colloidal particles migrate toward the oppositely charged electrode (electrophoresis). If particle motion is mechanically blocked, the dispersion medium moves instead (electroosmosis).

Neutralizing particle charges causes aggregation and settling, known as coagulation (achieved by electrophoresis, mutual coagulation of oppositely charged sols, boiling, persistent dialysis, or adding electrolytes).

Hardy–Schulze Rule: The greater the valence of the oppositely charged flocculating (coagulating) ion, the greater its coagulating power:
  • For a negative sol (e.g., \(\mathrm{As}_2\mathrm{S}_3\)): \(\mathrm{Al}^{3+} > \mathrm{Ba}^{2+} > \mathrm{Na}^+\)
  • For a positive sol (e.g., \(\mathrm{Fe(OH)}_3\)): \([\mathrm{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-} > \mathrm{PO}_4^{3-} > \mathrm{SO}_4^{2-} > \mathrm{Cl}^-\)
Coagulating Value: The minimum concentration of electrolyte (in \(\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\)) required to precipitate a sol in \(2\,\mathrm{h}\). Smaller coagulating value means higher coagulating power (\(\text{Coagulating power} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Coagulating value}}\)).

Protection of Colloids: Lyophilic sols form a protective solvated sheath around lyophobic sols, shielding them from electrolyte coagulation.

5. Emulsions & Colloids Around Us

Emulsions are liquid–liquid colloidal dispersions stabilized by an emulsifying agent (emulsifier) that forms an interfacial film around droplets:

  • Oil-in-Water (O/W): Water is the dispersion medium (e.g., milk, vanishing cream). Stabilized by proteins, gums, and natural/synthetic soaps.
  • Water-in-Oil (W/O): Oil is the dispersion medium (e.g., butter, cream). Stabilized by heavy metal salts of fatty acids, long-chain alcohols, and lampblack.

Emulsions can be diluted indefinitely with their dispersion medium, show Brownian motion and the Tyndall effect, and can be broken (demulsified) by heating, freezing, or centrifuging.

Everyday & Industrial Applications: Sky's blue colour (Rayleigh scattering by dust/water aerosols), delta formation (sea-water electrolytes coagulating negatively charged river clay), Cottrell smoke precipitator (high-voltage oppositely charged plates discharging carbon particles), alum in drinking-water purification (\(\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\) coagulating suspended clay), tanning (mutual coagulation of positively charged hide with negatively charged tannin), and colloidal medicines (argyrol silver sol, colloidal gold, milk of magnesia).

Worked Examples

1. Evaluating Thermodynamic Feasibility of Gas Adsorption

Problem: Under a hypothetical assumption, a gas adsorbs onto activated alumina at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) with an enthalpy change of \(\Delta H = -34.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) and an entropy change of \(\Delta S = -80.0\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\). Calculate \(\Delta G\) at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) and determine the temperature above which adsorption ceases to be spontaneous.

Solution:

Convert \(\Delta S\) to \(\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\): \(\Delta S = -0.080\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\).

\[ \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S = -34.0 - (300)(-0.080) = -34.0 + 24.0 = -10.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

Since \(\Delta G < 0\), adsorption is spontaneous at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\). Equilibrium (\(\Delta G = 0\)) is reached when:

\[ T_{\text{eq}} = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S} = \frac{-34.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}{-0.080\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}} = 425\,\mathrm{K} \]

Above \(425\,\mathrm{K}\), \(|T\Delta S| > |\Delta H|\), making \(\Delta G > 0\) (non-spontaneous).

2. Determining Freundlich Isotherm Constants from Experimental Data

Problem: In a hypothetical adsorption study of a gas on charcoal at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the plot of \(\log(x/m)\) versus \(\log p\) (with \(p\) in \(\mathrm{bar}\)) is linear with a slope of \(0.50\) and a \(y\)-intercept of \(0.3010\). Calculate the mass of gas adsorbed per gram of charcoal at \(p = 4.0\,\mathrm{bar}\).

Solution:

Comparing with \(\log(x/m) = \log k + \frac{1}{n}\log p\):

  • Slope \(\frac{1}{n} = 0.50\)
  • Intercept \(\log k = 0.3010 \implies k = 10^{0.3010} \approx 2.0\)

Substituting into the Freundlich equation at \(p = 4.0\,\mathrm{bar}\):

\[ \frac{x}{m} = k\,p^{1/n} = 2.0 \times (4.0)^{0.50} = 2.0 \times 2.0 = 4.0\,\mathrm{g\,gas\,/\,g\,charcoal} \]

3. Predicting Surface Charge and Preferential Adsorption on Sols

Problem: A student prepares two colloidal \(\mathrm{AgI}\) sols: Sol P by slowly adding \(25\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.02\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{AgNO}_3\) to \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.02\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{KI}\), and Sol Q by slowly adding \(25\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.02\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{KI}\) to \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.02\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{AgNO}_3\). Identify the fixed layer, diffused layer, and direction of electrophoretic migration for both sols.

Solution:

  • Sol P: Millimoles of \(\mathrm{KI} = 50 \times 0.02 = 1.0\,\mathrm{mmol}\) (in excess) vs. \(\mathrm{AgNO}_3 = 25 \times 0.02 = 0.5\,\mathrm{mmol}\). Excess \(\mathrm{I}^-\) ions are preferentially adsorbed on \(\mathrm{AgI}\), forming \(\mathrm{AgI}/\mathrm{I}^-\) (fixed layer) with \(\mathrm{K}^+\) counter-ions in the diffused layer. Being negatively charged, Sol P migrates toward the anode.
  • Sol Q: \(\mathrm{AgNO}_3\) is in excess (\(1.0\,\mathrm{mmol}\) vs. \(0.5\,\mathrm{mmol}\)). Excess \(\mathrm{Ag}^+\) ions are adsorbed, forming \(\mathrm{AgI}/\mathrm{Ag}^+\) (fixed layer) with \(\mathrm{NO}_3^-\) in the diffused layer. Being positively charged, Sol Q migrates toward the cathode.

4. Applying the Hardy–Schulze Rule to Compare Coagulating Values

Problem: Under hypothetical laboratory conditions, the coagulating values of \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{BaCl}_2\), and \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3\) for an \(\mathrm{As}_2\mathrm{S}_3\) sol are found to be \(52.0\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\), \(0.69\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\), and \(0.093\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\), respectively. Explain this trend and determine which salt has the highest coagulating power.

Solution:

\(\mathrm{As}_2\mathrm{S}_3\) is a negatively charged sulphide sol, so it is coagulated by the cations (\(\mathrm{Na}^+\), \(\mathrm{Ba}^{2+}\), \(\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\)). By the Hardy–Schulze rule, higher cation charge gives higher coagulating power (\(\mathrm{Al}^{3+} > \mathrm{Ba}^{2+} > \mathrm{Na}^+\)). Because coagulating power is inversely proportional to coagulating value, \(\mathrm{AlCl}_3\) (lowest coagulating value of \(0.093\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\)) has the highest coagulating power.

NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are modeled on the NEET UG examination pattern (single-correct, statement-based, assertion–reason, and matching MCQs).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which thermodynamic condition correctly characterizes the spontaneous adsorption of a gas onto a solid surface at constant temperature and pressure?

  • (A) \(\Delta H > 0\), \(\Delta S > 0\), \(\Delta G < 0\)
  • (B) \(\Delta H < 0\), \(\Delta S < 0\), \(\Delta G < 0\)
  • (C) \(\Delta H < 0\), \(\Delta S > 0\), \(\Delta G < 0\)
  • (D) \(\Delta H > 0\), \(\Delta S < 0\), \(\Delta G > 0\)
Answer: (B)

Adsorption reduces residual surface forces (exothermic, \(\Delta H < 0\)) and restricts gas molecular motion (\(\Delta S < 0\)). For spontaneity, \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\) must be negative.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Given the critical temperatures: \(\mathrm{H}_2\) (\(33\,\mathrm{K}\)), \(\mathrm{CH}_4\) (\(190\,\mathrm{K}\)), \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) (\(304\,\mathrm{K}\)), and \(\mathrm{SO}_2\) (\(630\,\mathrm{K}\)). Which gas will be adsorbed to the least extent on \(1\,\mathrm{g}\) of activated charcoal at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\)?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{SO}_2\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CO}_2\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{CH}_4\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{H}_2\)
Answer: (D)

In physisorption, the extent of adsorption increases with ease of liquefaction (higher critical temperature \(T_c\)). Since \(\mathrm{H}_2\) has the lowest \(T_c\) (\(33\,\mathrm{K}\)), it has the weakest van der Waals interactions and is adsorbed least.

Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid:
  • Temperature effect on chemisorption: Unlike physisorption (which monotonically decreases with \(T\)), chemisorption first increases with temperature to supply activation energy before eventually decreasing.
  • Coagulating power vs. coagulating value: They are inversely related. The best coagulating ion has the smallest coagulating value.
  • Milk vs. Butter: Milk and vanishing cream are O/W emulsions; butter and cream are W/O emulsions.
  • Bredig's Arc vs. Peptization: Bredig's Arc is for noble metals (\(\mathrm{Au}\), \(\mathrm{Ag}\), \(\mathrm{Pt}\)) via dispersion + condensation; peptization converts a fresh precipitate into a sol using a small amount of electrolyte.

NEET UG Quick Revision Checklist

  • Spontaneous adsorption: \(\Delta H < 0\), \(\Delta S < 0\), \(\Delta G < 0\); equilibrium when \(\Delta H = T\Delta S\).
  • Freundlich isotherm: \(\frac{x}{m} = k p^{1/n}\) (\(0 \le 1/n \le 1\)); fails at very high pressure.
  • Colloidal size range: \(1\text{–}1000\,\mathrm{nm}\) (\(10^{-9}\text{–}10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}\)).
  • Tyndall effect conditions: particle diameter comparable to light wavelength + large \(\Delta n\).
  • Zeta potential: potential difference between fixed and diffused layers of the Helmholtz double layer.
  • Hardy–Schulze rule: higher valence of oppositely charged ion \(\implies\) higher coagulating power.

Concise Final Recap

Surface chemistry unifies adsorption, catalysis, and colloids through the physics of high-area interfaces. Residual surface forces drive exothermic, entropy-decreasing adsorption governed at moderate pressures by the Freundlich isotherm. Solid and biological catalysts accelerate reactions selectively via surface intermediates or complementary active cavities. Finally, colloidal systems (\(1\text{–}1000\,\mathrm{nm}\)) derive their optical, kinetic, and electrical stability from Brownian motion, solvation, and the Helmholtz double layer—properties harnessed across medicine, water purification, smoke precipitation, and industrial synthesis.
