General Principles and Processes of Isolation of Elements

A Complete Conceptual, Thermodynamic, and Exam-Oriented Tutorial on Metallurgy

Learning Objectives and Roadmap

Metallurgy is the scientific and technological discipline concerned with extracting metals from their natural ores and purifying them for practical use. For NEET UG, questions from this chapter test ore compositions, concentration techniques, thermodynamic interpretation of Ellingham diagrams, blast furnace and electrolytic reactions, and metal-specific refining methods.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between minerals, ores, gangue, flux, and slag, and recall the principal ores of Al, Fe, Cu, and Zn.
  • Select the appropriate physical or chemical concentration (benefaction) method for a given ore.
  • Differentiate between calcination and roasting, and write the chemical equations for oxide conversion.
  • Apply Gibbs energy (\(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\)) and Ellingham diagrams to predict reducing agents and operating temperatures for Fe, Cu, Zn, and Al.
  • Explain electrochemical principles (\(\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ\)) in the Hall-Héroult process, hydrometallurgy, and oxidation-based extractions.
  • Identify and justify refining techniques including distillation, liquation, electrolysis, zone refining, vapour phase refining, and chromatography.

Every industrial extraction follows three sequential stages: (1) Concentration of the ore to remove earthly impurities, (2) Isolation of the crude metal via chemical or electrochemical reduction, and (3) Refining to achieve the required purity.

Occurrence of Metals: Minerals, Ores, and Gangue

In the Earth's crust, a few unreactive elements such as gold, silver, carbon, sulphur, and noble gases occur in the native (free) state, while most metals exist in combined forms as oxides, sulphides, carbonates, or silicates.

  • Mineral: Any naturally occurring chemical substance in the Earth's crust obtained by mining.
  • Ore: A mineral from which a metal can be extracted profitably and conveniently. Every ore is a mineral, but not every mineral is an ore.
  • Gangue (Matrix): Unwanted earthly or rocky impurities (such as sand, clay, and silicates) associated with an ore.
  • Flux and Slag: A flux is a substance added during smelting to combine with non-fusible gangue and form a fusible, easily separable molten product called slag.

Crustal Abundance & Notes: Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust (approximately 8.3% by weight) and the third most abundant element overall. Gemstones such as ruby and sapphire are impure forms of \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) containing trace \(\mathrm{Cr}\) and \(\mathrm{Co}\) impurities, respectively. Iron is the second most abundant metal. Although iron pyrites (\(\mathrm{FeS_2}\)) is an abundant iron mineral, oxide ores like haematite are preferred for iron extraction because sulphide roasting releases polluting \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) gas.

Concentration (Benefaction) of Ores

After crushing and grinding the mined ore, gangue is removed by exploiting differences in physical or chemical properties between the metal-bearing mineral and the impurities.

1. Physical Methods

  • Hydraulic Washing (Gravity Separation): Based on the difference in specific gravities. An upward stream of running water washes away lighter gangue particles while heavier ore particles settle down.
  • Magnetic Separation: Used when either the ore or the gangue is attracted by a magnetic field. Finely ground ore is dropped onto a conveyor belt passing over a magnetic roller. Magnetic particles (such as iron-bearing ores) cling briefly to the roller and fall closer to it, whereas non-magnetic particles are thrown farther away.
  • Froth Floatation Method: Specifically used for sulphide ores, based on differential wetting of ore by oil and gangue by water.
    • Collectors (e.g., pine oil, fatty acids, xanthates) enhance the non-wettability (hydrophobicity) of mineral particles.
    • Froth stabilisers (e.g., cresols, aniline) stabilise the air-bubble froth.
    • Depressants selectively prevent one sulphide from entering the froth when two sulphides are present together. For example, in a mixture of \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) and \(\mathrm{PbS}\), adding \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) selectively depresses \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) (forming a soluble complex) while allowing \(\mathrm{PbS}\) to rise with the froth.

2. Chemical Method: Leaching

Leaching is employed when the ore is soluble in a suitable chemical solvent while the gangue remains insoluble (or vice versa).

(a) Leaching of Alumina from Bauxite: Crude bauxite contains \(\mathrm{SiO_2}\), iron oxides, and \(\mathrm{TiO_2}\). Powdered bauxite is digested with concentrated \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at \(473\text{–}523\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(35\text{–}36\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure. \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) dissolves as sodium aluminate, and \(\mathrm{SiO_2}\) dissolves as sodium silicate, leaving iron and titanium oxides behind:

\[\mathrm{Al_2O_3(s)} + 2\mathrm{NaOH(aq)} + 3\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Na[Al(OH)_4](aq)}\]

The solution is neutralised by passing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) gas, and precipitation of hydrated alumina is induced by adding a small amount of freshly prepared hydrated \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) (seeding). Sodium silicate stays in solution:

\[2\mathrm{Na[Al(OH)_4](aq)} + 2\mathrm{CO_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O(s)} + 2\mathrm{NaHCO_3(aq)}\] \[\mathrm{Al_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O(s)} \xrightarrow{1470\,\mathrm{K}} \mathrm{Al_2O_3(s)} + x\mathrm{H_2O(g)}\]

(b) Leaching of Gold and Silver: The native metal (\(\mathrm{M = Ag}\) or \(\mathrm{Au}\)) is leached with dilute \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) or \(\mathrm{KCN}\) in the presence of air (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)), oxidising the metal into a soluble dicyano complex. The metal is then recovered by displacement using zinc:

\[4\mathrm{M(s)} + 8\mathrm{CN^-(aq)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 4[\mathrm{M(CN)_2}]^-\mathrm{(aq)} + 4\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\] \[2[\mathrm{M(CN)_2}]^-\mathrm{(aq)} + \mathrm{Zn(s)} \rightarrow [\mathrm{Zn(CN)_4}]^{2-}\mathrm{(aq)} + 2\mathrm{M(s)}\]

Thermodynamic Principles and Ellingham Diagrams

Whether a metal oxide \(\mathrm{M}_x\mathrm{O}_y\) can be reduced by a chosen reducing agent at a given temperature \(T\) depends on the standard Gibbs energy change (\(\Delta_r G^\circ\)) of the overall coupled reaction:

\[\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\]

A reduction is thermodynamically feasible when \(\Delta_r G^\circ < 0\). An Ellingham diagram plots \(\Delta_r G^\circ\) against \(T\) for the formation of oxides per mole of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) consumed: \(2x\mathrm{M(s)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{M}_x\mathrm{O(s)}\).

Tutorial Diagram 2: Schematic Ellingham Diagram (\(\Delta_r G^\circ\) vs. Temperature)

Key Rules for Reading an Ellingham Diagram:

  • Upward Slope of Metal Oxides: Gas (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)) is consumed to form a solid oxide, so \(\Delta S < 0\). Thus, \(-T\Delta S > 0\) and \(\Delta_r G^\circ\) becomes less negative as \(T\) increases.
  • Abrupt Slope Increases: When a metal melts (\(\mathrm{s} \rightarrow \mathrm{l}\)) or boils (\(\mathrm{l} \rightarrow \mathrm{g}\)), its entropy increases, making \(\Delta S\) of oxide formation even more negative and causing an upward bend (e.g., \(\mathrm{Zn}\)).
  • Why Carbon is Versatile: For \(\mathrm{C(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g)}\), \(\Delta n_g = 0\) (\(\Delta S \approx 0\)), giving a nearly horizontal line. For \(2\mathrm{C(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2CO(g)}\), 1 mole of gas produces 2 moles of gas (\(\Delta S > 0\)), so the line slopes downward and can cross below almost any metal oxide line at sufficiently high temperature.
  • Lower Line Reduces Upper Line: Any element whose oxide line lies below another metal oxide line at temperature \(T\) can thermodynamically reduce the upper oxide. For instance, below intersection point A (\(\sim 1623\,\mathrm{K}\) or \(1350\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)), \(\mathrm{Mg}\) can reduce \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\); above it, \(\mathrm{Al}\) can reduce \(\mathrm{MgO}\).
  • Limitations: Ellingham diagrams predict only thermodynamic feasibility, not reaction rate (kinetics), and assume reactants and products are in equilibrium. For example, \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3 + 2Al \rightarrow Al_2O_3 + 2Cr}\) has \(\Delta_r G^\circ = -421\,\mathrm{kJ}\), yet does not occur at room temperature because heating is needed to overcome the activation energy barrier.

Extraction of Important Metals

1. Iron: Reduction in a Blast Furnace

Concentrated oxide ore (\(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}\), \(\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}\)) is mixed with limestone (\(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\)) and coke (\(\mathrm{C}\)) and fed from the top of a blast furnace while hot air is blown from the bottom (reaching \(\sim 2170\,\mathrm{K}\)).

Tutorial Diagram 3: Temperature Zones and Key Reactions in a Blast Furnace

Thermodynamic Insight: Below \(1073\,\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{CO}\) is a better reducing agent than \(\mathrm{C}\) because the \(\mathrm{CO/CO_2}\) line lies below the \(\mathrm{C/CO}\) line. Above \(1073\,\mathrm{K}\), the \(\mathrm{C/CO}\) line drops below the \(\mathrm{Fe/FeO}\) line, making coke (\(\mathrm{C}\)) capable of reducing \(\mathrm{FeO}\) directly to \(\mathrm{Fe}\) (\(\Delta_r G^\circ \approx -53\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(1673\,\mathrm{K}\)).

Forms of Commercial Iron:

  • Pig Iron: Obtained directly from the blast furnace (~4% C plus small amounts of S, P, Si, Mn); hard and easily moulded.
  • Cast Iron: Made by melting pig iron with scrap iron and coke using hot air blast (~3% C); extremely hard and brittle.
  • Wrought Iron: The purest form of commercial iron, made by oxidising impurities of cast iron in a reverberatory furnace lined with haematite (\(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3 + 3C \rightarrow 2Fe + 3CO}\)), using limestone as flux.

2. Copper: Self-Reduction and Hydrometallurgy

Because the \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\) line lies near the top of the Ellingham diagram, \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\) is easily reduced by coke. For sulphide ores containing iron (e.g., \(\mathrm{CuFeS_2}\)), the ore is roasted with silica (\(\mathrm{SiO_2}\)) in a reverberatory furnace. Iron oxide slags off as \(\mathrm{FeSiO_3}\), leaving behind copper matte (a molten mixture of \(\mathrm{Cu_2S}\) and \(\mathrm{FeS}\)).

Copper matte is then charged into a silica-lined converter with hot air blast:

\[2\mathrm{FeS} + 3\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{FeO} + 2\mathrm{SO_2}, \qquad \mathrm{FeO} + \mathrm{SiO_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{FeSiO_3}\;\text{(slag)}\] \[2\mathrm{Cu_2S} + 3\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Cu_2O} + 2\mathrm{SO_2}, \qquad 2\mathrm{Cu_2O} + \mathrm{Cu_2S} \xrightarrow{\text{self-reduction}} 6\mathrm{Cu} + \mathrm{SO_2}\]

Solidified copper has a blistered appearance due to escaping \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and is called blister copper. For low-grade copper ores, hydrometallurgy is used: \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) is leached using acid or bacteria and displaced with scrap iron (cheaper than zinc) or \(\mathrm{H_2}\): \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq) + Fe(s) \rightarrow Cu(s) + Fe^{2+}(aq)}\).

3. Zinc: Reduction with Coke

Zinc blende (\(\mathrm{ZnS}\)) is roasted to \(\mathrm{ZnO}\), which is then reduced with coke at \(1673\,\mathrm{K}\) (higher than for copper) and rapidly chilled:

\[\mathrm{ZnO(s)} + \mathrm{C(s)} \xrightarrow{1673\,\mathrm{K}} \mathrm{Zn(g)} + \mathrm{CO(g)}\]

\(\mathrm{CO}\) is not used to reduce \(\mathrm{ZnO}\) because the \(\mathrm{Zn/ZnO}\) line lies below the \(\mathrm{CO/CO_2}\) line, making reduction by \(\mathrm{CO}\) thermodynamically unfavourable.

4. Aluminium: Electrochemical Principles (Hall-Héroult Process)

Electrolytic reduction is governed by \(\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ\). Highly electropositive metals like \(\mathrm{Al}\) cannot be reduced economically by carbon. In the Hall-Héroult process, purified \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) is dissolved in molten cryolite (\(\mathrm{Na_3AlF_6}\)) or \(\mathrm{CaF_2}\), which lowers the melting point of the mix and increases electrical conductivity.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Hall-Héroult Electrolytic Cell for Aluminium Extraction
\[\text{Cathode: }\mathrm{Al^{3+}(melt)} + 3\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Al(l)}\] \[\text{Anode: }\mathrm{C(s)} + \mathrm{O^{2-}(melt)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO(g)} + 2\mathrm{e^-}, \qquad \mathrm{C(s)} + 2\mathrm{O^{2-}(melt)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 4\mathrm{e^-}\] \[\text{Overall: }2\mathrm{Al_2O_3} + 3\mathrm{C} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{Al} + 3\mathrm{CO_2}\]

Because oxygen liberated at the anode reacts with the graphite rods to form \(\mathrm{CO}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), the carbon anodes are gradually consumed (about \(0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) of carbon per \(\mathrm{kg}\) of \(\mathrm{Al}\) produced) and must be replaced periodically.

5. Oxidation-Based Extraction

Non-metals such as chlorine are extracted by oxidation of brine (aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)):

\[2\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{OH^-(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{Cl_2(g)} \qquad (\Delta G^\circ = +422\,\mathrm{kJ},\; E^\circ = -2.2\,\mathrm{V})\]

An external potential greater than \(2.2\,\mathrm{V}\) (plus overpotential) is required to drive this electrolysis, yielding \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2}\), and \(\mathrm{NaOH(aq)}\). Electrolysis of molten \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), by contrast, yields \(\mathrm{Na}\) metal and \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\).

Refining of Crude Metals

Crude metals contain impurities that are removed by exploiting physical or chemical differences between the metal and its impurities.

Refining Method Core Principle Examples

Liquation Low-melting metal flows down a sloping hearth, leaving higher-melting impurities behind. \(\mathrm{Sn}\) (tin)
Electrolytic Refining Impure metal = anode; pure metal strip = cathode; soluble salt bath. Less basic impurities settle as anode mud (\(\mathrm{Sb, Se, Te, Ag, Au, Pt}\)). \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Zn}\)

Worked Examples

Example 1: Predicting Reduction Feasibility Using Gibbs Energy

Problem: Assume that at \(1200\,\mathrm{K}\), the standard Gibbs energies of formation per mole of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) for two hypothetical oxide reactions are:

\[\text{(i)}\; \frac{4}{3}\mathrm{M(s)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \frac{2}{3}\mathrm{M_2O_3(s)}, \qquad \Delta_r G^\circ_1 = -520\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

\[\text{(ii)}\; 2\mathrm{C(s)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{CO(g)}, \qquad \Delta_r G^\circ_2 = -440\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Using these assumed values, determine whether carbon can reduce \(\mathrm{M_2O_3}\) to \(\mathrm{M}\) at \(1200\,\mathrm{K}\).

Solution:

To obtain the reduction of \(\mathrm{M_2O_3}\) by \(\mathrm{C}\), reverse reaction (i) and add reaction (ii):

\[\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{M_2O_3(s)} + 2\mathrm{C(s)} \rightarrow \frac{4}{3}\mathrm{M(s)} + 2\mathrm{CO(g)}\]

The net standard Gibbs energy change is:

\[\Delta_r G^\circ_{\text{net}} = -\Delta_r G^\circ_1 + \Delta_r G^\circ_2 = -(-520) + (-440) = +80\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Since \(\Delta_r G^\circ_{\text{net}} > 0\), carbon cannot reduce \(\mathrm{M_2O_3}\) at \(1200\,\mathrm{K}\) (the \(\mathrm{C/CO}\) line lies above the \(\mathrm{M/M_2O_3}\) line at this temperature).

Example 2: Calculating Anode Mass Consumed in Hall-Héroult Electrolysis

Problem: In a hypothetical electrolytic run of the Hall-Héroult cell, \(54\,\mathrm{kg}\) of pure aluminium is produced. Assuming that all oxygen liberated at the graphite anode reacts exclusively to form \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)}\), calculate the mass of carbon anode consumed. (Atomic masses: \(\mathrm{Al} = 27\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm{C} = 12\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).)

Solution:

The overall balanced equation for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) formation is:

\[2\mathrm{Al_2O_3} + 3\mathrm{C} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{Al} + 3\mathrm{CO_2}\]

Moles of \(\mathrm{Al}\) produced \(= \dfrac{54000\,\mathrm{g}}{27\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 2000\,\mathrm{mol}\).

From stoichiometry, \(4\,\mathrm{mol\;Al}\) require \(3\,\mathrm{mol\;C}\):

\[\text{Moles of C consumed} = \frac{3}{4} \times 2000 = 1500\,\mathrm{mol}\]

\[\text{Mass of C consumed} = 1500\,\mathrm{mol} \times 12\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 18000\,\mathrm{g} = 18\,\mathrm{kg}\]

Example 3: Selecting Concentration and Refining Methods

Problem: A mineral sample contains galena (\(\mathrm{PbS}\)) mixed with sphalerite (\(\mathrm{ZnS}\)). Suggest a method to separate \(\mathrm{PbS}\) from \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) during concentration, and identify the refining method best suited if a trace semiconductor impurity like germanium is to be purified to electronic grade.

Solution:

1. Both \(\mathrm{PbS}\) and \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) are sulphide ores, so froth floatation is used. To separate them, \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) is added as a depressant, which selectively prevents \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) from entering the froth while allowing \(\mathrm{PbS}\) to float.

2. To purify germanium to electronic-grade purity, zone refining is used, because impurities are more soluble in the molten zone than in the recrystallising solid.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following pairs of ores consists exclusively of carbonate ores?

  1. (A) Siderite and Calamine
  2. (B) Malachite and Cuprite
  3. (C) Haematite and Zincite
  4. (D) Kaolinite and Copper glance
Answer: (A) Siderite and Calamine

Explanation: Siderite is \(\mathrm{FeCO_3}\) and calamine is \(\mathrm{ZnCO_3}\). Cuprite (\(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\)), haematite (\(\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}\)), and zincite (\(\mathrm{ZnO}\)) are oxides; kaolinite is an aluminosilicate clay; and copper glance (\(\mathrm{Cu_2S}\)) is a sulphide.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

In the froth floatation process for a sulphide ore containing both \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) and \(\mathrm{PbS}\), which pair correctly identifies the role of \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) and pine oil, respectively?

  1. (A) Froth stabiliser and depressant
  2. (B) Depressant and collector
  3. (C) Collector and froth stabiliser
  4. (D) Oxidising agent and flux
Answer: (B) Depressant and collector

Explanation: \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) acts as a depressant by selectively preventing \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) from rising with the froth while allowing \(\mathrm{PbS}\) to float. Pine oil (like xanthates and fatty acids) acts as a collector that enhances the non-wettability of mineral particles.

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

In an Ellingham diagram, the curve for \(2\mathrm{C(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2CO(g)}\) slopes downward with increasing temperature because:

  1. (A) \(\Delta H\) becomes increasingly negative with temperature.
  2. (B) \(\Delta S\) is positive, so \(-T\Delta S\) becomes more negative as \(T\) increases.
  3. (C) \(\Delta S\) is negative, causing \(\Delta G^\circ\) to decrease.
  4. (D) Carbon sublimes into gas at \(673\,\mathrm{K}\).
Answer: (B) \(\Delta S\) is positive, so \(-T\Delta S\) becomes more negative as \(T\) increases.

Explanation: In \(2\mathrm{C(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2CO(g)}\), 1 mole of gaseous \(\mathrm{O_2}\) produces 2 moles of gaseous \(\mathrm{CO}\) (\(\Delta n_g = +1 > 0\)). Thus, \(\Delta S > 0\), and in \(\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ\), the \(-T\Delta S^\circ\) term becomes more negative as \(T\) increases, giving a negative slope.

Practice Question 4 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Although \(\Delta_r G^\circ = -421\,\mathrm{kJ}\) for the reaction \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3 + 2Al \rightarrow Al_2O_3 + 2Cr}\), the reaction does not proceed spontaneously at room temperature.
Reason (R): A certain amount of activation energy is required to initiate the reaction, which necessitates heating.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

Explanation: A negative \(\Delta_r G^\circ\) guarantees thermodynamic feasibility, not kinetic rapidity. At room temperature, solid reactants have high activation energy barriers, so heating is required to initiate the thermite-type reduction.

Practice Question 5 Statement-Based MCQ

Statement I: In the Hall-Héroult process, cryolite (\(\mathrm{Na_3AlF_6}\)) is added to \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) to lower the melting point and increase the electrical conductivity of the melt.
Statement II: During the electrolysis of alumina, the graphite anodes remain completely unconsumed because oxygen gas escapes freely without reacting.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.

Explanation: Cryolite lowers the fusion temperature of \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) and improves conductivity. However, oxygen liberated at the graphite anode reacts with carbon to form \(\mathrm{CO}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), steadily burning away the anodes.

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

  4. (D) Oxidise \(\mathrm{Cu_2S}\) to \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\).
Answer: (B) Remove \(\mathrm{FeO}\) impurity as fusible \(\mathrm{FeSiO_3}\) slag.

Explanation: Silica acts as an acidic flux that combines with basic \(\mathrm{FeO}\) to form molten iron(II) silicate slag (\(\mathrm{FeO + SiO_2 \rightarrow FeSiO_3}\)), which separates from copper matte.

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding the hydrometallurgy and electrolysis of metals is incorrect?

  1. (A) Zinc is used to precipitate gold from \([\mathrm{Au(CN)_2}]^-\) solution.
  2. (B) Scrap iron is preferred over zinc for precipitating copper from leached \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) solution because iron is cheaper.
  3. (C) Electrolysis of aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) yields \(\mathrm{Na}\) metal at the cathode and \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) at the anode.
  4. (D) In electrolytic refining of copper, silver and gold collect in the anode mud.
Answer: (C) Electrolysis of aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) yields \(\mathrm{Na}\) metal at the cathode and \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) at the anode.

Explanation: Electrolysis of aqueous \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (brine) yields \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) and \(\mathrm{NaOH(aq)}\) along with \(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)}\). Metallic \(\mathrm{Na}\) is obtained only from the electrolysis of molten \(\mathrm{NaCl}\).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Avoid These Common NEET UG Traps:
  • \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) Dual Role: In froth floatation, \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) is a depressant for \(\mathrm{ZnS}\). In gold/silver metallurgy, \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) is a leaching agent forming \([\mathrm{M(CN)_2}]^-\).
  • Fluxes in Fe vs. Cu Extraction: Iron extraction uses basic flux \(\mathrm{CaO}\) (from \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\)) to remove acidic \(\mathrm{SiO_2}\) gangue as \(\mathrm{CaSiO_3}\). Copper extraction uses acidic flux \(\mathrm{SiO_2}\) to remove basic \(\mathrm{FeO}\) as \(\mathrm{FeSiO_3}\).
  • Reducing Agent for \(\mathrm{ZnO}\): \(\mathrm{ZnO}\) is reduced by coke (\(\mathrm{C}\)) at \(1673\,\mathrm{K}\), not by \(\mathrm{CO}\).
  • Bauxite Leaching: Both \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) and \(\mathrm{SiO_2}\) dissolve in hot concentrated \(\mathrm{NaOH}\); passing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) precipitates only hydrated \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\), leaving sodium silicate in solution.
  • Alloys to Remember: Brass (\(\mathrm{Cu}\) 60%, \(\mathrm{Zn}\) 40%), Bronze (\(\mathrm{Cu + Sn}\)), German silver (\(\mathrm{Cu + Zn + Ni}\); note: contains no silver!).

Concise Final Recap

  • Concentration: Hydraulic washing (density), magnetic separation (magnetic properties), froth floatation (sulphide ores; collectors, stabilisers, depressants), and leaching (bauxite with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\); \(\mathrm{Ag/Au}\) with \(\mathrm{CN^- + O_2}\)).
  • Oxide Conversion: Calcination (limited/no air, carbonates/hydrates) and roasting (excess air, sulphides).
  • Thermodynamics: A reduction is feasible when \(\Delta_r G^\circ < 0\). On an Ellingham diagram, the element of a lower curve can reduce the oxide of an upper curve.
  • Blast Furnace (Fe): \(\mathrm{CO}\) reduces iron oxides at \(500\text{–}800\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(900\text{–}1500\,\mathrm{K}\); coke reduces \(\mathrm{FeO}\) directly above \(1073\,\mathrm{K}\).
  • Cu, Zn, Al: Blister copper forms by self-reduction (\(2\mathrm{Cu_2O + Cu_2S \rightarrow 6Cu + SO_2}\)); \(\mathrm{ZnO}\) is reduced by coke at \(1673\,\mathrm{K}\); \(\mathrm{Al}\) is extracted by Hall-Héroult electrolysis using cryolite.
  • Refining: Distillation (\(\mathrm{Zn, Hg}\)), liquation (\(\mathrm{Sn}\)), electrolysis (\(\mathrm{Cu, Zn}\)), zone refining (\(\mathrm{Ge, Si}\)), Mond (\(\mathrm{Ni}\)), van Arkel (\(\mathrm{Zr, Ti}\)), and chromatography (minute quantities).
