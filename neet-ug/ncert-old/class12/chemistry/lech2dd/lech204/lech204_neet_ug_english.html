NEET UG Chemistry • Organic Nitrogen Compounds

Amines and Diazonium Salts

Structure, Classification, Synthesis, Basicity Trends, Chemical Tests, and Aromatic Transformations

Learning Objectives and Overview

Nitrogen-containing organic molecules form the backbone of proteins, alkaloids, vitamins, synthetic dyes, and pharmaceutical agents. Amines are organic derivatives of ammonia, while arenediazonium salts serve as versatile intermediates that unlock otherwise inaccessible aromatic substitutions. For NEET UG, questions from this topic frequently test aqueous vs. gas-phase basicity orders, named reactions for amine synthesis, chemical tests distinguishing primary, secondary, and tertiary amines, and multi-step aromatic conversions via diazonium salts.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Explain the \(sp^3\) hybridisation and pyramidal geometry of amines, and classify them as \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), or \(3^\circ\).
  • Assign systematic IUPAC and common names to aliphatic and aromatic amines.
  • Compare the six major preparative methods for amines, including chain-length changes and synthetic limitations.
  • Predict boiling point and water solubility trends using intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
  • Determine relative basic strength of aliphatic and aromatic amines in gas and aqueous phases.
  • Distinguish \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) amines using Hinsberg's reagent, carbylamine test, and nitrous acid.
  • Control electrophilic substitution in aniline and design aromatic syntheses using arenediazonium salts.

Structure, Classification, and Nomenclature

Hybridisation and Pyramidal Geometry

Amines are formed by replacing one, two, or all three hydrogen atoms of ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)) with alkyl (\(\mathrm{-R}\)) or aryl (\(\mathrm{-Ar}\)) groups. The central nitrogen atom is trivalent and retains one unshared pair of electrons (lone pair). Consequently, nitrogen is \(sp^3\) hybridised with three \(\sigma\) bonds and one lone pair occupying the fourth \(sp^3\) orbital, giving the molecule a trigonal pyramidal shape.

Because lone pair–bond pair repulsion exceeds bond pair–bond pair repulsion, the \(\mathrm{C-N-C}\) or \(\mathrm{C-N-H}\) bond angle is compressed slightly below the ideal tetrahedral angle of \(109.5^\circ\). For example, in trimethylamine, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}\), the \(\mathrm{C-N-C}\) angle is \(108^\circ\).

Classification of Amines

Classification depends on the number of carbon groups directly bonded to the nitrogen atom (not on the degree of the carbon atom itself):

  • Primary (\(1^\circ\)) amines: One hydrogen of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) is replaced (\(\mathrm{R-NH_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Ar-NH_2}\)). Note that tert-butylamine, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-NH_2}\), is a \(1^\circ\) amine because nitrogen is bonded to only one carbon.
  • Secondary (\(2^\circ\)) amines: Two hydrogens of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) are replaced (\(\mathrm{R-NH-R'}\)).
  • Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) amines: All three hydrogens of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) are replaced (\(\mathrm{R-N(R')R''}\)).
  • Simple vs. Mixed amines: Secondary and tertiary amines with identical alkyl/aryl groups are simple; those with different groups are mixed.

Common and IUPAC Nomenclature

In the common system, aliphatic amines are named as alkylamine (one word), using di- or tri- for identical groups. In the IUPAC system, the terminal -e of the parent alkane is replaced by -amine (alkanamine), and the position of the amino group is numbered. For \(2^\circ\) and \(3^\circ\) amines, the longest carbon chain forms the parent alkanamine, while other alkyl groups on nitrogen receive the locant N-. When multiple \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) groups are present, the -e of the alkane is retained (e.g., ethane-1,2-diamine). For arylamines, \(\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2}\) is named aniline (accepted by IUPAC) or benzenamine.

Methods of Preparation of Amines

1. Reduction of Nitro Compounds

Aliphatic or aromatic nitro compounds (\(\mathrm{R-NO_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Ar-NO_2}\)) are reduced to \(1^\circ\) amines by catalytic hydrogenation (\(\mathrm{H_2/Pd}\) or \(\mathrm{Ni}\) in ethanol) or active metals in acidic medium (\(\mathrm{Sn + HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{Fe + HCl}\)):

\[\mathrm{Ar-NO_2 + 6[H] \xrightarrow{Fe/HCl\text{ or }Sn/HCl} Ar-NH_2 + 2H_2O}\]
Industrial Preference for Iron Scrap and HCl: Reduction with \(\mathrm{Fe/HCl}\) is preferred over \(\mathrm{Sn/HCl}\) because the \(\mathrm{FeCl_2}\) formed during the reaction undergoes hydrolysis to regenerate \(\mathrm{HCl}\). Therefore, only a small catalytic quantity of \(\mathrm{HCl}\) is needed to initiate the reduction.

2. Ammonolysis of Alkyl and Benzyl Halides

Heating an alkyl or benzyl halide with ethanolic ammonia in a sealed tube at \(373\,\mathrm{K}\) cleaves the \(\mathrm{C-X}\) bond via nucleophilic substitution, initially forming a substituted ammonium salt. Treatment with a strong base (\(\mathrm{NaOH}\)) liberates the free amine.

Limitation: Because the newly formed \(1^\circ\) amine is also nucleophilic, it reacts further with \(\mathrm{R-X}\) to produce a mixture of \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) amines, and ultimately a quaternary ammonium salt (\(\mathrm{R_4N^+X^-}\)). Taking a large excess of ammonia drives the reaction toward the \(1^\circ\) amine as the major product. Reactivity order of halides: \(\mathrm{R-I > R-Br > R-Cl}\).

3. Reduction of Nitriles (Ascent of Series)

Nitriles (\(\mathrm{R-C\equiv N}\)) undergo reduction with \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\), \(\mathrm{H_2/Ni}\), or \(\mathrm{Na(Hg)/C_2H_5OH}\) (Mendius reduction) to yield \(1^\circ\) amines containing one carbon atom more than the starting alkyl halide used to make the nitrile:

\[\mathrm{R-X \xrightarrow{NaCN} R-C\equiv N \xrightarrow{LiAlH_4\text{ or }H_2/Ni} R-CH_2-NH_2}\]

4. Reduction of Amides

Amides are reduced by \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) followed by aqueous workup to give amines with the same number of carbon atoms:

\[\mathrm{R-CONH_2 \xrightarrow[(ii)\,H_2O]{(i)\,LiAlH_4} R-CH_2-NH_2}\]

5. Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis

This method selectively produces pure \(1^\circ\) aliphatic amines without contamination by \(2^\circ\) or \(3^\circ\) amines:

  1. Phthalimide reacts with ethanolic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) to form potassium phthalimide.
  2. Heating potassium phthalimide with an alkyl halide (\(\mathrm{R-X}\)) yields N-alkylphthalimide via \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) attack.
  3. Alkaline hydrolysis with \(\mathrm{NaOH(aq)}\) releases the \(1^\circ\) amine (\(\mathrm{R-NH_2}\)) and sodium phthalate.
Critical NEET Exception: Aromatic primary amines (such as aniline) cannot be prepared by Gabriel phthalimide synthesis because aryl halides do not undergo nucleophilic substitution (\(\mathrm{S_N2}\)) with the phthalimide anion (due to resonance-induced partial double-bond character of the \(\mathrm{C-X}\) bond).

6. Hoffmann Bromamide Degradation (Descent of Series)

Treating a primary amide with bromine in aqueous or ethanolic \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) yields a \(1^\circ\) amine containing one carbon atom less than the parent amide. During this reaction, the alkyl or aryl group migrates from the carbonyl carbon to the nitrogen atom:

\[\mathrm{R-CONH_2 + Br_2 + 4NaOH \longrightarrow R-NH_2 + Na_2CO_3 + 2NaBr + 2H_2O}\]

Physical Properties and Hydrogen Bonding

  • Physical State and Odour: Lower aliphatic amines are gases with a fishy odour. Primary amines with three or more carbons are liquids, and higher homologues are solids. Pure arylamines are colourless but darken on storage due to atmospheric oxidation.
  • Solubility: Lower aliphatic amines are water-soluble because they form hydrogen bonds with water. Solubility drops as the hydrophobic alkyl chain grows; higher amines and aniline are essentially insoluble in water. Alcohols (oxygen electronegativity \(3.5\)) are more polar and form stronger hydrogen bonds with water than comparable amines (nitrogen electronegativity \(3.0\)), so butan-1-ol is more soluble and has a higher boiling point than butan-1-amine.
  • Boiling Points of Isomeric Amines: Primary amines have two \(\mathrm{N-H}\) hydrogens for intermolecular hydrogen bonding, secondary amines have one, and tertiary amines have none. Thus, for isomeric amines of comparable molar mass, the boiling point order is:
    \[\text{Primary }(1^\circ) > \text{Secondary }(2^\circ) > \text{Tertiary }(3^\circ) > \text{Alkanes}\]
    However, all amines have lower boiling points than alcohols of similar molar mass because \(\mathrm{O-H\cdots O}\) hydrogen bonds are stronger than \(\mathrm{N-H\cdots N}\) hydrogen bonds.

Basic Character of Amines: Gas Phase vs. Aqueous Phase

Because of the unshared electron pair on nitrogen, amines behave as Lewis bases and react with mineral acids to form water-soluble ammonium salts. Treating the salt with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) regenerates the free amine—a property used to separate amines from water-insoluble neutral organic compounds.

Basic strength in water is quantified by \(K_b\) and \(\mathrm{p}K_b\):

\[\mathrm{R-NH_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons R-NH_3^+ + OH^-}, \qquad K_b = \frac{\mathrm{[R-NH_3^+][OH^-]}}{\mathrm{[R-NH_2]}}, \qquad \mathrm{p}K_b = -\log K_b\]

Rule: Larger \(K_b\) or smaller \(\mathrm{p}K_b\) indicates a stronger base. (For reference, \(\mathrm{p}K_b\) of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) is \(4.75\); aliphatic amines range from \(3.00\) to \(4.22\); aniline is \(9.38\).)

Alkanamines vs. Ammonia

Alkyl groups exert an electron-releasing inductive effect (\(+I\)), increasing electron density on nitrogen and dispersing the positive charge on the resulting ammonium cation.

  • In the Gaseous Phase: Only the \(+I\) effect operates. Therefore, basicity increases with the number of alkyl groups:
    \[\text{Gas phase: } \quad 3^\circ \text{ amine} > 2^\circ \text{ amine} > 1^\circ \text{ amine} > \mathrm{NH_3}\]
  • In the Aqueous Phase: Three competing factors govern the stability of the conjugate acid (\(\mathrm{RNH_3^+}\)):
    1. \(+I\) Inductive Effect: Favours \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\).
    2. Solvation (Hydration) Effect: Smaller ions with more \(\mathrm{N-H}\) bonds form more hydrogen bonds with \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), releasing more hydration energy. Stability by solvation follows \(1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ\).
    3. Steric Hindrance: Bulky alkyl groups (like ethyl) hinder water molecules from approaching the cation, while also crowding the nitrogen lone pair.
    The subtle balance of these three effects yields two famous NEET-tested aqueous basicity orders:
    \[\text{Methylamines (aq): } \quad \mathrm{(CH_3)_2NH} > \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} > \mathrm{(CH_3)_3N} > \mathrm{NH_3}\] \[\text{Ethylamines (aq): } \quad \mathrm{(C_2H_5)_2NH} > \mathrm{(C_2H_5)_3N} > \mathrm{C_2H_5NH_2} > \mathrm{NH_3}\]
    Notice that the secondary amine is the strongest base in both series in water!

Arylamines vs. Ammonia and Substituent Effects

Aniline (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 9.38\)) is a much weaker base than ammonia (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.75\)) and aliphatic amines. In aniline, the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring across five resonance structures, making it far less available for protonation. Meanwhile, the protonated anilinium ion (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_3^+}\)) has only two Kekulé resonance structures. Since aniline is resonance-stabilised relative to anilinium ion, protonation is unfavourable.

Note on Benzylamine (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2NH_2}\)): Here the \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) group is separated from the ring by a \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) spacer, so the lone pair is not conjugated with the ring. Thus, benzylamine (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.70\)) is much more basic than aniline and slightly more basic than ammonia.

Substituted Anilines: Electron-releasing groups (\(-\mathrm{OCH_3}\), \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\)) increase basic strength (lower \(\mathrm{p}K_b\)), whereas electron-withdrawing groups (\(-\mathrm{NO_2}\), \(-\mathrm{SO_3H}\), \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), \(-\mathrm{X}\)) decrease basic strength (higher \(\mathrm{p}K_b\)).

Key Chemical Reactions and Distinguishing Tests

1. Acylation and Benzoylation

Primary and secondary amines (both aliphatic and aromatic) react with acid chlorides, acid anhydrides, or esters to form substituted amides. A stronger base such as pyridine is added to scavenge the \(\mathrm{HCl}\) byproduct and shift the equilibrium forward. Tertiary amines lack an \(\mathrm{N-H}\) hydrogen and do not undergo acylation.

\[\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2 + (CH_3CO)_2O \xrightarrow{Pyridine} C_6H_5NHCOCH_3 + CH_3COOH}\]

Reaction with benzoyl chloride (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5COCl}\)) is termed benzoylation (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3NH_2 + C_6H_5COCl \rightarrow CH_3NHCOC_6H_5 + HCl}\)).

2. Carbylamine Reaction (Isocyanide Test)

Only primary amines (both aliphatic and aromatic) react on heating with chloroform and ethanolic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) to form foul-smelling isocyanides (carbylamines). Secondary and tertiary amines give no reaction, making this a definitive test for \(1^\circ\) amines:

\[\mathrm{R-NH_2 + CHCl_3 + 3KOH \xrightarrow{\Delta} R-NC + 3KCl + 3H_2O}\]

3. Reaction with Nitrous Acid (\(\mathrm{HNO_2}\))

Nitrous acid is generated in situ from \(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + HCl}\):

  • \(1^\circ\) Aliphatic Amines: Form highly unstable alkyldiazonium salts that immediately decompose to give an alcohol and quantitative evolution of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) gas: \[\mathrm{R-NH_2 + HNO_2 \xrightarrow{NaNO_2 + HCl} [R-N_2^+Cl^-] \xrightarrow{H_2O} R-OH + N_2\uparrow + HCl}\]
  • \(1^\circ\) Aromatic Amines: At \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\), form resonance-stabilised arenediazonium salts without immediate \(\mathrm{N_2}\) loss.

4. Hinsberg's Test (Benzenesulphonyl Chloride, \(\mathrm{C_6H_5SO_2Cl}\))

Benzenesulphonyl chloride (or p-toluenesulphonyl chloride) distinguishes and separates \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) amines based on the acidity of the sulphonamide product:

Amine Class Reaction with \(\mathrm{C_6H_5SO_2Cl}\) Solubility in Aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH / NaOH}\)
Primary (\(1^\circ\)) Forms N-alkylbenzenesulphonamide (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5SO_2NHR}\)) Soluble (the remaining \(\mathrm{N-H}\) proton is strongly acidic due to the \(-\mathrm{SO_2}-\) group)
Secondary (\(2^\circ\)) Forms N,N-dialkylbenzenesulphonamide (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5SO_2NR_2}\)) Insoluble (no \(\mathrm{N-H}\) hydrogen attached to nitrogen)
Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) No reaction Insoluble in alkali; dissolves in dilute acid

5. Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution in Aniline

Because the \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) group donates electron density into the ring by resonance, it is a powerful activating and ortho/para-directing group.

  • Bromination: Treating aniline with bromine water at room temperature gives an immediate white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromoaniline. To obtain a monosubstituted product (p-bromoaniline), the \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) group must first be protected by acetylation to acetanilide (\(-\mathrm{NHCOCH_3}\)), which reduces ring activation because the N lone pair is delocalised onto the carbonyl oxygen. After bromination (\(\mathrm{Br_2/CH_3COOH}\)), hydrolysis restores the \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) group.
  • Nitration: Direct nitration with \(\mathrm{HNO_3/H_2SO_4}\) at \(288\,\mathrm{K}\) causes partial oxidation (tarry products) and protonates aniline into the meta-directing anilinium ion (\(-\mathrm{NH_3^+}\)), yielding \(51\%\) para, \(47\%\) meta, and \(2\%\) ortho nitroanilines. Protecting \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) as acetanilide prior to nitration, followed by hydrolysis, gives pure p-nitroaniline as the major product.
  • Sulphonation: Aniline reacts with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) to form anilinium hydrogensulphate, which on heating at \(453\text{–}473\,\mathrm{K}\) rearranges to sulphanilic acid (p-aminobenzenesulphonic acid), existing predominantly as a dipolar zwitterion (\(\mathrm{^+H_3N-C_6H_4-SO_3^-}\)).
  • Failure of Friedel–Crafts Reactions: Aniline does not undergo Friedel–Crafts alkylation or acylation because it is a Lewis base and forms an insoluble salt with the Lewis acid catalyst \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2^+AlCl_3^-}\)). The positively charged nitrogen strongly deactivates the ring.

Arenediazonium Salts and Aromatic Synthesis

Arenediazonium salts (\(\mathrm{Ar-N_2^+X^-}\), where \(\mathrm{X^- = Cl^-, Br^-, HSO_4^-, BF_4^-}\)) are prepared by diazotisation of \(1^\circ\) aromatic amines with \(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + 2HCl}\) at \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\). Unlike alkyldiazonium salts, arenediazonium ions are stabilised in cold aqueous solution by resonance delocalisation of the positive charge into the benzene ring. Because they decompose on warming (except \(\mathrm{ArN_2^+BF_4^-}\), which is water-insoluble and stable at room temperature), they are used immediately after preparation.

Their reactions fall into two classes: (A) displacement of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) (enabling introduction of \(-\mathrm{F, -Cl, -Br, -I, -CN, -OH, -NO_2, -H}\) onto the ring) and (B) retention of the diazo group (azo coupling).

Worked Examples

Predicting Aqueous Basicity Order of Substituted Amines

Problem: Arrange the following compounds in increasing order of basic strength in aqueous solution: p-nitroaniline, p-methoxyaniline, aniline, benzylamine, and N-ethylethanamine.

Solution:

  1. Separate aromatic amines (where \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) is directly attached to the benzene ring) from aliphatic/aralkyl amines. Because of resonance delocalisation of the nitrogen lone pair into the ring, all three anilines are much weaker bases than benzylamine and N-ethylethanamine.
  2. Compare the three anilines: \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) is strongly electron-withdrawing (\(-R, -I\)), decreasing basicity, while \(-\mathrm{OCH_3}\) at the para position is electron-releasing (\(+R > -I\)), increasing basicity. Thus: p-nitroaniline < aniline < p-methoxyaniline.
  3. Compare benzylamine (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2NH_2}\)) and N-ethylethanamine (\(\mathrm{(C_2H_5)_2NH}\)): The phenyl group in benzylamine exerts a mild \(-I\) effect through the \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) group, whereas the two ethyl groups in N-ethylethanamine exert strong \(+I\) effects and optimal hydration. Thus, N-ethylethanamine is the strongest base.

Final Order: p-nitroaniline < aniline < p-methoxyaniline < benzylamine < N-ethylethanamine.

Selecting a Preparative Route for Pure Primary Aliphatic Amines

Problem: A student wishes to prepare pure butan-1-amine (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2NH_2}\)) without any \(2^\circ\) or \(3^\circ\) amine impurities. Compare using (a) 1-bromobutane with excess ethanolic \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), (b) 1-bromobutane via Gabriel phthalimide synthesis, and (c) pentanamide via Hoffmann bromamide degradation.

Solution:

  • (a) Ammonolysis: Even with excess \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), ammonolysis of 1-bromobutane can produce small amounts of \(2^\circ\) and \(3^\circ\) amines, requiring separation.
  • (b) Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis: Reacting potassium phthalimide with 1-bromobutane forms N-butylphthalimide, which upon alkaline hydrolysis yields pure butan-1-amine cleanly because the nitrogen can only be alkylated once.
  • (c) Hoffmann Bromamide Degradation: Pentanamide (\(\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_3CONH_2}\), 5 carbons) treated with \(\mathrm{Br_2/NaOH}\) loses the carbonyl carbon as \(\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}\) and yields pure butan-1-amine (4 carbons).

Both (b) and (c) yield pure \(1^\circ\) butan-1-amine; Gabriel synthesis is ideal starting from the 4-carbon halide, while Hoffmann degradation is ideal starting from the 5-carbon amide.

Distinguishing Isomeric Amines Using Chemical Tests

Problem: Three isomeric amines of molecular formula \(\mathrm{C_3H_9N}\)—propan-1-amine (X), N-methylethanamine (Y), and N,N-dimethylmethanamine (Z)—are given in unlabelled bottles. Identify each using Hinsberg's reagent and the carbylamine test.

Solution:

  • X (propan-1-amine, \(1^\circ\)): Gives a foul-smelling isocyanide (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2NC}\)) in the carbylamine test (\(\mathrm{CHCl_3 + alc.\,KOH/\Delta}\)). With Hinsberg's reagent (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5SO_2Cl}\)), it forms a sulphonamide soluble in aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\).
  • Y (N-methylethanamine, \(2^\circ\)): Fails the carbylamine test. With Hinsberg's reagent, it forms N-ethyl-N-methylbenzenesulphonamide, which is insoluble in aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\).
  • Z (N,N-dimethylmethanamine, \(3^\circ\)): Fails the carbylamine test and shows no reaction with Hinsberg's reagent.

Multi-Step Aromatic Conversion via Arenediazonium Salts

Problem: Design a synthesis of 1,3,5-tribromobenzene starting from aniline.

Solution:

Direct bromination of benzene cannot give 1,3,5-tribromobenzene because bromine is ortho/para-directing. Instead, we exploit the strongly activating \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) group of aniline and then remove it via diazotisation:

  1. Step 1 (Tribromination): Treat aniline with \(\mathrm{Br_2/H_2O}\) to obtain 2,4,6-tribromoaniline.
  2. Step 2 (Diazotisation): Treat 2,4,6-tribromoaniline with \(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + HCl}\) at \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\) to form 2,4,6-tribromobenzenediazonium chloride.
  3. Step 3 (Reductive Deamination): Reduce the diazonium salt with hypophosphorous acid (\(\mathrm{H_3PO_2 + H_2O}\)) or ethanol (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH}\)) to replace the \(-\mathrm{N_2^+Cl^-}\) group with \(-\mathrm{H}\), yielding 1,3,5-tribromobenzene.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding the structure and properties of trimethylamine, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}\), is incorrect?

  • (A) The nitrogen atom is \(sp^3\) hybridised with one unshared pair of electrons.
  • (B) The \(\mathrm{C-N-C}\) bond angle is \(108^\circ\), which is less than the ideal tetrahedral angle.
  • (C) It has a higher boiling point than its primary isomer propan-1-amine due to higher molecular symmetry.
  • (D) It behaves as a Lewis base and reacts with mineral acids to form water-soluble salts.
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Trimethylamine lacks any \(\mathrm{N-H}\) bond and therefore cannot form intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Propan-1-amine (\(1^\circ\)) forms extensive intermolecular \(\mathrm{N-H\cdots N}\) hydrogen bonds, giving it a significantly higher boiling point than trimethylamine. Statements (A), (B), and (D) are correct.
Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

What is the correct decreasing order of basic strength of the following amines in aqueous solution?

\(\text{I. }\mathrm{CH_3NH_2} \qquad \text{II. }\mathrm{(CH_3)_2NH} \qquad \text{III. }\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N} \qquad \text{IV. }\mathrm{NH_3}\)

  • (A) \(\text{III} > \text{II} > \text{I} > \text{IV}\)
  • (B) \(\text{II} > \text{I} > \text{III} > \text{IV}\)
  • (C) \(\text{II} > \text{III} > \text{I} > \text{IV}\)
  • (D) \(\text{I} > \text{II} > \text{III} > \text{IV}\)
Answer: (B)
Explanation: In aqueous solution, basicity is governed by the interplay of \(+I\) inductive effect, solvation of the ammonium cation, and steric hindrance. For methyl-substituted amines, the order is \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2NH} > \mathrm{CH_3NH_2} > \mathrm{(CH_3)_3N} > \mathrm{NH_3}\) (\(\text{II} > \text{I} > \text{III} > \text{IV}\)). Note that \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}\) is weaker than \(\mathrm{CH_3NH_2}\) due to poorer hydration of the bulky \(3^\circ\) ammonium ion.
Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following amines cannot be prepared by Gabriel phthalimide synthesis?

  • (A) Ethanamine
  • (B) Propan-1-amine
  • (C) Benzylamine
  • (D) Aniline
Answer: (D)
Explanation: Gabriel phthalimide synthesis requires an \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) nucleophilic attack by the phthalimide anion on an organic halide. Aryl halides (such as chlorobenzene or bromobenzene) do not undergo \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) substitution under these conditions, so aniline (\(1^\circ\) arylamine) cannot be prepared by this route. Benzylamine is prepared from benzyl chloride, which readily undergoes \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) substitution.
Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

In the Hoffmann bromamide degradation of propanamide (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CONH_2}\)), how many moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) and \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) are consumed per mole of amide, and what is the organic product?

  • (A) \(4\text{ mol }\mathrm{NaOH}\), \(1\text{ mol }\mathrm{Br_2}\); ethanamine
  • (B) \(2\text{ mol }\mathrm{NaOH}\), \(1\text{ mol }\mathrm{Br_2}\); propan-1-amine
  • (C) \(4\text{ mol }\mathrm{NaOH}\), \(2\text{ mol }\mathrm{Br_2}\); methanamine
  • (D) \(3\text{ mol }\mathrm{NaOH}\), \(1\text{ mol }\mathrm{Br_2}\); ethanamine
Answer: (A)
Explanation: The balanced equation is \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CONH_2 + Br_2 + 4NaOH \rightarrow CH_3CH_2NH_2 + Na_2CO_3 + 2NaBr + 2H_2O}\). Thus, \(1\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) and \(4\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) are required per mole of amide, yielding ethanamine (one carbon less than propanamide).
Practice Question 5 Single-Correct MCQ

An organic amine reacts with benzenesulphonyl chloride to yield a solid product that is insoluble in aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\). The amine also fails to give a foul-smelling product with \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) and alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\). The amine is:

  • (A) Propan-1-amine
  • (B) N-Methylethanamine
  • (C) N,N-Dimethylmethanamine
  • (D) Aniline
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Reacting with benzenesulphonyl chloride (Hinsberg's reagent) to give an alkali-insoluble sulphonamide is characteristic of a secondary (\(2^\circ\)) amine, because the resulting N,N-dialkylbenzenesulphonamide has no acidic \(\mathrm{N-H}\) proton. Secondary amines also fail the carbylamine test. Among the options, N-methylethanamine is a \(2^\circ\) amine.

Assertion (A): Direct nitration of aniline with a mixture of concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) at \(288\,\mathrm{K}\) produces a substantial amount (\(47\%\)) of m-nitroaniline.
Reason (R): In strongly acidic medium, aniline is protonated to form the anilinium ion, which is meta-directing.

  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).

  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Explanation: Although \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) is o/p-directing, in the strongly acidic nitration mixture a large fraction of aniline is protonated to \(\mathrm{-NH_3^+}\) (anilinium ion). Practice Question 7 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Aniline does not undergo Friedel–Crafts alkylation or acylation.

  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

When benzenediazonium chloride is treated with hypophosphorous acid (\(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\)) in the presence of water, the products formed are:

Practice Question 9 Single-Correct MCQ

Coupling of benzenediazonium chloride with phenol in a mildly alkaline medium yields:

  • (B) p-Hydroxyazobenzene (orange dye)
  • (C) Diphenyl ether
  • (D) o-Nitrophenol

Practice Question 10 Statement-Based MCQ

Consider the following reaction sequence:

\[\mathrm{CH_3CH_2Br \xrightarrow{KCN} A \xrightarrow{LiAlH_4} B \xrightarrow{HNO_2,\,273\text{–}278\,K} C + N_2\uparrow}\]

Statement I: Compound B is propan-1-amine.
Statement II: Compound C is propan-1-ol.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)
Explanation: Bromoethane reacts with \(\mathrm{KCN}\) to give propanenitrile (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CN}\), compound A). Reduction with \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) yields propan-1-amine (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2NH_2}\), compound B). Being a \(1^\circ\) aliphatic amine, propan-1-amine reacts with \(\mathrm{HNO_2}\) to form an unstable diazonium salt that decomposes immediately with \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) to give propan-1-ol (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2OH}\), compound C) and \(\mathrm{N_2}\) gas. Both statements are correct.

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Gas Phase vs. Aqueous Basicity: Never apply the gas-phase order (\(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\)) to aqueous solutions. In water, \(2^\circ\) is strongest; methylamines follow \(2^\circ > 1^\circ > 3^\circ > \mathrm{NH_3}\), whereas ethylamines follow \(2^\circ > 3^\circ > 1^\circ > \mathrm{NH_3}\).
  • Classification of tert-Butylamine: \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CNH_2}\) is a primary (\(1^\circ\)) amine because nitrogen is bonded to only one carbon atom.
  • Benzylamine vs. Aniline: Benzylamine (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2NH_2}\), \(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.70\)) is an aliphatic-like amine and is vastly more basic than aniline (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 9.38\)).
  • Iron vs. Tin in Nitro Reduction: Remember that \(\mathrm{Fe/HCl}\) is preferred industrially because \(\mathrm{FeCl_2}\) hydrolyses to regenerate \(\mathrm{HCl}\).
  • Reagents for Aryl Fluorides & Iodides: Direct halogenation fails for \(\mathrm{Ar-F}\) and \(\mathrm{Ar-I}\). Use Balz–Schiemann (\(\mathrm{HBF_4/\Delta}\)) for \(\mathrm{Ar-F}\) and \(\mathrm{KI}\) on diazonium salt for \(\mathrm{Ar-I}\).
High-Yield Revision Checklist
  • \(sp^3\) N in amines \(\rightarrow\) pyramidal, angle \(108^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3N}\).
  • Boiling points: Alcohols > \(1^\circ\) amines > \(2^\circ\) amines > \(3^\circ\) amines > alkanes.
  • \(1^\circ\) amine tests: Carbylamine (\(\mathrm{CHCl_3 + KOH}\)) gives foul-smelling \(\mathrm{R-NC}\); Hinsberg gives alkali-soluble sulphonamide.
  • Protection of aniline: \(\mathrm{(CH_3CO)_2O / Pyridine}\) \(\rightarrow\) acetanilide \(\rightarrow\) monosubstitution (para-major) \(\rightarrow\) hydrolysis.
  • Diazonium salts: Stable at \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\) (aryl only); \(\mathrm{H_3PO_2}\) or \(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}\) removes \(-\mathrm{N_2^+}\) to give \(\mathrm{Ar-H}\).

Concise Final Recap

Amines are \(sp^3\)-hybridised pyramidal derivatives of ammonia whose physical properties and Lewis basicity depend on hydrogen bonding, inductive effects, solvation, and resonance. Pure \(1^\circ\) aliphatic amines are best synthesised via Gabriel phthalimide synthesis or Hoffmann bromamide degradation, while \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) classes are cleanly separated using Hinsberg's reagent. In aromatic chemistry, controlling aniline's high reactivity through acetylation enables selective para-substitution, and converting arylamines into arenediazonium salts at \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\) provides a versatile gateway to aryl halides, cyanides, phenols, arenes, and coloured azo dyes.
