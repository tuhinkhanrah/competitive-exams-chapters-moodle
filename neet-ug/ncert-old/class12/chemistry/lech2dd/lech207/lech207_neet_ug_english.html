Chemistry in Everyday Life

Drug–Target Interactions, Therapeutic Agents, Food Additives, and Cleansing Chemistry

Learning Objectives and Overview

Chemical principles govern biological regulation, nutrition, and hygiene. For NEET UG, this topic tests your ability to connect molecular structure with biological function across three domains: medicines, food additives, and cleansing agents.

  • Distinguish between drugs, medicines, and chemotherapy, and classify drugs by pharmacological effect, drug action, chemical structure, and molecular target.
  • Explain how drugs inhibit enzymes (competitive vs. allosteric inhibition) and modulate cell-membrane receptors (agonists vs. antagonists).
  • Identify the mechanism, therapeutic role, and key examples of antacids, antihistamines, tranquilizers, analgesics, antimicrobials, and antifertility drugs.
  • Compare artificial sweeteners, food preservatives, and antioxidants by chemical class, relative sweetness, and thermal stability.
  • Contrast soaps with anionic, cationic, and non-ionic synthetic detergents in terms of hard-water behaviour and biodegradability.

Foundations of Medicinal Chemistry and Drug Classification

A drug is a chemical substance of low molecular mass (typically around \(100\text{ to }500\,\mathrm{u}\)) that interacts with macromolecular biological targets to trigger a biological response. When that response is therapeutic and beneficial—used in diagnosis, prevention, or treatment of disease—the substance is termed a medicine. Using chemical agents for therapeutic benefit is called chemotherapy.

Dose–Toxicity Principle Most medicinal drugs act as potential poisons when administered above their recommended therapeutic dose. Consequently, medical supervision is essential to maintain the concentration within a safe therapeutic window.

Four Ways to Classify Drugs

Classification Basis Core Principle and Utility Representative Example
Pharmacological Effect Groups drugs by their overall clinical outcome on the body. Most useful for physicians selecting a treatment category. Analgesics (relieve pain); Antacids (neutralise excess stomach acid); Antiseptics (arrest microbial growth).
Drug Action Groups drugs by their effect on a specific biochemical pathway or physiological mediator. All antihistamines inhibit the action of histamine, a compound that mediates inflammation.
Chemical Structure Groups drugs sharing a common structural core (pharmacophore), which often yields similar pharmacological activity. Sulphonamides share the common structural motif \(p\text{-}\mathrm{H_2N-C_6H_4-SO_2NHR}\).
Molecular Targets Groups drugs by the specific biomolecule (carbohydrate, lipid, protein, or nucleic acid) they bind. Most useful for medicinal chemists. Drugs targeting specific enzymes (e.g., monoamine oxidase) or membrane receptors (e.g., gastric histamine receptors).

Molecular Mechanisms: Drug–Target Interactions

Biological macromolecules perform specialised cellular roles: enzymes act as biological catalysts, receptors mediate intercellular communication, carrier proteins transport polar molecules across cell membranes, nucleic acids store coded genetic information, and lipids and carbohydrates form structural components of cell membranes. Drugs primarily target enzymes and receptors.

1. Enzymes as Drug Targets

During catalysis, an enzyme performs two essential functions:

  1. Substrate binding: The active site holds the substrate molecule in an optimal orientation using non-covalent forces—ionic bonding, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interactions, or dipole–dipole interactions—so a reagent can attack effectively.
  2. Chemical catalysis: Functional groups within the active site attack the bound substrate to drive the chemical transformation.

Drugs that block either binding or catalysis are called enzyme inhibitors. They operate via two distinct mechanisms:

  • Competitive Inhibition: The drug structurally resembles the natural substrate and competes directly for the active site. Increasing substrate concentration can outcompete a reversible competitive inhibitor.
  • Non-Competitive (Allosteric) Inhibition: The drug binds to a separate region called the allosteric site. This induces a conformational change that distorts the active site, preventing the natural substrate from recognising or binding to it.

If an inhibitor forms a strong covalent bond with the enzyme that cannot be broken easily, the enzyme is permanently inactivated. The cell must then degrade the enzyme–inhibitor complex and synthesise fresh enzyme molecules.

2. Receptors as Drug Targets

Receptors are protein molecules crucial to cellular communication. Most are embedded in the cell membrane with a small active binding site projecting outward into the extracellular space. Signals between two neurons, or between a neuron and a muscle, are transmitted by chemical messengers. When a chemical messenger binds to the receptor's outer pocket, the receptor undergoes an induced-fit shape change that relays the signal into the cell interior—without the messenger ever entering the cell. Once the message is delivered, the messenger detaches and the receptor regains its original shape.

Different receptors show strict selectivity for specific messengers because their binding pockets differ in shape, structure, and amino-acid composition.

  • Antagonists: Drugs that bind to the receptor site and block its natural function. Used when suppressing an overactive signal is required (e.g., blocking histamine receptors to reduce stomach acid or allergy symptoms).
  • Agonists: Drugs that mimic the natural chemical messenger and switch on the receptor. Used when the body lacks sufficient natural messenger.

Therapeutic Classes of Drugs

1. Antacids and Antihistamines: Receptor Specificity in Action

Excess hydrochloric acid (\(\mathrm{HCl}\)) in the stomach causes irritation, pain, and, in severe cases, gastric ulcers. Early treatments relied on bases to neutralise acid:

  • Sodium hydrogencarbonate (\(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\)): Rapidly neutralises acid, but excess \(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\) makes the stomach alkaline, triggering a rebound secretion of even more acid.
  • Metal hydroxides (\(\mathrm{Al(OH)_3}\), \(\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2}\)): Superior to \(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\) because they are insoluble in water and cannot raise the gastric \(\mathrm{pH}\) above neutrality. However, both types only treat symptoms rather than the underlying cause.

A major therapeutic breakthrough came with the discovery that histamine stimulates the secretion of pepsin and \(\mathrm{HCl}\) in the stomach. Drugs such as cimetidine (Tegamet) and ranitidine (Zantac) act as receptor antagonists: they prevent histamine from binding to receptors in the stomach wall, directly reducing acid release.

Outside the stomach, histamine is a potent vasodilator: it contracts smooth muscles in the bronchi and gut, relaxes smooth muscles in fine blood-vessel walls, and causes nasal congestion during a common cold or allergic reactions to pollen. Synthetic antiallergic antihistamines—such as brompheniramine (Dimetapp) and terfenadine (Seldane)—compete with histamine at allergic receptors.

Key NEET Concept: Why Antiallergics Do Not Cure Acidity Antiallergic antihistamines (brompheniramine, terfenadine) and antacid histamine blockers (cimetidine, ranitidine) do not interfere with each other's function because they bind to different types of histamine receptors in the body.

2. Neurologically Active Drugs

Neurologically active drugs alter message transfer from nerves to receptors and fall into two major classes: tranquilizers and analgesics.

(a) Tranquilizers

Tranquilizers treat stress, anxiety, irritability, and mild to severe mental disorders by inducing a sense of well-being; they are also essential components of sleeping pills.

  • Antidepressants (Enzyme Inhibitors): Low levels of the neurotransmitter noradrenaline lower signal-sending activity, causing depression. Antidepressants such as iproniazid and phenelzine (Nardil) inhibit the enzymes that catalyse noradrenaline degradation. Slower metabolism allows noradrenaline to activate its receptor for longer periods, counteracting depression.
  • Mild Tranquilizers: Chlordiazepoxide and meprobamate relieve tension; equanil controls both depression and hypertension.
  • Barbiturates: Derivatives of barbituric acid—veronal, amytal, nembutal, luminal, and seconal—act as hypnotic (sleep-producing) agents. Other notable tranquilizers include valium and serotonin.

(b) Analgesics

Analgesics relieve or abolish pain without causing impairment of consciousness, mental confusion, incoordination, or paralysis.

Category Mechanism & Therapeutic Use Key Examples & Notes
Non-narcotic (Non-addictive) Inhibit synthesis of prostaglandins (which stimulate tissue inflammation and pain). Effective for skeletal pain (e.g., arthritis) and fever reduction (antipyretic). Aspirin (also inhibits platelet coagulation, preventing heart attacks) and Paracetamol.
Narcotic (Opiates) Obtained from the opium poppy; relieve severe pain and induce sleep at medicinal doses. Used for post-operative pain, cardiac pain, terminal cancer pain, and childbirth. Morphine and its homologues (heroin, codeine). Toxic doses cause stupor, coma, convulsions, and death.

3. Antimicrobials: Antibiotics, Antiseptics, and Disinfectants

Antimicrobials selectively destroy or inhibit pathogenic microbes—bacteria (antibacterial), fungi (antifungal), viruses (antiviral), or parasites (antiparasitic).

(a) Antibiotics

Modern definition: an antibiotic is a substance produced wholly or partly by chemical synthesis that, in low concentrations, inhibits the growth or destroys microorganisms by intervening in their metabolic processes, while showing low toxicity toward humans and animals.

  • Historical Milestones: Paul Ehrlich (Nobel Prize, 1908) developed arsphenamine (salvarsan), an arsenic-based drug (\(-\mathrm{As=As}-\) linkage) for treating syphilis. Noting its structural resemblance to azodyes (\(-\mathrm{N=N}-\) linkage), he later developed prontosil (1932), which converts inside the body into the active antibacterial compound sulphanilamide—launching sulpha drugs such as sulphapyridine. Alexander Fleming discovered antibacterial action in Penicillium fungus (1929), sharing the 1945 Nobel Prize with H.W. Florey.
  • Action Type: Antibiotics are either bactericidal (kill microbes) or bacteriostatic (inhibit microbial growth).
Bactericidal (Killing) Bacteriostatic (Inhibitory)
Penicillin, Aminoglycosides, Ofloxacin Erythromycin, Tetracycline, Chloramphenicol
  • Spectrum of Action:
    • Broad-spectrum antibiotics act against a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria: ampicillin and amoxycillin (synthetic modifications of penicillin), chloramphenicol (rapidly absorbed orally; used for typhoid, dysentery, acute fever, meningitis, pneumonia, urinary infections), vancomycin, and ofloxacin.
    • Narrow-spectrum antibiotics target mainly Gram-positive or Gram-negative bacteria: Penicillin G (patients must be tested for penicillin allergy before administration).
    • Limited-spectrum antibiotics act against a single organism or disease. Note also that dysidazirine exhibits toxicity toward certain strains of cancer cells.

(b) Antiseptics and Disinfectants

Both kill or arrest microbial growth, but differ in site of application:

  • Antiseptics are applied to living tissues (wounds, cuts, ulcers, diseased skin): furacine, soframicine, dettol (a mixture of chloroxylenol and terpineol), bithionol (added to medicated soaps), tincture of iodine (2–3% iodine in alcohol–water mixture for wounds), iodoform (for wounds), and dilute aqueous boric acid (weak antiseptic for eyes).
  • Disinfectants are applied to inanimate objects (floors, drainage systems, instruments): chlorine (\(0.2\text{ to }0.4\,\mathrm{ppm}\) in aqueous solution) and sulphur dioxide (very low concentrations).
  • Concentration Dependence: A single chemical can serve both roles depending on concentration—for example, a 0.2% solution of phenol acts as an antiseptic, whereas a 1% solution of phenol acts as a disinfectant.

4. Antifertility Drugs

Oral contraceptive pills contain a combination of synthetic progesterone and estrogen derivatives (hormones). Progesterone suppresses ovulation, and synthetic derivatives are significantly more potent than natural progesterone. A widely used combination pairs norethindrone (synthetic progesterone derivative) with ethynylestradiol (novestrol) (synthetic estrogen derivative).

Chemicals in Food

Chemicals are added to food to preserve it, enhance visual and taste appeal, or add nutritive value. Main additive categories include food colours, flavours and sweeteners, fat emulsifiers and stabilisers, flour improvers (antistaling agents and bleaches), antioxidants, preservatives, and nutritional supplements (minerals, vitamins, amino acids). Except for nutritional supplements, none of these additives possess nutritive value.

Artificial Sweetening Agents

Natural sucrose adds calories, whereas artificial sweeteners provide sweetness without caloric load—making them valuable for diabetic patients and calorie-controlled diets.

Sweetener Sweetness (vs. Cane Sugar) Chemical Nature & Key Exam Properties
Aspartame 100× Methyl ester of a dipeptide formed from aspartic acid and phenylalanine. Most widely used, but unstable at cooking temperatures—limited to cold foods and soft drinks.
Saccharin 550× Ortho-sulphobenzimide (first popular artificial sweetener, discovered 1879). Inert, harmless, and excreted unchanged in urine.
Sucralose 600× Trichloro derivative of sucrose. Appearance and taste mimic sugar, provides zero calories, and is stable at cooking temperatures.
Alitame 2000× High-potency sweetener; more stable than aspartame, but its extreme sweetness makes controlling food sweetness difficult.

Food Preservatives and Antioxidants

  • Food Preservatives prevent microbial spoilage: common household preservatives include table salt, sugar, and vegetable oils; chemical preservatives include sodium benzoate (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5COONa}\)) (used in limited quantities and metabolised in the body) as well as salts of sorbic acid and propanoic acid.
  • Antioxidants retard oxygen-mediated food spoilage by reacting more readily with oxygen than the food itself. Key examples are BHT (butylated hydroxytoluene) and BHA (butylated hydroxyanisole)—adding BHA to butter extends its shelf life from months to years. Citric acid is often added alongside BHT/BHA for enhanced effect. Sulphur dioxide and sulphites serve as antioxidants in wine, beer, sugar syrups, and cut, peeled, or dried fruits and vegetables.

Cleansing Agents: Soaps and Synthetic Detergents

Both soaps and synthetic detergents act as surfactants that lower water's surface tension and emulsify oils and grease via micelle formation.

Soaps and Saponification

Soaps are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids (such as stearic, oleic, and palmitic acids). Heating fats or oils (glyceryl esters of fatty acids) with aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) hydrolyses the ester to yield soap and glycerol—a reaction called saponification:

\[ \mathrm{(C_{17}H_{35}COO)_3C_3H_5 + 3\,NaOH \longrightarrow 3\,C_{17}H_{35}COONa + C_3H_5(OH)_3} \]

Soap remains in colloidal form and is precipitated by adding \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (salting out). Glycerol is recovered from the remaining solution by fractional distillation. Only sodium and potassium soaps are water-soluble; potassium soaps are gentler (softer) on the skin than sodium soaps.

  • Specialty Soaps: Transparent soaps are made by dissolving soap in ethanol and evaporating excess solvent; floating soaps trap tiny air bubbles before hardening; shaving soaps contain glycerol (prevents rapid drying) and rosin (forms sodium rosinate for rich lather); scouring soaps contain abrasives (powdered pumice/sand) and builders (\(\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}\), \(\mathrm{Na_3PO_4}\)) to speed up action.
  • Why Soaps Fail in Hard Water: Hard water contains \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\) ions, which react with soluble sodium/potassium soaps to precipitate insoluble calcium or magnesium salts (scum):
    \[ \mathrm{2\,C_{17}H_{35}COONa + CaCl_2 \longrightarrow 2\,NaCl + (C_{17}H_{35}COO)_2Ca \downarrow} \]
    This gummy scum sticks to fabric fibres (preventing even dye absorption) and makes hair look dull.

Synthetic Detergents

Synthetic detergents contain no soap yet clean effectively in both soft and hard water (and even ice-cold water), because their calcium and magnesium salts are soluble.

Category Structural Feature Representative Example & Application
Anionic Detergents Sodium salts of sulphonated long-chain alcohols or alkylbenzenes; the anionic part performs cleansing. Sodium laurylsulphate \(\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_{10}CH_2OSO_3^-Na^+}\); Sodium dodecylbenzenesulphonate. Used in household cleaners and toothpastes.
Cationic Detergents Quaternary ammonium salts of amines with acetate, chloride, or bromide anions; cationic part has a long chain. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide \(\mathrm{[CH_3(CH_2)_{15}N(CH_3)_3]^+Br^-}\). Germicidal and expensive; used in hair conditioners.
Non-ionic Detergents Contain no ions; formed by esterification of fatty acids (e.g., stearic acid) with polyethyleneglycol. \(\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_{10}COO(CH_2CH_2O)_nCH_2CH_2OH}\). Used in liquid dishwashing detergents.
Tutorial Diagram 3: Dual-nature (amphiphilic) architecture shared by soaps and synthetic detergents.
Environmental Chemistry: Biodegradability Detergents with highly branched hydrocarbon chains resist bacterial degradation, persist after sewage treatment, and cause foaming and water pollution in rivers and ponds. Modern detergents use unbranched (straight) hydrocarbon chains so bacteria can biodegrade them rapidly.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classifying Drugs by Target vs. Pharmacological Effect

Scenario: A patient is prescribed ranitidine for hyperacidity and terfenadine for seasonal pollen allergy. Both drugs interfere with histamine, yet neither produces the clinical effect of the other. Explain this using two distinct drug classification criteria.

Solution:

  1. Pharmacological Effect Classification: Ranitidine is classified as an antacid because its clinical outcome is reducing gastric acid; terfenadine is classified as an antiallergic antihistamine because its clinical outcome is relieving allergy symptoms.
  2. Molecular Target Classification: Although both act as antagonists to histamine, they bind to different histamine receptor subtypes (gastric wall receptors vs. allergic response receptors). Because receptor binding sites differ in shape and amino-acid composition, the drugs do not cross-react.

Example 2: Selecting Artificial Sweeteners for Food Processing

Scenario: A food technologist is formulating two sugar-free products for diabetic consumers: (i) a baked biscuit requiring an oven temperature of \(180\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and (ii) a chilled lemon beverage. Which artificial sweetener among aspartame, sucralose, and alitame is best suited for each product?

Solution:

  • Baked biscuit (\(180\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): Sucralose is the optimal choice. It is a trichloro derivative of sucrose, tastes like sugar, provides zero calories, and remains stable at cooking temperatures. (Aspartame decomposes upon heating, and alitame's \(2000\times\) potency makes sweetness control difficult.)
  • Chilled lemon beverage: Aspartame is well suited because it is widely used in cold foods and soft drinks where thermal decomposition is not an issue.

Example 3: Predicting Cleansing Behaviour and Biodegradability

Scenario: Consider three cleansing agents: (X) sodium palmitate, (Y) sodium 4-(1,3,5-trimethylhexyl)benzenesulphonate (a highly branched alkylbenzenesulphonate), and (Z) sodium dodecylbenzenesulphonate (unbranched chain). Predict which agent(s) will lather in water containing dissolved \(\mathrm{Ca(HCO_3)_2}\), and which will cause persistent river foaming.

Solution:

  • Lathering in hard water (\(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) present): Both synthetic detergents Y and Z will lather effectively because their calcium salts are soluble. Soap X (sodium palmitate) will react with \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) to form insoluble calcium palmitate scum.
  • Environmental persistence: Detergent Y has a highly branched hydrocarbon chain that bacteria cannot easily degrade, leading to slow biodegradation and foaming in rivers. Detergent Z has an unbranched chain and is readily biodegradable.

Example 4: Calculating Saponification Stoichiometry

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Suppose a chemist hydrolyses \(1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of pure glyceryl tristearate (molar mass \(= 890\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) completely using aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) (molar mass \(= 40\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)). Calculate the minimum mass of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) required and the moles of sodium stearate soap produced.

Solution:

From the balanced saponification equation:

\[ \mathrm{(C_{17}H_{35}COO)_3C_3H_5 + 3\,NaOH \longrightarrow 3\,C_{17}H_{35}COONa + C_3H_5(OH)_3} \]

\(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of glyceryl tristearate reacts with \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to yield \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of sodium stearate and \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of glycerol.
Minimum mass of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) required \(= 3\,\mathrm{mol} \times 40\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 120\,\mathrm{g}\).
Moles of sodium stearate produced \(= 3.0\,\mathrm{mol}\).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following classifications of drugs is considered the most useful for medicinal chemists?

  • (A) On the basis of pharmacological effect
  • (B) On the basis of chemical structure
  • (C) On the basis of molecular targets
  • (D) On the basis of drug action
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Medicinal chemists design drugs to interact with specific biomolecules (enzymes, receptors, nucleic acids, etc.) so that side effects are minimised. Therefore, classification based on molecular targets is the most useful for medicinal chemists, whereas pharmacological effect is most useful for doctors.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A drug that binds to an enzyme at a site other than the active site, thereby altering the geometry of the active site so the substrate can no longer bind, is known as:

  • (A) A competitive inhibitor
  • (B) An allosteric (non-competitive) inhibitor
  • (C) A receptor agonist
  • (D) A chemical messenger
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Non-competitive inhibitors bind to an allosteric site away from the active site. This induces a conformational change that distorts the active site and prevents substrate recognition.

Practice Question 3 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Given below are two statements:
Statement I: Excess sodium hydrogencarbonate used as an antacid can trigger the production of even more acid in the stomach.
Statement II: Insoluble metal hydroxides such as \(\mathrm{Al(OH)_3}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2}\) do not increase the gastric \(\mathrm{pH}\) above neutrality.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Excessive \(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\) makes the stomach alkaline, which stimulates rebound acid secretion. Metal hydroxides are insoluble, so they neutralise excess acid without raising \(\mathrm{pH}\) above 7.

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following pairs consists exclusively of bactericidal antibiotics?

  • (A) Penicillin and Tetracycline
  • (B) Erythromycin and Chloramphenicol
  • (C) Ofloxacin and Aminoglycosides
  • (D) Tetracycline and Chloramphenicol
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Penicillin, aminoglycosides, and ofloxacin are bactericidal (kill bacteria), whereas erythromycin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol are bacteriostatic (inhibit bacterial growth).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Aspartame vs. Sucralose: Do not confuse their thermal stability. Aspartame (\(100\times\)) is unstable at cooking temperatures; sucralose (\(600\times\)) is heat-stable.
  • Phenol Concentration Flip: \(0.2\%\) phenol is an antiseptic (living tissue); \(1\%\) phenol is a disinfectant (inanimate objects).
  • Antacid vs. Antiallergic Histamine Blockers: Brompheniramine and terfenadine do not reduce stomach acid because they act on different histamine receptors than cimetidine and ranitidine.
  • Bactericidal vs. Bacteriostatic: Chloramphenicol and tetracycline are bacteriostatic, not bactericidal.
  • Chain Branching in Detergents: Unbranched chains are biodegradable; highly branched chains are non-biodegradable and cause river foaming.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Can you name the two functions of an enzyme's active site and distinguish competitive from allosteric inhibition?
  • Can you distinguish receptor agonists (mimic messenger) from antagonists (block messenger)?
  • Do you remember the active compound formed in the body from prontosil (sulphanilamide)?
  • Can you identify the constituents of Dettol (chloroxylenol + terpineol) and birth-control pills (norethindrone + ethynylestradiol)?
  • Can you write the chemical reason soaps form scum in hard water while synthetic detergents do not?

Concise Final Recap

Drugs are low-molecular-mass chemicals (\(100\text{–}500\,\mathrm{u}\)) that interact with macromolecular targets—chiefly enzymes (via competitive or allosteric inhibition) and membrane receptors (as agonists or antagonists). Therapeutic agents include receptor-specific antacids (cimetidine, ranitidine), antiallergics (brompheniramine, terfenadine), tranquilizers (iproniazid, equanil, barbiturates), analgesics (non-narcotic aspirin/paracetamol vs. narcotic morphine), antimicrobials (bactericidal/bacteriostatic antibiotics, concentration-dependent antiseptics/disinfectants), and antifertility hormones (norethindrone + ethynylestradiol). In food chemistry, non-nutritive additives such as artificial sweeteners (aspartame, saccharin, sucralose, alitame), preservatives (sodium benzoate), and antioxidants (BHT, BHA) extend utility and shelf life. Finally, while soaps precipitate as scum in hard water, anionic, cationic, and non-ionic synthetic detergents remain effective—with unbranched hydrocarbon chains ensuring biodegradability.
