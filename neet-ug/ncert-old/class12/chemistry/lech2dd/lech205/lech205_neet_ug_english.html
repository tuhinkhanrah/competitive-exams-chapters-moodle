Biomolecules: Structure, Reactivity, and Biological Function

A complete, exam-focused guide covering carbohydrates, amino acids, proteins, enzymes, vitamins, nucleic acids, and hormones.

Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

Living organisms are sustained by coordinated chemical reactions built from non-living molecules. Biochemistry examines how complex organic macromolecules—carbohydrates, proteins, and nucleic acids—along with essential regulatory molecules—vitamins and hormones—govern energy storage, catalysis, heredity, and metabolic balance.

  • Classify carbohydrates by hydrolysis behaviour, functional group, and reducing ability, and interpret the open-chain and cyclic structures of glucose and fructose.
  • Explain the zwitterionic nature, acid-base behaviour, and stereochemistry of \(\alpha\)-amino acids, and distinguish the four hierarchical levels of protein structure.
  • Describe enzyme catalysis in terms of substrate specificity and activation energy reduction.
  • Categorize vitamins by solubility and match each vitamin to its primary biological role and deficiency disorder.
  • Differentiate DNA and RNA chemically and structurally, including base-pairing rules and phosphodiester linkages.
  • Identify the chemical classes and physiological roles of key human hormones.

1. Carbohydrates

1.1 Chemical Definition and Classification

Early chemists noticed that many plant-derived sugars obeyed the empirical formula \(\mathrm{C}_x(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_y\) and termed them "hydrates of carbon." However, this formula is neither sufficient nor necessary:

  • Acetic acid (\(\mathrm{CH}_3\mathrm{COOH}\)) fits \(\mathrm{C}_2(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_2\) yet is a carboxylic acid, not a carbohydrate.
  • Rhamnose (\(\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_5\)) is a true carbohydrate even though it does not fit \(\mathrm{C}_x(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_y\).

Chemically, carbohydrates (saccharides) are defined as optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that yield such units upon hydrolysis.

Category (by Hydrolysis) Monomer Units Yielded Representative Examples Taste & Solubility
Monosaccharides Cannot be hydrolysed further (1 unit) Glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose Sweet, water-soluble
Oligosaccharides 2 to 10 units (disaccharides yield 2) Sucrose, maltose, lactose Sweet, water-soluble
Polysaccharides Large number (>10) of units Starch, cellulose, glycogen Tasteless (non-sugars), mostly insoluble

Reducing vs. Non-Reducing Sugars: Carbohydrates that reduce Tollens' reagent (ammoniacal silver nitrate) and Fehling's solution are reducing sugars. All monosaccharides—both aldoses (containing \(-\mathrm{CHO}\)) and ketoses (containing \(>\mathrm{C=O}\))—are reducing sugars because ketoses tautomerize to aldoses under alkaline test conditions. Disaccharides are reducing if at least one anomeric hemiacetal carbon remains free (e.g., maltose, lactose) and non-reducing if both anomeric carbons are locked in a glycosidic bond (e.g., sucrose).

1.2 Monosaccharides: Nomenclature and Glucose Chemistry

Monosaccharides are named by combining their carbonyl type (aldo- for aldehyde, keto- for ketone) with their carbon chain length: triose (3C), tetrose (4C), pentose (5C), hexose (6C), and heptose (7C). Thus, glucose is an aldohexose and fructose is a ketohexose.

Preparation of Glucose

  • From Sucrose (Cane Sugar): Boiling sucrose with dilute \(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\) in alcoholic solution yields equimolar glucose and fructose: \[\mathrm{C}_{12}\mathrm{H}_{22}\mathrm{O}_{11} + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H}^+} \mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6\text{ (Glucose)} + \mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6\text{ (Fructose)}\]
  • From Starch (Commercial Method): Hydrolysing starch with dilute \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{SO}_4\) at \(393\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{atm}\) pressure gives glucose: \[(\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{10}\mathrm{O}_5)_n + n\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H}^+,\ 393\,\mathrm{K},\ 2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{atm}} n\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6\]

Experimental Proof of Glucose's Open-Chain Structure

Reagent / Reaction Observed Product Structural Inference

D/L Configuration vs. Optical Rotation

The letters D and L denote relative stereochemical configuration compared with (+)-glyceraldehyde, not the sign of optical rotation. When a monosaccharide is drawn in a Fischer projection with the most oxidized carbon (\(-\mathrm{CHO}\)) at the top, if the \(-\mathrm{OH}\) group on the lowest asymmetric carbon (C5 in glucose) points to the right, it belongs to the D-series; if it points to the left, it belongs to the L-series. Dextrorotatory rotation is indicated by \((+)\) and laevorotatory rotation by \((-)\). Glucose is \(\mathrm{D}(+)\)-glucose, whereas fructose is \(\mathrm{D}(-)\)-fructose.

1.3 Cyclic Structure of Glucose and Fructose

Three key observations proved that glucose does not exist purely as an open-chain aldehyde:

  1. Glucose fails to give Schiff's test and does not form a bisulphite addition product with \(\mathrm{NaHSO}_3\).
  2. Glucose pentaacetate does not react with hydroxylamine (\(\mathrm{NH}_2\mathrm{OH}\)), showing no free \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) group is available.
  3. Glucose crystallizes in two distinct forms: \(\alpha\)-D-glucose (m.p. \(419\,\mathrm{K}\), crystallized from concentrated solution at \(303\,\mathrm{K}\)) and \(\beta\)-D-glucose (m.p. \(423\,\mathrm{K}\), crystallized from hot saturated aqueous solution at \(371\,\mathrm{K}\)).

Intramolecular nucleophilic attack by the C5 \(-\mathrm{OH}\) group on the C1 aldehyde produces a six-membered cyclic hemiacetal called a pyranose ring (analogous to pyran). The two stereoisomers differing only in configuration at the newly formed chiral C1 (the anomeric carbon) are called anomers (\(\alpha\) and \(\beta\)).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Haworth structures of \(\alpha\)-D-(+)-glucopyranose (C1 \(-\mathrm{OH}\) trans to C6 \(-\mathrm{CH}_2\mathrm{OH}\)) and \(\beta\)-D-(+)-glucopyranose (C1 \(-\mathrm{OH}\) cis to C6 \(-\mathrm{CH}_2\mathrm{OH}\)).

Fructose: Fructose (\(\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6\)) has a ketone group at C2. Addition of the C5 \(-\mathrm{OH}\) group to C2 forms a five-membered cyclic hemiketal called a furanose ring (analogous to furan), giving \(\alpha\)-D-\((-)\)-fructofuranose and \(\beta\)-D-\((-)\)-fructofuranose.

1.4 Disaccharides and Polysaccharides

Monosaccharides link together by losing a water molecule to form an oxide bridge (\(-\mathrm{O}-\)) called a glycosidic linkage.

Disaccharide Monomer Units Glycosidic Linkage Reducing? Key Exam Note
Sucrose \(\alpha\)-D-glucose + \(\beta\)-D-fructose C1 (\(\alpha\)-glucose) to C2 (\(\beta\)-fructose) No (Non-reducing) Hydrolysis gives invert sugar (dextro \(\rightarrow\) laevo)
Maltose Two \(\alpha\)-D-glucose units C1 of unit I to C4 of unit II Yes (Reducing) Free C1 hemiacetal on unit II
Lactose (milk sugar) \(\beta\)-D-galactose + \(\beta\)-D-glucose C1 (galactose) to C4 (glucose) Yes (Reducing) Free C1 hemiacetal on glucose unit
Why Sucrose Hydrolysis Produces "Invert Sugar" Sucrose itself is dextrorotatory. Upon acid or enzyme hydrolysis, it yields an equimolar mixture of D-(+)-glucose (\(+52.5^\circ\)) and D-(\(-\))-fructose (\(-92.4^\circ\)). Because the laevorotation of fructose exceeds the dextrorotation of glucose, the net optical rotation of the mixture becomes laevorotatory—inverting the sign of rotation from \((+)\) to \((-)\).

Polysaccharides serve as energy reserves or structural scaffolds:

  • Starch (plant energy storage): Polymer of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose with two fractions:
    • Amylose (15–20%): Water-soluble, unbranched chains of 200–1000 \(\alpha\)-D-glucose units joined by \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}4\) linkages.
    • Amylopectin (80–85%): Water-insoluble, branched polymer with \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}4\) chains and \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}6\) branch points.
  • Cellulose (plant cell walls): Straight-chain polymer of \(\beta\)-D-glucose joined exclusively by \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}4\) \(\beta\)-glycosidic linkages.
  • Glycogen (animal starch, stored in liver, muscles, and brain): Structurally similar to amylopectin but even more highly branched.

2. Amino Acids and Proteins

2.1 Classification and Properties of \(\alpha\)-Amino Acids

Proteins are polymers of \(\alpha\)-amino acids, which contain both an amino group (\(-\mathrm{NH}_2\)) and a carboxyl group (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)) attached to the same \(\alpha\)-carbon: \(\mathrm{R}-\mathrm{CH}(\mathrm{NH}_2)-\mathrm{COOH}\).

  • Acidic, Basic, and Neutral Amino Acids: Equal numbers of \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) and \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) groups give a neutral amino acid (e.g., glycine, alanine). More \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) groups give an acidic amino acid (e.g., aspartic acid, glutamic acid), whereas more \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) groups give a basic amino acid (e.g., lysine, arginine, histidine).
  • Essential vs. Non-Essential: The 10 essential amino acids cannot be synthesized by the human body and must come from the diet: Valine, Leucine, Isoleucine, Arginine, Lysine, Threonine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan, and Histidine. The remaining 10 (e.g., glycine, alanine, serine, tyrosine) are non-essential.
  • Stereochemistry: All naturally occurring \(\alpha\)-amino acids except glycine (\(\mathrm{R = H}\)) have an asymmetric \(\alpha\)-carbon and are optically active, predominantly existing in the L-configuration (with \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) on the left in a Fischer projection).

Zwitterion Formation and Amphoteric Behaviour

In aqueous solution, the acidic \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) group donates a proton to the basic \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) group, forming a dipolar ion called a zwitterion (\(\mathrm{R}-\mathrm{CH}(\mathrm{NH}_3^+)-\mathrm{COO}^-\)). Because of these strong electrostatic interactions, amino acids are high-melting, water-soluble crystalline solids—unlike simple amines or haloacids. In the zwitterionic form, they are amphoteric: the \(-\mathrm{COO}^-\) group accepts \(\mathrm{H}^+\) in acid, and the \(-\mathrm{NH}_3^+\) group donates \(\mathrm{H}^+\) to \(\mathrm{OH}^-\) in base.

2.2 Peptide Bond and Levels of Protein Structure

Condensation of the \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) group of one amino acid with the \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) group of another eliminates water to form an amide linkage (\(-\mathrm{CO}-\mathrm{NH}-\)), termed a peptide bond. Two amino acids give a dipeptide (1 peptide bond), three give a tripeptide (2 peptide bonds), and more than ten give a polypeptide. Generally, a polypeptide with more than 100 amino acid residues and molecular mass above \(10{,}000\,\mathrm{u}\) is classified as a protein (though smaller polypeptides with a well-defined conformation, such as insulin with 51 amino acids, are also proteins).

  • Fibrous vs. Globular Proteins: In fibrous proteins (e.g., keratin in hair/wool/silk, myosin in muscles), parallel polypeptide chains are held by hydrogen and disulphide bonds, making them water-insoluble. In globular proteins (e.g., insulin, albumins), chains coil into compact spheres that are usually water-soluble.
Structural Level What It Describes Stabilizing Forces
Primary (\(1^\circ\)) Specific linear sequence of amino acids in each polypeptide chain Covalent peptide bonds
Secondary (\(2^\circ\)) Local backbone folding: right-handed \(\alpha\)-helix or stretched \(\beta\)-pleated sheet Hydrogen bonds between \(>\mathrm{C=O}\) and \(-\mathrm{NH}-\) (intramolecular in \(\alpha\)-helix; intermolecular in \(\beta\)-sheet)
Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) Overall 3D folding of the polypeptide into fibrous or globular shapes Hydrogen bonds, disulphide linkages (\(-\mathrm{S}-\mathrm{S}-\)), van der Waals forces, and electrostatic attractions
Quaternary (\(4^\circ\)) Spatial arrangement of two or more polypeptide subunits (e.g., haemoglobin) Same non-covalent and disulphide interactions as \(3^\circ\) structure

Denaturation of Proteins: Subjecting a native protein to physical changes (temperature) or chemical changes (pH) disrupts hydrogen bonds and unfolds the globules/helices, destroying biological activity. During denaturation, secondary and tertiary structures are destroyed, but the primary structure remains intact. Examples include coagulation of egg white on boiling and curdling of milk by bacterial lactic acid.

3. Enzymes and Vitamins

3.1 Enzymes: Biological Catalysts

Almost all enzymes are globular proteins that act as highly specific biocatalysts under mild physiological conditions. They are named after the substrate (e.g., maltase hydrolyses maltose to glucose) or the reaction type (e.g., oxidoreductases), typically ending in -ase. Like chemical catalysts, enzymes accelerate reactions by lowering the activation energy (\(E_a\)). For instance, the activation energy for sucrose hydrolysis drops from \(6.22\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) (acid catalysis) to \(2.15\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) when catalysed by the enzyme sucrase.

3.2 Vitamins: Classification and Deficiency Diseases

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small dietary amounts for normal growth and health. They are classified by solubility:

  • Fat-Soluble Vitamins (A, D, E, K): Soluble in fats/oils, insoluble in water, and stored in the liver and adipose tissue.
  • Water-Soluble Vitamins (B-complex and C): Readily excreted in urine and must be supplied regularly in the diet because they cannot be stored in the body—with the sole exception of Vitamin \(\mathrm{B}_{12}\), which is stored in the liver.
Vitamin Chemical Name Key Dietary Sources Deficiency Disease / Symptoms
Vitamin A Retinol Fish liver oil, carrots, butter, milk Xerophthalmia (hardening of cornea), night blindness
Vitamin \(\mathrm{B}_1\) Thiamine Yeast, milk, green vegetables, cereals Beri-beri (loss of appetite, retarded growth)
Vitamin \(\mathrm{B}_2\) Riboflavin Milk, egg white, liver, kidney Cheilosis (fissuring at corners of mouth/lips), digestive disorders, burning skin
Vitamin \(\mathrm{B}_6\) Pyridoxine Yeast, milk, egg yolk, cereals, grams Convulsions
Vitamin \(\mathrm{B}_{12}\) Cyanocobalamin Meat, fish, egg, curd Pernicious anaemia (RBCs deficient in haemoglobin)
Vitamin C Ascorbic acid Citrus fruits, amla, green leafy vegetables Scurvy (bleeding gums)
Vitamin D Calciferol Sunlight exposure, fish, egg yolk Rickets (bone deformities in children), osteomalacia (soft bones in adults)
Vitamin E Tocopherol Vegetable oils (wheat germ, sunflower) Increased fragility of RBCs, muscular weakness
Vitamin K Phylloquinone Green leafy vegetables Increased blood clotting time

4. Nucleic Acids

4.1 Chemical Composition: Nucleosides and Nucleotides

Chromosomes carry hereditary information and consist of proteins and nucleic acids—polymers of nucleotides (polynucleotides). Complete hydrolysis of a nucleic acid yields three components:

  1. A Pentose Sugar: \(\beta\)-D-2-deoxyribose in DNA; \(\beta\)-D-ribose in RNA.
  2. Phosphoric Acid (\(\mathrm{H}_3\mathrm{PO}_4\)).
  3. Nitrogenous Heterocyclic Bases: Adenine (A), Guanine (G), Cytosine (C), and Thymine (T) in DNA; RNA contains A, G, C, and Uracil (U) instead of thymine.

Attaching a base to the \(1'\) carbon of the pentose sugar forms a nucleoside. Linking phosphoric acid to the \(5'\) position of a nucleoside forms a nucleotide. Adjacent nucleotides join via a \(3'\text{–}5'\) phosphodiester linkage to build the sugar-phosphate backbone.

4.2 DNA vs. RNA and Biological Roles

The linear sequence of nucleotides is the primary structure of a nucleic acid. In its secondary structure, DNA forms a double-stranded helix (Watson and Crick) in which two complementary strands are held together by hydrogen bonds: Adenine pairs with Thymine (\(\mathrm{A=T}\)) and Guanine pairs with Cytosine (\(\mathrm{G\equiv C}\)). By contrast, RNA is typically single-stranded (sometimes folding back on itself) and has no fixed molar ratio among its bases. Three functional types of RNA—mRNA (messenger), rRNA (ribosomal), and tRNA (transfer)—carry out protein synthesis using the genetic instructions stored in DNA. DNA also enables self-duplication during cell division and underpins DNA fingerprinting for forensic identification, paternity testing, and evolutionary studies.

5. Hormones

Hormones are intercellular chemical messengers secreted directly into the bloodstream by endocrine glands and transported to target tissues. Chemically, they fall into three classes:

  • Steroid Hormones: Produced by the adrenal cortex and gonads. Glucocorticoids regulate carbohydrate metabolism, inflammation, and stress; mineralocorticoids regulate salt and water excretion by the kidneys (adrenal cortex deficiency causes Addison's disease, marked by hypoglycaemia and weakness). Gonadal steroids include testosterone (male secondary sexual characteristics), estradiol (female secondary sexual characteristics and menstrual cycle), and progesterone (prepares the uterus for implantation).
  • Polypeptide Hormones: Insulin (lowers blood glucose) and glucagon (raises blood glucose) act antagonistically to maintain blood sugar homeostasis; endorphins also belong to this class.
  • Amino Acid Derivatives: Epinephrine and norepinephrine mediate responses to external stimuli. Thyroxine, secreted by the thyroid gland, is an iodinated derivative of tyrosine. Dietary iodine deficiency causes hypothyroidism (lethargy, obesity, thyroid enlargement), prevented by iodised table salt; excess thyroxine causes hyperthyroidism.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Identifying Reducing Sugars and Hydrolysis Products

Problem: Suppose a student is given two unlabelled aqueous sugar solutions, Sample X and Sample Y. Sample X gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent, and upon acid hydrolysis yields only D-(+)-glucose. Sample Y does not react with Tollens' reagent, and upon acid hydrolysis yields a laevorotatory mixture of two different monosaccharides. Identify Sample X and Sample Y, and state the glycosidic linkage in each.

Solution:

  1. Because Sample X reduces Tollens' reagent, it is a reducing disaccharide. Hydrolysis yielding only D-(+)-glucose identifies Sample X as maltose, which has an \(\alpha\text{-}1,4\) glycosidic linkage between C1 of one \(\alpha\)-D-glucose unit and C4 of the second \(\alpha\)-D-glucose unit.
  2. Because Sample Y fails to reduce Tollens' reagent, it is a non-reducing disaccharide. Hydrolysis giving a laevorotatory mixture (invert sugar) of D-(+)-glucose (\(+52.5^\circ\)) and D-(\(-\))-fructose (\(-92.4^\circ\)) identifies Sample Y as sucrose, which has a glycosidic linkage between C1 of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose and C2 of \(\beta\)-D-fructose.

Example 2: Classifying Amino Acids and Writing Amphoteric Reactions

Problem: Classify the hypothetical tripeptide components lysine, glutamic acid, and alanine as acidic, basic, or neutral, and write the reaction of alanine's zwitterion with (a) aqueous \(\mathrm{HCl}\) and (b) aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\).

Solution:

  • Lysine has two \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) groups and one \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) group \(\rightarrow\) basic amino acid.
  • Glutamic acid has one \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) group and two \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) groups \(\rightarrow\) acidic amino acid.
  • Alanine has one \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) group and one \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) group \(\rightarrow\) neutral amino acid.

In water, alanine exists as the zwitterion \(\mathrm{CH}_3-\mathrm{CH}(\mathrm{NH}_3^+)-\mathrm{COO}^-\):

  • (a) With acid (\(\mathrm{H}^+\)): \(\mathrm{CH}_3-\mathrm{CH}(\mathrm{NH}_3^+)-\mathrm{COO}^- + \mathrm{H}^+ \longrightarrow \mathrm{CH}_3-\mathrm{CH}(\mathrm{NH}_3^+)-\mathrm{COOH}\)
  • (b) With base (\(\mathrm{OH}^-\)): \(\mathrm{CH}_3-\mathrm{CH}(\mathrm{NH}_3^+)-\mathrm{COO}^- + \mathrm{OH}^- \longrightarrow \mathrm{CH}_3-\mathrm{CH}(\mathrm{NH}_2)-\mathrm{COO}^- + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\)

Example 3: Counting Peptide Linkages in a Polypeptide

Problem: Assume a newly synthesized linear peptide hormone fragment contains 14 \(\alpha\)-amino acid residues. How many peptide bonds are present in this fragment, and how many water molecules are eliminated during its condensation from free amino acids?

Solution:

Each peptide bond forms by condensing the \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) group of one amino acid with the \(-\mathrm{NH}_2\) group of the next, releasing one \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) molecule. For a linear chain of \(n\) amino acid residues, the number of peptide bonds is \(n - 1\). Here, \(n = 14\):

\[\text{Number of peptide bonds} = 14 - 1 = 13\]

Therefore, the fragment contains 13 peptide linkages, and 13 molecules of water are eliminated.

Example 4: Applying Complementary Base-Pairing Rules in DNA

Problem: Suppose a double-stranded DNA sample (assumed to obey standard Watson–Crick pairing) is analysed and found to contain \(22\%\) guanine (G) by mole. Calculate the percentage of cytosine (C), adenine (A), and thymine (T) in this DNA sample.

Solution:

In double-stranded DNA, G pairs exclusively with C, and A pairs exclusively with T, so \([\mathrm{G}] = [\mathrm{C}]\) and \([\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{T}]\).

  1. Since \([\mathrm{G}] = 22\%\), we have \([\mathrm{C}] = 22\%\).
  2. Together, \(\mathrm{G + C} = 22\% + 22\% = 44\%\).
  3. The remaining \(100\% - 44\% = 56\%\) is shared equally between A and T: \[[\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{T}] = \frac{56\%}{2} = 28\%\]

Thus, the composition is \(22\%\) C, \(28\%\) A, and \(28\%\) T.

NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1: Which of the following statements about D-(+)-glucose is INCORRECT?

  • (A) Prolonged heating with \(\mathrm{HI}\) produces n-hexane.
  • (B) Oxidation with bromine water yields gluconic acid.
  • (C) Glucose pentaacetate reacts readily with hydroxylamine to form an oxime.
  • (D) The letter 'D' indicates its relative configuration with respect to (+)-glyceraldehyde.

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Glucose pentaacetate does not react with hydroxylamine because the cyclic hemiacetal hydroxyl group at C1 is acetylated, preventing ring opening to form a free \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) group. Statements (A), (B), and (D) are correct.

Practice Question 2: Which pair consists of a non-reducing disaccharide and a reducing disaccharide, respectively?

  • (A) Maltose and Lactose
  • (B) Sucrose and Maltose
  • (C) Lactose and Sucrose
  • (D) Glucose and Fructose

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons (C1 of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose and C2 of \(\beta\)-D-fructose) are tied up in the glycosidic bond. Maltose (and lactose) retains a free hemiacetal group at C1 of one sugar unit and is therefore reducing.

Practice Question 3: Which of the following pairs represents a structural difference between amylose and cellulose?

  • (A) Amylose is a branched \(\alpha\)-D-glucose polymer; cellulose is an unbranched \(\alpha\)-D-glucose polymer.
  • (B) Amylose is a linear \(\alpha\)-D-glucose polymer with \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}4\) linkages; cellulose is a linear \(\beta\)-D-glucose polymer with \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}4\) linkages.
  • (C) Amylose contains \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}6\) branching; cellulose contains \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}2\) linkages.
  • (D) Amylose is water-insoluble; cellulose is water-soluble.

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Amylose is the water-soluble, unbranched fraction of starch composed of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose units joined by \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}4\) linkages. Cellulose is an unbranched, water-insoluble polymer of \(\beta\)-D-glucose units joined by \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}4\) linkages.

Practice Question 4: Which of the following naturally occurring \(\alpha\)-amino acids is optically INACTIVE?

  • (A) Alanine
  • (B) Valine
  • (C) Glycine
  • (D) Serine

Answer: (C)

Explanation: In glycine (\(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{N}-\mathrm{CH}_2-\mathrm{COOH}\)), the \(\alpha\)-carbon is bonded to two identical hydrogen atoms, so it has no chiral centre and is optically inactive.

Practice Question 5: Given below are two statements:
Statement I: During denaturation of a protein, secondary and tertiary structures are destroyed, but the primary structure remains intact.
Statement II: The \(\alpha\)-helix secondary structure of proteins is stabilized by intermolecular hydrogen bonds between adjacent stretched polypeptide chains.
Choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Statement I is true: denaturation disrupts weak non-covalent interactions (H-bonds) without breaking covalent peptide bonds. Statement II is false: the \(\alpha\)-helix is stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonds within a single coiled chain, whereas intermolecular hydrogen bonds between stretched chains stabilize the \(\beta\)-pleated sheet.

Practice Question 6: Which of the following sets contains ONLY essential amino acids?

  • (A) Glycine, Valine, Lysine
  • (B) Valine, Leucine, Tryptophan
  • (C) Alanine, Aspartic acid, Cysteine
  • (D) Serine, Methionine, Tyrosine

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Valine, leucine, and tryptophan are all among the 10 essential amino acids that must be obtained from the diet. Glycine, alanine, aspartic acid, cysteine, serine, and tyrosine are non-essential.

Practice Question 9: Complete hydrolysis of a DNA nucleotide containing thymine yields:

  • (D) \(\beta\)-D-2-deoxyribose and thymine only

Answer: (C)

Explanation: A DNA nucleotide consists of a \(\beta\)-D-2-deoxyribose sugar, a nitrogenous base (here thymine), and a phosphate group, all three of which are released upon complete hydrolysis.

Practice Question 10: Assertion (A): In RNA, the molar proportions of adenine and uracil are not necessarily equal.
Reason (R): RNA is a single-stranded polynucleotide molecule without complementary double-helix base pairing along its entire length.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Answer: (A)

Explanation: Unlike double-stranded DNA where \(\mathrm{A=T}\) and \(\mathrm{G\equiv C}\) force equal molar ratios, RNA is single-stranded, so there is no stoichiometric relationship among the quantities of its four bases (A, U, G, C).

Practice Question 11: Which hormone is an iodinated derivative of the amino acid tyrosine, and what condition results from its abnormally low secretion?

  • (A) Insulin; Addison's disease
  • (B) Thyroxine; Hypothyroidism
  • (C) Epinephrine; Hyperthyroidism
  • (D) Glucocorticoid; Hypoglycaemia

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Thyroxine is an iodinated derivative of tyrosine produced by the thyroid gland. Abnormally low levels lead to hypothyroidism (characterized by lethargy and obesity).

Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

  • Confusing D/L with \((+)\)/\((-)\): D and L indicate relative configuration (orientation of \(-\mathrm{OH}\) on the lowest chiral carbon), whereas \((+)\) and \((-)\) indicate the direction of plane-polarized light rotation. For example, fructose is \(\mathrm{D}(-)\)-fructose.
  • Assuming Ketoses Are Non-Reducing: Fructose is a ketose, yet it is a reducing sugar because it isomerizes to glucose/mannose in alkaline Tollens' or Fehling's solutions.
  • Mixing Up Sucrose and Lactose/Maltose: Sucrose is the only common disaccharide that is non-reducing (its \(\mathrm{C}1\text{–}\mathrm{C}2\) linkage blocks both anomeric carbons).
  • Forgetting the \(\mathrm{B}_{12}\) Exception: Water-soluble vitamins are not stored in the body except Vitamin \(\mathrm{B}_{12}\).
  • Denaturation Scope: Denaturation never breaks peptide bonds; the primary (\(1^\circ\)) sequence remains intact.
  • Nucleoside vs. Nucleotide: A nucleoside has no phosphate group (Sugar + Base); a nucleotide has a phosphate group (Sugar + Base + Phosphate).

Concise Final Recap

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones; glucose forms a 6-membered pyranose ring (\(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) anomers at C1), fructose forms a 5-membered furanose ring, sucrose is non-reducing (giving invert sugar on hydrolysis), and starch/cellulose differ in \(\alpha\)- vs. \(\beta\)-glycosidic linkages. Proteins are polymers of L-\(\alpha\)-amino acids linked by peptide bonds (\(-\mathrm{CO}-\mathrm{NH}-\)), exhibiting zwitterionic amphoteric behaviour and four structural levels, with \(2^\circ/3^\circ\) structures lost upon denaturation. Enzymes are globular proteins that lower activation energy. Vitamins are fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) or water-soluble (B-complex, C). Nucleic acids (DNA with deoxyribose and A, G, C, T; RNA with ribose and A, G, C, U) store and translate genetic information via \(3'\text{–}5'\) phosphodiester backbones and complementary base pairing. Hormones (steroids, polypeptides, amino acid derivatives) coordinate metabolic and physiological homeostasis.
