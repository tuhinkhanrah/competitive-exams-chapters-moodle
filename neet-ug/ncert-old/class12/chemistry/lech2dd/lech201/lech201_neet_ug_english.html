NEET UG Organic Chemistry

Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

Classification, Synthetic Routes, Nucleophilic Substitution, Stereochemistry, Elimination, and Haloarene Reactivity

Learning Objectives and Overview

Replacing one or more hydrogen atoms in an aliphatic or aromatic hydrocarbon with halogen atoms (\(\mathrm{F, Cl, Br, I}\)) yields haloalkanes (alkyl halides) and haloarenes (aryl halides), respectively. In haloalkanes, the halogen atom is bonded to an \(sp^3\)-hybridised carbon atom, whereas in haloarenes it is attached directly to an \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbon atom of an aromatic ring.

Organohalogen compounds are widely used as industrial solvents, synthetic intermediates, and pharmaceuticals. Notable examples include the soil-microorganism antibiotic chloramphenicol (used against typhoid fever), the endogenous iodine-containing hormone thyroxine (whose deficiency leads to goitre), the antimalarial drug chloroquine, the surgical anaesthetic halothane, and perfluorinated compounds investigated as blood substitutes.

Exam Preparation Targets
  • Classify organohalides by halogen count and carbon hybridisation, and assign unambiguous IUPAC names.
  • Predict physical trends in bond enthalpy, dipole moment, boiling point, melting point, density, and solubility.
  • Select appropriate reagents for preparing haloalkanes and haloarenes while avoiding competing side reactions.
  • Distinguish \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) and \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) pathways using kinetics, substrate structure, solvent effects, and stereochemical outcomes.
  • Apply Zaitsev's rule in \(\beta\)-elimination and evaluate substitution versus elimination competition.
  • Explain the low \(\mathrm{S_N}\) reactivity of haloarenes, nitro-group activation at ortho/para positions, and electrophilic substitution orientation.
  • Recall industrial applications and environmental or physiological hazards of key polyhalogen compounds.

Classification and IUPAC Nomenclature

1. Classification by Halogen Count and Carbon Hybridisation

Organohalides are grouped as monohalo, dihalo, or polyhalo (tri-, tetra-, etc.) derivatives depending on the number of halogen atoms present. Monohalogen compounds are further divided by the hybridisation state of the carbon atom bearing the halogen:

  • Compounds with an \(sp^3\text{ C–X}\) bond:
    • Alkyl halides (haloalkanes, \(\mathrm{R–X}\)): General formula \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+1}\mathrm{X}\). Classified as primary (\(1°\)), secondary (\(2°\)), or tertiary (\(3°\)) according to whether the carbon bearing \(\mathrm{X}\) is attached to one (or zero, for methyl), two, or three other carbon atoms.
    • Allylic halides: The halogen is bonded to an \(sp^3\) carbon adjacent to a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (for example, \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH–CH_2Br}\)).
    • Benzylic halides: The halogen is bonded to an \(sp^3\) carbon directly attached to an aromatic ring (for example, \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2Cl}\), which is \(1°\); replacing benzylic hydrogens with alkyl groups gives \(2°\) or \(3°\) benzylic halides).
  • Compounds with an \(sp^2\text{ C–X}\) bond:
    • Vinylic halides: The halogen is bonded directly to an \(sp^2\) carbon of a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (for example, \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH–Cl}\)).
    • Aryl halides (haloarenes): The halogen is bonded directly to an \(sp^2\) carbon of an aromatic ring (for example, \(\mathrm{C_6H_5Br}\)).

2. Geminal vs. Vicinal Dihalides and Nomenclature Rules

Dihaloalkanes carrying identical halogens are classified into two important common-name categories:

  • Geminal dihalides (gem-dihalides): Both halogen atoms are attached to the same carbon atom. In common nomenclature they are called alkylidene halides (for example, \(\mathrm{CH_3CHCl_2}\) is ethylidene chloride; IUPAC: 1,1-dichloroethane).
  • Vicinal dihalides (vic-dihalides): The two halogen atoms are attached to adjacent carbon atoms. In common nomenclature they are called alkylene dihalides (for example, \(\mathrm{ClCH_2CH_2Cl}\) is ethylene dichloride; IUPAC: 1,2-dichloroethane).

In the IUPAC system, halogens are treated as halo prefixes (fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-) numbered to give the lowest locant set, with multiple substituents listed alphabetically. For disubstituted benzenes, common names use ortho (\(o\)-), meta (\(m\)-), and para (\(p\)-), whereas IUPAC names use 1,2-, 1,3-, and 1,4-.

Nature of the C–X Bond and Physical Properties

1. Polarity, Bond Length, Bond Enthalpy, and Dipole Moment

Because halogens are more electronegative than carbon, the \(\mathrm{C–X}\) bond is polarised as \(\mathrm{C^{\delta+}–X^{\delta-}}\). Moving down Group 17 from fluorine to iodine, atomic radius increases steadily, causing the \(\mathrm{C–X}\) bond length to increase and the bond dissociation enthalpy to decrease:

\[\text{Bond Length: } \mathrm{C–F} < \mathrm{C–Cl} < \mathrm{C–Br} < \mathrm{C–I}\] \[\text{Bond Enthalpy: } \mathrm{C–F} > \mathrm{C–Cl} > \mathrm{C–Br} > \mathrm{C–I}\]

NEET Exam Alert — Dipole Moment Anomaly: Although fluorine is the most electronegative halogen, the dipole moment of chloromethane (\(\mathrm{CH_3Cl},\, 1.860\,\mathrm{D}\)) is slightly higher than that of fluoromethane (\(\mathrm{CH_3F},\, 1.847\,\mathrm{D}\)). Since dipole moment \(\mu = q \times d\), the much longer \(\mathrm{C–Cl}\) bond distance (\(178\,\mathrm{pm}\) vs. \(139\,\mathrm{pm}\)) outweighs the smaller partial charge separation. Thus, for halomethanes:

\[\text{Dipole Moment: } \mathrm{CH_3Cl} > \mathrm{CH_3F} > \mathrm{CH_3Br} > \mathrm{CH_3I}\]

2. Boiling Point, Melting Point, Density, and Solubility

  • Boiling points: Haloalkanes have higher boiling points than hydrocarbons of comparable molar mass due to stronger dipole–dipole and van der Waals attractions. For the same alkyl group \(\mathrm{R}\), boiling point increases with halogen size and electron count: \(\mathrm{R–I} > \mathrm{R–Br} > \mathrm{R–Cl} > \mathrm{R–F}\). Among isomeric haloalkanes, branching makes the molecule more compact, reducing surface area and van der Waals forces; hence boiling point decreases with branching (for example, 1-bromobutane \(>\) 2-bromobutane \(>\) 2-bromo-2-methylpropane).
  • Melting points of dihalobenzenes: Isomeric o-, m-, and p-dichlorobenzenes have nearly identical boiling points, but the para-isomer has a substantially higher melting point (\(323\,\mathrm{K}\) vs. \(256\,\mathrm{K}\) for ortho and \(249\,\mathrm{K}\) for meta). Its high molecular symmetry allows tighter packing in the crystal lattice.
  • Density: Bromo, iodo, and polychloro derivatives of hydrocarbons are denser than water. Density increases with the number of carbon atoms, number of halogen atoms, and atomic mass of the halogen.
  • Solubility: Haloalkanes are only sparingly soluble in water. Dissolving them requires energy to break strong hydrogen bonds between water molecules, and the new dipole–dipole attractions formed between haloalkanes and water are weaker than those broken hydrogen bonds. Conversely, they dissolve readily in organic solvents because solute–solvent intermolecular forces match those in the pure components.

Methods of Preparation

1. Preparing Haloalkanes

  • From Alcohols (\(\mathrm{R–OH}\)):
    • Thionyl chloride (\(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\)): \(\mathrm{R–OH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow R–Cl + SO_2\uparrow + HCl\uparrow}\). This is the preferred laboratory route because both byproducts are gases that escape, leaving pure alkyl chloride.
    • Halogen acids (\(\mathrm{HX}\)): Primary and secondary alcohols require anhydrous \(\mathrm{ZnCl_2}\) catalyst with \(\mathrm{HCl}\) (Lucas reagent), whereas \(3°\) alcohols react simply upon shaking with concentrated \(\mathrm{HCl}\) at room temperature. Alcohol reactivity follows \(3° > 2° > 1°\). Alkyl bromides are prepared using constant-boiling \(\mathrm{HBr}\) (\(48\%\)) or \(\mathrm{NaBr/H_2SO_4}\).
    • Phosphorus halides: \(\mathrm{3R–OH + PX_3 \rightarrow 3R–X + H_3PO_3}\) (\(\mathrm{X = Cl, Br}\)) or \(\mathrm{R–OH + PCl_5 \rightarrow R–Cl + POCl_3 + HCl}\). \(\mathrm{PBr_3}\) and \(\mathrm{PI_3}\) are usually generated in situ using red phosphorus with \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) or \(\mathrm{I_2}\).
    Why \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) Is Avoided with \(\mathrm{KI}\), and Why Phenols Fail

    1. When preparing alkyl iodides from alcohols and \(\mathrm{KI}\), concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) must not be used because it oxidises the generated \(\mathrm{HI}\) to \(\mathrm{I_2}\). Instead, \(95\%\) orthophosphoric acid (\(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)) is used.

    2. These alcohol-substitution methods do not convert phenols to aryl halides because the \(\mathrm{C–O}\) bond in phenols acquires partial double-bond character through resonance, making it too strong to cleave.

  • From Hydrocarbons:
    • Free-radical halogenation of alkanes: Chlorination or bromination under UV light or heat yields a mixture of isomeric mono- and polyhaloalkanes, limiting single-isomer yield unless all hydrogens in the alkane are equivalent (such as neopentane or cyclopentane).
    • Electrophilic addition of \(\mathrm{HX}\) to alkenes: Follows Markovnikov's rule (hydrogen adds to the less substituted alkene carbon; halide adds to the more substituted carbon via the more stable carbocation). In the presence of peroxides, \(\mathrm{HBr}\) (only \(\mathrm{HBr}\), not \(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{HI}\)) undergoes free-radical anti-Markovnikov addition.
    • Addition of \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\): Converts an alkene to a colourless vic-dibromide, discharging the reddish-brown colour of bromine (standard laboratory test for unsaturation).
  • Halogen Exchange Reactions:
    • Finkelstein reaction (for alkyl iodides): \(\mathrm{R–X + NaI \xrightarrow{\text{dry acetone}} R–I + NaX\downarrow}\) (\(\mathrm{X = Cl, Br}\)). Precipitation of \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) or \(\mathrm{NaBr}\) in dry acetone drives the equilibrium forward by Le Chatelier's principle.
    • Swarts reaction (for alkyl fluorides): Heating an alkyl chloride or bromide with a metallic fluoride such as \(\mathrm{AgF, Hg_2F_2, CoF_2,}\) or \(\mathrm{SbF_3}\) produces \(\mathrm{R–F}\).

2. Preparing Haloarenes

  • Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution: Treating arenes with \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in the dark with a Lewis acid catalyst (\(\mathrm{Fe}\) or anhydrous \(\mathrm{FeCl_3}\)) yields ortho and para haloisomers, separable by their melting-point difference. Iodination is reversible because \(\mathrm{HI}\) reduces the iodoarene back; hence an oxidising agent (\(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) or \(\mathrm{HIO_4}\)) is added to destroy \(\mathrm{HI}\). Direct fluorination is too violent to be practical.
  • Sandmeyer Reaction (from Primary Aromatic Amines): Aniline is diazotised with \(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + HX}\) at \(273–278\,\mathrm{K}\) to give a benzene diazonium halide (\(\mathrm{ArN_2^+X^-}\)). Treating this cold solution with \(\mathrm{Cu_2Cl_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Cu_2Br_2}\) replaces the diazonium group with \(-\mathrm{Cl}\) or \(-\mathrm{Br}\) and evolves \(\mathrm{N_2}\). For aryl iodides, shaking the diazonium salt with aqueous \(\mathrm{KI}\) suffices without a cuprous catalyst.

Reactions of Haloalkanes and Stereochemistry

1. Nucleophilic Substitution and Ambident Nucleophiles

In nucleophilic substitution, an electron-rich nucleophile (\(\mathrm{Nu^-}\) or \(\mathrm{Nu:}\)) attacks the electron-deficient \(\alpha\)-carbon of \(\mathrm{R–X}\), displacing the halide leaving group (\(\mathrm{X^-}\)). Certain nucleophiles possess two donor atoms and are called ambident nucleophiles:

  • Cyanide (\(\mathrm{^-C\equiv N:}\)): Ionic \(\mathrm{KCN}\) provides free \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) ions that attack primarily through carbon (forming a stronger \(\mathrm{C–C}\) bond) to yield alkyl cyanides (nitriles, \(\mathrm{R–CN}\)). Covalent \(\mathrm{AgCN}\) blocks carbon-site attack, so the nitrogen lone pair attacks to give alkyl isocyanides (isonitriles, \(\mathrm{R–NC}\)) as the major product.
  • Nitrite (\(\mathrm{^-O–N=O}\)): Ionic \(\mathrm{KNO_2}\) attacks predominantly through the negatively charged oxygen to form alkyl nitrites (\(\mathrm{R–O–N=O}\)), whereas covalent \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\) attacks through nitrogen to yield nitroalkanes (\(\mathrm{R–NO_2}\)).

2. Comparing \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) and \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) Mechanisms

Feature \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) (Bimolecular) \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) (Unimolecular)
Kinetics & Rate Law Second order: \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{RX}][\mathrm{Nu^-}]\) First order: \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{RX}]\)
Steps & Transition Single concerted step via a pentacoordinate transition state; no intermediate. Two steps: slow reversible \(\mathrm{C–X}\) cleavage to a planar \(sp^2\) carbocation, then fast \(\mathrm{Nu^-}\) attack.
Controlling Factor Steric hindrance around the \(\alpha\)-carbon. Stability of the carbocation intermediate.
Substrate Reactivity \(\mathrm{CH_3X} > 1° > 2° > 3°\) \(3° > 2° > 1° > \mathrm{CH_3X}\); allylic & benzylic halides are also very fast (resonance).
Stereochemistry \(100\%\) inversion of configuration (Walden inversion) via backside attack. Racemisation (50:50 mixture of retention and inversion) due to planar carbocation.
Favoured Solvent Polar aprotic solvents (acetone, DMSO). Polar protic solvents (\(\mathrm{H_2O, ROH, CH_3COOH}\)).

For a given alkyl group, leaving-group ability governs reactivity in both \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) and \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) mechanisms: \(\mathrm{R–I} > \mathrm{R–Br} > \mathrm{R–Cl} \gg \mathrm{R–F}\) because larger iodide ions form weaker \(\mathrm{C–I}\) bonds and depart more readily.

3. Key Stereochemical Principles

  • Optical activity: Compounds that rotate the plane of plane-polarised light (measured by a polarimeter) are optically active. Clockwise rotation is dextrorotatory (\(d\) or \(+\)); anticlockwise rotation is laevorotatory (\(l\) or \(-\)).
  • Chirality and Enantiomers: A carbon bonded to four different groups is an asymmetric carbon (stereocentre). Molecules non-superimposable on their mirror images are chiral and optically active; those superimposable on their mirror images are achiral and optically inactive. A pair of non-superimposable mirror-image stereoisomers are called enantiomers. Enantiomers share identical melting points, boiling points, and refractive indices, differing only in the sign of optical rotation.
  • Racemic mixture: An equimolar (50:50) mixture of \((+)\) and \((-)\) enantiomers, denoted \(dl\) or \((\pm)\), has zero net optical rotation due to external compensation.
  • Retention of configuration: If a reaction does not break any bond directly attached to the stereocentre, the spatial arrangement around that stereocentre is preserved (even if the sign of optical rotation happens to change).

4. Elimination (\(\beta\)-Elimination) vs. Substitution

Heating a haloalkane containing a \(\beta\)-hydrogen with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) (or \(\mathrm{C_2H_5O^-Na^+}\)) removes a \(\beta\)-\(\mathrm{H}\) and the \(\alpha\)-\(\mathrm{X}\) to form an alkene. According to Zaitsev's (Saytzeff's) rule, when multiple alkenes are possible, the major product is the more substituted (more stable) alkene—for example, 2-bromopentane gives pent-2-ene (\(81\%\)) over pent-1-ene (\(19\%\)).

In contrast, aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\) favours nucleophilic substitution to yield alcohols. Furthermore, bulky bases (such as tert-butoxide, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CO^-}\)), high temperatures, and \(3°\) substrates strongly favour elimination over substitution.

5. Reactions with Metals

  • Grignard Reagents (\(\mathrm{RMgX}\)): Treating \(\mathrm{R–X}\) with magnesium turnings in dry ether yields alkyl magnesium halide (\(\mathrm{R^{\delta-}–Mg^{\delta+}X}\)). Because the \(\mathrm{C–Mg}\) bond is strongly polar and carbanion-like, Grignard reagents react violently with any proton source (water, alcohols, amines) to produce alkanes: \(\mathrm{RMgX + H_2O \rightarrow RH + Mg(OH)X}\). Strict anhydrous conditions are therefore essential.
  • Wurtz Reaction: Alkyl halides couple with sodium metal in dry ether to form symmetrical alkanes with double the carbon count: \(\mathrm{2R–X + 2Na \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} R–R + 2NaX}\).

Reactions of Haloarenes

1. Low Reactivity Towards Nucleophilic Substitution

Aryl halides are far less reactive than alkyl halides in \(\mathrm{S_N}\) reactions for four main reasons:

  1. Resonance effect: Lone pairs on the halogen delocalise into the aromatic \(\pi\)-system, imparting partial double-bond character to the \(\mathrm{C–X}\) bond and making it harder to cleave.
  2. Carbon hybridisation: The aryl carbon is \(sp^2\)-hybridised (\(33.3\%\, s\)-character, more electronegative) rather than \(sp^3\) (\(25\%\, s\)-character). It holds the \(\mathrm{C–X}\) bonding electrons more tightly, resulting in a shorter, stronger bond (\(\mathrm{C–Cl}\) is \(169\,\mathrm{pm}\) in chlorobenzene vs. \(177\,\mathrm{pm}\) in chloroalkanes).
  3. Instability of phenyl cation: Self-ionisation would produce an \(sp\)-like phenyl cation unstabilised by resonance, ruling out \(\mathrm{S_N1}\).
  4. Electronic repulsion: The electron-rich \(\pi\)-cloud of the benzene ring repels approaching nucleophiles.

Consequently, converting chlorobenzene to phenol requires drastic conditions (\(623\,\mathrm{K}, 300\,\mathrm{atm}\) with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), followed by \(\mathrm{H^+}\)). However, introducing electron-withdrawing \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) groups at the ortho and para positions stabilises the negatively charged Meisenheimer intermediate by resonance, lowering the required temperature dramatically (\(443\,\mathrm{K}\) for 4-nitrochlorobenzene; \(368\,\mathrm{K}\) for 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene; warm water alone for 2,4,6-trinitrochlorobenzene). A \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) group at the meta position cannot delocalise the carbanion charge and shows negligible activation.

2. Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution and Metal Coupling

  • Orientation vs. Reactivity: In halobenzenes, the halogen is ortho, para-directing because its \(+R\) resonance effect increases electron density at the o- and p-positions (stabilising the arenium carbocation). However, its strong \(-I\) inductive effect outweighs resonance overall, making halogens ring-deactivating compared to benzene. Halobenzenes undergo halogenation, nitration, sulphonation, and Friedel–Crafts alkylation/acylation (with para as the major isomer) under slightly forcing conditions.
  • Wurtz–Fittig and Fittig Reactions: Heating an aryl halide with an alkyl halide and \(\mathrm{Na}\) in dry ether gives an alkylarene (Wurtz–Fittig reaction). Heating two equivalents of aryl halide with \(\mathrm{Na}\) in dry ether yields biphenyl/diphenyl (Fittig reaction).

Worked Examples

Example 1: Classifying Halides and Identifying Stereocentres

Problem: Consider the hypothetical haloalkene \(\mathrm{CH_3–CH=CH–CH(Br)–CH_2CH_3}\). Assign its IUPAC name, classify the \(\mathrm{C–Br}\) bond type, and determine whether the molecule is chiral.

Solution:

  1. Numbering the six-carbon chain to give the double bond the lowest locant gives hex-2-ene with bromo at C-4: 4-bromohex-2-ene.
  2. The bromine-bearing carbon (C-4) is \(sp^3\)-hybridised and directly adjacent to the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (C-2=C-3), and it is bonded to two other carbons (C-3 and C-5). Thus, it is a secondary (\(2°\)) allylic halide.
  3. At C-4, the four attached substituents are \(-\mathrm{H}\), \(-\mathrm{Br}\), \(-\mathrm{CH_2CH_3}\), and \(-\mathrm{CH=CHCH_3}\). Since all four groups are different, C-4 is an asymmetric carbon (stereocentre), making the molecule chiral and optically active.
Example 2: Comparing \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) and \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) Reaction Rates

Problem: Arrange the following three isomeric bromides in order of increasing reactivity towards (a) \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) displacement with \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) in acetone, and (b) \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) solvolysis in aqueous ethanol: \(\text{I: }\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_3Br}\), \(\text{II: }\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH_2Br}\), \(\text{III: }\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CBr}\).

Solution:

  • (a) \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) reactivity: Governed by steric hindrance at the \(\alpha\)-carbon. Compound III is \(3°\) (severely hindered, essentially unreactive in \(\mathrm{S_N2}\)). Both I and II are \(1°\), but II has a \(\beta\)-methyl branch (\(\text{–CH(CH}_3)_2\)) that crowds the backside trajectory more than the linear chain in I. Thus, \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) rate increases as: \(\text{III} < \text{II} < \text{I}\).
  • (b) \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) reactivity: Governed by carbocation stability. Compound III forms a highly stable \(3°\) carbocation, whereas II forms a \(1°\) carbocation with greater \(+I\) electron donation from the isopropyl group than the \(n\)-propyl group in I. Thus, \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) rate increases as: \(\text{I} < \text{II} < \text{III}\).
Example 3: Predicting Major Products in Elimination and Ambident Substitution

Problem: Predict the major organic product when 2-chloro-2-methylbutane is treated with: (a) alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) upon heating, and (b) \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\) in aqueous ethanol (assuming substitution pathway for comparison).

Solution:

  • (a) Alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\), heat: Dehydrohalogenation (\(\beta\)-elimination) occurs. Two \(\beta\)-carbons bear hydrogens: C-1 (giving 2-methylbut-1-ene, disubstituted alkene) and C-3 (giving 2-methylbut-2-ene, trisubstituted alkene). By Zaitsev's rule, the more substituted alkene predominates: 2-methylbut-2-ene.
  • (b) \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\): Nitrite is an ambident nucleophile. Because \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\) is predominantly covalent, nitrogen donates its lone pair to form the nitroalkane: 2-methyl-2-nitrobutane (unlike \(\mathrm{KNO_2}\), which yields the alkyl nitrite).
Example 4: Evaluating Haloarene Reactivity

Problem: Explain why 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene hydrolyses smoothly with aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at \(368\,\mathrm{K}\), whereas 1-chloro-3,5-dinitrobenzene remains largely unreactive under the same conditions.

Solution: Nucleophilic aromatic substitution (\(\mathrm{S_NAr}\)) proceeds via a negatively charged cyclohexadienyl (Meisenheimer) intermediate where the negative charge is delocalised specifically onto the carbons ortho and para to the leaving chlorine. In 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene, both \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) groups sit at ortho and para positions, directly accepting and stabilising this negative charge by resonance. In 1-chloro-3,5-dinitrobenzene, both \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) groups are meta to chlorine, so none of the resonance structures place the negative charge on the nitro-bearing carbons; hence, the intermediate is not resonance-stabilised by \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\).

NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: These are original practice questions modeled on the NEET UG examination pattern.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following compounds possesses the highest dipole moment?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{CH_3Br}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) is symmetrical (tetrahedral) with zero net dipole moment. Among halomethanes, \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\) (\(1.860\,\mathrm{D}\)) exceeds \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\) (\(1.847\,\mathrm{D}\)) because the substantially longer \(\mathrm{C–Cl}\) bond length compensates for chlorine's lower electronegativity compared to fluorine.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

An optically active alkyl halide \(\mathrm{(R)-2-bromobutane}\) undergoes hydrolysis in aqueous acetone (polar protic solvent) to yield butan-2-ol. The stereochemical outcome of the product mixture is best described as:

  • (A) \(100\%\) retention of configuration
  • (B) \(100\%\) inversion of configuration
  • (C) Racemisation forming an optically inactive \((\pm)\)-mixture
  • (D) Formation of a meso compound
Answer: (C)

Solution: Hydrolysis of a \(2°\) alkyl halide in a polar protic solvent with a weak nucleophile (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) proceeds predominantly via \(\mathrm{S_N1}\). The planar \(sp^2\) carbocation intermediate is attacked equally from either face, leading to racemisation and an optically inactive \((\pm)\)-butan-2-ol mixture.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which reagent converts an alcohol to an alkyl chloride with the highest purity because all byproducts escape as gases?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{PCl_3}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\)
  • (D) Conc. \(\mathrm{HCl}\) / anhydrous \(\mathrm{ZnCl_2}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: \(\mathrm{R–OH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow R–Cl + SO_2(g) + HCl(g)}\). Because both \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\) are gases, they escape from the reaction mixture, leaving pure \(\mathrm{R–Cl}\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following halides undergoes \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) reaction at the fastest rate?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2Cl}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH(C_6H_5)Br}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2Br}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) rate depends on carbocation stability. \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH(C_6H_5)Br}\) generates a diphenylmethyl carbocation stabilised by resonance across two phenyl rings, making it more stable than the benzyl carbocation from \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2Br}\) or the \(1°\) carbocation from \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2Cl}\). Chlorobenzene does not undergo \(\mathrm{S_N1}\).

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): p-Dichlorobenzene has a higher melting point than o- and m-dichlorobenzene.
Reason (R): p-Dichlorobenzene has a higher dipole moment than o-dichlorobenzene.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (C)

Solution: Assertion (A) is true because the symmetrical para-isomer packs more efficiently into a crystal lattice. Reason (R) is false because p-dichlorobenzene has zero net dipole moment (bond dipoles cancel), whereas o-dichlorobenzene has a nonzero dipole moment.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Identify the major product when 1-bromopropane is heated with \(\mathrm{NaI}\) in dry acetone:

  • (A) 1-Iodopropane
  • (B) Propene
  • (C) 2-Iodopropane
  • (D) Hexane
Answer: (A)

Solution: This is the Finkelstein halogen-exchange reaction (\(\mathrm{S_N2}\)): \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2Br + NaI \xrightarrow{\text{dry acetone}} CH_3CH_2CH_2I + NaBr\downarrow}\). Precipitation of \(\mathrm{NaBr}\) in dry acetone drives the reaction to completion.

Practice Question 7 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Statement I: Chlorobenzene is ortho, para-directing and activating in electrophilic aromatic substitution.
Statement II: In chlorobenzene, the \(-I\) inductive effect of chlorine is stronger than its \(+R\) resonance effect.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (D)

Solution: Statement I is incorrect because chlorine is ortho, para-directing yet deactivating. Statement II is correct: chlorine's stronger \(-I\) effect withdraws electron density overall (controlling reactivity/deactivation), while its \(+R\) effect selectively stabilises ortho and para arenium intermediates (controlling orientation).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Why must Grignard reagents be prepared and handled strictly under anhydrous conditions?

  • (A) Magnesium metal reacts with water to form \(\mathrm{MgO}\) precipitate that coats the halide.
  • (B) Grignard reagents react with water to form hydrocarbons (\(\mathrm{RH}\)).
  • (C) Water oxidises the halogen atom to \(\mathrm{X_2}\).
  • (D) Dry ether acts as a proton donor in the coupling step.
Answer: (B)

Solution: In \(\mathrm{R^{\delta-}–Mg^{\delta+}X}\), the alkyl group behaves like a strong base/carbanion and abstracts a proton from even weak acids such as water: \(\mathrm{RMgX + H_2O \rightarrow RH + Mg(OH)X}\).

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When chloroform (\(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\)) is exposed to air and sunlight, it slowly oxidises to an extremely poisonous gas. The formula of this toxic gas is:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{CO}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{COCl_2}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Air oxidation of chloroform in light yields carbonyl chloride (phosgene, \(\mathrm{COCl_2}\)) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\): \(\mathrm{2CHCl_3 + O_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2COCl_2 + 2HCl}\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Treating bromobenzene with ethyl bromide and sodium metal in dry ether yields ethylbenzene as the cross-coupled product. This reaction is known as:

  • (A) Wurtz reaction
  • (B) Fittig reaction
  • (C) Wurtz–Fittig reaction
  • (D) Sandmeyer reaction
Answer: (C)

Solution: Coupling an aryl halide (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5Br}\)) with an alkyl halide (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5Br}\)) using \(\mathrm{Na}\) in dry ether is the Wurtz–Fittig reaction (whereas two aryl halides give biphenyl in the Fittig reaction, and two alkyl halides couple in the Wurtz reaction).

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Mistakes to Avoid
  • Aqueous vs. Alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\): Aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\) gives substitution (alcohol); alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) gives \(\beta\)-elimination (alkene).
  • \(\mathrm{KCN}\) vs. \(\mathrm{AgCN}\): \(\mathrm{KCN}\) yields nitriles (\(\mathrm{RCN}\)); \(\mathrm{AgCN}\) yields isocyanides (\(\mathrm{RNC}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) with \(\mathrm{KI}\): Never pair \(\mathrm{KI}\) with \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) for alcohol-to-iodide conversion; use \(95\%\, \mathrm{H_3PO_4}\).
  • Peroxide Effect Scope: Anti-Markovnikov radical addition occurs only with \(\mathrm{HBr}\) in the presence of peroxides, not with \(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{HI}\).
  • Chlorobenzene Directing vs. Activating: Halogens on benzene are o,p-directing but deactivating (\(-I > +R\)).
  • Dipole Moment Order: \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl} > \mathrm{CH_3F}\), not the reverse.

Concise Final Recap

Haloalkanes contain polar \(sp^3\text{ C–X}\) bonds that undergo \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) (favoured by \(1°\)/methyl substrates, strong nucleophiles, polar aprotic solvents, giving inversion) and \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) (favoured by \(3°\)/allylic/benzylic substrates, polar protic solvents, giving racemisation) substitutions, alongside Zaitsev-governed \(\beta\)-elimination in alcoholic base. Haloarenes carry stronger, resonance-stabilised \(sp^2\text{ C–X}\) bonds that resist nucleophilic attack unless activated by ortho/para \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) groups, while undergoing o,p-directed electrophilic substitution at reduced rates. Key named transformations—Finkelstein, Swarts, Sandmeyer, Wurtz, Wurtz–Fittig, and Fittig—alongside polyhalogen hazards (phosgene from \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\), ozone depletion by \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\)/Freons, bioaccumulation of DDT) form the core high-yield pillars for NEET UG.
