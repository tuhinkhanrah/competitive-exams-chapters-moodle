NEET UG Chemistry Macromolecules & Synthetic Polymers

Polymers: Structure, Mechanisms, and Applications

A comprehensive, exam-oriented study guide covering monomer-polymer relationships, polymerisation pathways, intermolecular forces, elastomers, and biodegradable materials.

Learning Objectives and Syllabus Scope

Polymers form the structural backbone of biological systems as well as modern materials science—ranging from plastics, synthetic fibres, and elastomers to paints and biomedical implants. For NEET UG, questions frequently test exact monomer identification, polymerisation mechanisms, catalyst systems, relative intermolecular forces, and structural differences between closely related polymers.

  • Define monomers, polymers, macromolecules, degree of polymerisation, and monomer functionality.
  • Classify polymers systematically by origin, chain architecture, intermolecular forces, and mode of synthesis.
  • Trace the free radical chain-growth mechanism and contrast it with step-growth polycondensation.
  • Memorise and apply the monomers, catalysts, structures, and uses of key commercial addition and condensation polymers.
  • Explain the elastic behaviour of natural and synthetic rubbers and the chemical basis of vulcanisation.
  • Identify biodegradable synthetic polymers and their repeating linkages.

Core Foundations: Monomers, Macromolecules, and Functionality

A polymer (from Greek poly meaning many and mer meaning unit or part) is a very large molecule with a high molecular mass typically in the range of \(10^{3}\) to \(10^{7}\,\mathrm{u}\). Because of their enormous size, polymers are also called macromolecules. They are constructed by linking hundreds or thousands of small, reactive building blocks known as monomers through covalent bonds. The chemical reaction that converts monomers into a polymer is termed polymerisation.

\[\text{Molar Mass of Polymer} \approx n \times (\text{Molar Mass of Repeating Structural Unit})\]

Here, \(n\) is the degree of polymerisation, representing the number of repeating structural units in a single polymer chain. During synthesis, monomer availability varies locally in the reaction vessel, so a polymer sample always contains chains of different lengths. Consequently, the molecular mass of a polymer is always expressed as an average rather than a single fixed value.

Role of Monomer Functionality

The functionality of a monomer is the number of reactive bonding sites (such as double bonds or functional groups like \(-\mathrm{OH}\), \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), or \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\)) it possesses for forming covalent linkages with other molecules:

  • Bifunctional monomers (functionality = 2): Each molecule can bond at two sites, forming long, continuous linear chains (for example, ethene or ethylene glycol with terephthalic acid).
  • Trifunctional or polyfunctional monomers (functionality \(\ge 3\)): These create branch points and three-dimensional covalent bridges between chains, yielding cross-linked network polymers (for example, phenol reacting with formaldehyde at its two ortho and one para positions, or melamine with three \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) groups).

Classification of Polymers

Polymers are classified along four independent criteria. Understanding all four frameworks helps resolve structure–property questions rapidly.

1. Classification Based on Source

  • Natural polymers: Synthesised by plants and animals. Examples include proteins, starch, cellulose, natural rubber, and natural resins.
  • Semi-synthetic polymers: Chemically modified natural polymers designed to improve properties such as lustre or tensile strength. Examples include cellulose acetate (rayon) and cellulose nitrate.
  • Synthetic polymers: Completely man-made macromolecules produced industrially. Examples include polythene, Nylon 6,6, and Buna-S.

2. Classification Based on Chain Structure

  • Linear polymers: Long, straight chains packed closely together, resulting in high density, high melting point, and high tensile strength (for example, high-density polythene and polyvinyl chloride).
  • Branched-chain polymers: Linear backbones carrying irregular side chains that prevent close packing, lowering density and melting point (for example, low-density polythene).
  • Cross-linked (network) polymers: Bifunctional and trifunctional monomers form strong covalent bridges between adjacent chains, creating a rigid, infusible 3D network (for example, Bakelite and melamine-formaldehyde resin).

3. Classification Based on Intermolecular Forces

Mechanical properties such as elasticity, tensile strength, and thermal behaviour depend directly on the strength of intermolecular attractions between polymer chains:

Category Intermolecular Forces Key Properties Representative Examples
Elastomers Weakest van der Waals forces with sparse cross-links Highly elastic; chains stretch under tension and retract when released Natural rubber, Buna-S, Buna-N, Neoprene
Thermoplastics Intermediate forces (between elastomers and fibres); no cross-links Linear or slightly branched; soften repeatedly on heating and harden on cooling Polythene, Polystyrene, PVC, Teflon
Fibres Strong hydrogen bonding or dipole–dipole interactions High tensile strength, high modulus, sharp melting point, crystalline packing Nylon 6,6, Nylon 6, Terylene (Dacron)
Thermosetting Polymers Extensive covalent cross-linking Hard, infusible mass; cannot be softened, remoulded, or reused upon heating Bakelite, Urea-formaldehyde resin, Melamine-formaldehyde
NEET High-Yield Trend: Intermolecular Force Order

When comparing polymers across categories, intermolecular attraction increases in the order:
Elastomers < Thermoplastics < Fibres (for example: Buna-S < Polythene < Nylon 6,6).

4. Classification by Composition: Homopolymers vs. Copolymers

  • Homopolymers: Formed from a single type of monomeric species (for example, polythene from ethene, polystyrene from styrene, or Nylon 6 from caprolactam).
  • Copolymers: Formed by polymerising two or more different monomeric species together, yielding chains containing units of each monomer (for example, Buna-S from 1,3-butadiene and styrene, or Nylon 6,6 from hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid).

Addition (Chain-Growth) Polymerisation

In addition polymerisation (also called chain-growth polymerisation), molecules of the same or different unsaturated monomers (alkenes, alkadienes, and their derivatives) add sequentially without eliminating any small byproduct molecules. The empirical formula of the polymer is identical to that of the monomer (for homopolymers).

Free Radical Mechanism

Free radical addition is the most common pathway for alkenes. It requires a small amount of a free-radical generating initiator such as benzoyl peroxide, acetyl peroxide, or tert-butyl peroxide, together with heat or light. Consider the polymerisation of ethene using benzoyl peroxide:

  1. Chain Initiation: Homolytic cleavage of the weak \(\mathrm{O-O}\) peroxide bond produces benzoyloxy radicals, which rapidly lose \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to form phenyl radicals (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5^\bullet}\)). The phenyl radical adds to the \(\pi\)-bond of ethene, generating a new carbon-centred radical: \[ \mathrm{C_6H_5-C(=O)-O-O-C(=O)-C_6H_5} \longrightarrow 2\,\mathrm{C_6H_5-C(=O)-O^\bullet} \longrightarrow 2\,\mathrm{C_6H_5^\bullet} + 2\,\mathrm{CO_2} \] \[ \mathrm{C_6H_5^\bullet} + \mathrm{CH_2=CH_2} \longrightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_5-CH_2-\dot{C}H_2} \]
  2. Chain Propagation: The newly formed radical attacks another ethene molecule, extending the chain by two carbons while preserving the active radical site at the growing end: \[ \mathrm{C_6H_5-CH_2-\dot{C}H_2} + n\,\mathrm{CH_2=CH_2} \longrightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_5-(CH_2-CH_2)_n-CH_2-\dot{C}H_2} \]
  3. Chain Termination: Two growing macroradicals collide and couple (or disproportionate) to form a neutral, stable polymer molecule, ending chain growth: \[ 2\,\mathrm{C_6H_5-(CH_2-CH_2)_n-CH_2-\dot{C}H_2} \longrightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_5-(CH_2-CH_2)_n-CH_2-CH_2-CH_2-CH_2-(CH_2-CH_2)_n-C_6H_5} \]

Important Addition Polymers

Two industrial forms of polythene illustrate how reaction conditions control polymer architecture:

  • Low-Density Polythene (LDPE): Prepared from ethene at high pressure (\(1000\text{–}2000\,\mathrm{atm}\)) and \(350\text{–}570\,\mathrm{K}\) with traces of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) or a peroxide initiator. Free-radical addition accompanied by intramolecular H-atom abstraction produces a highly branched structure. LDPE is chemically inert, tough, flexible, and an electrical insulator—used in wire insulation, squeeze bottles, toys, and flexible pipes.
  • High-Density Polythene (HDPE): Prepared in a hydrocarbon solvent at mild conditions (\(333\text{–}343\,\mathrm{K}\), \(6\text{–}7\,\mathrm{atm}\)) using the Ziegler–Natta catalyst—a combination of triethylaluminium, \(\mathrm{(C_2H_5)_3Al}\), and titanium tetrachloride, \(\mathrm{TiCl_4}\). It yields unbranched, linear chains that pack closely, giving higher density, greater hardness, and rigidity—used in buckets, dustbins, bottles, and rigid pipes.

Condensation (Step-Growth) Polymerisation

In condensation polymerisation (also termed step-growth polymerisation), bifunctional or trifunctional monomers react stepwise, typically eliminating simple molecules such as \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\), or \(\mathrm{HCl}\). Because each intermediate product retains reactive functional groups at its ends, chains grow through independent condensation steps rather than via an active radical centre.

1. Polyamides (Nylons)

Polyamides contain the repeating amide linkage (\(-\mathrm{CONH}-\)). Strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding between \(\mathrm{C=O}\) and \(\mathrm{N-H}\) groups on neighbouring chains promotes crystalline packing and high tensile strength.

  • Nylon 6,6: Prepared by condensing equimolar hexamethylenediamine (6 carbons) and adipic acid (6 carbons) at \(553\,\mathrm{K}\) under high pressure, with loss of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\): \[ n\,\mathrm{H_2N-(CH_2)_6-NH_2} + n\,\mathrm{HOOC-(CH_2)_4-COOH} \xrightarrow[\text{High pressure}]{553\,\mathrm{K}} \left[-\mathrm{NH-(CH_2)_6-NH-C(=O)-(CH_2)_4-C(=O)}-\right]_n + n\,\mathrm{H_2O} \] Used in textile fibres, sheets, and brush bristles.
  • Nylon 6: Obtained by heating caprolactam (a 7-membered cyclic amide containing 6 carbon atoms) with water at \(533\text{–}543\,\mathrm{K}\): \[ n\,\text{Caprolactam} \xrightarrow[\mathrm{H_2O}]{533\text{–}543\,\mathrm{K}} \left[-\mathrm{C(=O)-(CH_2)_5-NH}-\right]_n \] Used in tyre cords, fabrics, and ropes.

2. Polyesters

Polyesters contain the repeating ester linkage (\(-\mathrm{COO}-\)), formed by condensing dicarboxylic acids with diols.

  • Terylene (Dacron): Prepared by heating ethylene glycol (ethane-1,2-diol) and terephthalic acid (benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid) at \(420\text{–}460\,\mathrm{K}\) in the presence of a zinc acetate–antimony trioxide catalyst. Dacron is crease-resistant and is blended with cotton/wool or used as glass-reinforcing material in safety helmets.
  • Glyptal: Prepared from ethylene glycol and phthalic acid (benzene-1,2-dicarboxylic acid). Used in the manufacture of paints and lacquers.

3. Cross-Linked Condensation Resins

  • Phenol–Formaldehyde Polymers (Novolac and Bakelite): Phenol reacts with formaldehyde in the presence of an acid or base catalyst to form o- and/or p-hydroxymethylphenol derivatives. Further condensation yields a linear polymer called Novolac (used in paints). Heating Novolac with excess formaldehyde introduces \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) cross-links between rings, producing an infusible thermoset called Bakelite (used in electrical switches, utensil handles, combs, and phonograph records).
  • Melamine–Formaldehyde Polymer: Condensation of melamine (2,4,6-triamino-1,3,5-triazine) with formaldehyde yields a hard, cross-linked thermoset used to manufacture unbreakable crockery.
  • Urea–Formaldehyde Resin: Formed from urea and formaldehyde, with repeating unit \(-\mathrm{[NH-CO-NH-CH_2]}_n-\); used for unbreakable cups and laminated sheets.

Elastomers: Natural Rubber, Vulcanisation, and Synthetic Rubbers

Natural Rubber

Natural rubber is obtained from rubber latex (a colloidal dispersion of rubber in water). Chemically, it is a linear homopolymer of isoprene (2-methylbuta-1,3-diene) with all double bonds in the cis configuration, known as cis-1,4-polyisoprene. Because the chains are coiled like springs and held together only by weak van der Waals forces, natural rubber stretches easily and retracts when the force is removed.

Vulcanisation of Rubber

Raw natural rubber has serious limitations: it becomes soft and sticky above \(335\,\mathrm{K}\), brittle below \(283\,\mathrm{K}\), absorbs water, dissolves in non-polar solvents, and is easily attacked by oxidising agents. To overcome these drawbacks, raw rubber is heated with sulphur and an appropriate additive at \(373\text{–}415\,\mathrm{K}\)—a process called vulcanisation. Sulphur forms covalent cross-links at the reactive allylic/double-bond sites between adjacent polyisoprene chains, stiffening the elastomer and preserving elasticity over a wide temperature range. Tyre rubber typically uses about \(5\%\) sulphur as the cross-linking agent.

Biodegradable Polymers

Most conventional synthetic plastics resist environmental breakdown, creating persistent solid-waste pollution. To address this, chemists designed biodegradable synthetic polymers containing hydrolysable functional groups (such as ester or amide linkages) similar to those in natural biopolymers, allowing microbial enzymes to degrade them.

  • PHBV (Poly-\(\beta\)-hydroxybutyrate-co-\(\beta\)-hydroxyvalerate): An aliphatic polyester copolymer formed from 3-hydroxybutanoic acid and 3-hydroxypentanoic acid. It undergoes bacterial degradation and is used in speciality packaging, orthopaedic devices, and controlled drug-release capsules.
  • Nylon 2–nylon 6: An alternating biodegradable polyamide copolymer of glycine (\(\mathrm{H_2N-CH_2-COOH}\), 2 carbons) and aminocaproic acid (\(\mathrm{H_2N-(CH_2)_5-COOH}\), 6 carbons).

Worked Examples

Calculating Polymer Molar Mass from Degree of Polymerisation

Problem: Suppose a laboratory sample of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) has an assumed average degree of polymerisation \(n = 1500\). Estimate the average molar mass of this PVC sample. (Atomic masses: \(\mathrm{C} = 12\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{H} = 1\,\mathrm{u}\), \(\mathrm{Cl} = 35.5\,\mathrm{u}\)).

Solution:

1. The repeating structural unit of PVC is \(-\mathrm{CH_2-CH(Cl)}-\), which has the formula \(\mathrm{C_2H_3Cl}\).

2. Molar mass of one repeating unit:

\[ M_{\text{unit}} = (2 \times 12) + (3 \times 1) + 35.5 = 24 + 3 + 35.5 = 62.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} \]

3. Average molar mass of the polymer:

\[ M_{\text{polymer}} = n \times M_{\text{unit}} = 1500 \times 62.5 = 93{,}750\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} \approx 9.38 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} \]

Identifying Monomers from a Polymer Repeating Unit

Problem: A synthetic fibre has the repeating structural unit \(\left[-\mathrm{O-CH_2-CH_2-O-C(=O)-C_6H_4-C(=O)}-\right]_n\), where \(\mathrm{C_6H_4}\) is a 1,4-disubstituted benzene ring. Identify the monomers, the class of polymer, and the small molecule eliminated during its formation.

Solution:

1. Inspect the linkages in the backbone: the repeating group \(-\mathrm{C(=O)-O}-\) is an ester linkage, so the polymer is a polyester (specifically Terylene/Dacron).

2. Hydrolysing the ester bonds conceptually restores \(-\mathrm{OH}\) to the \(\mathrm{O-CH_2-CH_2-O}\) fragment and \(-\mathrm{OH}\) to the carbonyls:

  • Diol monomer: \(\mathrm{HO-CH_2-CH_2-OH}\) (ethylene glycol or ethane-1,2-diol).
  • Dicarboxylic acid monomer: \(\mathrm{HOOC-C_6H_4-COOH}\) with 1,4-substitution (terephthalic acid).

3. Each esterification step eliminates one molecule of water (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)).

Ranking Polymers by Intermolecular Forces

Problem: Arrange the following three polymers in increasing order of intermolecular forces between their chains: Polystyrene, Nylon 6, and Neoprene.

Solution:

1. Classify each polymer by its mechanical behaviour and functional groups:

  • Neoprene: A synthetic rubber (elastomer) held together by weak van der Waals interactions.
  • Polystyrene: A thermoplastic polymer with intermediate dipole/van der Waals interactions between aromatic rings.
  • Nylon 6: A polyamide fibre possessing strong intermolecular \(\mathrm{N-H\cdots O=C}\) hydrogen bonds.

2. Therefore, the increasing order of intermolecular forces is: Neoprene < Polystyrene < Nylon 6.

Distinguishing Chain-Growth and Step-Growth Pathways

Problem: Suppose a chemist synthesises Polymer X from a single unsaturated monomer \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-CN}\) using a peroxide initiator, and Polymer Y from \(\mathrm{H_2N-CH_2-COOH}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2N-(CH_2)_5-COOH}\) with evolution of steam. Classify X and Y by polymerisation mechanism and biodegradability.

Solution:

1. Polymer X is formed from acrylonitrile via a peroxide-initiated radical addition across the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond with no byproduct. Thus, X is polyacrylonitrile (Orlon), formed by addition (chain-growth) homopolymerisation, and it is non-biodegradable.

2. Polymer Y is formed from glycine and aminocaproic acid with elimination of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). Thus, Y is Nylon 2–nylon 6, formed by condensation (step-growth) copolymerisation, and it is a biodegradable polyamide.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following catalyst combinations is used for the industrial manufacture of high-density polythene (HDPE) from ethene?

  • (A) Benzoyl peroxide at \(1500\,\mathrm{atm}\) and \(450\,\mathrm{K}\)
  • (B) Triethylaluminium and titanium tetrachloride at \(333\text{–}343\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(6\text{–}7\,\mathrm{atm}\)
  • (C) Zinc acetate and antimony trioxide at \(440\,\mathrm{K}\)
  • (D) Traces of dioxygen at \(2000\,\mathrm{atm}\) and \(550\,\mathrm{K}\)

Answer: (B)

Explanation: High-density polythene is produced via coordination polymerisation using the Ziegler–Natta catalyst, \(\mathrm{(C_2H_5)_3Al + TiCl_4}\), in a hydrocarbon solvent at \(333\text{–}343\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(6\text{–}7\,\mathrm{atm}\). Options (A) and (D) describe high-pressure free-radical conditions for LDPE, whereas option (C) is the catalyst for Terylene.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Identify the pair of monomers that undergoes condensation polymerisation to yield the biodegradable copolymer PHBV.

  • (A) Glycine and 6-aminohexanoic acid
  • (B) 3-Hydroxybutanoic acid and 3-hydroxypentanoic acid
  • (C) 2-Hydroxypropanoic acid and ethanoic acid
  • (D) Ethylene glycol and phthalic acid

Answer: (B)

Explanation: PHBV (poly-\(\beta\)-hydroxybutyrate-co-\(\beta\)-hydroxyvalerate) is an aliphatic polyester obtained by copolymerising 3-hydroxybutanoic acid and 3-hydroxypentanoic acid. Option (A) gives biodegradable Nylon 2–nylon 6, and option (D) gives Glyptal.

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): Vulcanised rubber retains its elasticity over a wider temperature range and resists oxidation better than raw natural rubber.
Reason (R): Heating raw rubber with sulphur at \(373\text{–}415\,\mathrm{K}\) introduces covalent sulphur cross-links at the reactive double-bond sites between polyisoprene chains.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) Both (A) and (R) are false.

Answer: (A)

Explanation: Raw natural rubber is soft above \(335\,\mathrm{K}\) and brittle below \(283\,\mathrm{K}\). Vulcanisation with sulphur at \(373\text{–}415\,\mathrm{K}\) creates covalent sulphur bridges at reactive double-bond sites, preventing chains from slipping past one another and reducing unsaturation-related vulnerability.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Which polymer among the following is a homopolymer formed from a chlorinated diene monomer?

  • (A) Polyvinyl chloride
  • (B) Buna-N
  • (C) Neoprene
  • (D) Teflon

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Neoprene (polychloroprene) is a homopolymer of chloroprene (2-chlorobuta-1,3-diene), which is a chlorinated conjugated diene. PVC is made from vinyl chloride (a monoalkene), Buna-N is a copolymer, and Teflon is made from tetrafluoroethene.

Practice Question 5 Statement I & II MCQ

Statement I: Novolac is a linear phenol–formaldehyde polymer used in paints, whereas Bakelite is a cross-linked thermosetting polymer.
Statement II: Bakelite can be repeatedly softened on heating and remoulded into new shapes.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.

Explanation: Statement I is accurate: Novolac is the linear condensation product of phenol and formaldehyde used in paints, and heating it with formaldehyde creates cross-links to form Bakelite. Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

In the free-radical polymerisation of ethene initiated by benzoyl peroxide, what is the role of the phenyl radical (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5^\bullet}\))?

  • (A) It acts as a chain-terminating agent by abstracting hydrogen from polythene.
  • (B) It initiates chain growth by adding to the double bond of ethene to form \(\mathrm{C_6H_5-CH_2-\dot{C}H_2}\).
  • (C) It cross-links adjacent polythene chains to increase density.
  • (D) It reacts with water to form a condensation byproduct.

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Homolysis of benzoyl peroxide followed by decarboxylation generates phenyl radicals (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5^\bullet}\)), which add to ethene's \(\pi\)-bond in the chain-initiating step to produce \(\mathrm{C_6H_5-CH_2-\dot{C}H_2}\).

Practice Question 9 Single-Correct MCQ

Suppose \(5\,\mathrm{mol}\) of hexamethylenediamine is condensed completely with \(5\,\mathrm{mol}\) of adipic acid to form a linear Nylon 6,6 chain (neglecting end-group correction). Approximately how many moles of water are eliminated?

  • (A) \(2.5\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (B) \(5\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (C) \(10\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • (D) \(20\,\mathrm{mol}\)

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Each repeating unit of Nylon 6,6 contains two amide linkages formed from one diamine molecule and one diacid molecule, eliminating \(2\,\mathrm{H_2O}\) per repeating unit (or \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) per mole of each monomer reacted: \(n\,\text{diamine} + n\,\text{diacid} \rightarrow \text{Nylon 6,6} + 2n\,\mathrm{H_2O}\) when counting both amide bonds per unit). With \(5\,\mathrm{mol}\) of diamine and \(5\,\mathrm{mol}\) of diacid, \(10\,\mathrm{mol}\) of amide bonds form, eliminating \(10\,\mathrm{mol}\) of water.

Common Pitfalls and NEET Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Nylon 6 vs. Nylon 6,6: Nylon 6 is a homopolymer made from a single 6-carbon cyclic monomer (caprolactam), whereas Nylon 6,6 is a copolymer of two 6-carbon monomers (hexamethylenediamine + adipic acid).
  • Dacron (Terylene) vs. Glyptal: Both use ethylene glycol, but Dacron uses terephthalic acid (1,4-isomer) for fibres, whereas Glyptal uses phthalic acid (1,2-isomer) for paints and lacquers.
  • Novolac vs. Bakelite: Novolac is linear and soluble (used in paints); Bakelite is cross-linked and thermosetting (used in electrical switches).
  • Buna-S vs. Buna-N: Both share 1,3-butadiene; Buna-S pairs it with Styrene, while Buna-N pairs it with acryloNitrile.
  • Natural Rubber Stereochemistry: Natural rubber is strictly cis-1,4-polyisoprene, not trans.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Degree of polymerisation links repeating unit mass to average polymer molar mass.
  • Intermolecular force hierarchy: Elastomers < Thermoplastics < Fibres < Thermosets (covalent network).
  • LDPE (free radical, branched, high pressure) vs. HDPE (Ziegler–Natta \(\mathrm{TiCl_4 + (C_2H_5)_3Al}\), linear, mild pressure).
  • Biodegradable polymers: PHBV (aliphatic polyester) and Nylon 2–nylon 6 (alternating polyamide).
  • Vulcanisation uses sulphur at \(373\text{–}415\,\mathrm{K}\) (~5% S for tyres) to cross-link polyisoprene.

Concise Final Recap

Polymers are high-molar-mass macromolecules built from repeating monomer units. Chain-growth (addition) polymerisation joins unsaturated monomers via free-radical or coordinated pathways without byproduct loss, producing plastics and elastomers such as LDPE, HDPE, Teflon, Orlon, and Neoprene. Step-growth (condensation) polymerisation links bifunctional or polyfunctional monomers with elimination of small molecules such as \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), yielding polyamides (Nylon 6,6, Nylon 6), polyesters (Terylene, Glyptal), cross-linked thermosets (Bakelite, Melamine), and biodegradable alternatives (PHBV, Nylon 2–nylon 6). Mastering monomer identities, linkage types, and intermolecular forces provides a reliable foundation for all polymer questions in NEET UG.
