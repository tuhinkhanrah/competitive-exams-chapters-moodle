Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids

Structure, Nomenclature, Reactivity Mechanisms, Named Syntheses, Acidity Trends, and Exam Practice

Learning Objectives and Overview

Carbonyl (\(>\mathrm{C=O}\)) and carboxyl (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)) compounds form the backbone of synthetic and biological organic chemistry. From natural fragrances like vanillin and cinnamaldehyde to essential metabolic intermediates and industrial polymers, their reactivity is governed by the polarity and geometry of the carbon–oxygen double bond.

What You Will Master
  • Classification & Naming: Assigning IUPAC and common names to aliphatic, cyclic, and aromatic aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids.
  • Bonding & Physical Trends: Connecting \(sp^2\) hybridisation, dipole–dipole interactions, and hydrogen-bonded dimerisation to boiling points and water solubility.
  • Synthetic Pathways: Selecting specific reagents (PCC, DIBAL-H, \(\mathrm{Pd-BaSO_4}\), \(\mathrm{CrO_2Cl_2}\), \(\mathrm{R_2Cd}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2/RMgX}\)) to target aldehydes, ketones, or carboxylic acids.
  • Mechanisms & Tests: Mastering nucleophilic addition, aldol and Cannizzaro reactions, Tollens', Fehling's, and iodoform distinguishing tests.
  • Carboxylic Acid Chemistry: Predicting \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) trends using inductive and resonance effects, and applying esterification, decarboxylation, and Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) halogenation.

1. Structure and Polarity of Carbonyl and Carboxyl Groups

The defining feature of aldehydes and ketones is the carbonyl group (\(>\mathrm{C=O}\)). When the carbonyl carbon is bonded to at least one hydrogen atom (\(\mathrm{R-CHO}\)), the compound is an aldehyde; when it is bonded to two carbon groups (\(\mathrm{R-CO-R'}\)), it is a ketone. Replacing one carbon group with a hydroxyl (\(-\mathrm{OH}\)) group produces a carboxylic acid (\(\mathrm{R-COOH}\)), while attachments to \(-\mathrm{X}\), \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\), \(-\mathrm{OR'}\), or \(-\mathrm{OCOR'}\) yield acyl halides, amides, esters, and acid anhydrides, respectively.

Hybridisation and Geometry

The carbonyl carbon is \(sp^2\)-hybridised and forms three coplanar \(\sigma\) bonds separated by approximately \(120^\circ\) (trigonal planar geometry). Its remaining unhybridised \(p\)-orbital overlaps sideways with a \(p\)-orbital on oxygen to form a \(\pi\) bond, placing the \(\pi\)-electron cloud above and below the molecular plane. Oxygen also retains two non-bonding lone pairs.

Tutorial Diagram 1: The \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar and electrophilic (\(\delta^+\)), making it susceptible to nucleophilic attack perpendicular to the plane.

Because oxygen is significantly more electronegative than carbon, the \(\pi\) electrons are drawn toward oxygen, giving the carbonyl group a large dipole moment (higher than that of ethers). Consequently, the carbonyl carbon behaves as an electrophile (Lewis acid) and the carbonyl oxygen as a nucleophile (Lewis base).

In carboxylic acids, the bonds around the carboxyl carbon also lie in one plane at roughly \(120^\circ\). However, lone-pair donation from the adjacent \(-\mathrm{OH}\) oxygen into the carbonyl group creates a delocalised resonance hybrid. This reduces the positive charge on the carboxyl carbon, making it less electrophilic than the carbonyl carbon of aldehydes and ketones.

2. Systematic Nomenclature (IUPAC and Common Systems)

Competitive exams frequently test both common names (derived from natural Latin/Greek roots or acyl prefixes) and IUPAC names.

  • Aldehydes: Replace the terminal -e of the parent alkane with -al, numbering the chain from the \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) carbon as C-1. When \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) is attached directly to a ring, use the suffix -carbaldehyde (e.g., cyclohexanecarbaldehyde; benzenecarbaldehyde is commonly accepted as benzaldehyde). If three or more \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) groups are attached to an open chain, all are treated equally using -tricarbaldehyde (e.g., propane-1,2,3-tricarbaldehyde). In common naming, substituent positions are denoted by \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta\) starting from the carbon adjacent to \(-\mathrm{CHO}\).
  • Ketones: Replace -e with -one, numbering from the end nearer to \(>\mathrm{C=O}\). In cyclic ketones, the carbonyl carbon is C-1. Common names list the two alkyl/aryl groups (e.g., dimethyl ketone is acetone), while alkyl aryl ketones use the acyl prefix + phenone (e.g., acetophenone, propiophenone, benzophenone).
  • Carboxylic Acids: Replace -e with -oic acid, with the \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) carbon as C-1. Dicarboxylic acids retain the -e and add -dioic acid (e.g., ethanedioic acid for oxalic acid, hexanedioic acid for adipic acid). Ring-attached acids use -carboxylic acid.

3. Physical Properties: Boiling Points and Solubility

For compounds of comparable molecular mass, boiling points follow a clear hierarchy governed by intermolecular forces:

\[ \text{Alkanes (van der Waals)} < \text{Ethers (weak dipole)} < \text{Aldehydes / Ketones (strong dipole–dipole)} < \text{Alcohols (H-bonding)} < \text{Carboxylic Acids (cyclic H-bonded dimers)} \]

Why do carboxylic acids boil higher than alcohols of similar molar mass? Carboxylic acids form strong, cyclic intermolecular hydrogen bonds that persist even in the vapour phase or in aprotic solvents, effectively doubling their apparent molecular mass as dimers.

Water Solubility: Lower aldehydes and ketones (methanal, ethanal, propanone) and lower carboxylic acids (up to 4 carbons) are completely miscible with water because their polar oxygen atoms form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. As the hydrophobic alkyl chain lengthens, solubility drops sharply. Benzoic acid is nearly insoluble in cold water.

4. Methods of Preparation of Aldehydes and Ketones

General Routes (Both Aldehydes and Ketones)

  • Oxidation or Dehydrogenation of Alcohols: Primary alcohols yield aldehydes (using mild, anhydrous oxidants such as \(\mathrm{PCC}\) or passing vapours over \(\mathrm{Cu/Ag}\) at \(573\,\mathrm{K}\)), whereas secondary alcohols yield ketones (using \(\mathrm{CrO_3}\), \(\mathrm{PCC}\), or \(\mathrm{Cu/Ag}\)).
  • Reductive Ozonolysis of Alkenes: Treating an alkene with \(\mathrm{O_3}\) followed by \(\mathrm{Zn/H_2O}\) cleaves the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond. Disubstituted alkene carbons give ketones, while monosubstituted or unsubstituted carbons give aldehydes.
  • Hydration of Alkynes (\(\mathrm{HgSO_4/H_2SO_4}\)): Ethyne gives ethanal (acetaldehyde), whereas all terminal and internal higher alkynes give ketones via Markovnikov addition and tautomerisation.

5. Chemical Reactions of Aldehydes and Ketones

Nucleophilic Addition Mechanism and Reactivity

Unlike alkenes (which undergo electrophilic addition), aldehydes and ketones undergo nucleophilic addition. A nucleophile (\(\mathrm{Nu^-}\)) attacks the electrophilic \(sp^2\) carbonyl carbon along a path nearly perpendicular to the trigonal plane, forming a tetrahedral \(sp^3\) alkoxide intermediate. Subsequent protonation yields the neutral addition product.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Two-step nucleophilic addition transforming an \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbonyl carbon into a tetrahedral \(sp^3\) product.

Why are aldehydes more reactive than ketones?

  1. Steric Factor: Aldehydes carry only one alkyl/aryl substituent and one small H atom, whereas ketones carry two bulky substituents that hinder the perpendicular approach of \(\mathrm{Nu^-}\).
  2. Electronic Factor: Two electron-donating (\(+I\)) alkyl groups in ketones reduce the \(\delta^+\) charge on the carbonyl carbon more strongly than one alkyl group in aldehydes.

Similarly, benzaldehyde is less reactive than propanal because resonance donation from the benzene ring into the \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) group decreases the electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon.

Key Nucleophilic Addition & Addition–Elimination Reactions

  • \(\mathrm{HCN}\) (Base-Catalysed): Pure \(\mathrm{HCN}\) reacts slowly; trace \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) generates \(\mathrm{CN^-}\), yielding cyanohydrins (\(\mathrm{RCH(OH)CN}\)).
  • Sodium Hydrogensulphite (\(\mathrm{NaHSO_3}\)): Forms crystalline, water-soluble bisulphite adducts. Equilibrium favours products for aldehydes and unhindered methyl ketones, making this useful for purification (regenerated with dilute acid or alkali).
  • Alcohols (Dry \(\mathrm{HCl}\) Catalyst): Aldehydes add 1 equivalent of \(\mathrm{R'OH}\) to form a hemiacetal, then a second equivalent to form a gem-dialkoxy acetal. Ketones react with ethylene glycol to form cyclic ethylene glycol ketals. Both are stable to base but hydrolyse back to carbonyls in aqueous acid.
  • Ammonia Derivatives (\(\mathrm{H_2N-Z}\), weakly acidic pH): Reversible addition followed by rapid dehydration forms \(\mathrm{>C=N-Z}\):
    • \(\mathrm{Z = -H}\): Imine; \(\mathrm{Z = -R}\): Substituted imine (Schiff's base).
    • \(\mathrm{Z = -OH}\): Oxime; \(\mathrm{Z = -NH_2}\): Hydrazone; \(\mathrm{Z = -NHC_6H_5}\): Phenylhydrazone.
    • \(\mathrm{Z = -NH-C_6H_3(NO_2)_2}\): 2,4-DNP derivative (yellow/orange-red precipitate, diagnostic test for \(>\mathrm{C=O}\)).
    • \(\mathrm{Z = -NHCONH_2}\): Semicarbazone. Important: Semicarbazide (\(\mathrm{H_2N-CO-NH-NH_2}\)) has two \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) groups, but the one attached to \(\mathrm{C=O}\) is resonance-delocalised and non-nucleophilic; reaction occurs exclusively at the terminal hydrazine \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\).

Reduction and Oxidation

  • Reduction to Alcohols: \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\), \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\), or \(\mathrm{H_2/Ni}\) reduces aldehydes to \(1^\circ\) alcohols and ketones to \(2^\circ\) alcohols.
  • Deoxygenation to Alkanes (\(>\mathrm{C=O} \rightarrow >\mathrm{CH_2}\)):
    • Clemmensen Reduction: \(\mathrm{Zn-Hg / conc.\ HCl}\) (avoid if molecule has acid-sensitive groups like \(-\mathrm{OH}\) or \(\mathrm{C=C}\)).
    • Wolff–Kishner Reduction: \(\mathrm{NH_2NH_2}\) followed by \(\mathrm{KOH / ethylene\ glycol,\ \Delta}\) (avoid if molecule has base-sensitive groups like esters or halides).
  • Tollens' Test (Silver Mirror): \(\mathrm{RCHO + 2[Ag(NH_3)_2]^+ + 3OH^- \rightarrow RCOO^- + 2Ag\downarrow + 2H_2O + 4NH_3}\). Positive for both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes; negative for ketones.
  • Fehling's Test: Alkaline \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) complex (Fehling A: aq. \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\); Fehling B: alkaline Rochelle salt) oxidises aliphatic aldehydes only to \(\mathrm{RCOO^-}\) with a red-brown \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\) precipitate. Aromatic aldehydes (e.g., benzaldehyde) and ketones do NOT respond.
  • Haloform / Iodoform Test: Compounds with a \(\mathrm{CH_3-CO-}\) group (or \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH(OH)-}\) group, which oxidises to \(\mathrm{CH_3-CO-}\)) react with \(\mathrm{I_2/NaOH}\) (\(\mathrm{NaOI}\)) to give a yellow precipitate of \(\mathrm{CHI_3}\) and a carboxylate salt with one fewer carbon. \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds remain untouched.

Reactions Driven by \(\alpha\)-Hydrogen Acidity

Hydrogens on the \(\alpha\)-carbon adjacent to \(>\mathrm{C=O}\) are weakly acidic because the carbonyl withdraws electron density and the resulting enolate ion is resonance-stabilised.

  • Aldol Condensation: Aldehydes or ketones with at least one \(\alpha\)-H react in dilute alkali (\(\mathrm{NaOH}\) or \(\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2}\)) to give \(\beta\)-hydroxy aldehydes (aldols) or \(\beta\)-hydroxy ketones (ketols), which dehydrate on heating to \(\alpha,\beta\)-unsaturated carbonyls.
  • Cross-Aldol Condensation: Between two different carbonyls. If both possess \(\alpha\)-H, a mixture of four products forms (two self-aldol + two cross-aldol). If one lacks \(\alpha\)-H (e.g., benzaldehyde + acetophenone), a single major cross-product forms cleanly.
  • Cannizzaro Reaction: Aldehydes without any \(\alpha\)-H (e.g., \(\mathrm{HCHO}\), \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CHO}\), \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CCHO}\)) undergo disproportionation on heating with concentrated alkali (\(\mathrm{NaOH}\) or \(\mathrm{KOH}\)): one molecule is reduced to an alcohol and another is oxidised to a carboxylate salt.
  • Ring Electrophilic Substitution: In aromatic aldehydes and ketones, \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) and \(-\mathrm{COR}\) are strongly deactivating and meta-directing.

6. Carboxylic Acids: Preparation, Acidity, and Reactions

Key Synthetic Routes

  • Oxidation of \(1^\circ\) Alcohols & Aldehydes: Using \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) (acidic/neutral/alkaline), \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7/H^+}\), or Jones reagent (\(\mathrm{CrO_3-H_2SO_4}\)).
  • Side-Chain Oxidation of Alkylbenzenes: Vigorous oxidation with \(\mathrm{KMnO_4-KOH/\Delta}\) followed by \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\) converts any \(1^\circ\) or \(2^\circ\) alkyl side chain (regardless of length) into \(-\mathrm{COOH}\). Exception: Tertiary alkyl groups (e.g., tert-butylbenzene) lack a benzylic hydrogen and are not oxidised.
  • Hydrolysis of Nitriles, Amides, Acyl Halides, Anhydrides, and Esters: Acidic or basic hydrolysis yields carboxylic acids (after acidification if base is used).
  • Grignard Carbonation: \(\mathrm{RMgX + CO_2\text{ (dry ice)} \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} RCOOMgX \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H_3O^+}} RCOOH}\). Increases the carbon chain by one carbon atom.

Acidity of Carboxylic Acids and Substituent Effects

Carboxylic acids (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 4\text{–}5\)) are weaker than mineral acids (\(\mathrm{HCl},\ \mathrm{p}K_a = -7\)) but much stronger than phenols (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 10\)) and alcohols (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 16\)). Although the phenoxide ion has more resonance structures, the carboxylate ion (\(\mathrm{RCOO^-}\)) is more stable because its negative charge is delocalised over two equivalent, electronegative oxygen atoms, whereas phenoxide places negative charge on less electronegative ring carbons.

\[ \mathrm{p}K_a = -\log_{10} K_a \qquad (\text{Smaller } \mathrm{p}K_a \implies \text{Stronger Acid}) \]
  • Electron-Withdrawing Groups (EWG: \(-\mathrm{CF_3 > -NO_2 > -CN > -F > -Cl > -Br > -I}\)): Stabilise \(\mathrm{RCOO^-}\) via \(-I\) (or \(-R\)) and increase acidity. Inductive effects weaken rapidly with distance from \(-\mathrm{COOH}\).
  • Electron-Donating Groups (EDG: alkyl, \(-\mathrm{OCH_3}\)): Destabilise \(\mathrm{RCOO^-}\) and decrease acidity.
  • Direct \(sp^2\) Attachment: Benzoic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.19\)) is stronger than acetic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.76\)) because the \(sp^2\)-hybridised phenyl carbon is more electronegative than an \(sp^3\) methyl carbon. On the aromatic ring, *para*-nitrobenzoic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 3.41\)) is stronger than benzoic acid, while *para*-methoxybenzoic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.46\)) is weaker.

Reactions of Carboxylic Acids

  • O–H Cleavage (Acidity Test): React with \(\mathrm{Na}\), \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), and even weak bases like \(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\) to evolve brisk \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) effervescence (distinguishes carboxylic acids from phenols, which do not react with \(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\)).
  • C–OH Cleavage:
    • Anhydrides: Heating with \(\mathrm{P_2O_5}\) or \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\).
    • Fischer Esterification: \(\mathrm{RCOOH + R'OH \xrightleftharpoons{\mathrm{H^+}} RCOOR' + H_2O}\) (nucleophilic acyl substitution; equilibrium driven forward by removing water or ester).
    • Acyl Chlorides: With \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\), \(\mathrm{PCl_3}\), or \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\) (\(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\) is preferred because byproducts \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\) are gases).
    • Amides: With \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) followed by heating. Phthalic acid gives phthalamide, which on strong heating loses \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) to form cyclic phthalimide.
  • Reduction & Decarboxylation: \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) or \(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\) reduces \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) to \(-\mathrm{CH_2OH}\) (\(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) is too weak and does not reduce carboxylic acids). Heating \(\mathrm{RCOONa}\) with sodalime (\(\mathrm{NaOH + CaO}\), \(3:1\)) removes \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to give \(\mathrm{R-H}\); Kolbe electrolysis of \(\mathrm{RCOONa(aq)}\) gives symmetrical alkanes \(\mathrm{R-R}\).
  • Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) Reaction: Carboxylic acids with an \(\alpha\)-H react with \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in the presence of catalytic red phosphorus, followed by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), to yield \(\alpha\)-halocarboxylic acids.
  • Ring Substitution: \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) is meta-directing and deactivating. Aromatic carboxylic acids do not undergo Friedel–Crafts alkylation or acylation because the Lewis acid catalyst (\(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\)) complexes irreversibly with the carboxyl group.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Predicting Nucleophilic Addition Reactivity

Problem: Arrange the following four carbonyl compounds (chosen as a representative comparison set) in increasing order of reactivity toward nucleophilic addition by \(\mathrm{HCN}\): 4-methoxybenzaldehyde, propanone, propanal, 4-nitrobenzaldehyde.

Solution:

  1. Aliphatic aldehyde vs. ketone: Propanal (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CHO}\)) has one alkyl group and one small H atom, whereas propanone (\(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3}\)) has two \(+I\) methyl groups causing greater steric hindrance and lower \(\delta^+\) on carbonyl carbon. Thus, \(\text{propanone} < \text{propanal}\).
  2. Substituted benzaldehydes: In 4-methoxybenzaldehyde, the strong \(+R\) effect of \(-\mathrm{OCH_3}\) donates electron density into the ring and onto the \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) carbon, sharply lowering its electrophilicity. Conversely, in 4-nitrobenzaldehyde, the strong \(-R/-I\) \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) group withdraws electron density, making the carbonyl carbon highly electrophilic (more reactive than propanal, though aromatic resonance slightly offsets this).
  3. Comparing 4-methoxybenzaldehyde and propanone: Resonance donation from the *para*-methoxyphenyl ring deactivates the carbonyl carbon more strongly than two methyl groups in propanone, though both are less reactive than propanal.

Order (increasing reactivity): \(\text{4-methoxybenzaldehyde} < \text{propanone} < \text{propanal} < \text{4-nitrobenzaldehyde}\).

Example 2: Distinguishing Carbonyl Isomers via Chemical Tests

Problem: A laboratory sample is known to be one of three \(\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O}\) isomers: pentanal, pentan-2-one, or pentan-3-one. Design a two-test sequence to uniquely identify each isomer.

Solution:

  • Test 1 — Tollens' Reagent (\([\mathrm{Ag(NH_3)_2}]^+/\mathrm{OH^-}\)): Warm each sample with Tollens' reagent. Only pentanal (an aldehyde) reduces \(\mathrm{Ag^+}\) to form a bright silver mirror. Both pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one give no reaction.
  • Test 2 — Iodoform Test (\(\mathrm{I_2/NaOH}\)): Warm the two remaining ketones with \(\mathrm{I_2/NaOH}\). Pentan-2-one (\(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_2CH_3}\)) contains the \(\mathrm{CH_3CO-}\) moiety and forms a yellow precipitate of \(\mathrm{CHI_3}\) (plus sodium butanoate). Pentan-3-one (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2COCH_2CH_3}\)) lacks the \(\mathrm{CH_3CO-}\) group and gives a negative test.

Example 3: Selective Cross-Aldol vs. Cannizzaro Behaviour

Problem: Predict the major organic product(s) when (a) 2,2-dimethylpropanal is heated with \(50\%\,\mathrm{KOH}\), and (b) a mixture of 2,2-dimethylpropanal and ethanal is treated with dilute \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) followed by gentle heating.

Solution:

  • Part (a): 2,2-Dimethylpropanal, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CHO}\), has no \(\alpha\)-hydrogen. With concentrated \(\mathrm{KOH}\), it undergoes the Cannizzaro reaction (disproportionation) to yield 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CH_2OH}\)) and potassium 2,2-dimethylpropanoate (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-COOK}\)).
  • Part (b): Ethanal (\(\mathrm{CH_3CHO}\)) has three \(\alpha\)-hydrogens and forms an enolate in dilute \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), whereas 2,2-dimethylpropanal cannot form an enolate and acts purely as an electrophile. Cross-aldol condensation followed by dehydration yields 4,4-dimethylpent-2-enal (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CH=CH-CHO}\)) alongside minor self-aldol product (but-2-enal).

Example 4: Ranking Acid Strength Using Inductive and Resonance Effects

Problem: Using standard \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) principles, arrange the following hypothetical comparison set in order of increasing acid strength: butanoic acid, 2-fluorobutanoic acid, 3-fluorobutanoic acid, 4-nitrobenzoic acid.

Solution:

  1. Butanoic acid (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2COOH}\)) has only an electron-donating (\(+I\)) propyl group, making it the weakest acid here.
  2. 3-Fluorobutanoic acid has a strongly electronegative \(-\mathrm{F}\) atom (\(-I\)) at C-3, which stabilises the carboxylate anion more than unsubstituted butanoic acid.
  3. 2-Fluorobutanoic acid places the \(-\mathrm{F}\) atom on the \(\alpha\)-carbon (C-2), closest to \(-\mathrm{COOH}\). Because inductive effects decay rapidly with distance, 2-fluorobutanoic acid is significantly stronger than 3-fluorobutanoic acid.
  4. 4-Nitrobenzoic acid benefits from both an \(sp^2\) phenyl carbon and a strong \(-R/-I\) *para*-nitro group, though an \(\alpha\)-fluoro group directly adjacent to \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) typically gives a lower \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) (\(\approx 2.6\)) than 4-nitrobenzoic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 3.41\)).

Order (increasing acidity): \(\text{butanoic acid} < \text{3-fluorobutanoic acid} < \text{4-nitrobenzoic acid} < \text{2-fluorobutanoic acid}\).

Example 5: Two-Step Synthesis via Grignard Carbonation and HVZ

Problem: Show how cyclopentyl bromide can be converted into 2-bromo-2-cyclopentylethanoic acid in three clean synthetic operations.

Solution:

  1. Grignard Formation: Treat cyclopentyl bromide with \(\mathrm{Mg}\) in dry ether to form cyclopentylmagnesium bromide (\(\mathrm{C_5H_9MgBr}\)).
  2. Carbonation: React with solid \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (dry ice) in dry ether followed by acidic workup (\(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)) to obtain cyclopentanecarboxylic acid (\(\mathrm{C_5H_9COOH}\)).
  3. Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) Bromination: Treat cyclopentanecarboxylic acid with \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) and catalytic red phosphorus, followed by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), to selectively brominate the \(\alpha\)-carbon of the ring, yielding 1-bromocyclopentanecarboxylic acid (note: carbonation adds one carbon, so the \(\alpha\)-carbon is C-1 of the cyclopentane ring).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following aldehydes will fail to give a red-brown precipitate when heated with Fehling's solution?

  1. (A) Methanal
  2. (B) Ethanal
  3. (C) Propanal
  4. (D) Benzaldehyde
Answer: (D)

Explanation: Fehling's reagent is a mild oxidising agent (alkaline \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) tartrate complex) that oxidises aliphatic aldehydes (methanal, ethanal, propanal) to carboxylate anions while forming a red-brown \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\) precipitate. Aromatic aldehydes such as benzaldehyde are stabilised by resonance and do not respond to Fehling's test (though they do give a positive Tollens' test).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the Rosenmund reduction of benzoyl chloride to benzaldehyde, the role of \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\) in \(\mathrm{Pd/BaSO_4}\) is to:

  1. (A) Act as a dehydrating agent to remove water formed during reaction
  2. (B) Partially poison the palladium catalyst so that benzaldehyde is not reduced further to benzyl alcohol
  3. (C) Neutralise the \(\mathrm{HCl}\) gas evolved during hydrogenation
  4. (D) Increase the surface area of palladium to speed up complete reduction
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Unmodified palladium is too active and reduces the aldehyde product all the way to a primary alcohol. Supporting Pd on \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\) (often with a trace of sulphur or quinoline) moderates its catalytic activity, stopping the hydrogenation cleanly at the aldehyde stage.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which reagent sequence cleanly converts 4-oxopentanoic acid (\(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_2COOH}\)) into pentanoic acid (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2COOH}\)) without affecting the carboxyl group?

  1. (A) \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) in dry ether, followed by \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)
  2. (B) \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) in methanol
  3. (C) \(\mathrm{NH_2NH_2}\), followed by \(\mathrm{KOH}\) in ethylene glycol with heating, then \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\) in THF, followed by \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Converting a ketone (\(>\mathrm{C=O}\)) to a methylene group (\(>\mathrm{CH_2}\)) requires Clemmensen or Wolff–Kishner reduction. Option (C) is the Wolff–Kishner reduction; although \(\mathrm{KOH}\) forms the potassium carboxylate salt during heating, acidification (\(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)) regenerates the intact \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) group. \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) and \(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\) reduce \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) to \(-\mathrm{CH_2OH}\), while \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) reduces the ketone only to a \(2^\circ\) alcohol.

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When a mixture of methanal (\(\mathrm{HCHO}\)) and benzaldehyde (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CHO}\)) is heated with concentrated \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), the major products obtained are:

  1. (A) Methanol and sodium benzoate
  2. (B) Benzyl alcohol and sodium formate
  3. (C) Methanol and benzyl alcohol only
  4. (D) Sodium formate and sodium benzoate only
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Neither methanal nor benzaldehyde has an \(\alpha\)-hydrogen, so they undergo a crossed Cannizzaro reaction. Methanal is more electrophilic (less sterically hindered and lacks ring resonance), so \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) attacks methanal preferentially, oxidising it to formate (\(\mathrm{HCOONa}\)) while reducing benzaldehyde to benzyl alcohol (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2OH}\)).

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Carboxylic acids are stronger acids than phenols.

Reason (R): The carboxylate anion is stabilised by two equivalent resonance structures in which the negative charge resides on electronegative oxygen atoms, whereas phenoxide has non-equivalent resonance structures placing negative charge on carbon atoms.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Even though phenoxide has more resonance contributors (five vs. two for carboxylate), equivalent resonance over two electronegative oxygen atoms provides much greater stabilisation than delocalisation onto less electronegative ring carbons. Hence, (A) and (R) are both true and (R) correctly explains (A).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following alkylbenzenes will not yield benzoic acid upon vigorous oxidation with hot alkaline \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) followed by acidification?

  1. (A) Methylbenzene (toluene)
  2. (B) Ethylbenzene
  3. (C) Isopropylbenzene (cumene)
  4. (D) tert-Butylbenzene
Answer: (D)

Explanation: Side-chain oxidation of alkylbenzenes by \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) requires at least one benzylic \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond. Primary (\(1^\circ\)) and secondary (\(2^\circ\)) alkyl groups possess benzylic hydrogens and oxidise completely to \(-\mathrm{COOH}\). Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) butylbenzene has no benzylic hydrogen and resists oxidation.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

  2. (B) Pentan-3-one
  3. (C) 3-Methylbutan-2-one
  4. (D) Pentanal
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Positive 2,4-DNP and negative Tollens' confirm a ketone. A positive iodoform test confirms a methyl ketone (\(\mathrm{CH_3CO-}\)), eliminating pentan-3-one.   2. (B) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3 < CH_3CH_2CH_2CHO < C_2H_5OC_2H_5 < CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2OH}\)

Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET Traps to Avoid
  • Fehling's Test Trap: Benzaldehyde gives a positive 2,4-DNP test and a positive Tollens' test, but a negative Fehling's test.
  • \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) Scope: Sodium borohydride reduces aldehydes and ketones, but does not reduce carboxylic acids or esters.
  • Semicarbazide Regioselectivity: Reaction with \(>\mathrm{C=O}\) occurs through the terminal \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2N-NH-CO-NH_2}\), never the amide \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) next to \(\mathrm{C=O}\).
  • 3° Alkylbenzene Oxidation: \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) oxidises toluene, ethylbenzene, and cumene to benzoic acid, but leaves tert-butylbenzene unreacted.
  • HVZ Requirement: Carboxylic acids without an \(\alpha\)-hydrogen (e.g., \(\mathrm{HCOOH}\), \(\mathrm{C_6H_5COOH}\), \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CCOOH}\)) do not undergo Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky halogenation.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Can I distinguish aliphatic vs. aromatic aldehydes using Fehling's vs. Tollens' reagent?
  • Can I identify \(\mathrm{CH_3CO-}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)-}\) groups using the iodoform test?
  • Do I remember which aldehydes undergo Cannizzaro (no \(\alpha\)-H) vs. Aldol (at least one \(\alpha\)-H)?
  • Can I rank substituted acetic and benzoic acids by \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) using \(-I/-R\) vs. \(+I/+R\) effects?
  • Do I know when to choose Clemmensen (\(\mathrm{Zn-Hg/HCl}\)) over Wolff–Kishner (\(\mathrm{NH_2NH_2/KOH}\))?

Concise Final Recap

Aldehydes and ketones feature a planar \(sp^2\) carbonyl carbon that undergoes nucleophilic addition, with aldehydes being more reactive than ketones due to lower steric hindrance and weaker \(+I\) donation. Mild oxidants (Tollens', Fehling's) and \(\alpha\)-H chemistry (Aldol vs. Cannizzaro) cleanly separate their structures. Carboxylic acids form hydrogen-bonded dimers with high boiling points, exhibit strong acidity stabilised by equivalent carboxylate resonance, and undergo nucleophilic acyl substitution, decarboxylation, and \(\alpha\)-halogenation (HVZ) to serve as versatile hubs in organic synthesis.
