NEET UG Chemistry Organic Chemistry — Oxygen-Containing Compounds

Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

A comprehensive, mechanism-driven tutorial covering classification, nomenclature, structure, preparation, physical trends, chemical reactivity, and NEET UG practice assessment.

Learning Objectives & Syllabus Scope

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Classify alcohols, phenols, and ethers based on hydroxyl count, carbon hybridisation, and substituent symmetry.
  • Assign systematic IUPAC names to acyclic, cyclic, polyhydric, and aromatic hydroxy/alkoxy derivatives.
  • Correlate C–O bond lengths, bond angles, and hydrogen-bonding networks with boiling points, solubility, and steam volatility.
  • Compare synthetic pathways for alcohols (alkene hydration, hydroboration–oxidation, carbonyl reduction, Grignard addition), phenols (Dow's process, sulphonation, diazonium hydrolysis, cumene process), and ethers (alcohol dehydration, Williamson synthesis).
  • Predict acidity trends, regioselectivity, and carbocation rearrangements across nucleophilic substitution, elimination, oxidation, and electrophilic aromatic substitution reactions.

1. Classification and Structural Geometry

Replacing a hydrogen atom of a hydrocarbon with a hydroxyl group (\(\mathrm{-OH}\)) or an alkoxy/aryloxy group (\(\mathrm{-OR}\) / \(\mathrm{-OAr}\)) generates three distinct functional classes:

  • Alcohols: Contain one or more \(\mathrm{-OH}\) groups bonded directly to an aliphatic carbon atom (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\)).
  • Phenols: Contain one or more \(\mathrm{-OH}\) groups bonded directly to an \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbon of an aromatic ring (e.g., \(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH}\)).
  • Ethers: Formed conceptually by replacing the hydroxyl hydrogen of an alcohol or phenol with an alkyl or aryl group (\(\mathrm{R-O-R'}\), \(\mathrm{Ar-O-R}\), or \(\mathrm{Ar-O-Ar'}\)).

Classification Criteria

Class Sub-category Structural Feature & Example
Alcohols (by –OH count) Monohydric, Dihydric, Trihydric One, two, or three \(\mathrm{-OH}\) groups respectively (e.g., ethanol; ethane-1,2-diol; propane-1,2,3-triol).
Alcohols (\(sp^3\ \mathrm{C-OH}\)) Primary (\(1^\circ\)), Secondary (\(2^\circ\)), Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) The \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group is attached to a carbon bonded to 1, 2, or 3 other carbons (e.g., propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol).
Alcohols (\(sp^3\ \mathrm{C-OH}\)) Allylic & Benzylic The \(\mathrm{-OH}\) is on an \(sp^3\) carbon adjacent to a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond (allylic, e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-CH_2OH}\)) or an aromatic ring (benzylic, e.g., \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2OH}\)). Each can be \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), or \(3^\circ\).
Alcohols (\(sp^2\ \mathrm{C-OH}\)) Vinylic The \(\mathrm{-OH}\) is bonded directly to an alkene carbon (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-OH}\)).
Phenols Mono-, Di-, Trihydric One, two, or three \(\mathrm{-OH}\) groups directly on a benzene ring (e.g., phenol, catechol, benzene-1,2,4-triol).
Ethers Simple (Symmetrical) vs Mixed (Unsymmetrical) Identical groups on oxygen (e.g., \(\mathrm{C_2H_5OC_2H_5}\)) vs different groups on oxygen (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3OC_2H_5}\), \(\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3}\)).

Bond Angles and Bond Lengths

In alcohols, oxygen uses \(sp^3\)-hybridised orbitals to form \(\sigma\) bonds with carbon and hydrogen. Because lone-pair–lone-pair repulsion is stronger than bond-pair–bond-pair repulsion, the \(\mathrm{C-O-H}\) bond angle in methanol compresses slightly to \(108.9^\circ\) (just below the ideal tetrahedral angle of \(109.5^\circ\)).

In phenol, the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length shrinks to \(136\ \mathrm{pm}\) (compared with \(142\ \mathrm{pm}\) in methanol) for two reasons: (i) resonance delocalisation of an oxygen lone pair into the benzene ring gives the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond partial double-bond character, and (ii) the ring carbon is \(sp^2\)-hybridised (higher \(s\)-character pulls bonding electrons closer). In methoxymethane (an ether), steric repulsion between the two bulky methyl groups widens the \(\mathrm{C-O-C}\) bond angle to \(111.7^\circ\), while the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length (\(141\ \mathrm{pm}\)) remains close to that of alcohols.

2. IUPAC Nomenclature Rules

Systematic naming follows three clear conventions depending on the principal functional group:

  • Alcohols: Select the longest carbon chain bearing the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group, number from the end nearest to \(\mathrm{-OH}\), and replace the terminal -e of the parent alkane with -ol (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CH_2CH_3}\) is butan-2-ol). For diols and triols, retain the terminal -e and add -diol or -triol (e.g., ethane-1,2-diol, propane-1,2,3-triol). In cyclic alcohols, the carbon bearing \(\mathrm{-OH}\) is always C-1.
  • Phenols: The parent name is phenol (accepted by IUPAC). Monosubstituted methylphenols are commonly called o-, m-, and p-cresols (2-, 3-, and 4-methylphenol). Dihydroxybenzenes are named benzene-1,2-diol (catechol), benzene-1,3-diol (resorcinol), and benzene-1,4-diol (hydroquinone/quinol).
  • Ethers: Named as alkoxyalkanes (or alkoxyarenes). The larger alkyl or aryl group serves as the parent hydrocarbon, while the smaller \(\mathrm{-OR}\) group is treated as an alkoxy substituent (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3OCH_2CH_2CH_3}\) is 1-methoxypropane; \(\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3}\) is methoxybenzene, commonly anisole; \(\mathrm{C_6H_5OC_2H_5}\) is ethoxybenzene, commonly phenetole).

3. Physical Properties & Hydrogen Bonding

Boiling Points

Alcohols and phenols exhibit markedly higher boiling points than hydrocarbons, haloalkanes, and ethers of comparable molecular mass. For instance, ethanol (molar mass \(46\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), b.p. \(351\ \mathrm{K}\)) boils more than \(100\ \mathrm{K}\) higher than methoxymethane (molar mass \(46\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), b.p. \(248\ \mathrm{K}\)) and propane (molar mass \(44\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), b.p. \(231\ \mathrm{K}\)).

This difference arises from strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding between \(\mathrm{-OH}\) groups in alcohols and phenols, which is absent in ethers and alkanes. Across homologous series, boiling points rise with increasing carbon chain length (stronger van der Waals forces) and fall with branching (smaller surface area weakens van der Waals attractions, so tert-butyl alcohol boils lower than butan-1-ol).

Solubility in Water

Lower alcohols and simple ethers (such as ethoxyethane) dissolve appreciably in water because their oxygen atoms form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. As the size of the hydrophobic alkyl or aryl group increases, water solubility decreases rapidly.

Steam Volatility of Nitrophenols

During nitration of phenol with dilute \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\), a mixture of o-nitrophenol and p-nitrophenol is obtained and separated by steam distillation. o-Nitrophenol is steam volatile because its adjacent \(\mathrm{-OH}\) and \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\) groups form an intramolecular hydrogen bond (a stable six-membered chelate ring), leaving molecules discrete. Conversely, p-nitrophenol is less volatile because its molecules associate extensively via intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

4. Preparation of Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

A. Synthesis of Alcohols

  1. Acid-Catalysed Hydration of Alkenes: Alkenes react with water in the presence of dilute acid (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) following Markovnikov's rule. The mechanism proceeds in three steps: (i) protonation of the alkene by \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\) to form the more stable carbocation, (ii) nucleophilic attack by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) to yield an oxonium ion, and (iii) deprotonation to regenerate \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\) and give the alcohol. Because a carbocation intermediate forms, hydride or alkyl shifts can occur.
  2. Hydroboration–Oxidation: Diborane, \(\mathrm{(BH_3)_2}\), adds across an alkene double bond to form a trialkylborane, which is oxidised by \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\) in aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to give an alcohol in high yield: \[ 3\,\mathrm{R-CH=CH_2} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{(BH_3)_2}} \mathrm{(R-CH_2-CH_2)_3B} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H_2O_2,\ OH^-}} 3\,\mathrm{R-CH_2-CH_2OH} + \mathrm{B(OH)_3} \] Boron attaches to the less substituted \(sp^2\) carbon; hence the overall result is anti-Markovnikov hydration without carbocation rearrangement.
  3. Reduction of Carbonyl Compounds & Carboxylic Acids:
    • Aldehydes \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{H_2/Pd}\text{ or }\mathrm{NaBH_4}}\) \(1^\circ\) alcohols; Ketones \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{NaBH_4}\text{ or }\mathrm{LiAlH_4}}\) \(2^\circ\) alcohols.
    • Carboxylic acids require the strong reducing agent \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) (followed by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) to form \(1^\circ\) alcohols; \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) does not reduce carboxylic acids or esters. Industrially, acids are esterified and then hydrogenated catalytically.
  4. Reaction of Grignard Reagents (\(\mathrm{RMgX}\)) with Carbonyls: Nucleophilic addition of \(\mathrm{RMgX}\) to a carbonyl followed by acidic/aqueous hydrolysis builds a new \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond:
    • Methanal (\(\mathrm{HCHO}\)) + \(\mathrm{RMgX} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H_2O}} \mathrm{RCH_2OH}\) (\(1^\circ\) alcohol)
    • Other aldehydes (\(\mathrm{R'CHO}\)) + \(\mathrm{RMgX} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H_2O}} \mathrm{R'CH(OH)R}\) (\(2^\circ\) alcohol)
    • Ketones (\(\mathrm{R'_2C=O}\)) + \(\mathrm{RMgX} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H_2O}} \mathrm{R'_2C(OH)R}\) (\(3^\circ\) alcohol)

B. Synthesis of Phenols

  • From Haloarenes (Dow's Process): Chlorobenzene fused with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at \(623\ \mathrm{K}\) and \(300\text{–}320\ \mathrm{atm}\) yields sodium phenoxide, which gives phenol upon acidification with \(\mathrm{HCl}\).
  • From Benzenesulphonic Acid: Benzene is sulphonated with oleum to benzenesulphonic acid, heated with molten \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to form sodium phenoxide, and acidified.
  • From Diazonium Salts: Treating an aromatic primary amine (aniline) with \(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + HCl}\) at \(273\text{–}278\ \mathrm{K}\) forms benzenediazonium chloride, which hydrolyses to phenol upon warming with water (\(\mathrm{N_2}\) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\) are evolved).
  • Industrial Cumene Process: Cumene (isopropylbenzene) is air-oxidised to cumene hydroperoxide, which undergoes acid-catalysed cleavage to yield phenol and acetone (propanone) as a valuable co-product.

C. Synthesis of Ethers

  1. Acid-Catalysed Dehydration of Alcohols: Heating ethanol with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) is temperature-sensitive: at \(443\ \mathrm{K}\), elimination gives ethene; at \(413\ \mathrm{K}\), bimolecular nucleophilic substitution (\(\mathrm{S_N2}\)) between an alcohol molecule and a protonated alcohol yields ethoxyethane. This method works only for unhindered \(1^\circ\) alcohols; \(2^\circ\) and \(3^\circ\) alcohols undergo elimination to alkenes instead.
  2. Williamson Ether Synthesis: An alkyl halide reacts with a sodium alkoxide (or phenoxide) via an \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) mechanism: \[ \mathrm{R-X} + \mathrm{R'-\ddot{O}:^-Na^+} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R-O-R'} + \mathrm{NaX} \] Crucial NEET Rule: Because alkoxides are strong bases as well as nucleophiles, the alkyl halide (\(\mathrm{R-X}\)) must be primary (or methyl). If a \(3^\circ\) alkyl halide is used with an alkoxide, E2 elimination dominates exclusively to give an alkene. To prepare tert-butyl ethyl ether, react sodium tert-butoxide with chloroethane—never sodium ethoxide with tert-butyl chloride. Similarly, to make anisole, react sodium phenoxide with iodomethane (halobenzene does not undergo \(\mathrm{S_N2}\)).

5. Chemical Reactivity & Key Mechanisms

Acidity of Alcohols and Phenols

Both alcohols and phenols react with active metals (\(\mathrm{Na}\), \(\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{Al}\)) to liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) gas. However, only phenols are acidic enough to react with aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to form water-soluble sodium phenoxide; alcohols do not react appreciably with aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\).

Acidity Trends
\[ \text{Alcohols: } \mathrm{H_2O} > 1^\circ\ \mathrm{ROH} > 2^\circ\ \mathrm{R_2CHOH} > 3^\circ\ \mathrm{R_3COH} \] \[ \text{Overall: } p\text{-Nitrophenol}\ (\mathrm{p}K_a = 7.1) > o\text{-Nitrophenol}\ (7.2) > m\text{-Nitrophenol}\ (8.3) > \text{Phenol}\ (10.0) > \text{Cresols}\ (10.1\text{–}10.2) > \mathrm{H_2O} > \text{Ethanol}\ (15.9) \]

Electron-releasing alkyl groups (\(\mathrm{+I}\) effect) intensify the negative charge on oxygen in alkoxide ions, destabilising them and reducing acidity. By contrast, phenol is roughly a million times more acidic than ethanol because: (i) the \(sp^2\)-hybridised ring carbon withdraws electron density inductively, increasing \(\mathrm{O-H}\) polarity, and (ii) the phenoxide ion is stabilised by resonance delocalisation of the negative charge onto the ortho and para ring carbons without any charge separation (unlike neutral phenol, whose resonance structures carry separated \(+\) and \(-\) charges).

Electron-withdrawing groups (such as \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\)) strongly increase phenol acidity—most pronounced at the ortho and para positions—whereas electron-donating groups (such as \(\mathrm{-CH_3}\) in cresols) slightly decrease acidity.

Reactions of Alcohols

  • Esterification & Acetylation: Alcohols and phenols react with carboxylic acids or acid anhydrides in the presence of conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), or with acid chlorides in the presence of pyridine (to neutralise \(\mathrm{HCl}\)). Acetylation of salicylic acid with acetic anhydride yields aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid).
  • Lucas Test (\(\mathrm{conc.\ HCl + anhyd.\ ZnCl_2}\)): Distinguishes \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) alcohols by the rate of carbocation formation and appearance of insoluble alkyl chloride turbidity: \(3^\circ\) gives immediate turbidity; \(2^\circ\) gives turbidity in ~5 minutes; \(1^\circ\) gives no turbidity at room temperature.
  • Acid-Catalysed Dehydration: Follows the ease order \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\) (matching carbocation stability). Step 1: fast protonation of \(\mathrm{-OH}\); Step 2 (rate-determining): slow loss of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) to form a carbocation (rearrangements via 1,2-hydride or methyl shifts occur when a more stable carbocation can form); Step 3: deprotonation to yield the more substituted alkene (Saytzeff/Zaitsev product).
  • Oxidation & Catalytic Dehydrogenation:
    • \(1^\circ\) alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{PCC}\text{ or }\mathrm{CrO_3\ (anhyd.)}}\) aldehyde; \(1^\circ\) alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{KMnO_4/H^+}\text{ or }\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}}\) carboxylic acid.
    • \(2^\circ\) alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CrO_3}}\) ketone.
    • \(3^\circ\) alcohol resists mild oxidation (vigorous \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) cleaves \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bonds).
    • Passing alcohol vapours over heated \(\mathrm{Cu}\) at \(573\ \mathrm{K}\): \(1^\circ \rightarrow\) aldehyde, \(2^\circ \rightarrow\) ketone, \(3^\circ \rightarrow\) alkene (dehydration).

Reactions of Phenols

The \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group strongly activates the benzene ring and directs incoming electrophiles to the ortho and para positions:

  • Nitration: Dilute \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) at \(298\ \mathrm{K}\) gives o- and p-nitrophenol. Concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) gives 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid, a strong acid due to three \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\) groups).
  • Halogenation: \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in low-polarity solvent (\(\mathrm{CS_2}\) or \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\)) at \(273\ \mathrm{K}\) gives p-bromophenol (major) and o-bromophenol (minor) without needing a Lewis acid. \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in water gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol.
  • Kolbe's Reaction: Sodium phenoxide reacts with \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (a weak electrophile), followed by acidification, to give 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid).
  • Reimer–Tiemann Reaction: Phenol treated with \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) and aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), followed by acidification, introduces a \(\mathrm{-CHO}\) group at the ortho position to form salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde).
  • With Zinc Dust & Oxidation: Heating phenol with \(\mathrm{Zn}\) dust reduces it to benzene (\(\mathrm{ZnO}\) by-product). Oxidation with \(\mathrm{Na_2Cr_2O_7 + H_2SO_4}\) yields benzoquinone.

Reactions of Ethers

Ethers are relatively unreactive, but their \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bonds cleave upon heating with concentrated \(\mathrm{HI}\) or \(\mathrm{HBr}\) (reactivity order: \(\mathrm{HI} > \mathrm{HBr} > \mathrm{HCl}\)).

Tutorial Diagram 3: Regioselectivity and mechanism of ether cleavage with concentrated \(\mathrm{HI}\).

In aromatic ethers (e.g., anisole), the \(\mathrm{-OCH_3}\) group is o,p-directing and activating: bromination in ethanoic acid (without \(\mathrm{FeBr_3}\)), Friedel–Crafts alkylation/acylation (\(\mathrm{Anhyd.\ AlCl_3}\)), and nitration (\(\mathrm{H_2SO_4/HNO_3}\)) all give the para isomer as the major product.

6. Commercially Important Alcohols

  • Methanol (\(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\), Wood Spirit): Manufactured by catalytic hydrogenation of \(\mathrm{CO}\) at \(573\text{–}673\ \mathrm{K}\) and \(200\text{–}300\ \mathrm{atm}\) over \(\mathrm{ZnO-Cr_2O_3}\). Highly toxic; biological oxidation forms methanal and methanoic acid, causing blindness and death.
  • Ethanol (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}\)): Produced industrially by hydration of ethene or anaerobic fermentation of sugars (sucrose \(\xrightarrow{\text{invertase}}\) glucose + fructose \(\xrightarrow{\text{zymase}}\) ethanol + \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)). Zymase is inhibited above \(14\%\) alcohol, and air must be excluded to prevent oxidation to ethanoic acid. Commercial ethanol is denatured with copper sulphate (colour) and pyridine (foul odour).

Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Designing a Williamson Ether Synthesis Without Elimination

Assume standard laboratory conditions using sodium alkoxide and an alkyl halide in an aprotic or alcoholic medium.

Problem: A student wishes to synthesise 2-ethoxy-2-methylbutane. Compare the two possible reagent pairs and identify which pair succeeds.

Solution:

  1. Identify the two groups attached to oxygen in 2-ethoxy-2-methylbutane: a tertiary 2-methylbutan-2-yl group, \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2C(CH_3)_2-}\), and a primary ethyl group, \(\mathrm{-CH_2CH_3}\).
  2. Attempt A (Fails): Reacting sodium ethoxide (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5O^-Na^+}\)) with 2-chloro-2-methylbutane (a \(3^\circ\) halide). Because \(\mathrm{C_2H_5O^-}\) is a strong base, E2 elimination dominates over \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) substitution, producing 2-methylbut-2-ene as the major alkene product instead of the ether.
  3. Attempt B (Succeeds): Reacting sodium 2-methylbutan-2-oxide, \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2C(CH_3)_2O^-Na^+}\), with bromoethane (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5Br}\), a \(1^\circ\) halide). The unhindered \(1^\circ\) halide undergoes clean \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) displacement to give 2-ethoxy-2-methylbutane in good yield.
Worked Example 2: Predicting Regioselectivity in Hydration vs Hydroboration–Oxidation

Assume excess water in dilute acid for hydration, and standard THF solvent for hydroboration followed by alkaline peroxide.

Problem: Predict the major organic products when 3-methylbut-1-ene reacts separately with (a) dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) and (b) \(\mathrm{(BH_3)_2}\) followed by \(\mathrm{H_2O_2/OH^-}\).

Solution:

  • (a) Acid-catalysed hydration: Protonation of \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CH-CH=CH_2}\) initially forms a \(2^\circ\) carbocation, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CH-\overset{+}{C}H-CH_3}\). A rapid 1,2-hydride shift converts it into the more stable \(3^\circ\) carbocation, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2\overset{+}{C}-CH_2CH_3}\). Attack by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) and deprotonation yields 2-methylbutan-2-ol as the major product.
  • (b) Hydroboration–oxidation: Boron adds to the terminal, less-substituted carbon without forming a carbocation. Subsequent oxidation replaces boron with \(\mathrm{-OH}\) at the terminal position, yielding 3-methylbutan-1-ol exclusively with no rearrangement.
Worked Example 3: Cleavage of a Mixed Aryl–Benzyl Ether with Concentrated HI

Assume heating with one equivalent of concentrated aqueous HI.

Problem: What products are formed when benzyl phenyl ether (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5-CH_2-O-C_6H_5}\)) is heated with concentrated \(\mathrm{HI}\)?

Solution:

Protonation of oxygen gives \(\mathrm{C_6H_5-CH_2-\overset{+}{O}(H)-C_6H_5}\). The \(\mathrm{O-C_6H_5}\) bond is stronger due to \(sp^2\) hybridisation of the phenyl carbon and partial double-bond character, and an aryl carbon cannot undergo nucleophilic substitution. Meanwhile, cleavage of the \(\mathrm{CH_2-O}\) bond generates a resonance-stabilised benzyl carbocation (or undergoes facile \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) attack by \(\mathrm{I^-}\)). Therefore, the products are phenol (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH}\)) and benzyl iodide (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2I}\)).

Worked Example 4: Comparing Acid Strength Using Electronic Effects

Assume aqueous solution at \(298\ \mathrm{K}\).

Problem: Arrange the following four compounds in increasing order of acid strength: 4-methylphenol, 2,4-dinitrophenol, propan-2-ol, and phenol.

Solution:

  1. Propan-2-ol is an aliphatic \(2^\circ\) alcohol; its alkoxide ion lacks resonance stabilisation and is destabilised by two \(\mathrm{+I}\) methyl groups, making it the weakest acid.
  2. 4-Methylphenol (p-cresol) is a phenol, so it is much stronger than an alcohol, but the electron-donating \(\mathrm{-CH_3}\) group destabilises the phenoxide ion slightly relative to unsubstituted phenol.
  3. 2,4-Dinitrophenol has two strongly electron-withdrawing \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\) groups at the ortho and para positions, which delocalise the phenoxide negative charge extensively, making it the strongest acid in this set.

Increasing order: Propan-2-ol < 4-methylphenol < phenol < 2,4-dinitrophenol.

Practice Question 1

Which of the following alcohols will produce immediate turbidity when treated with Lucas reagent (\(\mathrm{conc.\ HCl + anhyd.\ ZnCl_2}\)) at room temperature?

  • (A) Butan-1-ol
  • (B) Butan-2-ol
  • (C) 2-Methylpropan-1-ol
  • (D) 2-Methylpropan-2-ol
Correct Answer: (D) 2-Methylpropan-2-ol

Explanation: Lucas reagent reacts via a carbocation intermediate. 2-Methylpropan-2-ol is a \(3^\circ\) alcohol that forms a stable \(3^\circ\) carbocation immediately, yielding insoluble 2-chloro-2-methylpropane (immediate turbidity). Butan-2-ol (\(2^\circ\)) takes about 5 minutes, while butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol (\(1^\circ\)) show no turbidity at room temperature.

Practice Question 2

When vapours of 2-methylbutan-2-ol are passed over heated copper at \(573\ \mathrm{K}\), the major organic product formed is:

  • (A) 2-Methylbutanal
  • (B) 2-Methylbut-2-ene
  • (C) 3-Methylbutan-2-one
  • (D) 2-Methylbutanoic acid
Correct Answer: (B) 2-Methylbut-2-ene

Explanation: Over heated \(\mathrm{Cu}\) at \(573\ \mathrm{K}\), \(1^\circ\) and \(2^\circ\) alcohols undergo dehydrogenation to aldehydes and ketones respectively. However, \(3^\circ\) alcohols lack an \(\alpha\)-hydrogen and undergo dehydration to form the more substituted alkene (2-methylbut-2-ene).

Practice Question 3

Which of the following is the correct IUPAC name of the ether \(\mathrm{C_6H_5-O-CH_2-CH_2-CH(CH_3)_2}\)?

  • (A) 1-Phenoxy-3-methylbutane
  • (B) 3-Methylbutoxybenzene
  • (C) 2-Methyl-4-phenoxybutane
  • (D) Isopentyl phenyl ether
Correct Answer: (B) 3-Methylbutoxybenzene

Explanation: In IUPAC nomenclature for aryl alkyl ethers, the benzene ring (6 carbons) is chosen as the parent hydrocarbon when it is larger than the alkyl chain (5 carbons total in the isopentyl chain). Numbering the alkoxy chain from the point of attachment to oxygen gives 3-methylbutoxy, so the IUPAC name is 3-methylbutoxybenzene.

Practice Question 4

Heating 1-ethoxy-2-methylpropane with one equivalent of concentrated \(\mathrm{HI}\) yields:

  • (A) \(\mathrm{C_2H_5I}\) and \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH_2OH}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}\) and \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH_2I}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{C_2H_5I}\) and \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CI}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}\) and \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2C=CH_2}\)
Correct Answer: (A) \(\mathrm{C_2H_5I}\) and \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH_2OH}\)

Explanation: Both alkyl groups (ethyl and isobutyl) are primary, so cleavage proceeds by an \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) mechanism. The iodide ion attacks the less sterically hindered ethyl carbon, forming iodoethane (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5I}\)) and 2-methylpropan-1-ol (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH_2OH}\)).

Practice Question 5

Phenol reacts with \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) and aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) followed by acidification to form salicylaldehyde. This reaction is known as:

  • (A) Kolbe's reaction
  • (B) Williamson synthesis
  • (C) Reimer–Tiemann reaction
  • (D) Friedel–Crafts acylation
Correct Answer: (C) Reimer–Tiemann reaction

Explanation: Treating phenol with chloroform (\(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\)) in aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) introduces a \(\mathrm{-CHO}\) group at the ortho position via a substituted benzal chloride intermediate, yielding 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde (salicylaldehyde) upon acidification.

Practice Question 6

Consider the following two statements:
Statement I: The \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length in phenol (\(136\ \mathrm{pm}\)) is shorter than that in methanol (\(142\ \mathrm{pm}\)).
Statement II: In phenol, the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group is attached to an \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbon and the oxygen lone pair conjugates with the aromatic ring.
Choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.

Explanation: Both statements are accurate. Partial double-bond character from resonance and the higher electronegativity/smaller orbital size of the \(sp^2\) ring carbon shorten the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond in phenol to \(136\ \mathrm{pm}\).

Which reagent converts \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CH_2OH}\) selectively into \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CHO}\) without affecting the carbon–carbon double bond?

Common Pitfalls & NEET Revision Checklist

  • Williamson Synthesis Trap: Never pair a \(3^\circ\) alkyl halide with an alkoxide—E2 elimination gives an alkene. Always put the bulky group on the alkoxide and use a \(1^\circ\) alkyl halide.
  • \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) vs \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\): \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) reduces aldehydes and ketones selectively, leaving esters and carboxylic acids untouched. \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) reduces all of them.
  • Ether Cleavage Trap: If an ether contains a \(3^\circ\) alkyl group (e.g., tert-butyl methyl ether), \(\mathrm{HI}\) cleavage switches from \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) to \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) and produces the \(3^\circ\) iodide (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-I}\)) + methanol, not methyl iodide.
  • Bromination Medium Matters: Phenol + \(\mathrm{Br_2/CS_2}\) at \(273\ \mathrm{K}\) gives monobromophenols (p-major); Phenol + \(\mathrm{Br_2/H_2O}\) gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol.
  • Boiling Point vs Solubility: Ethers have boiling points close to alkanes (no H-bonding between ether molecules), yet their water solubility is comparable to alcohols of similar mass (ethers accept H-bonds from water).

Concise Final Recap

Alcohols, phenols, and ethers share the divalent oxygen atom, yet their chemistry diverges sharply based on hybridisation and resonance. In alcohols, \(sp^3\) bonding enables intermolecular H-bonding, nucleophilic substitution (Lucas test), carbocation-driven dehydration (\(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\)), and selective oxidation (PCC vs \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)). In phenols, conjugation of the oxygen lone pair with the aromatic ring shortens the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond (\(136\ \mathrm{pm}\)), enhances acidity (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 10\)) via phenoxide resonance, prevents \(\mathrm{C-O}\) cleavage, and strongly activates the ring toward ortho/para electrophilic substitution (nitration, bromination, Kolbe's, and Reimer–Tiemann reactions). Finally, ethers are prepared cleanly via \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) Williamson synthesis using \(1^\circ\) halides and undergo \(\mathrm{HI}\)-mediated cleavage via \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) (unhindered alkyls), \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) (\(3^\circ\) alkyls), or selective alkyl–oxygen bond scission (aryl alkyl ethers).
