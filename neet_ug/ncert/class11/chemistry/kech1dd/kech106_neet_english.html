1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Chemical equilibria govern vital biological and industrial systems. For example, the reversible binding of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) molecules with the protein haemoglobin enables oxygen transport from our lungs to our muscles, while a competing equilibrium involving \(\mathrm{CO}\) and haemoglobin explains carbon monoxide toxicity. After studying this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Identify the dynamic nature of equilibrium in both physical phase transformations and chemical reactions.
  • Formulate the Law of Chemical Equilibrium, write expressions for \(K_c\) and \(K_p\), and establish the quantitative relationship \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}\).
  • Predict the extent of a reaction from the magnitude of \(K\), determine the direction of net reaction using the reaction quotient \(Q\), and link \(K\) to standard Gibbs energy change (\(\Delta G^\circ\)).
  • Apply Le Chatelier's principle to predict how changes in concentration, partial pressure, volume, inert gas addition, temperature, and catalysts affect equilibrium state.
  • Classify acids and bases using Arrhenius, Brönsted-Lowry, and Lewis concepts, and identify conjugate acid-base pairs.
  • Calculate degree of ionization (\(\alpha\)), ionization constants (\(K_a, K_b\)), ionic product of water (\(K_w\)), and \(\mathrm{pH}\) across strong electrolytes, weak electrolytes, and very dilute acids.
  • Analyze the common ion effect, salt hydrolysis across different salt categories, buffer action using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, and solubility equilibria (\(K_{\mathrm{sp}}\)) including selective precipitation and \(\mathrm{pH}\)-dependent solubility.

2. Equilibrium in Physical Processes

When a liquid evaporates in a closed container, molecules with relatively higher kinetic energy escape the liquid surface into the vapour phase, while vapour molecules strike the liquid surface and condense back into the liquid phase. Eventually, a constant vapour pressure is established because the number of molecules leaving the liquid equals the number returning from the vapour. At this stage, the system has reached a dynamic equilibrium:

\[\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2O(vap)} \quad (\text{Rate of evaporation} = \text{Rate of condensation})\]

The double half-arrows (\(\rightleftharpoons\)) indicate that both opposing processes occur simultaneously at equal rates. The mixture of species at equilibrium is called an equilibrium mixture.

2.1 Phase Transformation Equilibria

  • Solid–Liquid Equilibrium: Ice and water kept in a perfectly insulated thermos flask at \(273\,\mathrm{K}\) and atmospheric pressure (\(1.013\,\mathrm{bar}\)) coexist with constant mass of both phases. Molecules of liquid water collide with ice and adhere to it at the exact same rate at which ice molecules escape into the liquid phase. For any pure substance at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) (\(1.013\,\mathrm{bar}\)) pressure, the unique temperature at which solid and liquid phases coexist at equilibrium is called the normal melting point or normal freezing point.
  • Liquid–Vapour Equilibrium: Consider a transparent closed box fitted with a mercury U-tube manometer. Initially, the air inside is dried using a desiccant such as anhydrous calcium chloride (\(\mathrm{CaCl_2}\)) or phosphorus pentoxide (\(\mathrm{P_4O_{10}}\)). When the drying agent is removed and a watch glass containing liquid water is quickly placed inside at constant temperature, the mercury level in the right limb of the manometer slowly rises and reaches a constant value while the volume of liquid water decreases. Initially, the rate of evaporation is constant and condensation is negligible. As vapour accumulates, the rate of condensation increases until \(\text{rate of evaporation} = \text{rate of condensation}\). The constant pressure exerted by water molecules at equilibrium at a given temperature is the equilibrium vapour pressure. Different liquids (e.g., methyl alcohol, acetone, ether, water) have different equilibrium vapour pressures at the same temperature. A liquid with a higher vapour pressure is more volatile and has a lower boiling point. In an open watch glass, vapour disperses into the room, condensation is negligible, and equilibrium can never be reached. For any pure liquid at \(1.013\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure, the temperature at which liquid and vapour coexist at equilibrium is the normal boiling point (\(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) for water). At high altitudes, atmospheric pressure decreases, so the boiling point decreases.
  • Solid–Vapour Equilibrium (Sublimation): When solid iodine is placed in a closed vessel, violet vapours appear and deepen in colour until the colour intensity becomes constant. At this point, the rate of sublimation equals the rate of deposition/condensation: \[\mathrm{I_2(solid)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{I_2(vapour)}\] Other examples include \(\text{Camphor(solid)} \rightleftharpoons \text{Camphor(vapour)}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_4Cl(solid)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{NH_4Cl(vapour)}\).

2.2 Dissolution of Solids and Gases in Liquids

  • Solids in Liquids: At a given temperature, a solution in which no more solute can be dissolved is called a saturated solution. In a saturated solution, a dynamic equilibrium exists between undissolved solid solute and dissolved solute: \[\text{Sugar(solution)} \rightleftharpoons \text{Sugar(solid)} \quad (\text{Rate of dissolution} = \text{Rate of crystallisation})\] When radioactive sugar crystals are added to a saturated solution of non-radioactive sugar, radioactivity is soon detected in both the solution and the solid phase, and the ratio of radioactive to non-radioactive molecules in solution increases until it attains a constant value. This confirms the dynamic exchange of molecules across phases at equal rates.
  • Gases in Liquids: In a sealed soda-water bottle, carbon dioxide gas is in dynamic equilibrium with dissolved carbon dioxide molecules: \[\mathrm{CO_2(gas)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CO_2(in\ solution)}\] This equilibrium is governed by Henry's Law, which states that the mass of a gas dissolved in a given mass of a solvent at any temperature is proportional to the pressure of the gas above the solvent. Solubility of a gas in a liquid decreases with an increase in temperature. When the bottle is opened, the partial pressure of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) above the liquid drops sharply to atmospheric partial pressure, causing dissolved \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) to fizz out to establish a new equilibrium state.

2.3 General Characteristics of Physical Equilibria

  1. Equilibrium is possible only in a closed system at a given temperature.
  2. Both opposing processes occur simultaneously at the exact same rate, creating a dynamic yet stable state.
  3. All measurable macroscopic properties of the system (pressure, concentration, colour, density) remain constant.
  4. Attainment of physical equilibrium is characterized by a constant value of one of its parameters at a given temperature.
  5. The magnitude of these characteristic quantities at any stage indicates the extent to which the physical process has proceeded before reaching equilibrium.

3. Equilibrium in Chemical Processes — Dynamic Nature

Reversible chemical reactions occur in both forward and backward directions. Consider a general reversible reaction in a closed vessel:

\[\mathrm{A + B \rightleftharpoons C + D}\]

Initially, if we start only with reactants \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\), the forward reaction rate is high while the reverse reaction rate is zero. With time, \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\) deplete and products \(\mathrm{C}\) and \(\mathrm{D}\) accumulate. Consequently, the rate of the forward reaction decreases and the rate of the reverse reaction increases until both rates become equal. At this stage, the system attains dynamic chemical equilibrium, and the concentrations of \(\mathrm{A}\), \(\mathrm{B}\), \(\mathrm{C}\), and \(\mathrm{D}\) remain constant with time.

Experimental Proof of Dynamic Nature & Bidirectional Attainment
  • Isotope Scrambling in Haber's Process: In the synthesis of ammonia, \(\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}\), if one equilibrium mixture prepared from \(\mathrm{N_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2}\) (containing \(\mathrm{N_2, H_2, NH_3}\)) and a second equilibrium mixture prepared under identical conditions using deuterium \(\mathrm{D_2}\) (containing \(\mathrm{N_2, D_2, ND_3}\)) are mixed together, the total ammonia concentration remains unchanged. However, mass spectrometry reveals scrambled species: \(\mathrm{NH_3, NH_2D, NHD_2, ND_3}\) as well as \(\mathrm{H_2, HD, D_2}\). This proves that forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates even after equilibrium is reached.
  • Attainment from Either Direction: In the reaction \(\mathrm{H_2(g) + I_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2HI(g)}\), provided the total number of \(\mathrm{H}\) and \(\mathrm{I}\) atoms in a given volume at a given temperature is the same, the exact same equilibrium composition is obtained whether we start with pure reactants (\(\mathrm{H_2 + I_2}\)) or pure product (\(\mathrm{HI}\)).
  • Two-Cylinder Transfer Analogy: If coloured water (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) solution) is repeatedly transferred back and forth between two \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) measuring cylinders using two glass tubes, the liquid levels in both cylinders eventually become constant even though liquid transfer continues in both directions. If tubes of different diameters are used, the final equilibrium levels in the two cylinders are different.

4. Law of Chemical Equilibrium & Equilibrium Constant

In 1864, Norwegian chemists Cato Maximillian Guldberg and Peter Waage proposed the Law of Mass Action (also known as the Law of Chemical Equilibrium): At a given temperature, the product of equilibrium concentrations of the reaction products raised to their respective stoichiometric coefficients in the balanced chemical equation divided by the product of equilibrium concentrations of the reactants raised to their individual stoichiometric coefficients has a constant value.

For a general balanced reversible reaction:

\[a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}\]
\[K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c[\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a[\mathrm{B}]^b}\]

Here, \([\mathrm{A}], [\mathrm{B}], [\mathrm{C}],\) and \([\mathrm{D}]\) represent equilibrium molar concentrations (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)). For example, for \(4\mathrm{NH_3(g)} + 5\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 4\mathrm{NO(g)} + 6\mathrm{H_2O(g)}\), the equilibrium constant is \(K_c = \dfrac{[\mathrm{NO}]^4[\mathrm{H_2O}]^6}{[\mathrm{NH_3}]^4[\mathrm{O_2}]^5}\).

4.3 Homogeneous & Heterogeneous Equilibria and Relationship Between \(K_p\) and \(K_c\)

In a homogeneous system, all reactants and products are in the same phase—for example, \(\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}\) in the gas phase, or \(\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5(aq) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons CH_3COOH(aq) + C_2H_5OH(aq)}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}(aq) + SCN^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons [Fe(SCN)]^{2+}(aq)}\) in the solution phase.

For gaseous reactions, using the ideal gas law \(pV = nRT \implies p = \dfrac{n}{V}RT = cRT = [\mathrm{gas}]RT\), where \(c\) is in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\), \(p\) is in \(\mathrm{bar}\), and \(R = 0.0831\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) (note that \(1\,\mathrm{bar} = 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} = 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)), the partial pressure equilibrium constant \(K_p\) for \(a\mathrm{A(g)} + b\mathrm{B(g)} \rightleftharpoons c\mathrm{C(g)} + d\mathrm{D(g)}\) is:

\[K_p = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{C}})^c(p_{\mathrm{D}})^d}{(p_{\mathrm{A}})^a(p_{\mathrm{B}})^b} = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c[\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a[\mathrm{B}]^b}(RT)^{(c+d)-(a+b)} \implies K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}\]

where \(\Delta n = (\text{number of moles of gaseous products}) - (\text{number of moles of gaseous reactants})\) in the balanced chemical equation. For \(\mathrm{H_2(g) + I_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2HI(g)}\), \(\Delta n = 2 - 2 = 0\), so \(K_p = K_c\). For \(\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}\), \(\Delta n = 2 - 4 = -2\), so \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{-2}\).

In a heterogeneous equilibrium, reactants and products exist in more than one phase. Because the molar concentration (density divided by molar mass) of a pure solid or a pure liquid is constant at a given temperature and independent of the amount present, pure solids and pure liquids are omitted from \(K_c\) and \(K_p\) expressions (though they must be physically present in the container for the equilibrium to exist):

Units and Dimensionless Form of \(K\): When concentrations are substituted in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) and pressures in \(\mathrm{bar}\) (or \(\mathrm{atm}\)), \(K_c\) has units of \((\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})^{\Delta n}\) and \(K_p\) has units of \((\mathrm{bar})^{\Delta n}\). However, when activities are defined relative to standard states—\(p^\circ = 1\,\mathrm{bar}\) for gases and \(c^\circ = 1\,\mathrm{M}\) for solutes—both \(K_p\) and \(K_c\) are strictly dimensionless quantities (though they have different numerical values when \(\Delta n \neq 0\) due to different standard states).

5. Applications of Equilibrium Constants, Gibbs Energy & Le Chatelier's Principle

5.1 Predicting the Extent and Direction of a Reaction

  • Extent of Reaction (from magnitude of \(K_c\)):
    • If \(K_c > 10^3\), products predominate strongly over reactants and the reaction proceeds nearly to completion. Examples: \(2\mathrm{H_2(g) + O_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2H_2O(g)}\) at \(500\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(K_c = 2.4 \times 10^{47}\)); \(\mathrm{H_2(g) + Cl_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2HCl(g)}\) at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(K_c = 4.0 \times 10^{31}\)); \(\mathrm{H_2(g) + Br_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2HBr(g)}\) at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(K_c = 5.4 \times 10^{18}\)).
    • If \(K_c < 10^{-3}\), reactants predominate strongly over products and the forward reaction proceeds rarely. Examples: decomposition of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) at \(500\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(K_c = 4.1 \times 10^{-48}\)); \(\mathrm{N_2(g) + O_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NO(g)}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(K_c = 4.8 \times 10^{-31}\)).
    • If \(10^{-3} \le K_c \le 10^3\), appreciable concentrations of both reactants and products are present at equilibrium. Examples: \(\mathrm{H_2(g) + I_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2HI(g)}\) at \(700\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(K_c = 57.0\)); \(\mathrm{N_2O_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NO_2(g)}\) at \(25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(K_c = 4.64 \times 10^{-3}\)).
  • Direction of Reaction (using Reaction Quotient \(Q\)): At any arbitrary time \(t\), the reaction quotient \(Q_c = \dfrac{[\mathrm{C}]_t^c[\mathrm{D}]_t^d}{[\mathrm{A}]_t^a[\mathrm{B}]_t^b}\) (or \(Q_p\) with partial pressures) is compared with \(K_c\):
    • If \(Q_c < K_c\): Net reaction proceeds from left to right (forward direction) until \(Q_c = K_c\).
    • If \(Q_c > K_c\): Net reaction proceeds from right to left (reverse direction) until \(Q_c = K_c\).
    • If \(Q_c = K_c\): The reaction mixture is already at equilibrium; no net reaction occurs.

5.2 Relationship Between Equilibrium Constant \(K\), Reaction Quotient \(Q\), and Gibbs Energy \(G\)

The equilibrium constant does not depend on the rate of reaction, but is directly governed by reaction thermodynamics (\(\Delta G\)):

  • If \(\Delta G < 0\), the reaction is spontaneous and proceeds in the forward direction.
  • If \(\Delta G > 0\), the forward reaction is non-spontaneous and the reverse reaction proceeds spontaneously.
  • If \(\Delta G = 0\), the reaction has achieved equilibrium.

At any stage of the reaction, \(\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln Q\). At equilibrium, \(\Delta G = 0\) and \(Q = K\), yielding:

\[\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K = -2.303\,RT \log K \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad K = e^{-\Delta G^\circ / RT}\]

Thus, if \(\Delta G^\circ < 0\), then \(K > 1\) (products predominate at equilibrium). If \(\Delta G^\circ > 0\), then \(K < 1\) (reactants predominate at equilibrium).

5.3 Le Chatelier's Principle and Factors Affecting Equilibria

Le Chatelier's Principle states that a change in any of the factors that determine the equilibrium conditions of a system will cause the system to change in such a manner so as to reduce or to counteract the effect of the change.

6. Ionic Equilibrium: Concepts of Acids, Bases, and Salts

Michael Faraday classified substances into electrolytes (which conduct electricity in aqueous solution due to ions) and non-electrolytes (such as sugar, which do not conduct). Electrolytes are further divided into strong electrolytes (almost \(100\%\) ionized in water, e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl, HCl, NaOH}\)) and weak electrolytes (\(<5\%\) ionized, e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH, NH_3}\), where an ionic equilibrium is established between ions and unionized molecules).

Water has a very high dielectric constant of \(80\), which reduces electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions (such as \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\)) by a factor of \(80\) and stabilizes the separated ions via hydration.

6.1 Three Classical Theories of Acids and Bases

  1. Arrhenius Concept: Acids dissociate in water to give hydrogen ions \(\mathrm{H^+(aq)}\) (or hydronium ions \(\mathrm{H_3O^+(aq)}\)), while bases dissociate in water to give hydroxyl ions \(\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\). Because a bare proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\), radius \(\sim 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\)) has an intense electric field, it bonds to a lone pair on a water oxygen atom to form a trigonal pyramidal hydronium ion \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\), which is further hydrated in solution to species like \(\mathrm{H_5O_2^+, H_7O_3^+,}\) and \(\mathrm{H_9O_4^+}\). Similarly, \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) is hydrated to \(\mathrm{H_3O_2^-, H_5O_3^-, H_7O_4^-}\). Limitation: Restricted to aqueous solutions and cannot explain the basicity of substances lacking an \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group (such as \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)).
  2. Brönsted–Lowry Concept (1923): An acid is a proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) donor and a base is a proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) acceptor. An acid-base pair that differs only by one proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) is called a conjugate acid-base pair: \[\underbrace{\mathrm{NH_3(aq)}}_{\text{Base}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{H_2O(l)}}_{\text{Acid}} \rightleftharpoons \underbrace{\mathrm{NH_4^+(aq)}}_{\text{Conjugate acid}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{OH^-(aq)}}_{\text{Conjugate base}}\] \[\underbrace{\mathrm{HCl(aq)}}_{\text{Acid}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{H_2O(l)}}_{\text{Base}} \rightleftharpoons \underbrace{\mathrm{H_3O^+(aq)}}_{\text{Conjugate acid}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)}}_{\text{Conjugate base}}\]
    • \(\text{Conjugate Acid} = \text{Base} + \mathrm{H^+}\); \(\quad \text{Conjugate Base} = \text{Acid} - \mathrm{H^+}\).
    • Reciprocal Strength Rule: A strong Brönsted acid (e.g., \(\mathrm{HClO_4, HCl, HBr, HI, HNO_3, H_2SO_4}\)) has a very weak conjugate base (\(\mathrm{ClO_4^-, Cl^-, Br^-, I^-, NO_3^-, HSO_4^-}\)), which is a much weaker base than \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). Conversely, a weak acid or very weak proton donor has a strong conjugate base (e.g., \(\mathrm{NH_2^-, O^{2-}, H^-}\) are much stronger bases than \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)). Every proton-transfer equilibrium favours the formation of the weaker acid and weaker base.
    • Amphoteric Species: Species like \(\mathrm{H_2O, HCO_3^-, HSO_4^-,}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) can act as both Brönsted acids and Brönsted bases.
  3. Lewis Concept (G.N. Lewis, 1923): A Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a Lewis base is an electron-pair donor. \[\underbrace{\mathrm{BF_3}}_{\text{Lewis acid}} + \underbrace{\mathrm{:NH_3}}_{\text{Lewis base}} \longrightarrow \mathrm{BF_3 \leftarrow :NH_3}\]
    • Common Lewis Acids: Electron-deficient molecules (\(\mathrm{BF_3, BCl_3, AlCl_3}\)) and cations (\(\mathrm{H^+, Co^{3+}, Mg^{2+}, Ag^+}\)). Note that \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\) has no vacant orbital to accept a lone pair, so it is a Brönsted acid but not a Lewis acid.
    • Common Lewis Bases: Neutral molecules with lone pairs (\(\mathrm{H_2O, NH_3, RNH_2}\)) and anions (\(\mathrm{OH^-, F^-, CN^-, Cl^-}\)).

6.2 Trends in Acid Strength (\(\mathrm{H\text{–}A}\) Bond Strength vs. Polarity)

The acid strength of \(\mathrm{HA}\) depends on two factors: the strength of the \(\mathrm{H\text{–}A}\) bond and the polarity of the \(\mathrm{H\text{–}A}\) bond:

  • Down a Group in the Periodic Table: \(\mathrm{H\text{–}A}\) bond strength is the deciding factor. As atomic size of \(\mathrm{A}\) increases down a group, \(\mathrm{H\text{–}A}\) bond strength decreases, cleavage becomes easier, and acid strength increases: \[\text{Group 17: } \mathrm{HF \ll HCl \ll HBr \ll HI} \qquad \text{Group 16: } \mathrm{H_2O < H_2S}\]
  • Across a Period (Same Row) in the Periodic Table: \(\mathrm{H\text{–}A}\) bond polarity (electronegativity of \(\mathrm{A}\)) is the deciding factor. As electronegativity of \(\mathrm{A}\) increases across a row, bond polarity and acidity increase: \[\text{Period 2: } \mathrm{CH_4 < NH_3 < H_2O < HF}\]

7. Ionization of Water, the pH Scale, and Weak Electrolytes

7.1 Self-Ionization of Water and Ionic Product (\(K_w\))

Pure water undergoes auto-protolysis where one water molecule acts as an acid and another as a base:

\[\mathrm{H_2O(l) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)}\]

Incorporating the constant molarity of pure liquid water (\([\mathrm{H_2O}] = \dfrac{1000\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}}{18.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 55.55\,\mathrm{M}\)) into the equilibrium constant gives the ionic product of water (\(K_w\)):

\[K_w = [\mathrm{H_3O^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] = [\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{M^2} \quad (\text{at } 298\,\mathrm{K})\]

In pure water at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), \([\mathrm{H^+}] = [\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\), so the ratio of dissociated to undissociated water is \(\dfrac{10^{-7}}{55.55} = 1.8 \times 10^{-9}\). Because auto-ionization of water is endothermic, \(K_w\) increases with temperature (e.g., at \(310\,\mathrm{K}\), \(K_w = 2.7 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{M^2}\)).

7.2 The pH Scale

The \(\mathrm{pH}\) of a solution is defined as the negative base-10 logarithm of the hydrogen ion activity (\(a_{\mathrm{H^+}} = [\mathrm{H^+}] / \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) in dilute solutions \(< 0.01\,\mathrm{M}\)):

\[\mathrm{pH} = -\log a_{\mathrm{H^+}} \approx -\log[\mathrm{H^+}], \qquad \mathrm{pOH} = -\log[\mathrm{OH^-}], \qquad \mathrm{p}K_w = \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14 \quad (\text{at } 298\,\mathrm{K})\]
  • At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): Acidic solution: \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] > [\mathrm{OH^-}] \implies \mathrm{pH} < 7\); Neutral solution: \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = [\mathrm{OH^-}] \implies \mathrm{pH} = 7\); Basic solution: \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] < [\mathrm{OH^-}] \implies \mathrm{pH} > 7\).
  • Because the \(\mathrm{pH}\) scale is logarithmic, decreasing \(\mathrm{pH}\) by \(1\) unit corresponds to a \(10\)-fold increase in \([\mathrm{H^+}]\), and a change of \(2\) units corresponds to a \(100\)-fold change in \([\mathrm{H^+}]\).
  • Acid-Base Indicators: Water-soluble weak organic acids (like phenolphthalein and bromothymol blue) exhibit different colours in their unionized acid (\(\mathrm{HIn}\), colour A) and conjugate base (\(\mathrm{In^-}\), colour B) forms: \(\mathrm{HIn(aq) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+(aq) + In^-(aq)}\).

7.3 Ionization of Weak Acids (\(K_a\)), Weak Bases (\(K_b\)), and Polyprotic Acids

For a weak monoprotic acid \(\mathrm{HX}\) of initial molarity \(c\) and degree of ionization \(\alpha\): \(\mathrm{HX(aq) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+(aq) + X^-(aq)}\). At equilibrium, \([\mathrm{HX}] = c(1-\alpha)\) and \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = [\mathrm{X^-}] = c\alpha\). Similarly, for a weak monoacidic base \(\mathrm{MOH(aq) \rightleftharpoons M^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)}\):

\[K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{X^-}]}{[\mathrm{HX}]} = \frac{c\alpha^2}{1-\alpha} \approx c\alpha^2 \quad (\text{if } \alpha \ll 1) \implies \alpha \approx \sqrt{\frac{K_a}{c}}, \quad [\mathrm{H^+}] = c\alpha \approx \sqrt{K_a c}, \quad \mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a\] \[K_b = \frac{[\mathrm{M^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}]}{[\mathrm{MOH}]} = \frac{c\alpha^2}{1-\alpha} \approx c\alpha^2 \quad (\text{if } \alpha \ll 1) \implies \alpha \approx \sqrt{\frac{K_b}{c}}, \quad [\mathrm{OH^-}] = c\alpha \approx \sqrt{K_b c}, \quad \mathrm{p}K_b = -\log K_b\]

This relationship (\(\alpha = \sqrt{K_a/c}\)) is known as Ostwald's Dilution Law: as the concentration \(c\) decreases (upon dilution), the degree of ionization \(\alpha\) of a weak electrolyte increases. Percent dissociation is defined as \(\dfrac{[\mathrm{HA}]_{\mathrm{dissociated}}}{[\mathrm{HA}]_{\mathrm{initial}}} \times 100\% = \alpha \times 100\%\).

Relation Between \(K_a\) and \(K_b\) for a Conjugate Acid–Base Pair: Adding the ionization of a weak base \(\mathrm{B}\) and its conjugate acid \(\mathrm{BH^+}\) yields the auto-ionization of water (\(2\mathrm{H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + OH^-}\)):

\[K_a \times K_b = K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathrm{p}K_a + \mathrm{p}K_b = \mathrm{p}K_w = 14 \quad (\text{at } 298\,\mathrm{K})\]

Di- and Polybasic Acids: Acids with more than one ionizable proton per molecule (such as oxalic acid \(\mathrm{H_2C_2O_4}\), \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), \(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\), \(\mathrm{H_2S}\), citric acid, and \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)) ionize in successive steps characterized by \(K_{a_1}, K_{a_2}, K_{a_3}\). Always:

\[K_{a_1} \gg K_{a_2} \gg K_{a_3}\]

because it is electrostatically much harder to remove a positively charged \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ion from a negatively charged ion (such as \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\) or \(\mathrm{H_2PO_4^-}\)) or doubly charged ion (\(\mathrm{HPO_4^{2-}}\)) than from a neutral molecule (\(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\) or \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)). Consequently, in an aqueous solution of a diprotic acid \(\mathrm{H_2A}\), \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}]\) comes almost entirely from the first dissociation step.

8. Common Ion Effect, Salt Hydrolysis, Buffers, and Solubility Product (\(K_{\mathrm{sp}}\))

8.1 Common Ion Effect in Weak Electrolytes

The common ion effect is a shift in an ionic equilibrium caused by adding a strong electrolyte that supplies an ionic species already present in the dissociation equilibrium. For example, adding \(\mathrm{CH_3COONa}\) (or \(\mathrm{HCl}\)) to \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH(aq) \rightleftharpoons H^+(aq) + CH_3COO^-(aq)}\) suppresses the degree of ionization of acetic acid in accordance with Le Chatelier's principle.

8.2 Hydrolysis of Salts and \(\mathrm{pH}\) of Salt Solutions

Hydrolysis is the interaction of cations, anions, or both of a salt with water to reform the parent weak acid or weak base. We classify salts into four types (at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)):

8.3 Buffer Solutions and the Henderson–Hasselbalch Equation

Solutions that resist changes in \(\mathrm{pH}\) upon dilution or upon addition of small amounts of acid or alkali are called buffer solutions. Human blood is naturally buffered around \(\mathrm{pH}\ 7.4\).

  • Acidic Buffer (weak acid \(\mathrm{HA}\) + its salt with a strong base, e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH + CH_3COONa}\), buffer around \(\mathrm{pH}\ 4.76\)):
    \[\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{Conjugate base, }\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\text{Acid, }\mathrm{HA}]} \approx \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{Salt}]}{[\text{Acid}]}\]
  • Basic Buffer (weak base \(\mathrm{B}\) + its salt with a strong acid, e.g., \(\mathrm{NH_3 + NH_4Cl}\), buffer around \(\mathrm{pH}\ 9.25\)):
    \[\mathrm{pOH} = \mathrm{p}K_b + \log\frac{[\text{Conjugate acid, }\mathrm{BH^+}]}{[\text{Base, }\mathrm{B}]} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a(\text{of }\mathrm{BH^+}) - \log\frac{[\mathrm{BH^+}]}{[\mathrm{B}]}\]

    (Note: Using \(\mathrm{p}K_w - \mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_w - \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\mathrm{BH^+}]}{[\mathrm{B}]}\), when \([\mathrm{BH^+}] = [\mathrm{B}]\), \(\mathrm{pOH} = \mathrm{p}K_b = 4.75\) and \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a = 9.25\). Dilution does not change buffer \(\mathrm{pH}\) because the concentration ratio inside the logarithm remains constant.)

8.4 Solubility Equilibria of Sparingly Soluble Salts (\(K_{\mathrm{sp}}\))

A salt dissolves in a solvent only if its solvation enthalpy (always negative) overcomes its lattice enthalpy. Salts are classified by molar solubility \(S\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) into: Category I (Soluble): \(S > 0.1\,\mathrm{M}\); Category II (Slightly Soluble): \(0.01\,\mathrm{M} < S < 0.1\,\mathrm{M}\); and Category III (Sparingly Soluble): \(S < 0.01\,\mathrm{M}\).

For a general sparingly soluble salt \(\mathrm{M}_x\mathrm{X}_y(\mathrm{s}) \rightleftharpoons x\mathrm{M}^{p+}(\mathrm{aq}) + y\mathrm{X}^{q-}(\mathrm{aq})\) (where \(x p^+ = y q^-\)) with molar solubility \(S\) (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)):

\[K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{M}^{p+}]^x[\mathrm{X}^{q-}]^y = (xS)^x(yS)^y = x^x \cdot y^y \cdot S^{(x+y)} \implies S = \left(\frac{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}{x^x \cdot y^y}\right)^{\frac{1}{x+y}}\]
  • \(1:1\) salt (\(\mathrm{AB}\), e.g., \(\mathrm{AgCl, BaSO_4, AgCN}\)): \(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = S^2 \implies S = \sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}\)
  • \(1:2\) or \(2:1\) salt (\(\mathrm{AB_2}\) or \(\mathrm{A_2B}\), e.g., \(\mathrm{PbCl_2, Ni(OH)_2, Ag_2CrO_4, Hg_2I_2}\) [which dissociates as \(\mathrm{Hg_2^{2+} + 2I^-}\)]): \(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 4S^3 \implies S = (K_{\mathrm{sp}}/4)^{1/3}\)
  • \(1:3\) salt (\(\mathrm{AB_3}\), e.g., \(\mathrm{Fe(OH)_3, Al(OH)_3}\)): \(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 27S^4 \implies S = (K_{\mathrm{sp}}/27)^{1/4}\)
  • \(2:3\) salt (\(\mathrm{A_2X_3}\)): \(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = (2S)^2(3S)^3 = 108S^5\); \(\quad 3:4\) salt (\(\mathrm{Zr_3(PO_4)_4}\)): \(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = (3S)^3(4S)^4 = 6912S^7\)

  • If ionic product \(Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}\), precipitation occurs until \(Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = K_{\mathrm{sp}}\). If \(Q_{\mathrm{sp}} < K_{\mathrm{sp}}\), the solution is unsaturated (no precipitation).
  • Adding a common ion decreases the molar solubility of a sparingly soluble salt (e.g., passing \(\mathrm{HCl}\) gas through saturated \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) solution precipitates high-purity \(\mathrm{NaCl}\); gravimetric estimation of \(\mathrm{Ag^+}\) as \(\mathrm{AgCl}\), \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) as \(\mathrm{Fe(OH)_3}\), and \(\mathrm{Ba^{2+}}\) as \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\)).
  • Effect of \(\mathrm{pH}\) on Solubility of Salts of Weak Acids (\(\mathrm{MX}\)): For a salt \(\mathrm{MX(s) \rightleftharpoons M^+(aq) + X^-(aq)}\) where \(\mathrm{HX}\) is a weak acid with dissociation constant \(K_a\), lowering the \(\mathrm{pH}\) (increasing \([\mathrm{H^+}]\)) protonates \(\mathrm{X^-}\) to \(\mathrm{HX}\). The fraction of anion remaining unprotonated as \(\mathrm{X^-}\) is \(f = \dfrac{[\mathrm{X^-}]}{[\mathrm{X^-}] + [\mathrm{HX}]} = \dfrac{K_a}{K_a + [\mathrm{H^+}]}\). Since \(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{M^+}][\mathrm{X^-}] = (S)(fS) = S^2\left(\dfrac{K_a}{K_a + [\mathrm{H^+]}}\right)\), the molar solubility \(S\) at any \(\mathrm{pH}\) is given by:
    \[S = \left[\frac{K_{\mathrm{sp}}([\mathrm{H^+}] + K_a)}{K_a}\right]^{1/2} = \sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp}}\left(1 + \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}]}{K_a}\right)}\]
    Thus, solubility \(S\) increases as \([\mathrm{H^+}]\) increases (\(\mathrm{pH}\) decreases).

9. Step-by-Step Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Calculating \(K_p\), \(K_c\), and Equilibrium Partial Pressures from Total Pressure

Problem: \(13.8\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\) was placed in a \(1\,\mathrm{L}\) reaction vessel at \(400\,\mathrm{K}\) and allowed to attain the equilibrium \(\mathrm{N_2O_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NO_2(g)}\). The total pressure at equilibrium was found to be \(9.15\,\mathrm{bar}\). Calculate \(K_p\), \(K_c\), and the partial pressures at equilibrium. (Given: Molar mass of \(\mathrm{N_2O_4} = 92\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), \(R = 0.083\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

Step 1: Find initial pressure of \(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\) using the ideal gas law.
Number of moles of \(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\), \(n = \dfrac{13.8\,\mathrm{g}}{92\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 0.15\,\mathrm{mol}\).
Initial pressure \(p_0 = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{0.15\,\mathrm{mol} \times 0.083\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \times 400\,\mathrm{K}}{1\,\mathrm{L}} = 4.98\,\mathrm{bar}\).

Step 2: Set up the ICE table in terms of partial pressures (bar).
For \(\mathrm{N_2O_4(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NO_2(g)}\), let \(x\,\mathrm{bar}\) be the decrease in partial pressure of \(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\). At equilibrium:
\(p_{\mathrm{N_2O_4}} = (4.98 - x)\,\mathrm{bar}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{NO_2}} = 2x\,\mathrm{bar}\).
Total pressure at equilibrium: \(p_{\mathrm{total}} = (4.98 - x) + 2x = 4.98 + x = 9.15\,\mathrm{bar} \implies x = 9.15 - 4.98 = 4.17\,\mathrm{bar}\).
Therefore, \(p_{\mathrm{N_2O_4}} = 4.98 - 4.17 = \mathbf{0.81\,\mathrm{bar}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{NO_2}} = 2 \times 4.17 = \mathbf{8.34\,\mathrm{bar}}\).

Step 3: Calculate \(K_p\) and \(K_c\).
\[K_p = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{NO_2}})^2}{p_{\mathrm{N_2O_4}}} = \frac{(8.34)^2}{0.81} = \mathbf{85.87}\] Since \(\Delta n = 2 - 1 = 1\) and \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}\): \[85.87 = K_c(0.083 \times 400)^1 \implies K_c = \frac{85.87}{33.2} = 2.586 \approx \mathbf{2.6}\]

Worked Example 2: \(\mathrm{pH}\) of an Extremely Dilute Strong Acid Solution

Problem: Calculate the \(\mathrm{pH}\) of a \(1.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{HCl}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\).

Solution:

If we naively wrote \(\mathrm{pH} = -\log(10^{-8}) = 8\), an acidic solution would appear basic, which is physically impossible! In very dilute acid solutions (\([\mathrm{HA}] < 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{M}\)), the \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\) contribution from the auto-ionization of water cannot be neglected.

Let \(x = [\mathrm{OH^-}]\) generated from \(\mathrm{2H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)}\). Since \(\mathrm{HCl}\) is completely ionized (\([\mathrm{H_3O^+}]_{\mathrm{HCl}} = 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}\)), the total hydronium concentration at equilibrium is:
\[[\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = (10^{-8} + x)\,\mathrm{M} \quad \text{and} \quad [\mathrm{OH^-}] = x\,\mathrm{M}\] Substituting into \(K_w = [\mathrm{H_3O^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] = 10^{-14}\): \[(10^{-8} + x)(x) = 10^{-14} \implies x^2 + 10^{-8}x - 10^{-14} = 0\] Solving the quadratic equation gives \(x = \dfrac{-10^{-8} + \sqrt{10^{-16} + 4 \times 10^{-14}}}{2} = 9.5 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M} = [\mathrm{OH^-}]\).
Therefore, \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = 10^{-8} + 9.5 \times 10^{-8} = 1.05 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\), or \(\mathrm{pOH} = -\log(9.5 \times 10^{-8}) = 7.02\), giving \(\mathrm{pH} = 14 - 7.02 = \mathbf{6.98}\).

Worked Example 3: Partial Neutralization of a Weak Base to Form a Basic Buffer

Problem: Calculate (a) the \(\mathrm{pH}\) of a \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\) ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)) solution, and (b) the \(\mathrm{pH}\) after \(50.0\,\mathrm{mL}\) of this solution is treated with \(25.0\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\ \mathrm{HCl}\). Given: \(K_b(\mathrm{NH_3}) = 1.77 \times 10^{-5}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\).

Solution:

Part (a) Before neutralization: For \(\mathrm{NH_3 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+ + OH^-}\), let \([\mathrm{NH_4^+}] = [\mathrm{OH^-}] = x\). Since \(x \ll 0.10\):
\[K_b = \frac{x^2}{0.10} = 1.77 \times 10^{-5} \implies x = [\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.33 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{M}\] \[[\mathrm{H^+}] = \frac{10^{-14}}{1.33 \times 10^{-3}} = 7.51 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{M} \implies \mathrm{pH} = -\log(7.51 \times 10^{-12}) = \mathbf{11.12}\]

Part (b) After adding \(25.0\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\ \mathrm{HCl}\):
Initial millimoles of \(\mathrm{NH_3} = 50.0\,\mathrm{mL} \times 0.10\,\mathrm{M} = 5.0\,\mathrm{mmol}\).
Added millimoles of \(\mathrm{HCl} = 25.0\,\mathrm{mL} \times 0.10\,\mathrm{M} = 2.5\,\mathrm{mmol}\).
The neutralization reaction \(\mathrm{NH_3 + HCl \longrightarrow NH_4^+ + Cl^-}\) consumes \(2.5\,\mathrm{mmol}\) of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and produces \(2.5\,\mathrm{mmol}\) of \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\) in a total volume of \(50.0 + 25.0 = 75.0\,\mathrm{mL}\):
\[[\mathrm{NH_3}]_{\text{remaining}} = \frac{2.5\,\mathrm{mmol}}{75.0\,\mathrm{mL}} = 0.033\,\mathrm{M}, \qquad [\mathrm{NH_4^+}]_{\text{formed}} = \frac{2.5\,\mathrm{mmol}}{75.0\,\mathrm{mL}} = 0.033\,\mathrm{M}\] Substituting into the base ionization equilibrium \(K_b = \dfrac{[\mathrm{NH_4^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}]}{[\mathrm{NH_3}]}\): \[1.77 \times 10^{-5} = \frac{(0.033)[\mathrm{OH^-}]}{(0.033)} \implies [\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.77 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{M}\] \[[\mathrm{H^+}] = \frac{10^{-14}}{1.77 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.65 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{M} \implies \mathrm{pH} = -\log(5.65 \times 10^{-10}) = \mathbf{9.25}\ \text{(or } 9.24\text{)}\]

Worked Example 4: Solubility of a Salt of a Weak Acid in a Buffer Solution

Problem: The ionization constant of benzoic acid (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5COOH}\)) is \(K_a = 6.46 \times 10^{-5}\) and \(K_{\mathrm{sp}}\) for silver benzoate (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5COOAg}\)) is \(2.5 \times 10^{-13}\). How many times is silver benzoate more soluble in a buffer of \(\mathrm{pH}\ 3.19\) compared to its solubility in pure water?

Solution:

Step 1: Solubility in pure water (\(S_0\)).
Silver benzoate is a \(1:1\) salt (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5COOAg(s) \rightleftharpoons C_6H_5COO^-(aq) + Ag^+(aq)}\)): \[S_0 = \sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp}}} = \sqrt{2.5 \times 10^{-13}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\]

Step 2: Solubility in a buffer of \(\mathrm{pH}\ 3.19\) (\(S_{\text{buffer}}\)).
At \(\mathrm{pH} = 3.19\), \([\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-3.19} = 6.46 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{M}\).
Using the \(\mathrm{pH}\)-dependent solubility equation \(S_{\text{buffer}} = \sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp}}\left(\dfrac{[\mathrm{H^+}] + K_a}{K_a}\right)}\): \[\frac{S_{\text{buffer}}}{S_0} = \sqrt{\frac{[\mathrm{H^+}] + K_a}{K_a}} = \sqrt{\frac{6.46 \times 10^{-4} + 6.46 \times 10^{-5}}{6.46 \times 10^{-5}}} = \sqrt{10 + 1} = \sqrt{11} \approx \mathbf{3.32}\] Thus, silver benzoate is \(3.32\) times more soluble in the \(\mathrm{pH}\ 3.19\) buffer than in pure water.

At a certain temperature \(T\), the equilibrium constant \(K_c\) for the reaction \(\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g)}\) is \(64\,\mathrm{L^2\,mol^{-2}}\). What will be the value of the equilibrium constant \(K'_c\) for the decomposition reaction \(\mathrm{NH_3(g) \rightleftharpoons \frac{1}{2}N_2(g) + \frac{3}{2}H_2(g)}\) at the same temperature?

  • (2) \(0.125\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)
  • (3) \(0.0156\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)
  • (4) \(32.0\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (2) \(0.125\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)

Worked Solution: The target equation \(\mathrm{NH_3(g) \rightleftharpoons \frac{1}{2}N_2(g) + \frac{3}{2}H_2(g)}\) is obtained by reversing the given synthesis equation and multiplying its stoichiometric coefficients by \(\frac{1}{2}\).

  • (4) Since \(\mathrm{FeO(s)}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe(s)}\) are solids, \(K_p \neq K_c\) and the reaction is already at equilibrium.

  • (1) \(2.2 : 1\)
  • (2) \(91.9 : 1\)
  • (3) \(0.011 : 1\)
  • (4) \(1.48 : 1\)
Correct Answer: (2) \(91.9 : 1\)

Worked Solution:

  • \(\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4(s) \rightleftharpoons 2Ag^+(aq) + CrO_4^{2-}(aq)}\) is a \(2:1\) salt: \[K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}) = (2S_1)^2(S_1) = 4S_1^3 = 1.1 \times 10^{-12} \implies S_1 = \left(\frac{1.1 \times 10^{-12}}{4}\right)^{1/3} = (2.75 \times 10^{-13})^{1/3} = 6.50 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{M}\]
  • \(\mathrm{AgBr(s) \rightleftharpoons Ag^+(aq) + Br^-(aq)}\) is a \(1:1\) salt: \[K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{AgBr}) = S_2^2 = 5.0 \times 10^{-13} \implies S_2 = \sqrt{5.0 \times 10^{-13}} = 7.07 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\]
Ratio \(\dfrac{S_1}{S_2} = \dfrac{6.50 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{M}}{7.07 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}} = 91.9\).

Practice Question 12 (Single-Correct MCQ • Selective Sulphide Precipitation Criterion)

The concentration of sulphide ion (\(\mathrm{S^{2-}}\)) in a \(0.1\,\mathrm{M}\ \mathrm{HCl}\) solution saturated with \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) is \(1.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{M}\). If \(10\,\mathrm{mL}\) of this solution is added to \(5\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.04\,\mathrm{M}\) separate solutions of \(\mathrm{FeSO_4}\), \(\mathrm{MnCl_2}\), \(\mathrm{ZnCl_2}\), and \(\mathrm{CdCl_2}\), in which solutions will precipitation of the metal sulphide occur?
(Given \(K_{\mathrm{sp}}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \(\mathrm{FeS} = 6.3 \times 10^{-18}\), \(\mathrm{MnS} = 2.5 \times 10^{-13}\), \(\mathrm{ZnS} = 1.6 \times 10^{-24}\), \(\mathrm{CdS} = 8.0 \times 10^{-27}\)).

  • (1) All four solutions (\(\mathrm{FeSO_4, MnCl_2, ZnCl_2,}\) and \(\mathrm{CdCl_2}\))
  • (2) \(\mathrm{FeSO_4}\) and \(\mathrm{MnCl_2}\) only
  • (3) \(\mathrm{ZnCl_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CdCl_2}\) only
  • (4) \(\mathrm{CdCl_2}\) only
Correct Answer: (3) \(\mathrm{ZnCl_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CdCl_2}\) only

Worked Solution: Upon mixing \(10\,\mathrm{mL}\) of the \(\mathrm{H_2S/HCl}\) solution with \(5\,\mathrm{mL}\) of the \(0.04\,\mathrm{M}\) metal salt solution, the total volume becomes \(15\,\mathrm{mL}\). Immediately after mixing:
\[[\mathrm{M^{2+}}] = 0.04\,\mathrm{M} \times \frac{5\,\mathrm{mL}}{15\,\mathrm{mL}} = 1.33 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{M}, \qquad [\mathrm{S^{2-}}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{M} \times \frac{10\,\mathrm{mL}}{15\,\mathrm{mL}} = 6.67 \times 10^{-20}\,\mathrm{M}\] Therefore, the ionic product \(Q_{\mathrm{sp}}\) in each mixture is: \[Q_{\mathrm{sp}} = [\mathrm{M^{2+}}][\mathrm{S^{2-}}] = (1.33 \times 10^{-2})(6.67 \times 10^{-20}) = 8.87 \times 10^{-22}\] Precipitation occurs only when \(Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}\):

  • For \(\mathrm{FeS}\) (\(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 6.3 \times 10^{-18}\)) and \(\mathrm{MnS}\) (\(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 2.5 \times 10^{-13}\)): \(Q_{\mathrm{sp}} < K_{\mathrm{sp}}\) \(\to\) no precipitation.
  • For \(\mathrm{ZnS}\) (\(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 1.6 \times 10^{-24}\)) and \(\mathrm{CdS}\) (\(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 8.0 \times 10^{-27}\)): \(Q_{\mathrm{sp}} > K_{\mathrm{sp}}\) \(\to\) precipitation takes place.

11. Common Pitfalls & High-Yield NEET UG Revision Checklist

Top 6 Exam Pitfalls to Avoid in NEET UG
  1. Counting Solids/Liquids in \(\Delta n\): When computing \(\Delta n\) for \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}\), count only gaseous species! Never include pure solids (\(\mathrm{s}\)) or pure liquids (\(\mathrm{l}\)).
  2. Mismatching \(R\) and Pressure Units: Use \(R = 0.0831\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) when \(K_p\) is in \(\mathrm{bar}\), \(R = 0.0821\,\mathrm{atm\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) when \(K_p\) is in \(\mathrm{atm}\), and \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) when calculating \(\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K\) in Joules.
  3. Confusing Inert Gas Addition at Constant \(V\) vs. Constant \(P\): Adding an inert gas at constant volume never shifts any equilibrium. Only at constant pressure (which increases volume) does it shift the reaction toward the side with more moles of gas.
  4. Forgetting Water Auto-Ionization in Dilute Acids/Bases: For \(10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\) or \(10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}\) strong acid, \(\mathrm{pH}\) is always slightly less than \(7\) (e.g., \(6.98\) for \(10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}\ \mathrm{HCl}\)), never \(> 7\).
  5. Comparing \(K_{\mathrm{sp}}\) Directly for Salts of Different Stoichiometry: You can compare \(K_{\mathrm{sp}}\) values directly to rank molar solubility only if the salts have the exact same formula type (e.g., \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) vs. \(\mathrm{AgBr}\)). For \(\mathrm{Ni(OH)_2}\) (\(1:2\), \(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = 4S^3\)) vs. \(\mathrm{AgCN}\) (\(1:1\), \(K_{\mathrm{sp}} = S^2\)), always calculate \(S\) explicitly!
  6. Forgetting Volume Dilution When Mixing Solutions: In precipitation (\(Q_{\mathrm{sp}}\)) or acid-base neutralization problems, always divide millimoles by the combined total volume (\(V_1 + V_2\)).

Concise Final Formula Recap

  • Equilibrium Constants & Thermodynamics: \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}\); \(\quad \Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q\); \(\quad \Delta G^\circ = -2.303\,RT\log K\).
  • Water & Weak Electrolytes (\(298\,\mathrm{K}\)): \(K_w = [\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] = 10^{-14}\); \(\quad \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14\); \(\quad K_a \cdot K_b = K_w\); \(\quad K_a = \dfrac{c\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}\).
  • Salt Hydrolysis (\(298\,\mathrm{K}\)): Weak acid + strong base: \(\mathrm{pH} = 7 + \frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{p}K_a + \log c)\); \(\quad\) Strong acid + weak base: \(\mathrm{pH} = 7 - \frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{p}K_b + \log c)\); \(\quad\) Weak acid + weak base: \(\mathrm{pH} = 7 + \frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{p}K_a - \mathrm{p}K_b)\).
  • Buffers & Solubility: \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\dfrac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}\); \(\quad K_{\mathrm{sp}}(\mathrm{M}_x\mathrm{X}_y) = x^x y^y S^{x+y}\); \(\quad S(\text{at }\mathrm{pH}) = \sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp}}\left(1 + \dfrac{[\mathrm{H^+}]}{K_a}\right)}\).
