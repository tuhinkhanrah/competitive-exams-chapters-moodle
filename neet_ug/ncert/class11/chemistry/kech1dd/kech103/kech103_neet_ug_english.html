NEET UG Chemistry • Inorganic Periodicity

Classification of Elements and Periodicity in Properties

A complete, self-contained conceptual guide with trend mechanisms, high-yield exceptions, worked examples, and NEET UG practice questions.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Roadmap

In NEET UG Chemistry, questions from periodicity test both fundamental electronic logic and memorized exceptions. Rather than treating each property as an isolated rule, this tutorial connects atomic structure—specifically nuclear charge, principal quantum number, and electron shielding—to every periodic trend.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Trace the shift from atomic-weight classification (Dobereiner, Newlands, Mendeleev) to Moseley’s atomic-number basis and the Modern Periodic Law.
  • Assign IUPAC systematic temporary names and three-letter symbols for superheavy elements with atomic number \(Z > 100\).
  • Locate any element in the \(s\)-, \(p\)-, \(d\)-, or \(f\)-block using its ground-state electronic configuration and identify key exceptions.
  • Predict and explain periodic trends—and their NEET-critical anomalies—in atomic/ionic radii, ionization enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, and electronegativity.
  • Correlate valence, oxidation states, diagonal relationships, and oxide acid–base character with an element’s position in the Periodic Table.

2. Evolution of Periodic Classification & Modern Periodic Law

When the number of known elements grew rapidly in the nineteenth century (from 31 in 1800 to 63 by 1865, and over 114 today including artificially synthesized elements), chemists needed a rational framework to organize chemical behavior and predict undiscovered matter.

Scientist (Year) Core Principle Significance & Limitation
Johann Dobereiner (1829) Law of Triads: Groups of three similar elements where the middle element’s atomic weight is roughly the arithmetic mean of the other two (e.g., Li, Na, K; Ca, Sr, Ba; Cl, Br, I). First recognition of quantitative trends among element families, but applied to only a limited number of known elements.
A.E.B. de Chancourtois (1862) Arranged elements by increasing atomic weight on a cylindrical helix to display recurring properties. Demonstrated geometric periodicity, though it received little immediate attention.
John Newlands (1865) Law of Octaves: When ordered by atomic weight, every eighth element resembled the first, analogous to musical octaves. Worked reliably only up to calcium; failed for heavier elements after the transition series appeared.
Lothar Meyer (1868–1869) Plotted physical properties (atomic volume, melting point, boiling point) against atomic weight, revealing repeating curves of varying length. Independently arrived at a table resembling the modern layout, though published after Mendeleev’s framework.
Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) Mendeleev’s Periodic Law: Properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic weights. Grouped elements by empirical formulas of oxides and hydrides. Left deliberate gaps for undiscovered elements—predicting Eka-aluminium (Gallium) and Eka-silicon (Germanium)—and reversed atomic weight order (e.g., placing Te before I) to preserve chemical similarity.

Moseley’s Breakthrough & the Modern Periodic Law

In 1913, Henry Moseley measured characteristic X-ray spectra of elements and discovered that a plot of the square root of emitted X-ray frequency (\(\sqrt{\nu}\)) against atomic number (\(Z\)) yielded a straight line, whereas plotting \(\sqrt{\nu}\) against atomic mass did not. Because \(Z\) equals the nuclear proton count (and electron count in a neutral atom), atomic number—not atomic mass—governs chemical behavior.

Modern Periodic Law: The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. Periodicity arises directly from the recurring pattern of outermost (valence) electronic configurations.

3. Architecture of the Long Form & IUPAC Nomenclature (\(Z > 100\))

The modern long-form Periodic Table organizes elements into 7 horizontal periods and 18 vertical groups (numbered 1 to 18 under IUPAC recommendations, replacing older Roman-numeral notations).

Periods and Orbital Filling

Each period number equals the highest principal quantum number (\(n\)) of the valence shell being filled. Because each atomic orbital holds at most 2 electrons, the capacity of a period equals twice the number of orbitals energetically available in that shell-filling sequence:

  • Period 1 (\(n=1\)): Fills \(1s\) (1 orbital) → 2 elements (H, He).
  • Period 2 (\(n=2\)): Fills \(2s, 2p\) (4 orbitals) → 8 elements (Li to Ne).
  • Period 3 (\(n=3\)): Fills \(3s, 3p\) (4 orbitals) → 8 elements (Na to Ar).
  • Period 4 (\(n=4\)): Fills \(4s, 3d, 4p\) (9 orbitals) → 18 elements (K to Kr).
  • Period 5 (\(n=5\)): Fills \(5s, 4d, 5p\) (9 orbitals) → 18 elements (Rb to Xe).
  • Period 6 (\(n=6\)): Fills \(6s, 4f, 5d, 6p\) (16 orbitals) → 32 elements (Cs to Rn; includes \(4f\) lanthanoids).
  • Period 7 (\(n=7\)): Fills \(7s, 5f, 6d, 7p\) (16 orbitals) → 32 elements (Fr to Og, \(Z = 118\); includes \(5f\) actinoids).

The Four Blocks & Broad Elemental Classes

Tutorial Diagram 1: Structural blocks of the Periodic Table, showing the \(s\)-, \(p\)-, \(d\)-, and \(f\)-blocks, the special placements of H and He, and the diagonal metalloid boundary.
  • \(s\)-Block (Groups 1–2): Alkali metals (\(ns^1\)) and alkaline earth metals (\(ns^2\)). Low ionization enthalpies, high reactivity (never found free in nature), predominantly ionic compounds (except Li and Be). Exceptions: Hydrogen (\(1s^1\)) resembles both Group 1 and Group 17 and is placed separately; Helium (\(1s^2\)) is placed in Group 18 due to its closed-shell noble gas stability.
  • \(p\)-Block (Groups 13–18): Outer configuration \(ns^2np^{1\text{–}6}\). Together with the \(s\)-block, these form the Representative (Main Group) Elements. Includes chalcogens (Group 16), halogens (Group 17, highly negative electron gain enthalpies), and noble gases (Group 18, closed \(ns^2np^6\) shell, very low reactivity).
  • \(d\)-Block (Groups 3–12): General configuration \((n-1)d^{1\text{–}10}ns^{0\text{–}2}\) (notable exception: Palladium is \(4d^{10}5s^0\)). All are metals; most form colored ions, show variable oxidation states, exhibit paramagnetism, and act as catalysts. However, Zn, Cd, and Hg (\((n-1)d^{10}ns^2\)) lack most typical transition-element properties.
  • \(f\)-Block (Inner-Transition Elements): General configuration \((n-2)f^{1\text{–}14}(n-1)d^{0\text{–}1}ns^2\). Lanthanoids (\(4f\), \(Z=58\text{–}71\)) show closely similar properties within the series; Actinoids (\(5f\), \(Z=90\text{–}103\)) are radioactive and exhibit a wider range of oxidation states. Elements beyond uranium (\(Z > 92\)) are transuranium elements.
  • Metals, Non-metals, and Metalloids: Over 78% of elements are metals (left/center; solids except Hg, with Ga and Cs having low melting points of \(303\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(302\,\mathrm{K}\)). Non-metals (top right) are brittle solids or gases with poor conductivity (except C and B in melting point). Diagonal border elements—Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te—are metalloids.

4. Periodic Trends in Physical Properties & Key Exceptions

Every physical trend is governed by the tug-of-war between nuclear attraction (increasing with \(Z\)) and inter-electronic repulsion / shielding by inner-core electrons. The net pull felt by a valence electron is the effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\)).

A. Atomic and Ionic Radii

Because an electron cloud has no sharp boundary, atomic size is estimated from internuclear distances:

  • Covalent radius (non-metals): Half the single-bond distance in a homonuclear molecule (e.g., \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) bond length \(= 198\,\mathrm{pm} \Rightarrow r_{\mathrm{cov}}(\mathrm{Cl}) = 99\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Metallic radius (metals): Half the internuclear distance between adjacent metal cores in a crystal (e.g., Cu–Cu distance \(= 256\,\mathrm{pm} \Rightarrow r_{\mathrm{met}}(\mathrm{Cu}) = 128\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Noble gas radii: Being monoatomic, noble gases have non-bonded van der Waals radii, which are much larger than the covalent radii of preceding halogens.

Trends: Across a period (left to right), electrons enter the same valence shell while nuclear charge rises, so \(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\) increases and atomic radius decreases (e.g., \(\mathrm{Li} = 152\,\mathrm{pm}\) to \(\mathrm{F} = 64\,\mathrm{pm}\)). Down a group, principal quantum number \(n\) increases and inner shells shield the valence shell, so atomic radius increases (e.g., \(\mathrm{F} = 64\,\mathrm{pm}\) to \(\mathrm{I} = 133\,\mathrm{pm}\)). Note that across the \(3d\) transition series and \(4f\) lanthanoid series, the decrease in radius is much more gradual than in main-group elements.

Ionic Radii & Isoelectronic Species: A cation is always smaller than its parent atom (fewer electrons, same nuclear charge, higher \(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\); e.g., \(\mathrm{Na} = 186\,\mathrm{pm} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na^+} = 95\,\mathrm{pm}\)). An anion is always larger than its parent atom (more electrons, greater inter-electronic repulsion, lower \(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\); e.g., \(\mathrm{F} = 64\,\mathrm{pm} \rightarrow \mathrm{F^-} = 136\,\mathrm{pm}\)).

\[\text{For isoelectronic species (same electron count): } \quad \text{Ionic Radius} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Nuclear Charge }(Z)}\]

Thus, for the 10-electron series: \(\mathrm{N^{3-} > O^{2-} > F^- > Na^+ > Mg^{2+} > Al^{3+}}\).

B. Ionization Enthalpy (\(\Delta_i H\))

Ionization enthalpy is the energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state:

\[\mathrm{X}(g) \longrightarrow \mathrm{X}^+(g) + e^- \qquad (\Delta_i H_1 > 0,\text{ always endothermic})\]

Successive ionization enthalpies always increase (\(\Delta_i H_1 < \Delta_i H_2 < \Delta_i H_3 \dots\)) because removing an electron from an increasingly positive ion requires more energy. A massive jump between two successive \(\Delta_i H\) values signals that all valence electrons have been removed and the next electron comes from a stable noble-gas core.

General Trends: \(\Delta_i H_1\) generally increases across a period (increasing \(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\)) and decreases down a group (increasing atomic size and core shielding). Maxima occur at noble gases; minima occur at alkali metals.

NEET High-Yield Ionization Enthalpy Exceptions

  • Group 2 > Group 13 (\(\mathrm{Be > B}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg > Al}\)): Removing a \(2s\) electron from Be (\(1s^2 2s^2\)) requires more energy than removing a \(2p\) electron from B (\(1s^2 2s^2 2p^1\)) because \(s\)-orbitals penetrate closer to the nucleus and experience weaker shielding than \(p\)-orbitals.
  • Group 15 > Group 16 (\(\mathrm{N > O}\) and \(\mathrm{P > S}\)): Nitrogen has three unpaired \(2p\) electrons in separate orbitals (half-filled stability). Oxygen (\(2p^4\)) has one paired \(2p\) orbital, creating inter-electronic repulsion that lowers the energy needed to remove the fourth electron.
  • Group 13 Anomaly (\(\mathrm{B > Tl > Ga > Al > In}\)): Poor shielding by inner \(3d^{10}\) electrons in Ga and \(4f^{14}5d^{10}\) electrons in Tl increases \(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\), making \(\Delta_i H_1\) of Ga (\(579\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) slightly higher than Al (\(577\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) and Tl (\(589\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) higher than In (\(558\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  • Isotopes: Two isotopes of the same element have identical nuclear charge and electronic configuration, so their \(\Delta_i H_1\) values are virtually identical.

C. Electron Gain Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{eg} H\))

Electron gain enthalpy is the enthalpy change when an electron is added to an isolated neutral gaseous atom:

\[\mathrm{X}(g) + e^- \longrightarrow \mathrm{X}^-(g) \qquad (\Delta_{eg} H\text{ can be negative or positive})\]

Halogens (Group 17) have large negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\) values because gaining one electron completes a noble-gas configuration. Noble gases (Group 18) have large positive \(\Delta_{eg}H\) values (e.g., \(\mathrm{Ne} = +116\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) because the added electron must enter the next higher principal shell. Note also that the second electron gain enthalpy (e.g., \(\mathrm{O}^-(g) + e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{O}^{2-}(g)\)) is always positive (endothermic) due to strong electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged ion and the incoming electron.

NEET High-Yield \(\Delta_{eg} H\) Exception: Period 3 > Period 2 (Magnitude)

Although \(\Delta_{eg}H\) generally becomes less negative down a group, Chlorine (\(-349\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) has a more negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\) than Fluorine (\(-328\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), and Sulfur (\(-200\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is more negative than Oxygen (\(-141\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)). Because the \(n=2\) valence shell of F and O is very compact, the incoming electron suffers strong inter-electronic repulsion; in the larger \(n=3\) shell of Cl and S, electrons are more spread out.

D. Electronegativity (\(\chi\))

Electronegativity is a qualitative measure of the ability of an atom within a chemical compound to attract shared bonding electrons toward itself. Unlike \(\Delta_i H\) and \(\Delta_{eg}H\), it is not directly measurable, and its value for a given element can vary slightly with its chemical environment (e.g., oxidation state or hybridization). On the widely used Pauling scale, Fluorine is assigned the maximum value of \(4.0\).

Trends: Electronegativity increases across a period (e.g., Li \(1.0\) to F \(4.0\)) and decreases down a group (e.g., F \(4.0\) to At \(2.2\)). Higher electronegativity directly correlates with stronger non-metallic character.

5. Periodic Trends in Chemical Properties & Reactivity

A. Valence and Oxidation States

For representative elements, valence is typically equal to the number of valence electrons (\(n_v\)) or \((8 - n_v)\). In modern usage, oxidation state denotes the apparent charge assigned to an atom based on electronegativity differences. For instance, in \(\mathrm{OF_2}\), fluorine (\(\chi = 4.0\)) is more electronegative than oxygen (\(\chi = 3.5\)), so F is \(-1\) and O is \(+2\); in \(\mathrm{Na_2O}\), oxygen is more electronegative than Na (\(\chi = 0.9\)), so O is \(-2\) and Na is \(+1\).

B. Anomalous Behavior of Period 2 Elements & Diagonal Relationship

The first member of each main group (Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F) differs markedly from heavier congeners. For example, Li and Be form predominantly covalent compounds, whereas Na, K, Mg, and Ca form predominantly ionic compounds. Moreover, Li resembles Mg and Be resembles Al—a pattern known as the diagonal relationship.

Four Root Causes:

  1. Exceptionally small atomic and ionic radii.
  2. High charge-to-radius ratio (polarizing power).
  3. High electronegativity.
  4. Absence of valence \(d\)-orbitals: Period 2 elements have only four valence orbitals (\(2s, 2p\)), restricting their maximum covalency to 4 (e.g., \([\mathrm{BF_4}]^-\) exists, whereas \([\mathrm{BF_6}]^{3-}\) does not; Al uses \(3d\) orbitals to form \([\mathrm{AlF_6}]^{3-}\)). They also readily form \(p\pi\text{–}p\pi\) multiple bonds (\(\mathrm{C=C, C\equiv C, N\equiv N, C=O}\)).

C. Chemical Reactivity & Nature of Oxides

  • Extremes vs. Center: Reactivity peaks at the left extreme (Group 1 alkali metals, strong reducing agents losing \(e^-\)) and the right extreme (Group 17 halogens, strong oxidizing agents gaining \(e^-\)), and is lowest near the center.
  • Down a Group: Reactivity of Group 1 metals increases down the group (\(\mathrm{Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs}\)) as \(\Delta_i H\) drops; reactivity of Group 17 non-metals decreases down the group (\(\mathrm{F > Cl > Br > I}\)) as oxidizing power and \(\Delta_{eg}H\) magnitude fall.
  • Oxide Acid–Base Character Across a Period: Shifts from strongly basic on the left (\(\mathrm{Na_2O + H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH}\)) to amphoteric in the middle (\(\mathrm{Al_2O_3, As_2O_3}\)) to strongly acidic on the right (\(\mathrm{Cl_2O_7 + H_2O \rightarrow 2HClO_4}\)). Oxides such as \(\mathrm{CO, NO, N_2O}\) are neutral.

6. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Predicting IUPAC Name, Symbol, and Block for a Hypothetical Superheavy Element

Problem: Suppose a research team synthesizes a new superheavy element with atomic number \(Z = 121\) (hypothetical scenario). Assign its systematic IUPAC temporary name and symbol, and predict its period, group, and block.

Solution:

  1. IUPAC Name & Symbol: The digits of \(121\) are \(1\) (un), \(2\) (bi), and \(1\) (un). Combining the roots with -ium gives Unbiunium, with symbol Ubu.
  2. Electronic Configuration & Position: Period 7 ends at Oganesson (\(Z = 118\), \([\mathrm{Rn}]\,5f^{14}6d^{10}7s^27p^6\)). Element \(Z = 119\) enters \(8s^1\) (Group 1), \(Z = 120\) enters \(8s^2\) (Group 2), and \(Z = 121\) enters the next available subshell (\(5g\) or \(8p\) depending on relativistic ordering, or formally the \(g\)-block/inner-transition extension in Period 8). Within standard Period 8 aufbau extension, \(n = 8\), so it lies in Period 8.
Worked Example 2: Ordering Radii of an Isoelectronic Series

Problem: Arrange the species \(\mathrm{S^{2-}}\), \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\), \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), \(\mathrm{P^{3-}}\), and \(\mathrm{K^+}\) in increasing order of ionic radius.

Solution:

Each species contains exactly 18 electrons (isoelectronic with Ar). Their nuclear charges (\(Z\)) are: \(\mathrm{P^{3-}} (15)\), \(\mathrm{S^{2-}} (16)\), \(\mathrm{Cl^-} (17)\), \(\mathrm{K^+} (19)\), and \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}} (20)\). Because greater nuclear charge pulls the 18-electron cloud more tightly inward, ionic radius decreases as \(Z\) increases:

\[\mathrm{Ca^{2+} < K^+ < Cl^- < S^{2-} < P^{3-}}\]
Worked Example 3: Deducing Group Identity from Hypothetical Successive Ionization Enthalpies

Problem: Assume a Period 3 element \(M\) has the following hypothetical successive ionization enthalpies (in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)): \(\Delta_i H_1 = 580\), \(\Delta_i H_2 = 1820\), \(\Delta_i H_3 = 2750\), \(\Delta_i H_4 = 11600\). Identify the group of \(M\) and the formula of its chloride.

Solution:

Notice the sharp, more than four-fold jump between \(\Delta_i H_3\) (\(2750\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) and \(\Delta_i H_4\) (\(11600\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)). This indicates that removing the fourth electron breaks into a stable noble-gas core, meaning \(M\) has 3 valence electrons. In Period 3, the element with 3 valence electrons is in Group 13 (aluminium, \(\mathrm{Al}\)), forming the chloride \(\mathrm{MCl_3}\) (\(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\)).

Worked Example 4: Comparing Electron Gain Enthalpies & Second Electron Addition

Problem: Among \(\mathrm{O, S, F, Cl}\), identify the element with the most negative first electron gain enthalpy and the element with the least negative first electron gain enthalpy. Also state the sign of \(\Delta_{eg}H_2\) for \(\mathrm{S}^-(g) + e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{S}^{2-}(g)\).

Solution:

  • Group 17 elements have more negative \(\Delta_{eg}H_1\) than Group 16 elements. Between F and Cl, Cl has a more negative \(\Delta_{eg}H_1\) (\(-349\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) than F (\(-328\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) due to lower \(3p\) inter-electronic repulsion. Thus, Cl is the most negative.
  • Between O and S, O (\(-141\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is less negative than S (\(-200\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) for the same \(2p\) repulsion reason. Thus, O is the least negative among the four.
  • Adding a second electron to \(\mathrm{S}^-(g)\) faces electrostatic repulsion from the existing negative charge, so \(\Delta_{eg}H_2\) is positive (endothermic).
Worked Example 5: Oxidation State vs. Covalency in a Complex Ion

Problem: Determine the oxidation state and covalency of gallium in the complex ion \([\mathrm{GaBr_2(H_2O)_4}]^+\).

Solution:

Let the oxidation state of Ga be \(x\). Each \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) contributes \(-1\) and each neutral \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) contributes \(0\):

\[x + 2(-1) + 4(0) = +1 \implies x = +3\]

However, gallium is directly bonded to \(2 + 4 = 6\) ligands, so its covalency is 6 while its oxidation state is +3. They are not equal.

7. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All items below are original practice questions designed for NEET UG preparation.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following sets of elements does not represent a Dobereiner Triad?

  • (1) \(\mathrm{Li, Na, K}\)
  • (2) \(\mathrm{Ca, Sr, Ba}\)
  • (3) \(\mathrm{Cl, Br, I}\)
  • (4) \(\mathrm{F, Cl, Br}\)

Correct Answer: (4)

Solution: In a Dobereiner Triad, the atomic weight of the middle element is approximately the mean of the first and third. For \(\mathrm{F (19), Cl (35.5), Br (80)}\), the mean of \(19\) and \(80\) is \(49.5\), which is not close to \(35.5\).

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

An element has the ground-state outer electronic configuration \((n-1)d^{10}ns^2np^3\) with \(n = 4\). Its IUPAC group number and period in the Modern Periodic Table are:

  • (1) Group 13, Period 4
  • (2) Group 15, Period 4
  • (3) Group 5, Period 4
  • (4) Group 15, Period 5

Correct Answer: (2)

Solution: For \(n = 4\), the highest principal quantum number is \(4\), so it belongs to Period 4. The last electron enters the \(4p\) subshell (\(p\)-block), and the group number for a \(p\)-block element is \(10 + \text{valence }(s + p)\text{ electrons} = 10 + (2 + 3) = 15\) (Arsenic, \(Z = 33\)).

Practice Question 3 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the two statements carefully:

Statement I: The first ionization enthalpy of \(\mathrm{N}\) is greater than that of \(\mathrm{O}\).

Statement II: The second ionization enthalpy of \(\mathrm{O}\) is greater than that of \(\mathrm{N}\).

  • (1) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (2) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (3) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (4) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Correct Answer: (1)

Solution: Statement I is correct because \(\mathrm{N}\) (\(2p^3\)) has half-filled stability compared to \(\mathrm{O}\) (\(2p^4\)). Statement II is also correct: after losing one electron, \(\mathrm{O}^+\) acquires a half-filled \(2p^3\) configuration, whereas \(\mathrm{N}^+\) becomes \(2p^2\); hence removing the second electron from \(\mathrm{O}^+\) requires more energy than from \(\mathrm{N}^+\).

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following orders of first electron gain enthalpy (more negative to less negative) is correct?

  • (1) \(\mathrm{F > Cl > O > S}\)
  • (2) \(\mathrm{Cl > F > S > O}\)
  • (3) \(\mathrm{Cl > S > F > O}\)
  • (4) \(\mathrm{F > O > Cl > S}\)

Correct Answer: (2)

Solution: Experimental \(\Delta_{eg}H_1\) values (in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) are: \(\mathrm{Cl}\,(-349)\), \(\mathrm{F}\,(-328)\), \(\mathrm{S}\,(-200)\), \(\mathrm{O}\,(-141)\). Both Period 3 elements release more energy than their Period 2 congeners because of reduced inter-electronic repulsion in the larger \(3p\) subshell.

Practice Question 5 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Among \(\mathrm{F^-}\), \(\mathrm{Ne}\), and \(\mathrm{Na^+}\), \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) has the smallest radius.

Reason (R): All three species have the same principal quantum number \(n = 2\) for their outermost shell, but \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) has the highest nuclear charge.

  • (1) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (2) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (3) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (4) (A) is false but (R) is true.

Correct Answer: (1)

Solution: \(\mathrm{F^-}\) (\(Z=9\)), \(\mathrm{Ne}\) (\(Z=10\)), and \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) (\(Z=11\)) are 10-electron isoelectronic species (\(1s^22s^22p^6\)). Higher nuclear charge draws the \(n=2\) shell closer to the nucleus, making \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) the smallest.

Practice Question 6 Match the Following

Match the oxides in Column I with their chemical nature in Column II:

Column I (Oxide) Column II (Nature)
(a) \(\mathrm{Cl_2O_7}\)(i) Amphoteric
(b) \(\mathrm{Na_2O}\)(ii) Neutral
(c) \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\)(iii) Strongly acidic
(d) \(\mathrm{N_2O}\)(iv) Strongly basic
  • (1) a–(iii), b–(iv), c–(i), d–(ii)
  • (2) a–(iv), b–(iii), c–(i), d–(ii)
  • (3) a–(iii), b–(iv), c–(ii), d–(i)
  • (4) a–(i), b–(ii), c–(iii), d–(iv)

Correct Answer: (1)

Solution: \(\mathrm{Cl_2O_7}\) reacts with water to form strong perchloric acid (\(\mathrm{HClO_4}\)); \(\mathrm{Na_2O}\) forms strong base \(\mathrm{NaOH}\); \(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\) reacts with both acids and bases (amphoteric); \(\mathrm{N_2O}\) is a neutral oxide.

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding the \(d\)-block and \(f\)-block elements is incorrect?

  • (1) Palladium (\(Z = 46\)) has the ground-state outer electronic configuration \(4d^{10}5s^0\).
  • (2) \(\mathrm{Zn, Cd, Hg}\) do not show most characteristic properties of transition elements.
  • (3) Early actinoids show fewer oxidation states than lanthanoids.
  • (4) All \(d\)-block and \(f\)-block elements are metals.

Correct Answer: (3)

Solution: Early actinoids exhibit a larger number of oxidation states than lanthanoids because the \(5f\), \(6d\), and \(7s\) orbitals have very comparable energies.

Practice Question 9 Single-Correct MCQ

Which pair of elements exhibits a diagonal relationship, and why?

  • (1) \(\mathrm{Li}\) and \(\mathrm{Na}\); same valence electron count
  • (2) \(\mathrm{Be}\) and \(\mathrm{Al}\); similar charge-to-radius ratio and electronegativity
  • (3) \(\mathrm{B}\) and \(\mathrm{C}\); same principal quantum number
  • (4) \(\mathrm{Mg}\) and \(\mathrm{Ca}\); both form basic oxides

Correct Answer: (2)

Solution: \(\mathrm{Be}\) (Period 2, Group 2) and \(\mathrm{Al}\) (Period 3, Group 13) lie diagonally across the Periodic Table and share nearly identical charge/radius ratios and electronegativities, leading to similar covalent and amphoteric behavior.

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

Consider the elements \(\mathrm{B, C, N, F, Si}\). The correct decreasing order of their non-metallic character is:

  • (1) \(\mathrm{F > N > C > B > Si}\)
  • (2) \(\mathrm{F > N > C > Si > B}\)
  • (3) \(\mathrm{Si > B > C > N > F}\)
  • (4) \(\mathrm{N > F > C > B > Si}\)

Correct Answer: (1)

Solution: Non-metallic character increases from left to right across Period 2 (\(\mathrm{B < C < N < F}\)) and decreases down Group 14 (\(\mathrm{C > Si}\)). Since \(\mathrm{B}\) is more non-metallic than \(\mathrm{Si}\) (a metalloid in Period 3), the order is \(\mathrm{F > N > C > B > Si}\).

8. Common Pitfalls & NEET Revision Checklist

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Confusing \(\Delta_{eg}H\) and Electron Affinity: In NCERT/NEET convention, exothermic electron gain has a negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\). “More negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\)” means greater energy released (Cl is more negative than F).
  • Forgetting Noble Gas Radii Type: Never compare covalent radii of halogens directly with van der Waals radii of noble gases without noting the jump.
  • Overlooking \(d\)-Block Column Count: The \(d\)-block has 10 columns (Groups 3–12), not 8, because a \(d\)-subshell holds up to 10 electrons.
  • Mixing Oxidation State with Covalency: For Period 2 elements, maximum covalency is strictly 4 (no \(2d\) orbitals), even when formal oxidation states reach higher values in Period 3+ elements.

Concise Final Recap

  • Modern Periodic Law: Properties are periodic functions of atomic number \(Z\) (proven by Moseley’s \(\sqrt{\nu}\) vs. \(Z\) plot).
  • Period & Block: Period = highest \(n\); Block = subshell receiving the last electron (\(s, p, d, f\)).
  • Across a Period (L → R): \(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\) ↑, Radius ↓, \(\Delta_i H\) ↑ (exceptions: \(\mathrm{Be > B}\), \(\mathrm{N > O}\)), \(\Delta_{eg}H\) more negative, Electronegativity ↑, Non-metallic character ↑, Oxide acidity ↑.
  • Down a Group (T → B): \(n\) ↑, Shielding ↑, Radius ↑, \(\Delta_i H\) ↓ (exceptions in Group 13), \(\Delta_{eg}H\) less negative (exceptions: \(\mathrm{Cl > F}\), \(\mathrm{S > O}\)), Electronegativity ↓, Metallic character ↑.
