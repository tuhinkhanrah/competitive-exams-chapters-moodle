Chemical Thermodynamics

A complete conceptual, mathematical, and problem-solving guide covering the First, Second, and Third Laws, Thermochemistry, Entropy, and Gibbs Energy for NEET UG.

1. Learning Objectives & Big-Picture Overview

Thermodynamics investigates energy transformations in macroscopic systems—collections of a very large number of molecules—moving between equilibrium states. Unlike chemical kinetics, thermodynamics does not tell us how fast a reaction occurs; instead, it answers three fundamental questions:

  • Energy Accounting: How much heat is absorbed or released, and how much work is performed during a physical or chemical change?
  • Direction of Change: Under a given set of conditions, is a process spontaneous (thermodynamically feasible) or non-spontaneous?
  • Extent of Reaction: At what equilibrium composition does a reversible chemical reaction settle?
NEET UG Mastery Targets

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Classify thermodynamic systems, boundaries, state/path functions, and extensive/intensive properties.
  • Apply the First Law (\(\Delta U = q + w\)) using strict IUPAC sign conventions and compute \(pV\)-work for reversible, irreversible, and free-expansion processes.
  • Relate \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta U\) via \(\Delta n_g RT\), interpret calorimetry (\(\Delta U\) vs. \(\Delta H\)), and apply Hess's Law across formation, combustion, bond, atomization, lattice, and solution enthalpies.
  • Predict reaction spontaneity using entropy (\(\Delta S_{\text{total}} > 0\)) and Gibbs energy (\(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S < 0\)), and connect \(\Delta_r G^\ominus\) to the equilibrium constant \(K\).

2. Thermodynamic Foundations & Classification

2.1 System, Surroundings, and Boundary

The system is the specific portion of the universe chosen for observation (for example, a reacting gas mixture inside a cylinder). Everything outside the system that can interact with it forms the surroundings. Together, they make up the universe:

\[\text{Universe} = \text{System} + \text{Surroundings}\]

The real or imaginary surface separating the system from its surroundings is the boundary. Boundaries govern whether matter and energy can cross:

  • Open System: Exchanges both matter and energy with the surroundings (e.g., water boiling in an uncovered beaker).
  • Closed System: Exchanges energy (as heat or work) but no matter with the surroundings (e.g., gas sealed inside a conducting steel cylinder).
  • Isolated System: Exchanges neither matter nor energy with the surroundings (e.g., reactants inside a perfectly insulated, sealed thermos flask).
Tutorial Diagram 1: Classification of thermodynamic systems based on matter and energy exchange across the boundary.

2.2 State Functions vs. Path Functions

A thermodynamic state is specified by macroscopic properties such as pressure (\(p\)), volume (\(V\)), temperature (\(T\)), and amount of substance (\(n\)).

  • State Functions (State Variables): Properties whose values depend only on the initial and final states of the system, not on the route taken. Examples: \(p\), \(V\), \(T\), internal energy (\(U\)), enthalpy (\(H\)), entropy (\(S\)), and Gibbs energy (\(G\)). For any cyclic process, the net change in a state function is zero (\(\oint dX = 0\)).
  • Path Functions: Quantities whose values depend on the exact pathway or mechanism used to go from one state to another. Examples: heat (\(q\)) and work (\(w\)).

2.3 Extensive vs. Intensive Properties

Properties of matter are divided by whether they scale with system size:

Property Type Definition Common Examples
Extensive Depends on the quantity or size of matter present in the system (additive when subsystems are combined). Mass (\(m\)), Volume (\(V\)), Internal Energy (\(U\)), Enthalpy (\(H\)), Entropy (\(S\)), Gibbs Energy (\(G\)), Heat Capacity (\(C\))
Intensive Independent of the quantity or size of matter present (remains unchanged if a system is partitioned). Temperature (\(T\)), Pressure (\(p\)), Density (\(\rho\)), Specific Heat (\(c\)), Molar Properties (\(\chi_m = \chi / n\)), Concentration

Key NEET Rule: The ratio of two extensive properties is always an intensive property. For example, molar volume \(V_m = V/n\), molar enthalpy \(H_m = H/n\), and density \(\rho = m/V\) are all intensive.

3. The First Law of Thermodynamics: \(U\), \(q\), and \(w\)

3.1 Internal Energy (\(U\)) and the First Law

The internal energy (\(U\)) is the sum of all forms of energy stored within a system (translational, rotational, vibrational, electronic, and intermolecular). While the absolute value of \(U\) cannot be measured, changes in internal energy (\(\Delta U = U_2 - U_1\)) are measurable.

When a system undergoes a change of state via both heat transfer (\(q\)) and work (\(w\)), the First Law of Thermodynamics states:

\[\Delta U = q + w\]

For an isolated system (\(q = 0\) and \(w = 0\)), \(\Delta U = 0\); hence, the total energy of an isolated system remains constant (law of conservation of energy).

Critical NEET Check: IUPAC Sign Conventions
  • \(q > 0\): Heat is absorbed by the system (endothermic).
  • \(q < 0\): Heat is released by the system (exothermic).
  • \(w > 0\): Work is done on the system (compression, \(\Delta V < 0\)).
  • \(w < 0\): Work is done by the system (expansion, \(\Delta V > 0\)).

3.2 Pressure–Volume (\(pV\)) Work

Consider a gas in a cylinder fitted with a frictionless piston of cross-sectional area \(A\) against a constant external pressure \(p_{\text{ex}}\). If the volume changes by \(\Delta V = V_f - V_i\):

\[w = -p_{\text{ex}}\,\Delta V = -p_{\text{ex}}(V_f - V_i)\]

If the external pressure varies continuously such that \(p_{\text{ex}} = p_{\text{in}} \pm dp\) at every stage, the process is reversible (infinitely slow, passing through continuous equilibrium states). For \(n\) moles of an ideal gas undergoing a reversible isothermal (\(T = \text{constant}\)) change:

\[w_{\text{rev}} = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} p_{\text{in}}\,dV = -nRT \ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right) = -2.303\,nRT \log\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right)\]

Since \(p_i V_i = p_f V_f\) at constant \(T\), this can also be written as \(w_{\text{rev}} = -2.303\,nRT \log(p_i / p_f)\).

3.3 Special Processes for an Ideal Gas

Process Condition Key First-Law Result
Isochoric Constant volume (\(\Delta V = 0\)) \(w = 0 \implies \Delta U = q_V\)
Adiabatic No heat exchange (\(q = 0\)) \(\Delta U = w_{\text{ad}}\)
Isothermal (Ideal Gas) Constant temperature (\(\Delta T = 0 \implies \Delta U = 0\)) \(q = -w\)
Free Expansion Expansion into vacuum (\(p_{\text{ex}} = 0\)) \(w = 0\); if isothermal ideal gas, \(q = 0\) and \(\Delta U = 0\)

3.4 Heat Capacity and \(C_p - C_v = R\)

The heat \(q\) needed to raise the temperature of a system by \(\Delta T\) is \(q = C\,\Delta T\), where \(C\) is the heat capacity. Using specific heat capacity \(c\) (per unit mass) or molar heat capacity \(C_m = C/n\):

\[q = m\,c\,\Delta T = n\,C_m\,\Delta T\]

At constant volume, \(q_V = C_V\,\Delta T = \Delta U\). At constant pressure, \(q_p = C_p\,\Delta T = \Delta H\). For 1 mol of an ideal gas, \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta(RT) = \Delta U + R\,\Delta T\), which yields Mayer's relation:

\[C_p - C_v = R \quad (\text{for 1 mol ideal gas}; \quad C_p - C_v = nR \text{ for } n \text{ mol})\]

4. Enthalpy (\(H\)), Calorimetry, & Thermochemistry

4.1 Enthalpy (\(H\)) and \(\Delta H\) vs. \(\Delta U\)

Most laboratory reactions occur in open vessels at constant atmospheric pressure rather than at constant volume. To track heat flow at constant pressure, we define enthalpy (\(H\)):

\[H = U + pV \implies \Delta H = \Delta U + p\,\Delta V = q_p \quad (\text{at constant } p)\]

For reactions involving only solids and liquids, \(p\,\Delta V\) is negligible, so \(\Delta H \approx \Delta U\). When gases participate at constant \(T\) and \(p\), applying the ideal gas law gives \(p\,\Delta V = \Delta n_g RT\), where \(\Delta n_g = (\text{moles of gaseous products}) - (\text{moles of gaseous reactants})\):

\[\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\]

Remember: \(\Delta H < 0\) for exothermic reactions and \(\Delta H > 0\) for endothermic reactions.

4.2 Calorimetry: Measuring \(\Delta U\) and \(\Delta H\)

  • Bomb Calorimeter (Constant Volume): A sealed, rigid steel vessel (the bomb) containing the sample and excess \(\mathrm{O}_2\) is submerged in a water bath. Because \(\Delta V = 0\), no \(pV\)-work is done, and the heat measured gives \(\Delta U = q_V = -C_{\text{cal}}\,\Delta T\).
  • Constant-Pressure Calorimeter: An insulated vessel (such as a foamed polystyrene cup) open to atmospheric pressure measures \(q_p = \Delta H\).

4.3 Standard States, Thermochemical Equations & Hess's Law

The standard state of a substance at a specified temperature (commonly \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)) is its pure form at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure. Standard enthalpy changes carry the superscript \(\ominus\) (\(\Delta H^\ominus\)).

  • In a thermochemical equation, physical states \((s, l, g, aq)\) and allotropes (e.g., \(\mathrm{C}_{\text{graphite}}\)) must be specified.
  • Stoichiometric coefficients represent moles. Multiplying an equation by \(k\) multiplies \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) by \(k\); reversing an equation reverses the sign of \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\).
  • Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation: Because \(H\) is a state function, the overall enthalpy change for a reaction is identical whether it occurs in one step or multiple steps: \[\Delta_r H^\ominus = \Delta_r H_1^\ominus + \Delta_r H_2^\ominus + \Delta_r H_3^\ominus + \dots\]

4.4 Important Types of Enthalpy Changes

Enthalpy Term Definition & Key Formula Sign & NEET Notes
Enthalpy of Formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)) Enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound forms from its constituent elements in their most stable reference states at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\). \(\Delta_f H^\ominus = 0\) for elements in reference states (e.g., \(\mathrm{H}_2(g)\), \(\mathrm{O}_2(g)\), \(\mathrm{C}_{\text{graphite}}\), \(\mathrm{Br}_2(l)\), \(\mathrm{S}_{\text{rhombic}}\)).
\(\Delta_r H^\ominus = \sum a_i \Delta_f H^\ominus(\text{prod}) - \sum b_i \Delta_f H^\ominus(\text{react})\)
Enthalpy of Combustion (\(\Delta_c H^\ominus\)) Enthalpy change when 1 mol of a substance undergoes complete combustion in excess \(\mathrm{O}_2\) under standard conditions. Always negative (\(\Delta_c H^\ominus < 0\)).
\(\Delta_r H^\ominus = \sum \Delta_c H^\ominus(\text{react}) - \sum \Delta_c H^\ominus(\text{prod})\)
Phase-Change Enthalpies Fusion (\(\Delta_{\text{fus}}H^\ominus\)), Vaporization (\(\Delta_{\text{vap}}H^\ominus\)), Sublimation (\(\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\ominus\)) for 1 mol at constant \(T, p\). Always positive (endothermic).
\(\Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\ominus = \Delta_{\text{fus}}H^\ominus + \Delta_{\text{vap}}H^\ominus\)
Enthalpy of Atomization (\(\Delta_a H^\ominus\)) Enthalpy change on breaking 1 mol of substance completely into isolated gaseous atoms. Always positive. For metals like \(\mathrm{Na}(s) \to \mathrm{Na}(g)\), \(\Delta_a H^\ominus = \Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\ominus\).
Bond Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^\ominus\)) Energy required to break 1 mol of a specific gaseous bond. For polyatomic molecules (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH}_4\)), mean bond enthalpy is used. For gas-phase reactions:
\(\Delta_r H^\ominus \approx \sum \text{BE}(\text{reactants}) - \sum \text{BE}(\text{products})\)
Lattice Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus\)) Enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic crystal dissociates into gaseous ions: \(\mathrm{MX}(s) \to \mathrm{M}^+(g) + \mathrm{X}^-(g)\). Positive as defined for dissociation; determined indirectly via the Born–Haber cycle.
Enthalpy of Solution (\(\Delta_{\text{sol}}H^\ominus\)) & Dilution Enthalpy change when 1 mol of solute dissolves in a specified amount of solvent. \(\Delta_{\text{sol}}H^\ominus = \Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus + \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\ominus\). Enthalpy of dilution is the heat change upon adding more solvent.

4.5 The Born–Haber Cycle for Ionic Solids

Because lattice enthalpy cannot be measured directly, Hess's Law is applied to construct a closed thermodynamic cycle (Born–Haber cycle) connecting the formation of an ionic solid \(\mathrm{MX}(s)\) to its gaseous ions \(\mathrm{M}^+(g) + \mathrm{X}^-(g)\):

\[\Delta_f H^\ominus = \Delta_{\text{sub}}H^\ominus + \Delta_i H^\ominus + \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\text{bond}}H^\ominus + \Delta_{eg}H^\ominus - \Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus\]

(Here \(\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus\) is taken as positive for dissociation \(\mathrm{MX}(s) \to \mathrm{M}^+(g) + \mathrm{X}^-(g)\), so forming the crystal releases \(-\Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus\).)

5. Spontaneity, Entropy (\(S\)), & the Second and Third Laws

5.1 What Is a Spontaneous Process?

A spontaneous process is one that has the natural potential to proceed in a given direction without continuous external assistance. Note two essential points:

  • Spontaneity implies nothing about reaction rate (e.g., \(\mathrm{H}_2\) and \(\mathrm{O}_2\) react imperceptibly slowly at room temperature without a spark, yet the reaction is thermodynamically spontaneous).
  • A spontaneous process is irreversible on its own and can only be reversed by an external agency.

A negative enthalpy change (\(\Delta H < 0\)) favors spontaneity, but it is not a universal criterion—many endothermic processes (such as melting of ice above \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) or dissolution of \(\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{NO}_3\) in water) are spontaneous.

5.2 Entropy (\(S\)) and the Second Law

Entropy (\(S\)) is a state function that measures the degree of randomness or disorder in a system. For a given substance:

\[S_{\text{crystalline solid}} < S_{\text{liquid}} < S_{\text{gas}}\]

Quantitatively, for a reversible heat transfer \(q_{\text{rev}}\) at temperature \(T\) (in Kelvin):

\[\Delta S = \frac{q_{\text{rev}}}{T}\]

Second Law of Thermodynamics: Every spontaneous process increases the total entropy of the universe:

\[\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{system}} + \Delta S_{\text{surroundings}} > 0 \quad (\text{spontaneous})\]

At equilibrium, entropy reaches a maximum and \(\Delta S_{\text{total}} = 0\). For a process at constant \(T\) and \(p\), the surroundings absorb heat equal to \(-\Delta H_{\text{sys}}\), so:

\[\Delta S_{\text{surr}} = -\frac{\Delta H_{\text{sys}}}{T}\]

5.3 Third Law of Thermodynamics

Third Law: The entropy of any pure, perfectly crystalline substance approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero (\(0\,\mathrm{K}\)). Unlike \(U\) and \(H\), this allows absolute molar entropies (\(S_m^\ominus\)) of pure substances to be determined.

6. Gibbs Energy (\(G\)) & Chemical Equilibrium

6.1 Gibbs Energy and the Spontaneity Criterion

To avoid calculating \(\Delta S_{\text{surr}}\) for every reaction, we define the Gibbs energy (\(G\)), an extensive state function:

\[G = H - TS \implies \Delta G = \Delta H - T\,\Delta S \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } p)\]

Because \(\Delta G = -T\,\Delta S_{\text{total}}\), the sign of \(\Delta G\) directly determines spontaneity at constant \(T\) and \(p\):

  • \(\Delta G < 0\): Process is spontaneous (exergonic).
  • \(\Delta G = 0\): System is at equilibrium.
  • \(\Delta G > 0\): Process is non-spontaneous in the forward direction (spontaneous in reverse).

6.2 Effect of Temperature on Spontaneity

\(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) \(\Delta_r S^\ominus\) \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = \Delta_r H^\ominus - T\Delta_r S^\ominus\) Spontaneity Behavior
\(-\) (Exothermic) \(+\) (More disorder) Always \(-\) Spontaneous at all temperatures
\(+\) (Endothermic) \(-\) (Less disorder) Always \(+\) Non-spontaneous at all temperatures
\(-\) (Exothermic) \(-\) (Less disorder) \(-\) at low \(T\), \(+\) at high \(T\) Spontaneous at low \(T\) (when \(|\Delta H| > |T\Delta S|\))
\(+\) (Endothermic) \(+\) (More disorder) \(+\) at low \(T\), \(-\) at high \(T\) Spontaneous at high \(T\) (when \(T\Delta S > \Delta H\))

The crossover temperature at which \(\Delta G = 0\) is \(T_{\text{eq}} = \Delta H / \Delta S\).

6.3 Standard Gibbs Energy and Equilibrium Constant (\(K\))

For a reversible reaction at equilibrium (\(\Delta_r G = 0\)), the standard Gibbs energy change \(\Delta_r G^\ominus\) is linked to the thermodynamic equilibrium constant \(K\) by:

\[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -RT \ln K = -2.303\,RT \log K\]
  • If \(\Delta_r G^\ominus < 0\), then \(K > 1\) (products are favored at equilibrium).
  • If \(\Delta_r G^\ominus > 0\), then \(K < 1\) (reactants are favored at equilibrium).
  • If \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = 0\), then \(K = 1\).

7. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: First Law & Reversible Isothermal Expansion

Problem (hypothetical values): Assume \(2.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal gas expands isothermally and reversibly at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) from an initial volume of \(5.0\,\mathrm{L}\) to a final volume of \(25.0\,\mathrm{L}\). Calculate \(w\), \(\Delta U\), and \(q\) in kJ. (Use \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\log 5 = 0.6990\).)

Solution:

1. Since the process is isothermal for an ideal gas, \(\Delta T = 0\), so \(\Delta U = 0\).

2. Work done in reversible isothermal expansion:

\[w_{\text{rev}} = -2.303\,nRT\log\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right) = -2.303 \times 2.0 \times 8.314 \times 300 \times \log\left(\frac{25.0}{5.0}\right)\] \[w_{\text{rev}} = -2.303 \times 2.0 \times 8.314 \times 300 \times 0.6990 = -8031\,\mathrm{J} \approx -8.03\,\mathrm{kJ}\]

3. From the First Law, \(\Delta U = q + w \implies 0 = q + (-8.03\,\mathrm{kJ}) \implies q = +8.03\,\mathrm{kJ}\).

Worked Example 2: Relating \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta U\)

Problem (hypothetical values): Consider the hypothetical gas-phase reaction \(\mathrm{X}_2(g) + 3\,\mathrm{Y}_2(g) \to 2\,\mathrm{XY}_3(g)\). If \(\Delta U = -85.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(400\,\mathrm{K}\), calculate \(\Delta H\) at the same temperature. (Use \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\).)

Solution:

1. Calculate \(\Delta n_g = n_{\text{products}(g)} - n_{\text{reactants}(g)} = 2 - (1 + 3) = -2\,\mathrm{mol}\).

2. Apply \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\):

\[\Delta H = -85.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} + (-2)(8.314 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}})(400\,\mathrm{K})\] \[\Delta H = -85.0 - 6.65 = -91.65\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]
Worked Example 3: Hess's Law & Bond Enthalpies

Problem (hypothetical values): Estimate \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) for the gas-phase hydrogenation \(\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_4(g) + \mathrm{H}_2(g) \to \mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_6(g)\) using assumed mean bond enthalpies: \(\mathrm{C=C} = 610\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm{C-C} = 348\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm{C-H} = 414\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), and \(\mathrm{H-H} = 436\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Solution:

In \(\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_4 + \mathrm{H}_2 \to \mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_6\), one \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond and one \(\mathrm{H-H}\) bond are broken, while one \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond and two new \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bonds are formed (four \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bonds cancel on both sides).

\[\Delta_r H^\ominus = \left[\text{BE}(\mathrm{C=C}) + \text{BE}(\mathrm{H-H})\right] - \left[\text{BE}(\mathrm{C-C}) + 2\,\text{BE}(\mathrm{C-H})\right]\] \[\Delta_r H^\ominus = (610 + 436) - (348 + 2 \times 414) = 1046 - 1176 = -130\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]
Worked Example 4: Temperature Threshold for Spontaneity

Problem (hypothetical values): For a thermal decomposition reaction, assume \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = +170\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(\Delta_r S^\ominus = +160\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) remain constant with temperature. Above what minimum temperature does the reaction become spontaneous?

Solution:

Since \(\Delta_r H^\ominus > 0\) and \(\Delta_r S^\ominus > 0\), the reaction is spontaneous when \(T\,\Delta_r S^\ominus > \Delta_r H^\ominus\):

\[T > \frac{\Delta_r H^\ominus}{\Delta_r S^\ominus} = \frac{170 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}}{160\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}} = 1062.5\,\mathrm{K}\]

Thus, the reaction becomes spontaneous above \(1062.5\,\mathrm{K}\).

Worked Example 5: Standard Gibbs Energy & Equilibrium Constant

Problem (hypothetical values): At \(300\,\mathrm{K}\), an isomerization reaction has an equilibrium constant \(K = 100\). Calculate \(\Delta_r G^\ominus\) in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). (Use \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\).)

Solution:

\[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303\,RT\log K = -2.303 \times 8.314 \times 300 \times \log(100)\] \[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303 \times 8.314 \times 300 \times 2 = -11488\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx -11.49\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All items below are original practice questions modeled on the NEET UG single-correct MCQ format.

Practice Question 1 Conceptual

Which of the following sets contains only intensive thermodynamic properties?

  • (A) Enthalpy, temperature, density
  • (B) Molar heat capacity, density, temperature
  • (C) Volume, pressure, specific heat
  • (D) Internal energy, molar volume, entropy
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Molar heat capacity (\(C/n\)), density (\(m/V\)), and temperature (\(T\)) do not depend on the amount of matter. Enthalpy, volume, internal energy, and entropy are extensive properties.

Practice Question 2 Numerical

A closed system absorbs \(420\,\mathrm{J}\) of heat from the surroundings and performs \(180\,\mathrm{J}\) of work on the surroundings. What is the change in internal energy (\(\Delta U\)) of the system?

  • (A) \(+600\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(-600\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(+240\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(-240\,\mathrm{J}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: By IUPAC convention, heat absorbed \(q = +420\,\mathrm{J}\), and work done by the system \(w = -180\,\mathrm{J}\). Therefore, \(\Delta U = q + w = 420 + (-180) = +240\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 3 Numerical

An ideal gas expands from \(2.0\,\mathrm{L}\) to \(6.0\,\mathrm{L}\) against a constant external pressure of \(2.0\,\mathrm{atm}\). What is the work done in Joules? (Take \(1\,\mathrm{L\cdot atm} = 101.3\,\mathrm{J}\))

  • (A) \(-810.4\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(+810.4\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(-405.2\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(+405.2\,\mathrm{J}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: \(w = -p_{\text{ex}}(V_f - V_i) = -2.0\,\mathrm{atm} \times (6.0 - 2.0)\,\mathrm{L} = -8.0\,\mathrm{L\cdot atm}\). Converting to Joules: \(w = -8.0 \times 101.3 = -810.4\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 4 Conceptual

For which of the following reactions is \(\Delta H\) strictly equal to \(\Delta U\) at constant temperature and pressure?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{N}_2(g) + 3\,\mathrm{H}_2(g) \to 2\,\mathrm{NH}_3(g)\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{PCl}_5(g) \to \mathrm{PCl}_3(g) + \mathrm{Cl}_2(g)\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{H}_2(g) + \mathrm{I}_2(g) \to 2\,\mathrm{HI}(g)\)
  • (D) \(2\,\mathrm{SO}_2(g) + \mathrm{O}_2(g) \to 2\,\mathrm{SO}_3(g)\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\). For \(\mathrm{H}_2(g) + \mathrm{I}_2(g) \to 2\,\mathrm{HI}(g)\), \(\Delta n_g = 2 - (1 + 1) = 0\), so \(\Delta H = \Delta U\).

Practice Question 5 Numerical

In a bomb calorimeter, \(0.50\,\mathrm{g}\) of a solid fuel (molar mass \(50\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) is completely burnt, causing the calorimeter temperature to rise by \(2.5\,\mathrm{K}\). If the heat capacity of the calorimeter is \(10.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}\), what is \(\Delta U\) per mole of fuel?

  • (A) \(-25\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(-2500\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(+2500\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(-1250\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Heat released by \(0.50\,\mathrm{g}\) (\(0.01\,\mathrm{mol}\)) is \(q_V = -C_{\text{cal}}\,\Delta T = -10.0 \times 2.5 = -25.0\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Per mole: \(\Delta U = -25.0\,\mathrm{kJ} / 0.01\,\mathrm{mol} = -2500\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 6 Numerical

Given the hypothetical standard enthalpies of formation at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \(\Delta_f H^\ominus[\mathrm{A}(g)] = +50\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\Delta_f H^\ominus[\mathrm{B}(g)] = -120\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), and \(\Delta_f H^\ominus[\mathrm{C}(g)] = -200\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Calculate \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) for the reaction \(2\,\mathrm{A}(g) + \mathrm{B}(g) \to 2\,\mathrm{C}(g)\).

  • (A) \(-380\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(-420\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(+380\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(-130\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = 2\,\Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathrm{C}) - [2\,\Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathrm{A}) + \Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathrm{B})] = 2(-200) - [2(50) + (-120)] = -400 - (-20) = -380\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 7 Hess's Law

Given the following hypothetical thermochemical equations:
(i) \(\mathrm{P}(s) + \mathrm{O}_2(g) \to \mathrm{PO}_2(g);\ \Delta H^\ominus = -400\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
(ii) \(\mathrm{PO}_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{O}_2(g) \to \mathrm{PO}_3(g);\ \Delta H^\ominus = -100\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
What is \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) for \(2\,\mathrm{PO}_3(g) \to 2\,\mathrm{P}(s) + 3\,\mathrm{O}_2(g)\)?

  • (A) \(-1000\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(+500\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(+1000\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(-500\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Adding (i) and (ii) gives \(\mathrm{P}(s) + \frac{3}{2}\mathrm{O}_2(g) \to \mathrm{PO}_3(g)\) with \(\Delta H^\ominus = -500\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Reversing and multiplying by 2 gives \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = +2 \times 500 = +1000\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 8 Conceptual

For which of the following processes is the enthalpy change directly equal to the standard enthalpy of formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)) of the product at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{C}(\text{diamond}) + \mathrm{O}_2(g) \to \mathrm{CO}_2(g)\)
  • (B) \(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{H}_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Br}_2(g) \to \mathrm{HBr}(g)\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{C}(\text{graphite}) + 2\,\mathrm{H}_2(g) \to \mathrm{CH}_4(g)\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{CaO}(s) + \mathrm{CO}_2(g) \to \mathrm{CaCO}_3(s)\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\) requires forming 1 mol of compound from elements in their most stable reference states at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\). In (A), diamond is not the reference state of carbon (graphite is); in (B), \(\mathrm{Br}_2(l)\) is the reference state, not \(\mathrm{Br}_2(g)\); in (D), reactants are compounds, not elements.

Practice Question 9 Numerical

The lattice enthalpy of an ionic salt \(\mathrm{MX}(s)\) is \(+760\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) and its enthalpy of hydration is \(-775\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). What is its standard enthalpy of solution (\(\Delta_{\text{sol}}H^\ominus\))?

  • (A) \(-15\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(+15\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(-1535\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(+1535\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: \(\Delta_{\text{sol}}H^\ominus = \Delta_{\text{lattice}}H^\ominus + \Delta_{\text{hyd}}H^\ominus = +760 + (-775) = -15\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 10 Entropy

In which of the following changes does the entropy of the system decrease?

  • (A) Sublimation of solid iodine
  • (B) Dissociation of \(\mathrm{N}_2\mathrm{O}_4(g)\) into \(2\,\mathrm{NO}_2(g)\)
  • (C) Adsorption of a gas onto a solid metal surface
  • (D) Heating a crystalline solid from \(10\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(200\,\mathrm{K}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: When gas molecules are adsorbed onto a solid surface, their translational freedom is severely restricted, creating a more ordered state and decreasing entropy (\(\Delta S < 0\)). All other options increase disorder.

Practice Question 11 Statement-Based

Statement I: An exothermic reaction with a negative entropy change (\(\Delta H < 0,\ \Delta S < 0\)) is spontaneous at all temperatures.
Statement II: For any spontaneous process in an isolated system, \(\Delta S > 0\).
Choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false
  • (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false
  • (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true
Correct Answer: (D)

Explanation: When \(\Delta H < 0\) and \(\Delta S < 0\), \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\) is negative only at low temperatures (where \(|\Delta H| > |T\Delta S|\)), so Statement I is false. Statement II is the Second Law for an isolated system and is true.

Practice Question 12 Numerical

For a reaction at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = -298\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). What is the entropy change of the surroundings (\(\Delta S_{\text{surr}}\)) at constant pressure and \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)?

  • (A) \(-1000\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(+1000\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(+1.0\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(-1.0\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: \(\Delta S_{\text{surr}} = -\Delta_r H^\ominus / T = -(-298 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}) / 298\,\mathrm{K} = +1000\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 13 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Many endothermic reactions become spontaneous at sufficiently high temperatures.
Reason (R): If \(\Delta S > 0\), increasing \(T\) makes the \(-T\Delta S\) term more negative, which can outweigh a positive \(\Delta H\) in \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\).

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A)
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Both statements are scientifically accurate, and (R) directly explains the thermodynamic mechanism behind (A) via the Gibbs equation.

Practice Question 14 Equilibrium

If the standard Gibbs energy change for a reaction at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = +5.74\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), what is the value of \(\log_{10} K\)? (Take \(2.303 \times 8.314 \times 300 \approx 5744\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\).)

  • (A) \(+1.0\)
  • (B) \(-1.0\)
  • (C) \(+10.0\)
  • (D) \(0.0\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K \implies 5744\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} = -5744\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \times \log_{10} K \implies \log_{10} K = -1.0\) (so \(K = 0.1 < 1\)).

9. Common Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

Top 5 NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid
  1. Mixing Physics and IUPAC Work Conventions: In Chemistry (IUPAC), \(w = -p_{\text{ex}}\Delta V\) and \(\Delta U = q + w\). Expansion gives \(w < 0\).
  2. Forgetting Unit Consistency in \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\): \(\Delta U\) is usually in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), whereas \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\). Always multiply \(R\) by \(10^{-3}\) to convert to \(\mathrm{kJ}\)!
  3. Counting Solids/Liquids in \(\Delta n_g\): Only gaseous moles contribute to \(\Delta n_g\).
  4. Bond Enthalpy Formula Reversal: Remember \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = \sum \text{BE}(\text{reactants}) - \sum \text{BE}(\text{products})\), which is opposite to \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\) (products minus reactants).
  5. Confusing \(\Delta G\) with \(\Delta G^\ominus\): \(\Delta G = 0\) at equilibrium, whereas \(\Delta G^\ominus = 0\) only when \(K = 1\).
Rapid Formula & Concept Checklist
  • First Law: \(\Delta U = q + w\); \(q_V = \Delta U\); \(q_p = \Delta H\).
  • Reversible Isothermal Ideal Gas: \(\Delta U = 0\), \(w_{\text{rev}} = -2.303\,nRT\log(V_f/V_i)\), \(q = -w_{\text{rev}}\).
  • Enthalpy–Internal Energy Link: \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\); \(C_p - C_v = R\) (1 mol ideal gas).
  • Entropy & Second Law: \(\Delta S = q_{\text{rev}}/T\); \(\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} > 0\) for spontaneity.
  • Gibbs Energy & Equilibrium: \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S < 0\) (spontaneous); \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303\,RT\log K\).

10. Concise Final Recap

Chemical thermodynamics unifies energy conservation (First Law), directional spontaneity (Second Law via entropy and Gibbs energy), and absolute entropy scaling (Third Law). By mastering state functions, IUPAC sign conventions, Hess's Law cycles, and the \(\Delta G^\ominus\)–\(K\) relationship, you are equipped to solve every conceptual and numerical thermodynamics problem on the NEET UG examination with speed and precision.
