Overview: Chemical energy stored by molecules can be released as heat during chemical reactions when a fuel like methane, cooking gas, or coal burns in air. It may also be used to do mechanical work in an engine or provide electrical energy through a galvanic cell like a dry cell. Thermodynamics deals with the study of these energy transformations in macroscopic systems involving a large number of molecules rather than microscopic systems containing a few molecules. Thermodynamics is not concerned with how and at what rate these transformations occur, but is based on the initial and final equilibrium states of a system. Macroscopic properties like pressure and temperature do not change with time for a system in an equilibrium state.

Through thermodynamics, we answer three central questions in chemistry:

  1. How do we determine the energy changes involved in a chemical reaction or process?
  2. Will a given chemical reaction or process occur spontaneously or not, and what drives it?
  3. To what extent do chemical reactions proceed before reaching equilibrium?

2. Thermodynamic Terms & Classification of Systems

2.1 The System, Surroundings, and Boundary

In thermodynamics, a system refers to that part of the universe in which observations are made, and the remaining universe constitutes the surroundings. Together, the system and the surroundings constitute the universe:

\[\text{The Universe} = \text{The System} + \text{The Surroundings}\]

For all practical purposes, the surroundings are that portion of the remaining universe in the immediate neighbourhood of the system which can interact with it. For example, if we study a reaction between two substances A and B kept in a beaker, the reaction mixture inside the beaker is the system, and the room where the beaker is kept is the surroundings.

The system is separated from the surroundings by a real or imaginary wall called the boundary, which allows us to control and track all movements of matter and energy in or out of the system. A system may be defined by physical boundaries (like a beaker or test tube) or by a set of Cartesian coordinates specifying a particular volume in space.

2.2 Types of Thermodynamic Systems

Systems are classified according to the movements of matter and energy across their boundary:

  • Open System: Both energy and matter can be exchanged between the system and the surroundings. Example: Reactants present in an open beaker.
  • Closed System: There is no exchange of matter, but exchange of energy is possible between the system and the surroundings. Example: Reactants in a closed vessel made of a thermally conducting material such as copper or steel.
  • Isolated System: There is no exchange of energy or matter between the system and the surroundings. Example: Reactants kept in a closed thermos flask or any other closed, thermally insulated vessel.

2.3 The State of the System and State Functions

In mechanics, the state of a system at a given instant is specified by the position and velocity of each mass point. Thermodynamics introduces a simpler macroscopic concept: the state of a thermodynamic system is described by its measurable macroscopic (bulk) properties such as pressure (\(p\)), volume (\(V\)), temperature (\(T\)), and amount of substance (\(n\)).

  • State Functions (or State Variables): Variables like \(p, V, T, U, H, S, G\) whose values depend only on the current state of the system and not on the path taken to reach that state. For example, changing the temperature of water from \(25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(35\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) gives \(\Delta T = +10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) regardless of whether we heat it directly or cool it first and then heat it. Similarly, the volume of water in a pond is independent of whether it was filled by rain, by a tubewell, or both.
  • Path Functions: Quantities like heat (\(q\)) and work (\(w\)) whose values depend on the specific path or manner in which a change of state is carried out.

To completely define the state of a system, it is not necessary to define all properties, as only a minimum number of independent macroscopic properties need to be fixed; once fixed, all other properties automatically take definite values.

3. Internal Energy (\(U\)) and the First Law of Thermodynamics

3.1 Internal Energy as a State Function

The total energy of a system—representing the sum of chemical, electrical, mechanical, and all other forms of energy—is called its internal energy (\(U\)). The internal energy of a system changes when:

  1. Heat passes into or out of the system,
  2. Work is done on or by the system, or
  3. Matter enters or leaves the system.

Important Note on Absolute Values: Unlike volume, which has an unambiguous absolute value in a particular state, we cannot specify the absolute value of internal energy \(U\). Thermodynamics measures only the change in internal energy (\(\Delta U\)) accompanying a process.

3.2 Adiabatic Work (\(w_{\mathrm{ad}}\)) and Joule's Experiments

An adiabatic system is thermally insulated so that no heat can be exchanged between the system and surroundings (\(q = 0\)) across its boundary (called an adiabatic wall). Any process occurring in such a system is an adiabatic process.

Between 1840 and 1850, J. P. Joule performed experiments on water kept in an adiabatic vessel initially at state A (temperature \(T_{\mathrm{A}}\), internal energy \(U_{\mathrm{A}}\)):

  • Path 1 (Mechanical Work): Doing \(1\,\mathrm{kJ}\) of mechanical work by rotating a set of small paddles (churning water) raised the temperature to \(T_{\mathrm{B}}\) (state B, internal energy \(U_{\mathrm{B}}\)), giving \(\Delta T = T_{\mathrm{B}} - T_{\mathrm{A}}\).
  • Path 2 (Electrical Work): Doing an equal amount (\(1\,\mathrm{kJ}\)) of electrical work with an immersion rod produced the exact same temperature change \(\Delta T = T_{\mathrm{B}} - T_{\mathrm{A}}\).

Joule proved that a given amount of adiabatic work done on a system produces the same change of state irrespective of the path, establishing that internal energy \(U\) is a state function:

\[\Delta U = U_2 - U_1 = w_{\mathrm{ad}}\]

3.3 Heat (\(q\)) and IUPAC Sign Conventions

Internal energy can also be changed by transferring heat across thermally conducting walls (such as a copper container placed in a heat reservoir) without doing work. At constant volume (no work done), \(\Delta U = q\).

IUPAC Sign Conventions in Chemical Thermodynamics

  • Heat absorbed by the system from the surroundings: \(q > 0\) (positive, internal energy increases).
  • Heat released by the system to the surroundings: \(q < 0\) (negative, internal energy decreases).
  • Work done ON the system (compression): \(w > 0\) (positive, internal energy increases).
  • Work done BY the system (expansion): \(w < 0\) (negative, internal energy decreases).

3.4 Mathematical Statement of the First Law of Thermodynamics

When a change of state is brought about both by doing work and by transfer of heat, the change in internal energy is:

\[\Delta U = q + w\]

While \(q\) and \(w\) individually depend on the path, their sum \(q + w = \Delta U\) depends only on the initial and final states. For an isolated system, \(q = 0\) and \(w = 0\), so \(\Delta U = 0\).

First Law of Thermodynamics: The energy of an isolated system is constant. Equivalently, it is the law of conservation of energy: energy can neither be created nor destroyed.

4. Pressure-Volume Work & Thermodynamic Processes

4.1 Mechanical Work of Compression and Expansion

Consider a cylinder fitted with a frictionless piston of cross-sectional area \(A\), containing \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal gas of initial volume \(V_i\) and internal pressure \(p\). If a constant external pressure \(p_{\mathrm{ex}} > p\) pushes the piston inward a distance \(l\) in a single step until the final volume is \(V_f\):

  • Volume change: \(\Delta V = V_f - V_i = -l \cdot A\)
  • Force on piston: \(\text{Force} = p_{\mathrm{ex}} \cdot A\)
  • Work done on the system: \(w = \text{Force} \times \text{distance} = (p_{\mathrm{ex}} \cdot A) \cdot l = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}(V_f - V_i) = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}\Delta V\)
\[w = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}\Delta V = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}(V_f - V_i)\]

During compression, \(V_f < V_i\), so \(\Delta V\) is negative, and \(w = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}\Delta V\) becomes positive (work done on the system). If the external pressure changes in a series of finite steps, total work is \(w = -\sum p_{\mathrm{ex}}\Delta V\).

4.2 Reversible vs. Irreversible Processes

A process is said to be reversible if a change is brought about in such a way that the process could, at any moment, be reversed by an infinitesimal change. A reversible process proceeds infinitely slowly through a series of equilibrium states such that the system and surroundings are always in near-equilibrium with each other (\(p_{\mathrm{ex}} = p_{\mathrm{in}} \pm dp\)). Processes other than reversible processes are called irreversible processes.

Under reversible conditions, since \(dp \cdot dV\) is negligibly small:

\[w_{\mathrm{rev}} = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} p_{\mathrm{ex}}\,dV = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} (p_{\mathrm{in}} \pm dp)\,dV = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} p_{\mathrm{in}}\,dV\]

For \(n\) moles of an ideal gas (\(p = \dfrac{nRT}{V}\)) undergoing an isothermal reversible process (\(T = \text{constant}\)):

\[w_{\mathrm{rev}} = -\int_{V_i}^{V_f} nRT\,\frac{dV}{V} = -nRT\ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right) = -2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right)\]

4.3 Summary of Special Thermodynamic Processes for an Ideal Gas

  • Constant Volume (Isochoric Process, \(\Delta V = 0\)): Since \(w = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}\Delta V = 0\), the First Law gives: \[\Delta U = q_V\] where \(q_V\) is the heat supplied at constant volume.
  • Free Expansion (Expansion into Vacuum, \(p_{\mathrm{ex}} = 0\)): No work is done during free expansion (\(w = 0\)) whether the process is reversible or irreversible. For an isothermal free expansion of an ideal gas, Joule determined experimentally that \(q = 0\) and \(\Delta U = 0\).
  • Isothermal Process (\(T = \text{constant}\), so \(\Delta U = 0\) for an ideal gas):
    • Irreversible isothermal change: \(q = -w = p_{\mathrm{ex}}(V_f - V_i)\)
    • Reversible isothermal change: \(q = -w = nRT\ln\left(\dfrac{V_f}{V_i}\right) = 2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\left(\dfrac{V_f}{V_i}\right)\)
  • Adiabatic Process (\(q = 0\)): \(\Delta U = w_{\mathrm{ad}}\).

5. Enthalpy (\(H\)), Heat Capacity, and Calorimetry

5.1 Enthalpy (\(H\)) — A Useful State Function

Most laboratory chemical reactions are carried out in flasks or test tubes open to constant atmospheric pressure rather than at constant volume. At constant pressure, the First Law can be written as:

\[\Delta U = U_2 - U_1 = q_p - p(V_2 - V_1) \implies q_p = (U_2 + pV_2) - (U_1 + pV_1)\]

We define a new thermodynamic state function called enthalpy (\(H\)) (from the Greek enthalpien, to warm or heat content) as:

\[H = U + pV\]

Because \(U\), \(p\), and \(V\) are all state functions, \(H\) is also a state function. For finite changes at constant pressure (\(\Delta p = 0\)):

\[\Delta H = q_p = \Delta U + p\Delta V\]
  • For exothermic reactions (which evolve heat to the surroundings), \(\Delta H\) is negative (\(\Delta H < 0\)).
  • For endothermic reactions (which absorb heat from the surroundings), \(\Delta H\) is positive (\(\Delta H > 0\)).

5.2 Relationship Between \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta U\) for Gaseous Reactions

For reactions involving only solids and liquids, volume changes upon heating or reacting are negligible (\(\Delta H \approx \Delta U\)). When gases are involved at constant temperature \(T\) and pressure \(p\), let \(n_A\) (total volume \(V_A\)) be the moles of gaseous reactants and \(n_B\) (total volume \(V_B\)) be the moles of gaseous products. By the ideal gas law:

\[pV_A = n_A RT \quad \text{and} \quad pV_B = n_B RT \implies p(V_B - V_A) = p\Delta V = (n_B - n_A)RT = \Delta n_g RT\]
\[\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\]

where \(\Delta n_g = (\text{moles of gaseous products}) - (\text{moles of gaseous reactants})\).

5.3 Extensive and Intensive Properties

  • Extensive Properties: Properties whose value depends on the quantity or size of matter present in the system. Examples: mass (\(m\)), volume (\(V\)), internal energy (\(U\)), enthalpy (\(H\)), entropy (\(S\)), Gibbs energy (\(G\)), heat capacity (\(C\)).
  • Intensive Properties: Properties whose value does not depend on the quantity or size of matter present in the system. Examples: temperature (\(T\)), pressure (\(p\)), density (\(\rho\)), refractive index, viscosity, surface tension, and any molar property (\(\chi_{\mathrm{m}} = \chi / n\)) such as molar volume (\(V_{\mathrm{m}}\)), molar heat capacity (\(C_{\mathrm{m}}\)), and specific heat capacity (\(c\)).

Partition Test: If a container of gas of volume \(V\) at temperature \(T\) is divided into two equal halves by a partition, each compartment has volume \(\dfrac{V}{2}\) (showing \(V\) is extensive), while the temperature in each remains \(T\) (showing \(T\) is intensive).

5.4 Heat Capacity and the Relation \(C_p - C_V = R\)

The heat (\(q\)) absorbed by a system is proportional to its temperature rise (\(\Delta T\)):

\[q = C\Delta T = n C_{\mathrm{m}} \Delta T = m c \Delta T\]
  • Heat Capacity (\(C\)): Heat required to raise the temperature of the entire sample by \(1\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (or \(1\,\mathrm{K}\)). (Extensive property, unit: \(\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\)).
  • Molar Heat Capacity (\(C_{\mathrm{m}} = C/n\)): Heat required to raise the temperature of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a substance by \(1\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (or \(1\,\mathrm{K}\)). (Intensive property, unit: \(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).
  • Specific Heat Capacity (\(c = C/m\)): Heat required to raise the temperature of unit mass (\(1\,\mathrm{g}\) or \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\)) of a substance by \(1\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (or \(1\,\mathrm{K}\)). (Intensive property, unit: \(\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).

Derivation of \(C_p - C_V = R\) for \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an Ideal Gas:
At constant volume, \(q_V = C_V\Delta T = \Delta U\). At constant pressure, \(q_p = C_p\Delta T = \Delta H\).
For \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal gas, \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta(pV) = \Delta U + \Delta(RT) = \Delta U + R\Delta T\).
Substituting \(\Delta H = C_p\Delta T\) and \(\Delta U = C_V\Delta T\):

\[C_p\Delta T = C_V\Delta T + R\Delta T \implies C_p - C_V = R\]

5.5 Experimental Measurement of \(\Delta U\) and \(\Delta H\): Calorimetry

1. Measurement of \(\Delta U\) (Bomb Calorimeter): A combustible substance is burnt in pure dioxygen inside a sealed steel vessel (the bomb) immersed in a water bath at constant volume (\(\Delta V = 0\)). Because the volume does not change, no pressure-volume work is done (\(w = 0\)) even if gases are involved. The heat evolved transfers to the calorimeter and water bath, and the temperature rise \(\Delta T\) is converted to \(q_V = \Delta U\) using the heat capacity \(C_V\) of the calorimeter:

\[\Delta U = q_V = -C_V \times \Delta T\]

2. Measurement of \(\Delta H\) (Constant-Pressure Calorimeter): Carried out in a foamed polystyrene cup open to constant atmospheric pressure. The heat change measured is \(q_p = \Delta_r H\).

6. Reaction Enthalpy (\(\Delta_r H\)), Hess's Law, and Types of Enthalpies

6.1 Reaction Enthalpy and Standard States

The enthalpy change accompanying a chemical reaction is called the reaction enthalpy (\(\Delta_r H\)):

\[\Delta_r H = \sum_i a_i H_{\mathrm{products}} - \sum_i b_i H_{\mathrm{reactants}}\]

where \(a_i\) and \(b_i\) are the stoichiometric coefficients of products and reactants in the balanced chemical equation.

Standard State (\(\ominus\)): The standard state of a substance at a specified temperature is its pure form at a pressure of \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) (data are conventionally reported at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)). For example, the standard state of liquid ethanol at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) is pure liquid ethanol at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\); the standard state of solid iron at \(500\,\mathrm{K}\) is pure solid iron at \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\). Standard enthalpy changes are denoted by \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\).

6.2 Enthalpy Changes During Phase Transformations

  • Standard Enthalpy of Fusion (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}} H^\ominus\)): The enthalpy change accompanying the melting of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a solid substance in its standard state at constant temperature (melting point \(T_f\)) and standard pressure (\(1\,\mathrm{bar}\)). Melting is always endothermic (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}} H^\ominus > 0\)). Example: \[\mathrm{H_2O(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O(l)}; \quad \Delta_{\mathrm{fus}} H^\ominus = +6.00\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\] Freezing is the reverse process and releases an equal amount of heat (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{freeze}} H^\ominus = -\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}} H^\ominus\)).
  • Standard Enthalpy of Vaporization (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{vap}} H^\ominus\)): Amount of heat required to vaporize \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a liquid at constant temperature (boiling point \(T_b\)) and under standard pressure (\(1\,\mathrm{bar}\)). Example: \[\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O(g)}; \quad \Delta_{\mathrm{vap}} H^\ominus = +40.79\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]
  • Standard Enthalpy of Sublimation (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{sub}} H^\ominus\)): The change in enthalpy when \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a solid substance converts directly into vapour at a constant temperature and under standard pressure (\(1\,\mathrm{bar}\)). Example: Solid \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) ('dry ice') sublimes at \(195\,\mathrm{K}\) with \(\Delta_{\mathrm{sub}} H^\ominus = 25.2\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\); naphthalene has \(\Delta_{\mathrm{sub}} H^\ominus = 73.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

The magnitude of phase transformation enthalpies depends on the strength of intermolecular forces (e.g., strong hydrogen bonding in water vs. weaker dipole-dipole interactions in acetone).

6.3 Standard Molar Enthalpy of Formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\))

The standard molar enthalpy of formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)) is the standard enthalpy change for the formation of one mole of a compound from its constituent elements in their most stable states of aggregation (called reference states) at \(25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(298\,\mathrm{K}\)) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure.

  • Reference states of common elements: dihydrogen is \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\), dioxygen is \(\mathrm{O_2(g)}\), carbon is \(\mathrm{C(graphite, s)}\), sulphur is \(\mathrm{S(rhombic, s)}\), and bromine is \(\mathrm{Br_2(l)}\).
  • Convention: The standard enthalpy of formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)) of any element in its reference state is taken as zero.
  • Using \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\) values, the standard enthalpy of any reaction is calculated as:
\[\Delta_r H^\ominus = \sum_i a_i \Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathrm{products}) - \sum_i b_i \Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathrm{reactants})\]

Crucial Distinction: When is \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) Equal to \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)?

A reaction represents \(\Delta_f H^\ominus\) only when exact \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of the compound is formed directly from its constituent elements in their reference states:

  • \(\mathrm{CaO(s)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CaCO_3(s)}\) (\(\Delta_r H^\ominus = -178.3\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is NOT \(\Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathrm{CaCO_3})\) because it is formed from compounds, not elements.
  • \(\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{Br_2(l)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{HBr(g)}\) (\(\Delta_r H^\ominus = -72.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) forms \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{HBr}\), so \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = 2\Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathrm{HBr})\). Thus \(\Delta_f H^\ominus[\mathrm{HBr(g)}] = -36.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

6.4 Conventions for Thermochemical Equations

  1. A balanced chemical equation together with its value of \(\Delta_r H\) and physical states (including allotropic states) of all species is called a thermochemical equation.
  2. The stoichiometric coefficients refer strictly to the number of moles (never molecules) of reactants and products.
  3. The unit \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) means per mole of reaction as written. If the stoichiometric coefficients of an equation are multiplied or divided by a factor, \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) is multiplied or divided by the same factor (showing enthalpy is extensive).
  4. When a chemical equation is reversed, the magnitude of \(\Delta_r H^\ominus\) remains the same, but its sign is reversed.

6.5 Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation

Statement: If a reaction takes place in several steps, then its standard reaction enthalpy is the sum of the standard enthalpies of the intermediate reactions into which the overall reaction may be divided at the same temperature.

\[\Delta_r H = \Delta_r H_1 + \Delta_r H_2 + \Delta_r H_3 + \cdots\]

6.6 Enthalpies for Different Types of Reactions

  • Standard Enthalpy of Combustion (\(\Delta_c H^\ominus\)): The enthalpy change per mole of a substance when it undergoes complete combustion with all reactants and products in their standard states at the specified temperature. Combustion reactions are exothermic (\(\Delta_c H^\ominus < 0\)).
    • Butane: \(\mathrm{C_4H_{10}(g)} + \frac{13}{2}\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 5\mathrm{H_2O(l)}; \quad \Delta_c H^\ominus = -2658.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
    • Glucose: \(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6(s)} + 6\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 6\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 6\mathrm{H_2O(l)}; \quad \Delta_c H^\ominus = -2802.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • Enthalpy of Atomization (\(\Delta_a H^\ominus\)): The enthalpy change on breaking \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a substance completely into isolated atoms in the gas phase.
    • For diatomic gases (e.g., \(\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{H(g)}\), \(\Delta_a H^\ominus = 435.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), \(\Delta_a H^\ominus\) equals the bond dissociation enthalpy.
    • For metals (e.g., \(\mathrm{Na(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na(g)}\), \(\Delta_a H^\ominus = 108.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), \(\Delta_a H^\ominus\) equals the enthalpy of sublimation.
    • For methane: \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{C(g)} + 4\mathrm{H(g)}\), \(\Delta_a H^\ominus = 1665\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).
  • Bond Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{bond}} H^\ominus\)):
    • Bond Dissociation Enthalpy: Change in enthalpy when \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of covalent bonds of a gaseous covalent compound is broken to form products in the gas phase (e.g., \(\Delta_{\mathrm{Cl-Cl}} H^\ominus = 242\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\Delta_{\mathrm{O=O}} H^\ominus = 498\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).

6.7 Lattice Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{lattice}} H^\ominus\)) and the Born-Haber Cycle

The lattice enthalpy of an ionic compound is the enthalpy change when \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ionic compound dissociates completely into its ions in the gaseous state:

\[\mathrm{Na^+Cl^-(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na^+(g)} + \mathrm{Cl^-(g)}; \quad \Delta_{\mathrm{lattice}} H^\ominus = +788\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Because lattice enthalpy cannot be measured directly by experiment, we construct a thermodynamic cycle based on Hess's law called the Born-Haber Cycle. For \(\mathrm{NaCl(s)}\):

  1. Sublimation of sodium: \(\mathrm{Na(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na(g)}; \quad \Delta_{\mathrm{sub}} H^\ominus = +108.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  2. Ionization of sodium: \(\mathrm{Na(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na^+(g)} + \mathrm{e^-(g)}; \quad \Delta_i H^\ominus = +496\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  3. Atomization of chlorine: \(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Cl_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl(g)}; \quad \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{bond}} H^\ominus = +121\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  4. Electron gain by chlorine: \(\mathrm{Cl(g)} + \mathrm{e^-(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl^-(g)}; \quad \Delta_{eg} H^\ominus = -348.6\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  5. Formation of \(\mathrm{NaCl(s)}\): \(\mathrm{Na(s)} + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Cl_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{NaCl(s)}; \quad \Delta_f H^\ominus = -411.2\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)

Applying Hess's law around the cycle for \(\mathrm{NaCl(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na^+(g)} + \mathrm{Cl^-(g)}\):

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{lattice}} H^\ominus = -\Delta_f H^\ominus + \Delta_{\mathrm{sub}} H^\ominus + \Delta_i H^\ominus + \frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{bond}} H^\ominus + \Delta_{eg} H^\ominus = 411.2 + 108.4 + 496 + 121 - 348.6 = +788\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Since \(\Delta n_g = 2\) for \(\mathrm{NaCl(s)} \rightarrow \mathrm{Na^+(g)} + \mathrm{Cl^-(g)}\), the internal energy of lattice dissociation is smaller by \(2RT\) (\(\approx +783\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Relation at Temperature \(T\) vs. Absolute Zero: Ionization energy (\(E_0\)) and electron affinity (\(A\)) are defined at \(0\,\mathrm{K}\). Taking \(C_p = \frac{5}{2}R\) for each gaseous particle (\(\mathrm{M(g)}, \mathrm{M^+(g)}, \mathrm{M^-(g)}, \mathrm{e^-(g)}\)), \(\Delta_r C_p^\ominus = +\frac{5}{2}R\) for ionization and \(-\frac{5}{2}R\) for electron gain. Thus at temperature \(T\):

\[\Delta_i H^\ominus(T) = E_0 + \frac{5}{2}RT \quad \text{and} \quad \Delta_{eg} H^\ominus(T) = -A - \frac{5}{2}RT\]

6.8 Enthalpy of Solution (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}} H^\ominus\)) and Enthalpy of Dilution

Enthalpy of Solution (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}} H^\ominus\)): The enthalpy change when \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a solute dissolves in a specified amount of solvent (or in an infinite amount of solvent at infinite dilution, where ion-ion interactions are negligible). For an ionic solid \(\mathrm{AB(s)}\) dissolving in water:

\[\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}} H^\ominus = \Delta_{\mathrm{lattice}} H^\ominus + \Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}} H^\ominus\]

For \(\mathrm{NaCl(s)}\), \(\Delta_{\mathrm{lattice}} H^\ominus = +788\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}} H^\ominus = -784\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), so \(\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}} H^\ominus = +4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). For most ionic compounds, \(\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}} H^\ominus > 0\) (endothermic), so their solubility in water increases with rise in temperature.

Enthalpy of Dilution: The enthalpy change associated with adding additional solvent to a solution of specified original concentration at constant \(T\) and \(p\). For example, given:

  • \(\mathrm{HCl(g)} + 25\,\mathrm{aq.} \rightarrow \mathrm{HCl \cdot 25\,aq.}; \quad \Delta H_1 = -72.03\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  • \(\mathrm{HCl(g)} + 40\,\mathrm{aq.} \rightarrow \mathrm{HCl \cdot 40\,aq.}; \quad \Delta H_2 = -72.79\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)

Subtracting the first from the second gives the enthalpy of dilution for \(\mathrm{HCl \cdot 25\,aq.} + 15\,\mathrm{aq.} \rightarrow \mathrm{HCl \cdot 40\,aq.}\) as \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{dil}} = -72.79 - (-72.03) = -0.76\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). At infinite dilution (\(\mathrm{HCl \cdot \infty\,aq.}\)), \(\Delta H\) approaches \(-74.85\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

7. Spontaneity, Entropy (\(S\)), Gibbs Energy (\(G\)), and Equilibrium

7.1 What is a Spontaneous Process?

A spontaneous process is one that has the potential to proceed without the assistance of an external agency. It is unidirectional and irreversible on its own, and can only be reversed by an external agency. Spontaneity is a thermodynamic concept and does not indicate the rate of the reaction (for instance, \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) react spontaneously at room temperature, but at an imperceptibly slow rate).

While many spontaneous reactions are exothermic (\(\Delta_r H^\ominus < 0\), such as formation of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{HCl}\), and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)), several endothermic processes are also spontaneous—such as \(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{N_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{NO_2(g)}\) (\(\Delta_r H^\ominus = +33.2\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), \(\mathrm{C(graphite, s)} + 2\mathrm{S(l)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CS_2(l)}\) (\(\Delta_r H^\ominus = +128.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)), melting of ice above \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), or diffusion of two ideal gases into each other (\(\Delta H = 0\)). Thus, decrease in enthalpy is a contributory factor, not a universal criterion for spontaneity.

7.2 Entropy (\(S\)) and the Second & Third Laws of Thermodynamics

Entropy (\(S\)) is a thermodynamic state function and extensive property that measures the degree of randomness or disorder in a system. For a given substance:

\[S_{\text{crystalline solid}} < S_{\text{liquid}} \ll S_{\text{gas}}\]

Heat added to a system increases molecular motion (randomness), and a given quantity of heat added at a lower temperature causes a greater relative increase in randomness than at a higher temperature. For a reversible process:

\[\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} = \frac{q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\]
  • Second Law of Thermodynamics: In any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe (system + surroundings) always increases: \[\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = \Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} + \Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}} > 0 \quad (\text{Spontaneous Process})\] At equilibrium, entropy reaches its maximum and \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = 0\).
  • Discriminating Reversible vs. Irreversible Isothermal Expansion of an Ideal Gas: For both reversible and irreversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas, \(\Delta U = 0\). However, \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = \Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} + \Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}} = 0\) for a reversible process and \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} > 0\) for an irreversible process (such as free expansion). Thus, \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}}\) discriminates between reversible and irreversible processes, whereas \(\Delta U\) does not.
  • Third Law of Thermodynamics: The entropy of any pure crystalline substance approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero (\(0\,\mathrm{K}\)). This allows the determination of absolute entropies (\(S^\ominus\)) of pure substances by summing \(\dfrac{q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\) increments from \(0\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). (Note: Solutions and supercooled liquids retain residual disorder and do not have zero entropy at \(0\,\mathrm{K}\)).

7.3 Gibbs Energy (\(G\)) and Criterion of Spontaneity

To express spontaneity purely in terms of system properties at constant temperature and pressure, we define the Gibbs energy (or Gibbs function, \(G\)):

\[G = H - TS \implies \Delta G_{\mathrm{sys}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{sys}} - T\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } p)\]

Since \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}} = -\dfrac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{sys}}}{T}\) at constant pressure, \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = \Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} - \dfrac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{sys}}}{T}\), which gives:

\[T\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = T\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} - \Delta H_{\mathrm{sys}} = -\Delta G_{\mathrm{sys}}\]

Here, \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{sys}}\) is the total enthalpy change, \(T\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}}\) is the energy unavailable to do useful work, and \(-\Delta G\) is the net free energy available to do useful (non-expansion) work. At constant \(T\) and \(p\):

  • If \(\Delta G < 0\) (negative): The process is spontaneous.
  • If \(\Delta G = 0\): The system is at equilibrium.
  • If \(\Delta G > 0\) (positive): The process is non-spontaneous in the forward direction (spontaneous in reverse).

7.4 Gibbs Energy Change and Chemical Equilibrium

For a reversible chemical reaction \(\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{C} + \mathrm{D}\), dynamic equilibrium is reached when the Gibbs energy of the system is at a minimum (\(\Delta_r G = 0\)). The standard Gibbs energy change (\(\Delta_r G^\ominus\)) is related to the equilibrium constant (\(K\)) by:

\[\Delta_r G^\ominus = \Delta_r H^\ominus - T\Delta_r S^\ominus = -RT\ln K = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K\]
  • If \(\Delta_r G^\ominus < 0\), then \(\ln K > 0 \implies K > 1\) (products predominate at equilibrium).
  • If \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = 0\), then \(\ln K = 0 \implies K = 1\).
  • If \(\Delta_r G^\ominus > 0\), then \(\ln K < 0 \implies K < 1\) (reactants predominate at equilibrium).

Worked Example 1: First Law & Nature of System Boundaries Conceptual Application

Problem: Express the change in internal energy (\(\Delta U\)) of a system and identify the type of wall or system when:
(i) No heat is absorbed by the system from the surroundings, but work (\(w\)) is done on the system.
(ii) No work is done on the system, but \(q\) amount of heat is taken out from the system and given to the surroundings.
(iii) \(w\) amount of work is done by the system and \(q\) amount of heat is supplied to the system.

Solution:

  • (i) Here \(q = 0\) and work is done on the system (\(+w\)). From \(\Delta U = q + w\), we get \(\Delta U = w_{\mathrm{ad}}\). Since no heat is permitted to cross the boundary, the wall is an adiabatic wall.
  • (ii) Here \(w = 0\) and heat is lost by the system (\(-q\)). Thus \(\Delta U = -q\). Since heat transfers across the boundary, the system has thermally conducting walls.
  • (iii) Heat supplied to the system is \(+q\) and work done by the system is \(-w\). Thus \(\Delta U = q - w\). Since energy is exchanged as both heat and work without transfer of matter, it is a closed system.
Worked Example 2: Isothermal Expansion — Vacuum vs. Irreversible vs. Reversible Ideal Gas Work

Problem: \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal gas initially occupying \(2\,\mathrm{L}\) at a pressure of \(10\,\mathrm{atm}\) expands isothermally at \(25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(298\,\mathrm{K}\)) until its final volume is \(10\,\mathrm{L}\). Calculate the heat absorbed (\(q\)) and work done (\(w\)) when the expansion occurs:
(a) Into a vacuum (free expansion),
(b) Against a constant external pressure of \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\), and
(c) Reversibly.

Solution: Since the process is isothermal (\(T = 298\,\mathrm{K} = \text{constant}\)) for an ideal gas, \(\Delta U = 0\), so \(q = -w\).

  • (a) Expansion into vacuum (\(p_{\mathrm{ex}} = 0\)):
    \[w = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}(V_f - V_i) = -0 \times (10 - 2) = 0\,\mathrm{L\,atm}, \quad q = 0\,\mathrm{L\,atm}\] No work is done and no heat is absorbed.
  • (b) Against constant external pressure (\(p_{\mathrm{ex}} = 1\,\mathrm{atm}\)):
    \[w = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}(V_f - V_i) = -1\,\mathrm{atm} \times (10 - 2)\,\mathrm{L} = -8\,\mathrm{L\,atm}\] \[q = -w = +8\,\mathrm{L\,atm}\]
  • (c) Reversible isothermal expansion:
    \[w_{\mathrm{rev}} = -2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right) = -2.303 \times (1\,\mathrm{mol}) \times (0.08206\,\mathrm{L\,atm\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}) \times (298\,\mathrm{K}) \times \log_{10}\left(\frac{10}{2}\right)\] \[w_{\mathrm{rev}} = -2.303 \times 0.08206 \times 298 \times 0.6990 = -39.366\,\mathrm{L\,atm}\] \[q = -w_{\mathrm{rev}} = +39.366\,\mathrm{L\,atm}\] Key Takeaway: Work done by the gas during reversible expansion (\(39.366\,\mathrm{L\,atm}\)) is significantly greater than during single-step irreversible expansion (\(8\,\mathrm{L\,atm}\)).
Worked Example 3: Relating \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta U\) for Phase Change and Combustion Enthalpy & Internal Energy

Problem:
(a) A swimmer coming out from a pool is covered with a film of water weighing \(18\,\mathrm{g}\). How much heat must be supplied to evaporate this water at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)? Also calculate the internal energy of vaporization (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{vap}} U^\ominus\)) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), given \(\Delta_{\mathrm{vap}} H^\ominus = 44.01\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\).
(b) The reaction of solid cyanamide, \(\mathrm{NH_2CN(s)}\), with dioxygen was carried out in a bomb calorimeter, and \(\Delta U\) was found to be \(-742.7\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). Calculate \(\Delta H^\ominus\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) for the reaction: \[\mathrm{NH_2CN(s)} + \frac{3}{2}\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{N_2(g)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)}\]

Solution (a):
Moles of water in \(18\,\mathrm{g}\): \(n = \dfrac{18\,\mathrm{g}}{18\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 1\,\mathrm{mol}\).
For the evaporation \(\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O(g)}\), heat supplied at constant pressure is: \[q_p = n\Delta_{\mathrm{vap}} H^\ominus = 1\,\mathrm{mol} \times 44.01\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} = 44.01\,\mathrm{kJ}\] Here \(\Delta n_g = 1 - 0 = 1\). Using \(\Delta_{\mathrm{vap}} U^\ominus = \Delta_{\mathrm{vap}} H^\ominus - \Delta n_g RT\): \[\Delta_{\mathrm{vap}} U^\ominus = 44.01\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} - (1) \times (8.314 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}) \times (298\,\mathrm{K}) = 44.01 - 2.48 = 41.53\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Solution (b):
In \(\mathrm{NH_2CN(s)} + \frac{3}{2}\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{N_2(g)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)}\):
Moles of gaseous products \(= 1 + 1 = 2\,\mathrm{mol}\); moles of gaseous reactants \(= \frac{3}{2} = 1.5\,\mathrm{mol}\).
Therefore, \(\Delta n_g = 2 - 1.5 = +0.5\,\mathrm{mol}\). \[\Delta H^\ominus = \Delta U^\ominus + \Delta n_g RT = -742.7\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} + (0.5) \times (8.314 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}) \times (298\,\mathrm{K})\] \[\Delta H^\ominus = -742.7 + 1.24 = -741.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 4: Bomb Calorimetry Calculation Calorimetry

Problem: \(1.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of graphite is burnt in a bomb calorimeter in excess of oxygen at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) pressure according to \(\mathrm{C(graphite)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\). During the reaction, the temperature rises from \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) to \(299\,\mathrm{K}\). If the heat capacity of the bomb calorimeter is \(20.7\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}\), what is the molar enthalpy change (\(\Delta H\)) for the reaction at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\)?

Solution:
Heat evolved by combustion of \(1.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of graphite is absorbed by the calorimeter: \[q_V = -C_V \times \Delta T = -20.7\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}} \times (299 - 298)\,\mathrm{K} = -20.7\,\mathrm{kJ}\] For \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) (\(12.0\,\mathrm{g}\)) of graphite, the internal energy change is: \[\Delta U = \frac{-20.7\,\mathrm{kJ}}{1.0\,\mathrm{g}} \times 12.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = -248.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \approx -2.48 \times 10^2\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\] For \(\mathrm{C(graphite, s)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\), \(\Delta n_g = 1 - 1 = 0\). Therefore: \[\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT = \Delta U = -2.48 \times 10^2\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 5: Multi-Step Cooling and Phase Transformation Phase Transformation & Heat Capacity

Problem: Calculate the total enthalpy change on freezing \(1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of water at \(10.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to ice at \(-10.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Given: \(\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}} H = 6.03\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), \(C_p[\mathrm{H_2O(l)}] = 75.3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), and \(C_p[\mathrm{H_2O(s)}] = 36.8\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\).

Solution: By Hess's law, divide the overall change into three sequential steps:

  1. Step 1 — Cooling \(1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) liquid water from \(10.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(\Delta T = -10.0\,\mathrm{K}\)):
    \[\Delta H_1 = n C_p[\mathrm{H_2O(l)}]\Delta T = 1.0\,\mathrm{mol} \times 75.3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \times (-10.0\,\mathrm{K}) = -753\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} = -0.753\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]
  2. Step 2 — Freezing \(1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) liquid water to ice at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\):
    \[\Delta H_2 = -\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}} H = -6.03\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]
  3. Step 3 — Cooling \(1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) solid ice from \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(-10.0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(\Delta T = -10.0\,\mathrm{K}\)):
    \[\Delta H_3 = n C_p[\mathrm{H_2O(s)}]\Delta T = 1.0\,\mathrm{mol} \times 36.8\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \times (-10.0\,\mathrm{K}) = -368\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} = -0.368\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Total Enthalpy Change: \[\Delta H_{\mathrm{total}} = \Delta H_1 + \Delta H_2 + \Delta H_3 = -0.753 - 6.03 - 0.368 = -7.151\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Adding (1) and (2) gives \(\Delta_f H^\ominus[\mathrm{CCl_4(g)}] = -135.5 + 30.5 = -105.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).
Now, for \(\mathrm{CCl_4(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{C(g)} + 4\mathrm{Cl(g)}\): \[\Delta_a H^\ominus = \Delta_a H^\ominus(\mathrm{C}) + 2\Delta_a H^\ominus(\mathrm{Cl_2}) - \Delta_f H^\ominus[\mathrm{CCl_4(g)}] = 715.0 + 2(242) - (-105.0) = 1304.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\] Since \(4\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{C-Cl}\) bonds are broken per mole of \(\mathrm{CCl_4(g)}\): \[\text{Mean }\mathrm{C-Cl}\text{ Bond Enthalpy} = \frac{1304.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}{4} = 326.0\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 7: Entropy of System & Surroundings and Spontaneity Second Law & Gibbs Energy

Problem:
(a) For the oxidation of iron, \(4\mathrm{Fe(s)} + 3\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{Fe_2O_3(s)}\), the entropy change of the system (\(\Delta_r S_{\mathrm{sys}}^\ominus\)) is \(-549.4\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), and \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = -1648 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\). In spite of the negative entropy change of the system, why is the reaction spontaneous?
(b) For the reaction \(2\mathrm{A(g)} + \mathrm{B(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{D(g)}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), \(\Delta U^\ominus = -10.5\,\mathrm{kJ}\) and \(\Delta S^\ominus = -44.1\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\). Calculate \(\Delta G^\ominus\) for the reaction and predict whether it can occur spontaneously.

Solution (a):
Spontaneity is determined by \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = \Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} + \Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}}\). At constant pressure and \(T = 298\,\mathrm{K}\): \[\Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}} = -\frac{\Delta_r H^\ominus}{T} = -\frac{-1648 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}}{298\,\mathrm{K}} = +5530.2\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\] \[\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = -549.4 + 5530.2 = +4980.8\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} > 0\] Because \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}}\) is positive, the reaction is spontaneous.

Solution (b):
For \(2\mathrm{A(g)} + \mathrm{B(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{D(g)}\), \(\Delta n_g = 2 - (2 + 1) = -1\).
First calculate \(\Delta H^\ominus\) at \(T = 298\,\mathrm{K}\): \[\Delta H^\ominus = \Delta U^\ominus + \Delta n_g RT = -10.5\,\mathrm{kJ} + (-1)(8.314 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}})(298\,\mathrm{K}) = -10.5 - 2.48 = -12.98\,\mathrm{kJ}\] Now calculate \(\Delta G^\ominus\): \[\Delta G^\ominus = \Delta H^\ominus - T\Delta S^\ominus = -12.98\,\mathrm{kJ} - (298\,\mathrm{K})(-44.1 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kJ\,K^{-1}}) = -12.98 + 13.14 = +0.16\,\mathrm{kJ}\] Since \(\Delta G^\ominus = +0.16\,\mathrm{kJ} > 0\) (positive), the reaction is non-spontaneous at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\).

Worked Example 8: Equilibrium Constant (\(K_p\)) and Standard Gibbs Energy (\(\Delta_r G^\ominus\)) Chemical Equilibrium

Problem:
(a) Find the equilibrium constant \(K\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) for the reaction \(2\mathrm{NH_3(g)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{NH_2CONH_2(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)}\), given \(\Delta_r G^\ominus = -13.6\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).
(b) At \(60\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(333\,\mathrm{K}\)), dinitrogen tetroxide (\(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\)) is \(50\%\) dissociated according to \(\mathrm{N_2O_4(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NO_2(g)}\) at a total pressure of \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\). Calculate the standard free energy change (\(\Delta_r G^\ominus\)) at this temperature.

Solution (a): \[\log_{10} K = \frac{-\Delta_r G^\ominus}{2.303\,RT} = \frac{-(-13.6 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}})}{2.303 \times 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \times 298\,\mathrm{K}} = 2.38\] \[K = \mathrm{antilog}(2.38) = 2.4 \times 10^2\]

Solution (b):
Start with \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{N_2O_4(g)}\). At \(50\%\) dissociation (\(\alpha = 0.5\)):
Moles at equilibrium: \(n(\mathrm{N_2O_4}) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5\,\mathrm{mol}\); \(n(\mathrm{NO_2}) = 2 \times 0.5 = 1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\); total moles \(= 1.5\,\mathrm{mol}\).
Partial pressures at \(p_{\mathrm{total}} = 1\,\mathrm{atm}\): \[p_{\mathrm{N_2O_4}} = \frac{0.5}{1.5} \times 1\,\mathrm{atm} = \frac{1}{3}\,\mathrm{atm}, \quad p_{\mathrm{NO_2}} = \frac{1.0}{1.5} \times 1\,\mathrm{atm} = \frac{2}{3}\,\mathrm{atm}\] \[K_p = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{NO_2}})^2}{p_{\mathrm{N_2O_4}}} = \frac{(2/3)^2}{1/3} = \frac{4}{3} = 1.33\,\mathrm{atm}\] \[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K_p = -2.303 \times (8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}) \times (333\,\mathrm{K}) \times \log_{10}(1.33)\] \[\Delta_r G^\ominus = -6375.9 \times 0.1239 = -789.9\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx -0.79\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 1 NEET UG — Single Choice MCQ

Which of the following sets contains only thermodynamic state functions and intensive properties?

  • (1) Internal energy (\(U\)), Molar enthalpy (\(H_{\mathrm{m}}\)), Temperature (\(T\))
  • (2) Pressure (\(p\)), Temperature (\(T\)), Molar heat capacity (\(C_{\mathrm{m}}\))
  • (3) Specific heat capacity (\(c\)), Work (\(w\)), Density (\(\rho\))
  • (4) Enthalpy (\(H\)), Entropy (\(S\)), Gibbs energy (\(G\))
Correct Answer: (2)

Worked Solution:
State functions depend only on the state of the system, and intensive properties are independent of the amount of matter present.
• In (1), \(U\) is a state function but an extensive property.
• In (2), pressure (\(p\)), temperature (\(T\)), and molar heat capacity (\(C_{\mathrm{m}} = C/n\)) are all state functions (or state variables) and are all intensive properties.
• In (3), work (\(w\)) is a path function and extensive.
• In (4), \(H, S,\) and \(G\) are state functions, but all three are extensive properties.

Practice Question 13 NEET UG — Assertion-Reason

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as Assertion (A) and the other is labelled as Reason (R).

  • (2) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).

  • (4) (A) is false but (R) is true.

10. Common Pitfalls & Exam Revision Checklist

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in NEET UG

  1. Physics vs. Chemistry Sign Convention for Work: In IUPAC chemical thermodynamics, work done ON the system is positive (\(w > 0\)) and work done BY the system is negative (\(w < 0\)), with \(\Delta U = q + w\). Never mix this up with the older convention \(\Delta U = q - w\).
  2. Unit Mismatch in \(\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\) and \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\): \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta U\) are almost always given in \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), whereas \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(\Delta S\) are given in \(\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\). Always convert \(R\) and \(\Delta S\) to \(\mathrm{kJ}\) by multiplying by \(10^{-3}\) before adding or subtracting!
  3. Counting Solids or Liquids in \(\Delta n_g\): Only gaseous reactants and products are included when computing \(\Delta n_g = n_g(\mathrm{products}) - n_g(\mathrm{reactants})\). Ignore all solids, liquids, and aqueous species.
  4. Reactants vs. Products Order in Enthalpy Formulas:
    • Using Enthalpies of Formation (\(\Delta_f H^\ominus\)): \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = \sum \Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathbf{products}) - \sum \Delta_f H^\ominus(\mathbf{reactants})\).
    • Using Bond Enthalpies (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{bond}} H^\ominus\)) or Combustion Enthalpies (\(\Delta_c H^\ominus\)): \(\Delta_r H^\ominus = \sum (\mathbf{reactants}) - \sum (\mathbf{products})\).
  5. Confusing \(\Delta U\) and \(\Delta S\) in Isothermal Expansion of an Ideal Gas: For an ideal gas at constant \(T\), \(\Delta U = 0\) and \(\Delta H = 0\) whether the expansion is reversible or irreversible. However, \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = 0\) for reversible expansion and \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} > 0\) for irreversible expansion.

Concise Master Formula Recap

  • First Law of Thermodynamics: \(\Delta U = q + w\) (For isolated system: \(q = 0, w = 0 \implies \Delta U = 0\))
  • Irreversible \(pV\)-Work: \(w_{\mathrm{irrev}} = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}\Delta V = -p_{\mathrm{ex}}(V_f - V_i)\)
  • Reversible Isothermal \(pV\)-Work (Ideal Gas): \(w_{\mathrm{rev}} = -nRT\ln\left(\dfrac{V_f}{V_i}\right) = -2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\left(\dfrac{V_f}{V_i}\right)\)
  • Enthalpy & Internal Energy Relation: \(H = U + pV \implies \Delta H = \Delta U + p\Delta V = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT\)
  • Heat Capacity Relations: \(q = C\Delta T = n C_{\mathrm{m}}\Delta T = mc\Delta T\); \(\quad q_V = \Delta U = n C_{V,\mathrm{m}}\Delta T\); \(\quad q_p = \Delta H = n C_{p,\mathrm{m}}\Delta T\); \(\quad C_p - C_V = R\) (for \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) ideal gas)
  • Enthalpy of Solution: \(\Delta_{\mathrm{sol}} H^\ominus = \Delta_{\mathrm{lattice}} H^\ominus + \Delta_{\mathrm{hyd}} H^\ominus\)
  • Entropy & Second Law: \(\Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} = \dfrac{q_{\mathrm{rev}}}{T}\); \(\quad \Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}} = -\dfrac{\Delta H_{\mathrm{sys}}}{T}\) (at constant \(p, T\)); \(\quad \Delta S_{\mathrm{total}} = \Delta S_{\mathrm{sys}} + \Delta S_{\mathrm{surr}} > 0\) (spontaneous)
  • Gibbs Energy & Equilibrium: \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\); \(\quad \Delta_r G^\ominus = -RT\ln K = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K\)
