2. Physical and Chemical Equilibrium

2.1 Core Intuition: What Does "Dynamic" Mean?

When a liquid evaporates inside an evacuated, sealed container at a fixed temperature, molecules with higher kinetic energy escape into the vapour phase. As vapour accumulates, gas molecules collide with the liquid surface and condense back. Eventually, the rate of evaporation equals the rate of condensation, and the pressure exerted by the vapour becomes constant (the equilibrium vapour pressure).

Macroscopically, nothing appears to change: mass, concentration, pressure, and colour remain steady. Microscopically, both opposing processes continue simultaneously at identical speeds. This is dynamic equilibrium, and it requires a closed system at a constant temperature.

Physical Process Representative System Constant Property at Fixed Temperature
Solid ⇌ Liquid \(\mathrm{H_2O(s)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2O(l)}\) Melting/freezing point at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) (\(1.013\,\mathrm{bar}\)) is fixed (\(273\,\mathrm{K}\) for pure water).
Liquid ⇌ Vapour \(\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_2O(g)}\) Equilibrium vapour pressure \(p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}\) is constant; higher vapour pressure means higher volatility and lower boiling point.
Solid ⇌ Vapour \(\mathrm{I_2(s)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{I_2(g)}\) Vapour pressure (and colour intensity for \(\mathrm{I_2}\)) is constant; seen in subliming solids like camphor and \(\mathrm{NH_4Cl}\).
Solid ⇌ Solution \(\mathrm{Sugar(s)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Sugar(aq)}\) Concentration of solute in a saturated solution (solubility) is constant.
Gas ⇌ Dissolved Gas \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CO_2(aq)}\) Governed by Henry's Law: mass of dissolved gas is proportional to gas pressure above the liquid; decreases as \(T\) rises.

2.2 Chemical Equilibrium & Reversibility from Either Direction

In a reversible chemical reaction such as \(\mathrm{H_2(g) + I_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{2HI(g)}\), starting with pure reactants causes the forward rate to fall and the reverse rate to rise until both rates match. Equally, starting with pure \(\mathrm{HI(g)}\) in the same volume with the same total atoms reaches the exact same equilibrium composition. Dynamic exchange at equilibrium is proven experimentally using isotopes (for example, mixing \(\mathrm{H_2/N_2/NH_3}\) and \(\mathrm{D_2/N_2/ND_3}\) at equilibrium scrambles \(\mathrm{H}\) and \(\mathrm{D}\) into \(\mathrm{HD, NH_2D, NHD_2}\)).

2.3 Law of Chemical Equilibrium: \(K_c\), \(K_p\), and Manipulation Rules

Proposed by Guldberg and Waage, the Law of Chemical Equilibrium states that at a fixed temperature, the ratio of equilibrium product concentrations (raised to their stoichiometric coefficients) to reactant concentrations (raised to theirs) is constant.

Key Equilibrium Constant Relations

For a general balanced reaction \(a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}\):

\[ K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{C}]^c[\mathrm{D}]^d}{[\mathrm{A}]^a[\mathrm{B}]^b}, \qquad K_p = \frac{(p_{\mathrm{C}})^c(p_{\mathrm{D}})^d}{(p_{\mathrm{A}})^a(p_{\mathrm{B}})^b} \]

Using the ideal gas law \(p = cRT\) (with \(R = 0.0831\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)):

\[ K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}, \quad \text{where } \Delta n_g = (\text{moles of gaseous products}) - (\text{moles of gaseous reactants}) \]
  • Reversing an equation: \(K_{\text{rev}} = \dfrac{1}{K_{\text{fwd}}}\).
  • Multiplying an equation by \(n\): \(K_{\text{new}} = (K_{\text{orig}})^n\). (For halving an equation, \(n = \frac{1}{2}\), so \(K_{\text{new}} = \sqrt{K_{\text{orig}}}\)).
  • Adding two or more reactions: \(K_{\text{net}} = K_1 \times K_2 \times \cdots\).
  • Temperature dependence: \(K\) depends only on temperature and the balanced stoichiometry, never on initial concentrations, pressure, or catalysts.

2.4 Homogeneous vs. Heterogeneous Equilibria

In a homogeneous equilibrium, all species occupy the same phase (e.g., all gases or all aqueous solutes). In a heterogeneous equilibrium, more than one phase coexists, such as \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CaO(s)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\).

Because the molar concentration (active mass) of a pure solid or pure liquid is constant regardless of the amount present, pure solids and pure liquids are omitted from \(K_c\) and \(K_p\) expressions (though a small amount of each solid/liquid must physically be present for equilibrium to exist):

\[ K_c = [\mathrm{CO_2(g)}], \qquad K_p = p_{\mathrm{CO_2}} \]

2.5 Applications of \(K\): Extent, Direction (\(Q\)), and Gibbs Energy (\(\Delta G^\circ\))

  • Predicting Extent: If \(K_c > 10^3\), products dominate (reaction nears completion). If \(K_c < 10^{-3}\), reactants dominate (reaction barely proceeds). If \(10^{-3} \le K_c \le 10^3\), appreciable amounts of both reactants and products coexist.
  • Predicting Direction via Reaction Quotient (\(Q\)): \(Q_c\) has the exact same algebraic form as \(K_c\), but uses concentrations at an arbitrary instant \(t\).
Tutorial Diagram 3: Predicting Reaction Direction by Comparing Qc and Kc
Thermodynamics of Equilibrium
\[ \Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln Q \]

At equilibrium, \(\Delta G = 0\) and \(Q = K\), giving:

\[ \Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K = -2.303\,RT \log_{10} K \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad K = e^{-\Delta G^\circ / RT} \]

If \(\Delta G^\circ < 0\), then \(K > 1\) (products favoured). If \(\Delta G^\circ > 0\), then \(K < 1\) (reactants favoured).

2.6 Le Chatelier's Principle

When a system at equilibrium is disturbed by changing concentration, pressure, volume, or temperature, it shifts in the direction that partially counteracts the imposed change.

Disturbance Direction of Equilibrium Shift Effect on \(K\)
Add reactant / Remove product Shifts forward (consumes added reactant / replenishes product). No change
Increase pressure (decrease volume) Shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas. No shift if \(\Delta n_g = 0\). No change
Add inert gas at constant volume No shift (partial pressures of reacting gases remain unchanged). No change
Add inert gas at constant pressure Total volume increases; shifts toward more moles of gas (if \(\Delta n_g \ne 0\)). No change
Increase temperature Shifts in the endothermic direction (\(\Delta H > 0\) forward; \(\Delta H < 0\) reverse). Increases if \(\Delta H > 0\); decreases if \(\Delta H < 0\)
Add a catalyst No shift; lowers \(E_a\) of forward and reverse paths equally, reaching equilibrium faster. No change
Worked Example 1: ICE Table, \(K_c\), and \(K_p\) Calculation

Problem: Suppose \(0.40\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{SO_2Cl_2(g)}\) is placed in an evacuated \(2.0\,\mathrm{L}\) vessel at an assumed temperature of \(400\,\mathrm{K}\). At equilibrium, \(25\%\) of \(\mathrm{SO_2Cl_2}\) dissociates via \(\mathrm{SO_2Cl_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \mathrm{Cl_2(g)}\). Calculate \(K_c\) and \(K_p\) (take \(R = 0.083\,\mathrm{bar\,L\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).

Solution:

Initial molarity of \(\mathrm{SO_2Cl_2} = \dfrac{0.40\,\mathrm{mol}}{2.0\,\mathrm{L}} = 0.20\,\mathrm{M}\). Amount dissociated \(= 0.25 \times 0.20 = 0.05\,\mathrm{M}\).

At equilibrium: \([\mathrm{SO_2Cl_2}] = 0.20 - 0.05 = 0.15\,\mathrm{M}\), \([\mathrm{SO_2}] = 0.05\,\mathrm{M}\), \([\mathrm{Cl_2}] = 0.05\,\mathrm{M}\).

\[ K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{SO_2}][\mathrm{Cl_2}]}{[\mathrm{SO_2Cl_2}]} = \frac{0.05 \times 0.05}{0.15} = \frac{1}{60} \approx 0.0167\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}} \]

Here, \(\Delta n_g = (1 + 1) - 1 = 1\). Therefore:

\[ K_p = K_c(RT)^1 = 0.0167 \times 0.083 \times 400 \approx 0.553\,\mathrm{bar} \]
Worked Example 2: Predicting Direction Using \(Q_c\)

Problem: For \(\mathrm{N_2(g)} + 3\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\mathrm{NH_3(g)}\) at an assumed temperature of \(500\,\mathrm{K}\), \(K_c = 0.50\). A \(2.0\,\mathrm{L}\) flask contains \(0.40\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(0.80\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{H_2}\), and \(0.40\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). In which direction will the net reaction proceed?

Solution:

Initial concentrations: \([\mathrm{N_2}] = 0.20\,\mathrm{M}\), \([\mathrm{H_2}] = 0.40\,\mathrm{M}\), \([\mathrm{NH_3}] = 0.20\,\mathrm{M}\).

\[ Q_c = \frac{[\mathrm{NH_3}]^2}{[\mathrm{N_2}][\mathrm{H_2}]^3} = \frac{(0.20)^2}{(0.20)(0.40)^3} = \frac{0.04}{0.20 \times 0.064} = 3.125 \]

Since \(Q_c (3.125) > K_c (0.50)\), the net reaction proceeds in the reverse direction (forming more \(\mathrm{N_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2}\)).

Worked Example 3: Standard Gibbs Energy and \(K\)

Problem: For a hypothetical gas-phase isomerisation \(\mathrm{A(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B(g)}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), \(\Delta G^\circ = -5.70\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Estimate \(K\) (given \(2.303\,RT \approx 5705\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)).

Solution:

\[ \Delta G^\circ = -2.303\,RT \log_{10} K \implies -5700 = -5705 \log_{10} K \] \[ \log_{10} K \approx 1.00 \implies K \approx 10 \]

3. Ionic Equilibrium in Aqueous Solutions

3.1 Strong vs. Weak Electrolytes & Ostwald's Dilution Law

Substances that conduct electricity in water via mobile ions are electrolytes. Strong electrolytes (strong acids like \(\mathrm{HCl}\), strong bases like \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), and soluble salts like \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)) ionize nearly \(100\%\). Weak electrolytes (such as \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\) or \(\mathrm{NH_4OH}\)) ionize only partially, establishing an equilibrium between unionized molecules and ions.

For a weak monoprotic acid \(\mathrm{HA}\) of initial molarity \(c\) and degree of ionization \(\alpha\):

\[ K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} = \frac{c\alpha^2}{1 - \alpha} \]

When \(\alpha \ll 1\) (typically \(\alpha < 0.05\)), \(1 - \alpha \approx 1\), yielding Ostwald's Dilution Law: \(\alpha \approx \sqrt{\dfrac{K_a}{c}}\). Thus, dilution increases the degree of ionization (\(\alpha \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\)), even though \([\mathrm{H^+}] = c\alpha = \sqrt{K_a c}\) decreases.

3.2 Three Theories of Acids and Bases

Theory Acid Definition Base Definition Key Scope / Examples
Arrhenius Produces \(\mathrm{H^+}\) (\(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)) in water. Produces \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) in water. Aqueous only; \(\mathrm{HCl}\), \(\mathrm{NaOH}\).
Brønsted–Lowry Proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) donor. Proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) acceptor. Conjugate pairs differ by one \(\mathrm{H^+}\); e.g., \(\mathrm{NH_4^+ / NH_3}\).
Lewis Electron-pair acceptor. Electron-pair donor. Includes proton-free acids: \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\), \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\).
Tutorial Diagram 4: Conjugate Acid–Base Pairs Differ by Exactly One Proton (H⁺)

Crucial Rule: A stronger Brønsted acid always has a weaker conjugate base, and vice versa. For any conjugate acid–base pair in water:

\[ K_a \times K_b = K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \quad (\text{at } 298\,\mathrm{K}) \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \mathrm{p}K_a + \mathrm{p}K_b = \mathrm{p}K_w = 14 \]

3.3 Periodic Trends in Acid Strength

  • Down a Group: \(\mathrm{H{-}A}\) bond strength decreases as atomic size increases, making proton release easier. Thus, \(\mathrm{HF} \ll \mathrm{HCl} \ll \mathrm{HBr} \ll \mathrm{HI}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O} < \mathrm{H_2S}\).
  • Across a Period: \(\mathrm{H{-}A}\) bond polarity increases with electronegativity of \(\mathrm{A}\). Thus, \(\mathrm{CH_4} < \mathrm{NH_3} < \mathrm{H_2O} < \mathrm{HF}\).
  • Polyprotic Acids: Successive ionization constants drop sharply (\(K_{a1} \gg K_{a2} \gg K_{a3}\)) because removing a \(\mathrm{H^+}\) from a negatively charged anion is electrostatically harder than from a neutral molecule.

3.4 Autoionization of Water and the pH Scale

Water is amphiprotic: \(2\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_3O^+(aq)} + \mathrm{OH^-(aq)}\), with ionic product \(K_w = [\mathrm{H_3O^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{M^2}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). Since autoionization is endothermic, \(K_w\) increases with temperature (so neutral \(\mathrm{pH}\) drops slightly below \(7\) above \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)).

pH, pOH, and Neutrality at 298 K
\[ \mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{H_3O^+}], \qquad \mathrm{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{OH^-}], \qquad \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14 \]

Acidic: \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] > [\mathrm{OH^-}]\) (\(\mathrm{pH} < 7\)) • Neutral: \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = [\mathrm{OH^-}]\) (\(\mathrm{pH} = 7\)) • Basic: \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] < [\mathrm{OH^-}]\) (\(\mathrm{pH} > 7\))

3.5 Common Ion Effect and Buffer Solutions

Adding a strong electrolyte that shares an ion with a weak electrolyte suppresses the ionization of the weak electrolyte (Le Chatelier's principle). This common ion effect is the basis of buffer solutions, which resist \(\mathrm{pH}\) changes upon dilution or small additions of strong acid or base.

Henderson–Hasselbalch Equations
  • Acidic Buffer (weak acid \(\mathrm{HA}\) + salt with strong base, e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH + CH_3COONa}\)): \[ \mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}\frac{[\text{Salt}]}{[\text{Acid}]} \]
  • Basic Buffer (weak base \(\mathrm{B}\) + salt with strong acid, e.g., \(\mathrm{NH_4OH + NH_4Cl}\)): \[ \mathrm{pOH} = \mathrm{p}K_b + \log_{10}\frac{[\text{Salt}]}{[\text{Base}]}, \qquad \mathrm{pH} = 14 - \mathrm{pOH} \]

When \([\text{Salt}] = [\text{Acid}]\), \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a\). Dilution does not alter buffer \(\mathrm{pH}\) because the ratio inside the logarithm remains unchanged.

3.6 Salt Hydrolysis

Salt Type Example Solution Nature (298 K) Key Result
Strong Acid + Strong Base \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{KNO_3}\) Neutral (\(\mathrm{pH} = 7\)) Neither ion hydrolyses.
Weak Acid + Strong Base \(\mathrm{CH_3COONa}\) Basic (\(\mathrm{pH} > 7\)) Anionic hydrolysis produces \(\mathrm{OH^-}\).
Strong Acid + Weak Base \(\mathrm{NH_4Cl}\) Acidic (\(\mathrm{pH} < 7\)) Cationic hydrolysis produces \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\).
Weak Acid + Weak Base \(\mathrm{CH_3COONH_4}\) Depends on \(\mathrm{p}K_a - \mathrm{p}K_b\) \(\mathrm{pH} = 7 + \frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{p}K_a - \mathrm{p}K_b)\); independent of concentration.

3.7 Solubility Equilibria of Sparingly Soluble Salts (\(K_{sp}\))

For a sparingly soluble salt \(\mathrm{M}_x\mathrm{X}_y(\mathrm{s}) \rightleftharpoons x\mathrm{M}^{p+}(\mathrm{aq}) + y\mathrm{X}^{q-}(\mathrm{aq})\) with molar solubility \(S\) in pure water:

\[ K_{sp} = [\mathrm{M}^{p+}]^x[\mathrm{X}^{q-}]^y = (xS)^x(yS)^y = x^x y^y S^{x+y} \]
  • AB type (e.g., \(\mathrm{AgCl}\), \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\)): \(K_{sp} = S^2\)
  • AB₂ or A₂B type (e.g., \(\mathrm{CaF_2}\), \(\mathrm{Ag_2CrO_4}\)): \(K_{sp} = 4S^3\)
  • A₂B₃ type (e.g., \(\mathrm{Bi_2S_3}\)): \(K_{sp} = 108S^5\)
  • Precipitation Criterion: Compare ionic product \(Q_{sp}\) with \(K_{sp}\). Precipitation occurs if and only if \(Q_{sp} > K_{sp}\).
  • Common Ion & pH Effects: Adding a common ion decreases molar solubility \(S\). Conversely, salts of weak acids (e.g., carbonates, phosphates, benzoates) become more soluble at lower \(\mathrm{pH}\) because \(\mathrm{H^+}\) protonates the anion, shifting dissolution forward.
Worked Example 4: Weak Acid pH via Ostwald's Dilution Law

Problem: A \(0.04\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous solution of a weak monobasic acid \(\mathrm{HA}\) has \(K_a = 1.6 \times 10^{-5}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) (assumed values). Find \(\alpha\), \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}]\), and \(\mathrm{pH}\). (Given \(\log_{10} 8 = 0.903\)).

Solution:

\[ \alpha = \sqrt{\frac{K_a}{c}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.6 \times 10^{-5}}{0.04}} = \sqrt{4 \times 10^{-4}} = 0.02 \text{ (or } 2\%) \]

Since \(\alpha = 0.02 \ll 1\), the approximation is valid. Now:

\[ [\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = c\alpha = 0.04 \times 0.02 = 8.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{M} \] \[ \mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}(8.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 4 - \log_{10} 8 = 4 - 0.903 = 3.10 \]
Worked Example 5: Designing an Acidic Buffer

Problem: \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.20\,\mathrm{M}\) formic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 3.74\)) is mixed with \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.40\,\mathrm{M}\) sodium formate. Calculate the \(\mathrm{pH}\) of the buffer. (Given \(\log_{10} 2 = 0.301\)).

Solution:

Total volume \(= 200\,\mathrm{mL}\). Dilution halves both concentrations: \([\mathrm{Acid}] = 0.10\,\mathrm{M}\) and \([\mathrm{Salt}] = 0.20\,\mathrm{M}\).

\[ \mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}\frac{[\text{Salt}]}{[\text{Acid}]} = 3.74 + \log_{10}\frac{0.20}{0.10} = 3.74 + 0.301 = 4.04 \]
Worked Example 6: Solubility Product and Common Ion Suppression

Problem: Assume \(K_{sp}\) of \(\mathrm{CaF_2}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(4.0 \times 10^{-11}\). Calculate its molar solubility in (a) pure water, and (b) \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{NaF}\) solution. (Take \(10^{1/3} \approx 2.15\)).

Solution:

(a) Pure water: \(K_{sp} = S(2S)^2 = 4S^3 = 4.0 \times 10^{-11} \implies S^3 = 10 \times 10^{-12} \implies S \approx 2.15 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{M}\).

(b) In \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{NaF}\): Total \([\mathrm{F^-}] = 0.10 + 2S' \approx 0.10\,\mathrm{M}\). Thus:

\[ 4.0 \times 10^{-11} = S'(0.10)^2 \implies S' = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-11}}{0.01} = 4.0 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{M} \]

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

A pure liquid is in equilibrium with its vapour inside a closed cylinder fitted with a movable piston at constant temperature \(T\). If the piston is pulled outward to double the volume and the system is allowed to return to equilibrium at the same temperature \(T\) (with some liquid still remaining), the final vapour pressure will:

  1. Become half of the initial vapour pressure
  2. Become double the initial vapour pressure
  3. Remain equal to the initial vapour pressure
  4. Decrease slightly and then stay lower than before
Answer: C

Solution: Equilibrium vapour pressure of a pure liquid depends solely on the nature of the liquid and temperature. Doubling the volume initially lowers the pressure, causing net evaporation until the original equilibrium vapour pressure is restored at temperature \(T\).

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

At a given temperature, the equilibrium constant for the reaction \(\mathrm{2SO_2(g) + O_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{2SO_3(g)}\) is \(64\). What is the equilibrium constant for \(\mathrm{SO_3(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{O_2(g)}\) at the same temperature?

  1. \(0.125\)
  2. \(0.0156\)
  3. \(8.0\)
  4. \(32.0\)
Answer: A

Solution: The target equation is obtained by reversing the original equation (\(K' = 1/64\)) and multiplying all coefficients by \(\frac{1}{2}\) (\(K'' = (1/64)^{1/2} = 1/8 = 0.125\)).

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

For which of the following gas-phase equilibria is the relation \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{-2}\) valid?

  1. \(\mathrm{H_2(g) + I_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{2HI(g)}\)
  2. \(\mathrm{PCl_5(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{PCl_3(g) + Cl_2(g)}\)
  3. \(\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{2NH_3(g)}\)
  4. \(\mathrm{2SO_3(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{2SO_2(g) + O_2(g)}\)
Answer: C

Solution: Since \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\), we need \(\Delta n_g = -2\). For \(\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3H_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{2NH_3(g)}\), \(\Delta n_g = 2 - (1 + 3) = -2\).

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Consider the endothermic dissociation \(\mathrm{PCl_5(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{PCl_3(g) + Cl_2(g)}\) (\(\Delta H > 0\)) at equilibrium. Which change will shift the equilibrium in the forward direction?

  1. Adding \(\mathrm{Cl_2(g)}\) at constant volume
  2. Lowering the temperature
  3. Adding argon gas at constant volume
  4. Increasing the volume of the container at constant temperature
Answer: D

Solution: Increasing volume decreases pressure, so equilibrium shifts toward the side with more gaseous moles (forward: \(1 \rightarrow 2\) moles). Adding \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) or lowering \(T\) shifts it backward; adding Ar at constant \(V\) causes no shift.

Practice Question 5 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the two statements carefully and choose the correct option:

Statement I: A catalyst increases the rates of both the forward and reverse reactions by the same factor.

Statement II: Adding a catalyst increases the numerical value of the equilibrium constant \(K_c\).

  1. Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  2. Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  3. Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect
  4. Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct
Answer: C

Solution: A catalyst lowers the activation energy of both forward and reverse paths by the exact same amount, accelerating both rates equally. However, it does not alter \(\Delta G^\circ\) or \(K_c\).

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following pairs represents a valid Brønsted–Lowry conjugate acid–base pair?

  1. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) and \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\)
  2. \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\)
  3. \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\) and \(\mathrm{OH^-}\)
  4. \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_2^-}\)
Answer: B

Solution: A conjugate acid–base pair differs by exactly one proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)). \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\) loses one \(\mathrm{H^+}\) to form \(\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\). The other pairs differ by two protons.

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

What is the approximate \(\mathrm{pH}\) of a \(1.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}\) aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{HCl}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)?

  1. \(8.00\)
  2. \(7.00\)
  3. \(6.98\)
  4. \(6.00\)
Answer: C

Solution: An acid solution cannot have \(\mathrm{pH} \ge 7\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). Accounting for water autoionization, let \(x = [\mathrm{OH^-}]\) from water; then \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = 10^{-8} + x\). Solving \(x(10^{-8} + x) = 10^{-14}\) gives \(x \approx 9.5 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}\), so \([\mathrm{H_3O^+}] \approx 1.05 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\) and \(\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}(1.05 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 6.98\).

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

If the base ionization constant \(K_b\) of a weak base \(\mathrm{B}\) is \(2.0 \times 10^{-5}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), what is the acid ionization constant \(K_a\) of its conjugate acid \(\mathrm{BH^+}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\)?

  1. \(5.0 \times 10^{-10}\)
  2. \(2.0 \times 10^{-9}\)
  3. \(5.0 \times 10^{-9}\)
  4. \(2.0 \times 10^{-19}\)
Answer: A

Solution: For a conjugate pair at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), \(K_a \times K_b = K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\). Hence, \(K_a = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{2.0 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-10}\).

Practice Question 9 Single-Correct MCQ

A buffer solution at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) contains \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{NH_4OH}\) (\(\mathrm{p}K_b = 4.75\)) and \(0.10\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{NH_4Cl}\). What is the \(\mathrm{pH}\) of this buffer?

  1. \(4.75\)
  2. \(7.00\)
  3. \(9.25\)
  4. \(10.25\)
Answer: C

Solution: Using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation for a basic buffer: \(\mathrm{pOH} = \mathrm{p}K_b + \log_{10}\left(\dfrac{0.10}{0.10}\right) = 4.75 + 0 = 4.75\). At \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), \(\mathrm{pH} = 14 - 4.75 = 9.25\).

Practice Question 10 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): An aqueous solution of \(\mathrm{NH_4Cl}\) is acidic at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\).

Reason (R): \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\) ions undergo cationic hydrolysis with water to generate \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\) ions, whereas \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) ions do not hydrolyse.

  1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
  2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
  3. A is true, but R is false
  4. A is false, but R is true
Answer: A

Solution: \(\mathrm{NH_4Cl}\) is a salt of a weak base (\(\mathrm{NH_4OH}\)) and a strong acid (\(\mathrm{HCl}\)). \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) is the very weak conjugate base of a strong acid and does not hydrolyse, while \(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\) hydrolyses (\(\mathrm{NH_4^+ + H_2O} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{NH_4OH + H^+}\)), lowering \(\mathrm{pH}\) below \(7\).

Match the aqueous salt solutions in List I with their \(\mathrm{pH}\) behaviour at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) in List II:

Practice Question 12 Single-Correct MCQ

If the molar solubility of a sparingly soluble salt \(\mathrm{MX_2}\) in pure water at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(1.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\), what is its solubility product constant \(K_{sp}\)?

  1. \(1.0 \times 10^{-8}\)
  2. \(4.0 \times 10^{-12}\)
  3. \(1.0 \times 10^{-12}\)
  4. \(2.0 \times 10^{-12}\)
Answer: B

Solution: For \(\mathrm{MX_2(s)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{M^{2+}(aq)} + 2\mathrm{X^-(aq)}\), \(K_{sp} = S(2S)^2 = 4S^3\). Substituting \(S = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{M}\) gives \(K_{sp} = 4 \times (1.0 \times 10^{-4})^3 = 4.0 \times 10^{-12}\).

5. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

Trap 1 — Including Solids/Liquids in \(\Delta n_g\) or \(K\): Only gaseous species contribute to \(\Delta n_g\) in \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\), and pure solids or liquids never appear in \(K_c\) or \(K_p\).

Trap 2 — Inert Gas Addition: Adding an inert gas at constant volume never shifts equilibrium; it shifts equilibrium only at constant pressure when \(\Delta n_g \ne 0\).

Trap 3 — Dilute Strong Acids: Never report \(\mathrm{pH} = 8\) for \(10^{-8}\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\); always include \([\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{M}\) from water autoionization (\(\mathrm{pH} \approx 6.98\)).

Trap 4 — Comparing \(K_{sp}\) Directly: A larger \(K_{sp}\) does not always mean higher molar solubility \(S\) unless the salts have the same stoichiometry (e.g., \(\mathrm{AB}\) vs. \(\mathrm{AB_2}\)). Always compute \(S\) explicitly.

6. Concise Final Recap

Chemical Equilibrium: At equilibrium, \(r_f = r_r\), \(Q = K\), and \(\Delta G = 0\). \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\) and \(\Delta G^\circ = -2.303\,RT\log_{10} K\). Only temperature changes \(K\).
Acids, Bases & Buffers: \(K_a \times K_b = K_w = 10^{-14}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\). Weak acid \(\mathrm{pH}\) uses \(\alpha = \sqrt{K_a/c}\) (when \(\alpha \ll 1\)). Buffer \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log_{10}([\text{Salt}]/[\text{Acid}])\).
Solubility Product: \(K_{sp} = x^x y^y S^{x+y}\) for \(\mathrm{M}_x\mathrm{X}_y\). Precipitation occurs when \(Q_{sp} > K_{sp}\). Common ions suppress solubility; lower \(\mathrm{pH}\) increases solubility of weak-acid salts.
