NEET UG Chemistry Physical & Inorganic Foundations

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

A complete, exam-focused tutorial covering electron-pair models, molecular geometry, hybridisation, molecular orbital theory, and intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

1. Learning Objectives and Big Picture

With the exception of noble gases, isolated atoms rarely exist freely under ordinary conditions. Atoms combine into molecules, ions, and extended crystal lattices because bonded arrangements sit at a lower potential energy and therefore offer greater stability. A chemical bond is the net attractive force holding constituent atoms or ions together in a chemical species.

What You Will Master for NEET UG

  • Electronic Theories: Kössel–Lewis octet concept, Lewis dot structures, formal charge calculations, and exceptions to the octet rule.
  • Ionic Bonding & Polarisation: Lattice enthalpy, favourable energetic conditions for ionic solids, and Fajans' rules for partial covalent character.
  • Bond Parameters & Polarity: Bond length, bond angle, bond enthalpy, bond order, resonance hybrids, and vector addition of dipole moments.
  • Molecular Geometry: Predicting shapes and bond-angle distortions using Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory.
  • Quantum Mechanical Bonding: Valence Bond Theory (VBT), directional orbital overlap (\(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) bonds), and hybridisation (\(sp\) through \(sp^3d^2\) and \(dsp^2\)).
  • Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT): LCAO energy diagrams (with and without \(2s\text{–}2p\) mixing), bond order, relative stability, and magnetic character.
  • Hydrogen Bonding: Intermolecular versus intramolecular hydrogen bonding and their effects on molecular properties.

2. Kössel–Lewis Approach, Octet Rule, and Ionic Bonding

2.1 Valence Electrons and the Octet Rule

Only the outermost shell electrons—called valence electrons—participate in chemical combination; inner core electrons remain tightly held and shielded. In Lewis symbols, dots placed around an element's symbol represent its valence electrons. For main-group elements, the common group valence is either equal to the number of valence dots or \(8\) minus the number of valence dots.

According to the octet rule (Kössel and Lewis, 1916), atoms combine either by transferring valence electrons (forming ions) or by sharing valence electron pairs (forming covalent bonds) so that each atom attains a stable outer-shell configuration of eight electrons (\(ns^2np^6\)), or a duplet (\(1s^2\)) in the case of hydrogen and helium.

2.2 Writing Lewis Structures and Computing Formal Charge

To construct a Lewis structure for a polyatomic molecule or ion:

  1. Sum the valence electrons of all atoms. Add \(1\) electron per unit negative charge for anions, and subtract \(1\) electron per unit positive charge for cations.
  2. Place the least electronegative atom at the centre (hydrogen and fluorine always occupy terminal positions).
  3. Connect atoms with single covalent bonds (each costing \(2\) electrons), then distribute remaining electrons as lone pairs to complete octets of terminal atoms first, followed by the central atom.
  4. If the central atom still lacks an octet, convert terminal lone pairs into multiple bonds (double or triple) until octets are satisfied.
Formal Charge (F.C.) on an atom in a Lewis structure: \[ \mathrm{F.C.} = V - L - \frac{1}{2}B \] where \(V\) = valence electrons in the free neutral atom, \(L\) = total non-bonding (lone-pair) electrons, and \(B\) = total bonding (shared) electrons.

Formal charges are bookkeeping tools assuming pure equal sharing; they do not represent actual ionic charges. Among alternative valid Lewis structures, the lowest-energy (most stable) contributor is the one with the smallest formal charges on atoms (and any negative formal charge residing on the more electronegative atom).

2.3 Limitations and Exceptions to the Octet Rule

Exception Category Core Reason Representative Examples
Incomplete Octet Central atom has fewer than 4 valence electrons, leaving < 8 electrons after bonding. \(\mathrm{LiCl}\), \(\mathrm{BeH_2}\) (4 e⁻), \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), \(\mathrm{BCl_3}\), \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) (6 e⁻)

Additionally, the octet rule fails to explain why noble gases such as xenon and krypton form compounds (\(\mathrm{XeF_2}\), \(\mathrm{KrF_2}\), \(\mathrm{XeOF_2}\)), and it provides no information about molecular shape or relative bond energies.

2.4 Ionic (Electrovalent) Bonds, Lattice Enthalpy, and Fajans' Rules

An ionic bond arises from electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by electron transfer. Two atomic factors favour ionic bond formation: a low ionization enthalpy of the metal (easy cation formation) and a large negative electron gain enthalpy of the non-metal (favourable anion formation). However, the sum of ionization enthalpy and electron gain enthalpy is often positive in the gas phase (for instance, forming gaseous \(\mathrm{Na}^+\) and \(\mathrm{Cl}^-\) from \(\mathrm{Na(g)}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl(g)}\) is endothermic by \(+147.1\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)). Ionic solids are stabilized primarily by the large exothermic energy released when gaseous ions pack into a three-dimensional crystal lattice.

Lattice Enthalpy is defined as the energy required to completely separate one mole of a solid ionic compound into its gaseous constituent ions at infinite distance (for \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), this is \(788\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Fajans' Rules: Covalent Character in Ionic Bonds

No bond is \(100\%\) ionic or \(100\%\) covalent. A cation distorts (polarises) the electron cloud of an anion, pulling electron density into the internuclear region and introducing covalent character. Covalent character increases with:

  • Smaller cation size and larger anion size: Higher charge density on a small cation gives greater polarising power; a large, diffuse anion has higher polarisability.
  • Higher charge on the cation (or anion): Greater electrostatic pull distorts the anion more strongly (e.g., covalent character increases along \(\mathrm{NaCl} < \mathrm{MgCl_2} < \mathrm{AlCl_3}\)).
  • Pseudo-noble-gas configuration: For cations of comparable size and charge, a transition-metal cation with an \((n-1)d^{10}ns^0\) configuration (poorly shielding \(d\)-electrons) is more polarising than an alkali/alkaline-earth cation with an \(ns^2np^6\) noble-gas core.

3. Bond Parameters, Resonance, and Dipole Moment

3.1 Bond Length, Bond Angle, Bond Enthalpy, and Bond Order

  • Bond Length: The equilibrium internuclear distance between two bonded atoms. For a covalent bond \(\mathrm{A–B}\), \(R = r_{\mathrm{A}} + r_{\mathrm{B}}\), where \(r_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(r_{\mathrm{B}}\) are covalent radii. The covalent radius is half the internuclear distance between two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond, whereas the van der Waals radius is half the distance between two identical non-bonded atoms in adjacent molecules in the solid state. Consequently, \(r_{\text{vdW}} > r_{\text{cov}}\) (for \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\), \(r_{\text{cov}} = 99\,\mathrm{pm}\) while \(r_{\text{vdW}} = 180\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Bond Angle: The angle between orbitals containing bonding electron pairs around the central atom, expressed in degrees (°).
  • Bond Enthalpy: The enthalpy needed to break one mole of a specific bond in the gaseous state. In polyatomic molecules such as \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), breaking the first \(\mathrm{O–H}\) bond (\(502\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) differs from breaking the second (\(427\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) due to the altered chemical environment; hence, an average (mean) bond enthalpy (\(464.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) for \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) is reported.
  • Bond Order: In the Lewis model, bond order equals the number of shared electron pairs between two atoms (\(1\) for \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(2\) for \(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(3\) for \(\mathrm{N_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO}\)). Isoelectronic species share the same bond order (e.g., \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{CO}\), and \(\mathrm{NO^+}\) each have \(14\) electrons and bond order \(3\)).
Core Correlation: As Bond Order increases, Bond Enthalpy increases and Bond Length decreases.

3.2 Resonance

Whenever a single Lewis structure cannot match experimentally observed bond lengths and energies—such as ozone (\(\mathrm{O_3}\), where both \(\mathrm{O–O}\) bonds measure \(128\,\mathrm{pm}\), intermediate between \(148\,\mathrm{pm}\) single and \(121\,\mathrm{pm}\) double bonds) or the carbonate ion (\(\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\), where all three \(\mathrm{C–O}\) bonds are equivalent)—the real species is represented as a resonance hybrid of multiple canonical structures having identical nuclear positions and similar energies.

Critical NEET Misconceptions About Resonance

  • Canonical structures have no real physical existence; the molecule does not oscillate or spend fractions of time in different canonical forms.
  • There is no equilibrium between canonical forms (unlike keto–enol tautomerism).
  • The resonance hybrid is a single, fixed electronic structure that is lower in energy (more stable) than any individual canonical contributor and averages bond characteristics across the molecule.

3.3 Bond Polarity and Dipole Moment

In a homonuclear molecule (\(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)), the bonding pair is shared equally, yielding a nonpolar covalent bond. In a heteronuclear bond (\(\mathrm{HF}\)), the more electronegative atom pulls electron density towards itself, creating a polar covalent bond with a net dipole moment (\(\mu\)):

\[ \mu = Q \times r \qquad \left(1\,\mathrm{D} = 3.33564 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\right) \]

Dipole moment is a vector quantity. In chemistry, it is drawn as a crossed arrow (\(\rightarrow\)) pointing from the positive end toward the negative end (showing the shift of electron density). For polyatomic molecules, the net molecular dipole moment is the vector sum of all individual bond dipoles and any lone-pair orbital dipoles:

  • Symmetrical cancellation (\(\mu = 0\)): Linear \(\mathrm{BeF_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) have two equal bond dipoles pointing at \(180^\circ\); trigonal planar \(\mathrm{BF_3}\) has three equal bond dipoles at \(120^\circ\); tetrahedral \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) and \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) also cancel completely.
  • Asymmetric / Bent molecules (\(\mu \neq 0\)): \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (bent, \(104.5^\circ\)) has a large net dipole moment of \(1.85\,\mathrm{D}\) (\(6.17 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) versus \(\mathrm{NF_3}\): Both are trigonal pyramidal with one lone pair on nitrogen. Even though \(\mathrm{F}\) is more electronegative than \(\mathrm{H}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) has a much larger dipole moment (\(1.47\,\mathrm{D}\)) than \(\mathrm{NF_3}\) (\(0.23\,\mathrm{D}\)). In \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), the three \(\mathrm{N–H}\) bond dipoles point toward \(\mathrm{N}\) and reinforce the lone-pair orbital dipole; in \(\mathrm{NF_3}\), the three \(\mathrm{N–F}\) bond dipoles point away from \(\mathrm{N}\) and oppose the lone-pair dipole.

4. Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) Theory

Proposed by Sidgwick and Powell (1940) and refined by Nyholm and Gillespie (1957), VSEPR theory predicts three-dimensional molecular shapes from the number of valence-shell electron pairs (both bonding pairs, \(\mathrm{bp}\), and lone pairs, \(\mathrm{lp}\)) surrounding the central atom. Electron pairs repel one another and adopt positions on a spherical surface that maximize their mutual separation. Multiple bonds are treated as a single electron-pair domain (super pair).

Repulsion Hierarchy: \[ \text{lp–lp} > \text{lp–bp} > \text{bp–bp} \]

Because a lone pair is held by only one nucleus, its electron cloud spreads out more broadly than a bonding pair shared between two nuclei. This stronger \(\mathrm{lp–bp}\) and \(\mathrm{lp–lp}\) repulsion compresses bond angles below ideal geometric values (e.g., \(109.5^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) drops to \(107^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and \(104.5^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)).

5. Valence Bond Theory (VBT) and Hybridisation

5.1 Energetics of Bond Formation and Orbital Overlap

Valence Bond Theory (Heitler–London, developed by Pauling) explains covalent bonding through quantum mechanical overlap of half-filled valence atomic orbitals containing electrons with opposite spins. As two hydrogen atoms approach each other, new attractive forces (nucleus–other electron) outweigh new repulsive forces (nucleus–nucleus and electron–electron), lowering potential energy until a minimum is reached at the equilibrium bond length (\(74\,\mathrm{pm}\) for \(\mathrm{H_2}\)), releasing the bond enthalpy (\(435.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Tutorial Diagram 4: Potential energy variation with internuclear distance during \(\mathrm{H_2}\) bond formation.

For a covalent bond to form, overlapping orbitals must have the same wave-function phase (sign) and proper spatial orientation (positive overlap). Out-of-phase overlap (negative overlap) or perpendicular orientation along the bond axis (e.g., \(s\) with \(p_x\) when the \(z\)-axis is the internuclear axis) gives zero net overlap.

  • Sigma (\(\sigma\)) bond: Formed by axial (head-on) overlap (\(s\text{–}s\), \(s\text{–}p\), or \(p\text{–}p\) along the internuclear axis). Electron density is cylindrically symmetrical around the bond axis. Larger overlap makes \(\sigma\) bonds stronger than \(\pi\) bonds.
  • Pi (\(\pi\)) bond: Formed by lateral (sidewise) overlap of parallel \(p\) orbitals perpendicular to the internuclear axis, producing electron clouds above and below the molecular plane. A double bond consists of \(1\sigma + 1\pi\); a triple bond consists of \(1\sigma + 2\pi\).

5.2 Hybridisation of Atomic Orbitals

Pure atomic \(p\)-orbitals are mutually at \(90^\circ\), which cannot account for observed bond angles such as \(109.5^\circ\) in \(\mathrm{CH_4}\). Hybridisation is the intermixing of valence-shell atomic orbitals of slightly different energies on the same atom to produce an equal number of equivalent hybrid orbitals oriented in space for minimum repulsion.

  • Key Rules: Number of hybrid orbitals formed = number of atomic orbitals mixed. Promotion of electrons is not mandatory prior to hybridisation; even completely filled valence orbitals (lone-pair orbitals) can hybridise.
  • Hybridisation with \(d\)-orbitals: Period 3+ elements can mix \(3s, 3p, 3d\) (or \(3d, 4s, 4p\)) orbitals because their energies are comparable; however, \(3p, 3d, 4s\) mixing does not occur due to the large energy gap between \(3p\) and \(4s\).

Why Axial Bonds Are Longer in \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\): In trigonal bipyramidal \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) (\(sp^3d\)), three equatorial \(\mathrm{P–Cl}\) bonds lie at \(120^\circ\) in a plane, while two axial \(\mathrm{P–Cl}\) bonds stand perpendicular at \(90^\circ\) to that plane. Each axial bond pair experiences three strong \(90^\circ\) repulsions from the equatorial bond pairs (whereas equatorial pairs experience only two \(90^\circ\) repulsions). Consequently, axial bonds are slightly longer and weaker, making \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) thermally reactive.

6. Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)

Developed by Hund and Mulliken (1932), MOT treats electrons as occupying polycentric molecular orbitals (MOs) delocalized over the entire molecule, constructed by Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO):

  • Conditions for LCAO: Combining atomic orbitals must have (1) comparable energy, (2) identical symmetry about the internuclear axis (conventionally the \(z\)-axis), and (3) maximum overlap.
  • Bonding versus Antibonding MOs: Constructive interference (\(\psi_{\mathrm{A}} + \psi_{\mathrm{B}}\)) concentrates electron density between the nuclei, lowering energy (\(\sigma, \pi\)). Destructive interference (\(\psi_{\mathrm{A}} - \psi_{\mathrm{B}}\)) creates a nodal plane between the nuclei, raising energy (\(\sigma^*, \pi^*\)).
  • Filling Rules: MOs are filled in increasing energy order following the Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion principle, and Hund's rule.

6.1 Critical Energy-Level Ordering (With vs. Without \(2s\text{–}2p\) Mixing)

For second-period homonuclear diatomics, the relative energy of \(\sigma 2p_z\) versus \(\pi 2p_x = \pi 2p_y\) depends on \(2s\text{–}2p\) orbital mixing:

For \(\le 14\) electrons (\(\mathrm{Li_2, Be_2, B_2, C_2, N_2}\)) — with \(2s\text{–}2p\) mixing: \[ \sigma 1s < \sigma^* 1s < \sigma 2s < \sigma^* 2s < (\pi 2p_x = \pi 2p_y) < \sigma 2p_z < (\pi^* 2p_x = \pi^* 2p_y) < \sigma^* 2p_z \] For \(> 14\) electrons (\(\mathrm{O_2, F_2, Ne_2}\)) — without \(2s\text{–}2p\) mixing: \[ \sigma 1s < \sigma^* 1s < \sigma 2s < \sigma^* 2s < \sigma 2p_z < (\pi 2p_x = \pi 2p_y) < (\pi^* 2p_x = \pi^* 2p_y) < \sigma^* 2p_z \]

6.2 Bond Order, Stability, and Magnetic Properties

\[ \text{Bond Order} = \frac{1}{2}\left(N_{\mathrm{b}} - N_{\mathrm{a}}\right) \] \(N_{\mathrm{b}}\) = electrons in bonding MOs; \(N_{\mathrm{a}}\) = electrons in antibonding MOs.
  • Stability: A molecule is stable if \(N_{\mathrm{b}} > N_{\mathrm{a}}\) (bond order \(> 0\)). Species with bond order \(0\) (\(\mathrm{He_2}\), \(\mathrm{Be_2}\), \(\mathrm{Ne_2}\)) are unstable and do not exist as stable molecules.
  • Magnetic Nature: If any MO contains an unpaired electron, the species is paramagnetic (e.g., \(\mathrm{B_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\)). If all electrons are paired, it is diamagnetic (e.g., \(\mathrm{C_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{F_2}\)).
  • Special Case of \(\mathrm{C_2}\): With configuration \(\mathrm{KK}(\sigma 2s)^2(\sigma^* 2s)^2(\pi 2p_x^2 = \pi 2p_y^2)\), \(\mathrm{C_2}\) has bond order \(2\), is diamagnetic, and both bonds are \(\pi\) bonds (four electrons in two \(\pi\) bonding MOs).

7. Hydrogen Bonding

When hydrogen is covalently bonded to a small, highly electronegative atom (\(\mathrm{F}\), \(\mathrm{O}\), or \(\mathrm{N}\)), the shared electron pair shifts strongly toward that electronegative atom, leaving hydrogen with a significant partial positive charge (\(\delta^+\)). A hydrogen bond (represented as a dotted line, \(\cdots\)) is the attractive electrostatic force binding this \(\mathrm{H}^{\delta+}\) atom to a lone pair on another electronegative \(\mathrm{F}\), \(\mathrm{O}\), or \(\mathrm{N}\) atom. Although weaker than a covalent bond, it is stronger than ordinary van der Waals dispersion forces, and its magnitude is greatest in the solid state and smallest in the gaseous state.

  • Intermolecular Hydrogen Bonding: Occurs between different molecules of the same or different substances (e.g., \(\mathrm{HF}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), alcohols), raising boiling points and viscosity.
  • Intramolecular Hydrogen Bonding: Occurs when \(\mathrm{H}\) lies between two electronegative atoms within the same molecule, forming a stable ring (e.g., o-nitrophenol).

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Formal Charge and Preferred Lewis Structure of Thiocyanate Ion (\(\mathrm{SCN^-}\))

Problem: Determine the formal charges on \(\mathrm{S}\), \(\mathrm{C}\), and \(\mathrm{N}\) in two valid octet Lewis structures of \(\mathrm{SCN^-}\): (I) \([:\ddot{\mathrm{S}}=\mathrm{C}=\ddot{\mathrm{N}}:]^-\) and (II) \([:\stackrel{\cdot\cdot}{\mathrm{S}}-\mathrm{C}\equiv\mathrm{N}:]^-\), and identify the lower-energy contributor.

Solution:

  • Total valence electrons: \(6\,(\mathrm{S}) + 4\,(\mathrm{C}) + 5\,(\mathrm{N}) + 1\,(\text{charge}) = 16\).
  • Structure I (\(:\ddot{\mathrm{S}}=\mathrm{C}=\ddot{\mathrm{N}}:\)): \(\mathrm{F.C.(S)} = 6 - 4 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = 0\); \(\mathrm{F.C.(C)} = 4 - 0 - \frac{1}{2}(8) = 0\); \(\mathrm{F.C.(N)} = 5 - 4 - \frac{1}{2}(4) = -1\).
  • Structure II (\(:\stackrel{\cdot\cdot}{\mathrm{S}}\text{–}\mathrm{C}\equiv\mathrm{N}:\), with \(6\) non-bonding e⁻ on \(\mathrm{S}\) and \(2\) on \(\mathrm{N}\)): \(\mathrm{F.C.(S)} = 6 - 6 - \frac{1}{2}(2) = -1\); \(\mathrm{F.C.(C)} = 4 - 0 - \frac{1}{2}(8) = 0\); \(\mathrm{F.C.(N)} = 5 - 2 - \frac{1}{2}(6) = 0\).
  • Conclusion: Both have minimal formal-charge magnitudes, but nitrogen is more electronegative than sulphur. Hence, Structure I (with \(-1\) on \(\mathrm{N}\)) is the lower-energy, preferred contributor.
Worked Example 2: Hypothetical Bond Polarity Calculation

Problem: Assume a hypothetical diatomic molecule \(\mathrm{XY}\) has an equilibrium bond length of \(120\,\mathrm{pm}\) and an experimentally measured dipole moment of \(1.44\,\mathrm{D}\). Calculate the fractional ionic character of the \(\mathrm{X–Y}\) bond. (Use \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{D} = 3.336 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\); note that the numerical values for \(\mathrm{XY}\) are assumed for illustration.)

Solution:

  • Theoretical dipole moment for \(100\%\) ionic transfer (\(Q = e\), \(r = 120 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m}\)): \[ \mu_{\text{ionic}} = (1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}) \times (1.20 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}) = 1.922 \times 10^{-29}\,\mathrm{C\,m} \]
  • Convert \(\mu_{\text{obs}}\) to SI units: \[ \mu_{\text{obs}} = 1.44 \times 3.336 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m} = 4.804 \times 10^{-30}\,\mathrm{C\,m} \]
  • Percentage ionic character: \[ \%\,\text{Ionic character} = \frac{4.804 \times 10^{-30}}{1.922 \times 10^{-29}} \times 100\% \approx 25.0\% \]
Worked Example 3: VSEPR Geometry and Hybridisation of \(\mathrm{XeOF_4}\) and \(\mathrm{ICl_4^-}\)

Problem: Predict the hybridisation of the central atom, electron-pair arrangement, and molecular shape of (a) \(\mathrm{XeOF_4}\) and (b) \(\mathrm{ICl_4^-}\).

Solution:

  • (a) \(\mathrm{XeOF_4}\): Xenon has \(8\) valence electrons. It forms one double bond to \(\mathrm{O}\) (counts as \(1\sigma\) domain) and four single bonds to \(\mathrm{F}\) (\(4\sigma\) domains), leaving \(8 - (2 + 4) = 2\) non-bonding electrons (\(1\) lone pair). Total steric domains \(= 5 + 1 = 6 \Rightarrow\) \(sp^3d^2\) hybridisation, octahedral electron-pair arrangement, and square pyramidal molecular shape.
  • (b) \(\mathrm{ICl_4^-}\): Iodine has \(7\) valence electrons plus \(1\) from the negative charge \(= 8\) electrons. Four single bonds to \(\mathrm{Cl}\) use \(4\) electrons, leaving \(4\) electrons (\(2\) lone pairs). Total steric domains \(= 4 + 2 = 6 \Rightarrow\) \(sp^3d^2\) hybridisation, octahedral electron-pair arrangement, and square planar shape (the two lone pairs sit opposite each other at \(180^\circ\)).
Worked Example 4: Comparing \(\mathrm{N_2^+}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2^-}\) via MOT

Problem: Using Molecular Orbital Theory, compare the bond order and magnetic behaviour of \(\mathrm{N_2^+}\) (\(13\,\mathrm{e^-}\)) and \(\mathrm{O_2^-}\) (\(17\,\mathrm{e^-}\)).

Solution:

  • \(\mathrm{N_2^+}\) (\(13\,\mathrm{e^-}\), uses \(2s\text{–}2p\) mixing order): \(\mathrm{KK}(\sigma 2s)^2(\sigma^* 2s)^2(\pi 2p_x^2 = \pi 2p_y^2)(\sigma 2p_z)^1\). Here \(N_{\mathrm{b}} = 9\), \(N_{\mathrm{a}} = 4\), so \(\text{B.O.} = \frac{9 - 4}{2} = 2.5\). It has \(1\) unpaired electron in \(\sigma 2p_z\) \(\Rightarrow\) paramagnetic.
  • \(\mathrm{O_2^-}\) (\(17\,\mathrm{e^-}\), no \(2s\text{–}2p\) mixing): \(\mathrm{KK}(\sigma 2s)^2(\sigma^* 2s)^2(\sigma 2p_z)^2(\pi 2p_x^2 = \pi 2p_y^2)(\pi^* 2p_x^2 = \pi^* 2p_y^1)\). Here \(N_{\mathrm{b}} = 10\), \(N_{\mathrm{a}} = 7\), so \(\text{B.O.} = \frac{10 - 7}{2} = 1.5\). It has \(1\) unpaired electron in \(\pi^* 2p_y\) \(\Rightarrow\) paramagnetic.
  • Comparison: Both ions are paramagnetic with \(1\) unpaired electron, but \(\mathrm{N_2^+}\) has a higher bond order (\(2.5 > 1.5\)) and therefore a stronger, shorter bond.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following species does not obey the octet rule around the central atom?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{SiCl_4}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{BrF_5}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{NF_3}\)

Answer: (C) — In \(\mathrm{BrF_5}\), bromine forms \(5\) single bonds (\(10\) bonding electrons) and retains \(1\) lone pair (\(2\) non-bonding electrons), giving \(12\) valence electrons around \(\mathrm{Br}\) (expanded octet). \(\mathrm{SiCl_4}\), \(\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\), and \(\mathrm{NF_3}\) each have \(8\) valence electrons around their central atom.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

According to Fajans' rules, which of the following chlorides exhibits the highest covalent character?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{LiCl}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{BeCl_2}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{MgCl_2}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{CaCl_2}\)

Answer: (B) — Covalent character increases with higher cation charge and smaller cation radius (greater polarising power). Among the divalent cations, \(\mathrm{Be^{2+}}\) has the smallest ionic radius and a \(+2\) charge, giving it greater polarising power than \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\), \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\), or univalent \(\mathrm{Li^+}\).

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

Which set contains only molecules with a zero net dipole moment?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{BeF_2}\), \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\)

Answer: (B) — \(\mathrm{BeF_2}\) (linear), \(\mathrm{BF_3}\) (trigonal planar), and \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) (tetrahedral) are completely symmetrical with identical terminal atoms, so their bond dipoles cancel to zero. \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{NF_3}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\), and \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) all have non-zero dipole moments.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

In which of the following pairs do both species have the same molecular shape and the same hybridisation of the central atom?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{SF_4}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{BF_3}\) and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)

Answer: (C) — Both \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_3}\) belong to the \(\mathrm{AB_2E}\) type (\(2\) bond domains + \(1\) lone pair), giving \(sp^2\) hybridisation and a bent (V-shaped) geometry. In (A), \(\mathrm{SF_4}\) is see-saw (\(sp^3d\)) while \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) is tetrahedral (\(sp^3\)); in (B), \(\mathrm{BF_3}\) is trigonal planar (\(sp^2\)) while \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) is pyramidal (\(sp^3\)); in (D), \(\mathrm{ClF_3}\) is T-shaped (\(sp^3d\)) while \(\mathrm{BF_3}\) is trigonal planar (\(sp^2\)).

Practice Question 5 Single-Correct MCQ

How many \(\sigma\) bonds and \(\pi\) bonds are present in a single molecule of propene (\(\mathrm{CH_3–CH=CH_2}\))?

  • (A) \(8\sigma\) and \(1\pi\)
  • (B) \(7\sigma\) and \(2\pi\)
  • (C) \(9\sigma\) and \(1\pi\)
  • (D) \(6\sigma\) and \(1\pi\)

Answer: (A) — Propene has \(6\) \(\mathrm{C–H}\) single bonds (\(6\sigma\)), \(1\) \(\mathrm{C–C}\) single bond (\(1\sigma\)), and \(1\) \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (\(1\sigma + 1\pi\)). Total \(= 6 + 1 + 1 = 8\sigma\) bonds and \(1\pi\) bond.

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

If the \(x\)-axis is taken as the internuclear axis, which pair of orbitals will undergo zero net overlap?

  • (A) \(1s\) and \(2p_x\)
  • (B) \(2p_x\) and \(2p_x\)
  • (C) \(1s\) and \(2p_y\)
  • (D) \(2p_y\) and \(2p_y\)

Answer: (C) — Along the \(x\)-axis, \(1s\) and \(2p_x\) form a \(\sigma\) bond, \(2p_x\) and \(2p_x\) form a \(\sigma\) bond, and \(2p_y\) and \(2p_y\) overlap sidewise to form a \(\pi\) bond. However, the positive lobe and negative lobe of \(2p_y\) overlap equally with the spherical \(1s\) orbital, cancelling out to zero net overlap.

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

During the reaction \(\mathrm{BF_3} + \mathrm{NH_3} \rightarrow \mathrm{F_3B\leftarrow NH_3}\), how do the hybridisations of boron and nitrogen change?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{B}\): \(sp^2 \rightarrow sp^3\); \(\mathrm{N}\): \(sp^3 \rightarrow sp^3\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{B}\): \(sp^2 \rightarrow sp^2\); \(\mathrm{N}\): \(sp^3 \rightarrow sp^2\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{B}\): \(sp^3 \rightarrow sp^2\); \(\mathrm{N}\): \(sp^3 \rightarrow sp^3\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{B}\): \(sp^2 \rightarrow sp^3\); \(\mathrm{N}\): \(sp^2 \rightarrow sp^3\)

Answer: (A) — In \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), boron has \(3\) bond pairs and \(0\) lone pairs (\(sp^2\)).   • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.

Practice Question 10 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): In \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\), the two axial \(\mathrm{P–Cl}\) bonds are longer than the three equatorial \(\mathrm{P–Cl}\) bonds.
Reason (R): Each axial bond pair experiences three \(90^\circ\) repulsive interactions from equatorial bond pairs, whereas each equatorial bond pair experiences only two \(90^\circ\) interactions.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.

Answer: (A) — In trigonal bipyramidal geometry, the \(90^\circ\) bond-pair repulsions experienced by axial \(\mathrm{P–Cl}\) bonds (3 interactions at \(90^\circ\)) exceed those on equatorial bonds (2 interactions at \(90^\circ\) plus 2 weaker ones at \(120^\circ\)), pushing the axial \(\mathrm{Cl}\) atoms slightly farther from \(\mathrm{P}\).

10. Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in the Exam

  • Using the wrong MO energy diagram: Always count total electrons first. Use \(\pi 2p < \sigma 2p\) for \(\le 14\,\mathrm{e^-}\) (\(\mathrm{B_2, C_2, N_2, N_2^+}\)) and \(\sigma 2p < \pi 2p\) for \(> 14\,\mathrm{e^-}\) (\(\mathrm{O_2, O_2^+, O_2^-, F_2}\)).
  • Confusing electron-pair geometry with molecular shape: \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) has a tetrahedral arrangement of electron pairs, but its molecular shape is bent.
  • Placing lone pairs axially in trigonal bipyramidal (\(\mathrm{AB_4E}, \mathrm{AB_3E_2}\)): Lone pairs always occupy equatorial positions to minimize \(90^\circ\) repulsions.
  • Misreading \(\mathrm{NF_3}\) vs. \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) dipole moment: Higher electronegativity of \(\mathrm{F}\) does not give \(\mathrm{NF_3}\) a larger dipole moment; vector opposition with the lone pair makes \(\mu(\mathrm{NF_3}) < \mu(\mathrm{NH_3})\).

Rapid Revision Checklist

  • Formal charge: \(\mathrm{F.C.} = V - L - \frac{1}{2}B\).
  • Fajans' rules: Small cation + high charge + large anion = greater covalent character.
  • Isoelectronic species (\(\mathrm{N_2, CO, NO^+}\)) share bond order \(3\).
  • VSEPR repulsion: \(\text{lp–lp} > \text{lp–bp} > \text{bp–bp}\); angles: \(\mathrm{CH_4}\,(109.5^\circ) > \mathrm{NH_3}\,(107^\circ) > \mathrm{H_2O}\,(104.5^\circ)\).
  • Bond order \(\propto\) Bond enthalpy \(\propto \frac{1}{\text{Bond length}}\).
  • \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{B_2}\) are paramagnetic; \(\mathrm{C_2}\) and \(\mathrm{N_2}\) are diamagnetic.

11. Concise Final Recap

Chemical bonding lowers system potential energy through electron transfer (ionic lattices stabilized by lattice enthalpy) or electron sharing (covalent bonds). While Lewis structures, formal charges, and resonance capture electron distribution, VSEPR theory translates valence-shell repulsions into 3D shapes. Valence Bond Theory and hybridisation explain directional \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) overlap, whereas Molecular Orbital Theory accounts for bond orders, fractional-bond ions, and paramagnetism in species like \(\mathrm{O_2}\). Finally, hydrogen bonding bridges electronegative \(\mathrm{F, O, N}\) centres, governing intermolecular association and molecular properties.
