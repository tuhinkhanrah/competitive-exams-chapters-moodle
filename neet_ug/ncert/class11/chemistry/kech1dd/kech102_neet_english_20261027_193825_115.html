Structure of Atom

The rich diversity of chemical behaviour of different elements can be traced to the differences in the internal structure of atoms of these elements.

Historical Context & Dalton's Atomic Theory

Early Indian and Greek philosophers (around 400 B.C.) proposed that matter is composed of fundamental building blocks called atoms (derived from the Greek word a-tomio, meaning "uncut-able" or "non-divisible"). These early ideas were speculative and lacked experimental proof.

In 1808, British school teacher John Dalton proposed the Dalton's Atomic Theory on a firm scientific basis, regarding the atom as the ultimate indivisible particle of matter. While Dalton's theory successfully explained the law of conservation of mass, law of constant composition, and law of multiple proportions, it failed to explain why substances like glass or ebonite become electrically charged when rubbed with silk or fur. Experiments in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries established that atoms are divisible and consist of sub-atomic particles: electrons, protons, and neutrons.

2. Discovery of Sub-Atomic Particles

A fundamental rule governs the behaviour of charged particles: "Like charges repel each other and unlike charges attract each other."

2.1 Discovery of the Electron (Cathode Rays)

In 1830, Michael Faraday demonstrated that passing electricity through an electrolyte solution causes chemical reactions at the electrodes, liberating or depositing matter. This suggested the particulate nature of electricity.

In the mid-1850s, scientists studied electrical discharge in partially evacuated glass tubes containing two thin metal electrodes, known as cathode ray discharge tubes. Electrical discharge through gases is observed only at very low pressures and very high voltages. When sufficiently high voltage is applied across the electrodes, a stream of particles flows from the negative electrode (cathode) to the positive electrode (anode). These were called cathode rays. By making a perforation (hole) in the anode and coating the glass tube behind it with a phosphorescent material like zinc sulphide (\(\mathrm{ZnS}\)), a bright spot develops when the rays strike the coating.

Tutorial Diagram 1: A cathode ray discharge tube with a perforated anode and zinc sulphide fluorescent screen.

Key Characteristics of Cathode Rays:

  1. Cathode rays start from the cathode and move towards the anode.
  2. These rays themselves are not visible, but their behaviour can be observed with fluorescent or phosphorescent materials (such as \(\mathrm{ZnS}\)) which glow when hit by them. (Television picture tubes are cathode ray tubes).
  3. In the absence of an electrical or magnetic field, these rays travel in straight lines.
  4. In the presence of an electrical or magnetic field, their deflection is similar to that expected from negatively charged particles, proving that cathode rays consist of negatively charged particles called electrons.
  5. Universal Nature: The characteristics of cathode rays (electrons) do not depend upon the material of the electrodes or the nature of the gas present in the cathode ray tube. Thus, electrons are basic constituents of all atoms.

2.2 Charge to Mass Ratio of Electron (\(e/m_e\))

In 1897, British physicist J.J. Thomson measured the ratio of electrical charge (\(e\)) to the mass of the electron (\(m_e\)) using a cathode ray tube by applying electrical and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other as well as to the path of the electrons.

Tutorial Diagram 2: J.J. Thomson's apparatus to determine the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron.

Thomson argued that the amount of deviation of the particles from their path in the presence of an electrical or magnetic field depends upon three factors:

  1. Magnitude of the negative charge on the particle: Greater the magnitude of the charge, greater is the interaction with the electric or magnetic field, and thus greater is the deflection.
  2. Mass of the particle: Lighter the particle, greater is the deflection.
  3. Strength of the electrical or magnetic field: The deflection of electrons increases with an increase in the voltage across the electrodes or the strength of the magnetic field.

When only the electric field is applied, electrons deviate to point A. When only the magnetic field is applied, electrons strike at point C. By carefully balancing the electrical and magnetic field strengths, the electron beam is brought back to its original straight-line path (striking at point B). From accurate measurements, Thomson determined the specific charge (\(e/m_e\)) as:

\[ \frac{e}{m_e} = 1.758820 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}} \]

where \(m_e\) is the mass of the electron in \(\mathrm{kg}\) and \(e\) is the magnitude of the charge on the electron in coulomb (\(\mathrm{C}\)). Since electrons are negatively charged, the charge on an electron is \(-e\).

2.3 Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment & Mass of the Electron

R.A. Millikan (1906–14) devised the oil drop experiment to measure the charge on the electron. In this method, oil droplets in the form of mist produced by an atomiser entered through a tiny hole in the upper plate of an electrical condenser. Their downward motion was viewed through a telescope equipped with a micrometer eyepiece to measure the mass of the droplets from their rate of fall. Passing a beam of X-rays ionized the air inside the chamber, and oil droplets acquired electrical charge by colliding with gaseous ions.

Inside the chamber, three forces act on a moving charged oil drop: gravitational force, electrostatic force (due to the electrical field), and a viscous drag force. By adjusting the polarity and strength of the voltage applied to the plates, the fall of charged droplets could be retarded, accelerated, or made stationary. Millikan concluded that the magnitude of electrical charge \(q\) on the droplets is always an integral multiple of the fundamental electrical charge \(e\):

\[ q = n e \quad (\text{where } n = 1, 2, 3, \dots) \]

Millikan found the charge on the electron to be \(-1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) (present accepted value is \(-1.602176 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)). Combining this with Thomson's \(e/m_e\) ratio gave the mass of the electron (\(m_e\)):

\[ m_e = \frac{e}{e/m_e} = \frac{1.602176 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}}{1.758820 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}} = 9.1094 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg} \]

2.4 Discovery of Protons (Canal Rays) and Neutrons

Electrical discharge carried out in a modified cathode ray tube led to the discovery of canal rays carrying positively charged particles. Their characteristics contrast sharply with cathode rays:

  • Unlike cathode rays, the mass of positively charged particles (canal rays) depends upon the nature of the gas present in the cathode ray tube. They are simply positively charged gaseous ions.
  • The charge-to-mass ratio (\(q/m\)) of these particles depends on the gas from which they originate.
  • Some of the positively charged particles carry a multiple of the fundamental unit of electrical charge.
  • Their behaviour in magnetic or electrical fields is opposite to that observed for electrons (cathode rays).

The smallest and lightest positive ion was obtained from hydrogen and was called the proton (characterised in 1919). In 1932, James Chadwick discovered the electrically neutral constituent of the atom by bombarding a thin sheet of beryllium (\(\mathrm{Be}\)) with \(\alpha\)-particles, emitting electrically neutral particles having a mass slightly greater than that of protons. He named these particles neutrons.

3. Classical Atomic Models, X-Rays & Radioactivity

3.1 Thomson Model of Atom (1898)

J.J. Thomson proposed that an atom possesses a spherical shape (radius approximately \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)) in which the positive charge is uniformly distributed, and electrons are embedded into it to give the most stable electrostatic arrangement. Known as the plum pudding, raisin pudding, or watermelon model, an important feature is that the mass of the atom is assumed to be uniformly distributed over the atom. While it explained the overall electrical neutrality of the atom, it failed to explain the results of later scattering experiments.

3.2 X-Rays and Radioactivity

  • X-Rays (Wilhelm Röentgen, 1895): Produced effectively when electrons in a cathode ray tube strike a dense metal anode (target). X-rays cause fluorescence in materials placed outside the tube, are not deflected by electric and magnetic fields, have very short wavelengths (\(\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{nm}\)), possess electromagnetic character, and have very high penetrating power through matter.
  • Radioactivity (Henri Becquerel, Marie Curie, Pierre Curie, Rutherford, Frederick Soddy): Certain elements spontaneously emit radiation on their own. Three kinds of rays are emitted:
    • \(\alpha\)-rays: High-energy particles carrying \(+2\) units of charge and \(4\,\mathrm{u}\) of atomic mass (identical to helium nuclei, \(\mathrm{He^{2+}}\); combining with two electrons yields helium gas).
    • \(\beta\)-rays: Negatively charged particles similar to electrons.
    • \(\gamma\)-rays: High-energy neutral electromagnetic radiations like X-rays; they do not consist of particles.
    • Penetrating Power Order: \(\alpha\text{-particles (least)} < \beta\text{-rays (100 times }\alpha\text{)} < \gamma\text{-rays (1000 times }\alpha\text{)}\).

3.3 Rutherford's Nuclear Model of Atom

Ernest Rutherford and his students (Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden) bombarded a very thin gold foil (thickness \(\sim 100\,\mathrm{nm}\)) with a stream of high-energy \(\alpha\)-particles from a radioactive source, surrounded by a circular fluorescent zinc sulphide (\(\mathrm{ZnS}\)) screen.

Exam Note — Effect of Foil Material: In Rutherford's experiment, thin foils of heavy atoms like gold or platinum are used because their nuclei have a large positive charge and large mass, producing strong repulsive forces. If a thin foil of light atoms like aluminium (\(\mathrm{Al}\)) is used, the nuclear charge (\(Z = 13\)) and mass are much smaller; consequently, the repulsive force is weaker and the number of \(\alpha\)-particles deflected at large angles (or rebounding) will be negligibly small compared to gold (\(Z = 79\)).

Drawbacks of Rutherford's Model:

  1. Inability to Explain Atomic Stability: While the Coulomb electrostatic force \(\left(\frac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2}\right)\) is mathematically similar to the gravitational force \(\left(\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}\right)\) in the solar system, an electron moving in a circular orbit undergoes centripetal acceleration due to changing direction. According to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, accelerated charged particles must emit electromagnetic radiation continuously. Thus, the revolving electron should lose energy, its orbit should shrink, and it should spiral into the nucleus in about \(10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s}\). Furthermore, electrons cannot be considered stationary because electrostatic attraction would pull them directly into the nucleus.
  2. Silent on Electronic Structure: It says nothing about the distribution of electrons around the nucleus and the relative energies of these electrons.

3.4 Atomic Number (\(Z\)), Mass Number (\(A\)), Isotopes & Isobars

An atom is represented by the symbol \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\), where the left subscript \(Z\) is the atomic number and the left superscript \(A\) is the mass number. (Note: Symbols like \(^{79}_{35}\mathrm{Br}\) or \(^{79}\mathrm{Br}\) are acceptable because the chemical symbol \(\mathrm{Br}\) inherently fixes \(Z = 35\), whereas \(^{35}_{79}\mathrm{Br}\) or \(^{35}\mathrm{Br}\) are not acceptable because mass number must be the superscript).

\[ \text{Atomic Number }(Z) = \text{Number of protons in the nucleus} = \text{Number of electrons in a neutral atom} \] \[ \text{Mass Number }(A) = \text{Total number of nucleons} = \text{Number of protons }(Z) + \text{Number of neutrons }(n) \]
  • Isotopes: Atoms with identical atomic number (\(Z\)) but different mass number (\(A\)) — i.e., different numbers of neutrons. Examples:
    • Hydrogen isotopes: Protium (\(^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}\), \(99.985\%\), \(1\mathrm{p}, 0\mathrm{n}\)), Deuterium (\(^{2}_{1}\mathrm{D}\), \(0.015\%\), \(1\mathrm{p}, 1\mathrm{n}\)), and Tritium (\(^{3}_{1}\mathrm{T}\), trace amounts, \(1\mathrm{p}, 2\mathrm{n}\)).
    • Carbon isotopes: \(^{12}_{6}\mathrm{C}, ^{13}_{6}\mathrm{C}, ^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) (containing \(6, 7,\) and \(8\) neutrons respectively).
    • Chlorine isotopes: \(^{35}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}, ^{37}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\) (containing \(18\) and \(20\) neutrons respectively).
    Chemical Behaviour of Isotopes: Chemical properties are controlled by the number of electrons (determined by protons). Neutrons have very little effect on chemical properties, so all isotopes of a given element show the same chemical behaviour.
  • Isobars: Atoms with the same mass number (\(A\)) but different atomic numbers (\(Z\)). Example: \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N}\).
  • Isoelectronic Species: Atoms, molecules, or ions having the exact same number of electrons (e.g., \(\mathrm{Na^+}, \mathrm{Mg^{2+}}, \mathrm{F^-}, \mathrm{O^{2-}}\) all have \(10\) electrons; \(\mathrm{K^+}, \mathrm{Ca^{2+}}, \mathrm{S^{2-}}, \mathrm{Ar}\) all have \(18\) electrons).

4. Electromagnetic Radiation, Planck's Quantum Theory & Photoelectric Effect

4.1 Wave Nature of Electromagnetic Radiation

In 1870, James Clerk Maxwell proposed that when an electrically charged particle moves under acceleration, alternating electrical and magnetic fields are produced and transmitted in the form of waves called electromagnetic waves or electromagnetic radiation (experimentally confirmed by Heinrich Hertz).

Tutorial Diagram 4: Mutually perpendicular electric and magnetic field components of an electromagnetic wave.

Key Properties of Electromagnetic Waves:

  • The oscillating electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to each other and both are perpendicular to the direction of propagation of the wave. They have the same wavelength, frequency, speed, and amplitude.
  • Unlike sound waves or water waves, electromagnetic waves do not require a medium and can move in a vacuum.
  • In a vacuum, all types of electromagnetic radiations, regardless of wavelength, travel at the same constant speed called the speed of light (\(c\)): \(c = 2.997925 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
  • Electromagnetic Spectrum Order (Increasing Frequency \(\nu\) / Decreasing Wavelength \(\lambda\)):
    \(\text{Long Radio waves} < \text{AM/FM Radio waves } (\sim 10^6\text{–}10^8\,\mathrm{Hz}) < \text{Microwaves } (\sim 10^{10}\,\mathrm{Hz}, \text{ radar}) < \text{Infrared } (\sim 10^{13}\,\mathrm{Hz}, \text{ heating}) < \text{Visible light } (\sim 10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz}, 400\,\mathrm{nm}\text{ violet to }750\,\mathrm{nm}\text{ red}) < \text{Ultraviolet } (\sim 10^{16}\,\mathrm{Hz}) < \text{X-rays } (\sim 10^{18}\,\mathrm{Hz}) < \gamma\text{-rays } (\sim 10^{20}\text{–}10^{24}\,\mathrm{Hz}) < \text{Cosmic rays}\).

Fundamental Wave Equations:

\[ c = \nu \lambda \qquad \text{and} \qquad \bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{\nu}{c} \qquad \text{and} \qquad T = \frac{1}{\nu} \]
  • Frequency (\(\nu\)): Number of waves that pass a given point in one second. SI unit: hertz (\(\mathrm{Hz}\) or \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)).
  • Wavelength (\(\lambda\)): Distance between two consecutive crests or troughs. SI unit: metre (\(\mathrm{m}\)). Common conversions: \(1\,\mathrm{nm} = 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}\); \(1\,\mathrm{pm} = 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m}\); \(1\,\mathrm{Å} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m} = 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{cm}\); \(1\,\mathrm{µm} = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}\).
  • Wavenumber (\(\bar{\nu}\)): Number of wavelengths per unit length. SI unit: \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\); commonly used spectroscopy unit: \(\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\).
  • Time Period (\(T\)): Time taken for one complete wave to pass a point (\(\mathrm{s}\)).

4.2 Particle Nature of Electromagnetic Radiation: Planck's Quantum Theory

While wave nature explains diffraction (bending of a wave around an obstacle) and interference (combination of waves), classical electromagnetic theory failed to explain four major observations:

  1. The nature of emission of radiation from hot bodies (black-body radiation).
  2. Ejection of electrons from a metal surface when radiation strikes it (photoelectric effect).
  3. Variation of heat capacity of solids as a function of temperature.
  4. Line spectra of atoms with special reference to hydrogen.

Black-Body Radiation & Planck's Hypothesis (1900):

An ideal body that emits and absorbs radiations of all frequencies uniformly is called a black body, and the radiation emitted by it is called black-body radiation (approximated by carbon black or a cavity with a tiny hole). At a given temperature, the intensity of radiation emitted increases with wavelength, reaches a maximum at a particular wavelength, and then decreases at longer wavelengths. As temperature increases (\(T_2 > T_1\)), the maximum of the curve shifts towards shorter wavelengths (e.g., a heated iron rod turns dull red \(\rightarrow\) bright red \(\rightarrow\) white \(\rightarrow\) blue).

Tutorial Diagram 5: Wavelength-intensity relationship for black-body radiation at two temperatures.

Max Planck proposed that atoms and molecules emit or absorb energy only in discrete quantities (like standing on steps of a staircase) and not in a continuous manner. The smallest quantity of energy that can be emitted or absorbed in the form of electromagnetic radiation is called a quantum:

\[ E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \qquad (\text{For } n \text{ quanta: } E = n h\nu, \text{ where } n = 1, 2, 3, \dots) \]

where Planck's constant \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\).

4.3 Photoelectric Effect (Hertz 1887; Explained by Einstein 1905)

When certain metals (such as potassium, rubidium, caesium) are exposed to a beam of light of suitable frequency, electrons are ejected from the metal surface. Key experimental observations:

  1. No Time Lag: Electrons are ejected instantaneously as soon as the light beam strikes the metal surface.
  2. Effect of Intensity (Brightness): The number of electrons ejected is directly proportional to the intensity or brightness of light (number of incident photons), provided \(\nu > \nu_0\).
  3. Threshold Frequency (\(\nu_0\)) & Kinetic Energy: For each metal, there is a characteristic minimum frequency \(\nu_0\) (threshold frequency) below which no photoelectric effect is observed, regardless of how bright the light is or how long it shines. At \(\nu > \nu_0\), the kinetic energy of ejected photoelectrons increases linearly with the frequency of light used and does not depend on the intensity of light.

Example: For potassium metal, \(\nu_0 = 5.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Red light (\(\nu = 4.3 \text{ to } 4.6 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\)) of any brightness ejects no electrons, whereas even very weak yellow light (\(\nu = 5.1 \text{ to } 5.2 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\)) causes photoelectric emission.

Einstein's Explanation: Treating the light beam as a stream of energy particles called photons (each of energy \(h\nu\)), a collision between a photon and an electron in the metal transfers energy instantaneously. The minimum energy required to eject the electron is the work function (\(W_0 = h\nu_0 = hc/\lambda_0\)), and the excess energy becomes the kinetic energy of the photoelectron:

\[ h\nu = h\nu_0 + \frac{1}{2}m_e v^2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mathrm{K.E.} = \frac{1}{2}m_e v^2 = h(\nu - \nu_0) = hc\left(\frac{1}{\lambda} - \frac{1}{\lambda_0}\right) = eV_{\text{stop}} \]

(where \(V_{\text{stop}}\) is the stopping potential voltage required to stop the fastest photoelectron, and \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\)).

5. Atomic Spectra & Bohr's Model for Hydrogen Atom

5.1 Continuous vs. Line Spectra (Emission & Absorption)

When white light passes through a prism, waves with shorter wavelengths (violet, \(400\,\mathrm{nm}\), \(7.50 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\)) bend more than waves with longer wavelengths (red, \(750\,\mathrm{nm}\), \(4.00 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\)), forming a continuous spectrum where colours merge smoothly into one another.

  • Emission Line Spectrum: When atoms in the gas phase absorb energy (by heating or electric discharge), they become excited and subsequently emit light only at specific discrete wavelengths with dark spaces between them, appearing as bright lines.
  • Absorption Line Spectrum: Like the photographic negative of an emission spectrum; when continuous white light passes through an unexcited sample, specific wavelengths are absorbed, leaving dark lines at the exact same wavelengths that the excited sample emits.
  • Chemical Fingerprinting: Each element has a unique line emission spectrum. Robert Bunsen pioneered using line spectra to identify elements. Elements such as rubidium (\(\mathrm{Rb}\)), caesium (\(\mathrm{Cs}\)), thallium (\(\mathrm{Tl}\)), indium (\(\mathrm{In}\)), gallium (\(\mathrm{Ga}\)), and scandium (\(\mathrm{Sc}\)) were discovered spectroscopically in minerals, and helium (\(\mathrm{He}\)) was discovered in the sun by spectroscopic method.

5.2 Line Spectrum of Hydrogen & Rydberg Formula

When an electric discharge passes through gaseous \(\mathrm{H_2}\), the molecules dissociate and excited hydrogen atoms emit radiation in several series of lines. Johannes Rydberg showed that all series of lines in the hydrogen spectrum are described by:

\[ \bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = 109,677\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}} \qquad (\text{For H-like ions: } \bar{\nu} = R_H Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)) \]

where \(R = 109,677\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}} = 1.09677 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\) is the Rydberg constant for hydrogen, \(n_1 = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) and \(n_2 = n_1 + 1, n_1 + 2, \dots\)

5.3 Postulates & Equations of Bohr's Model (1913)

  1. Stationary States (Orbits): The electron in the hydrogen atom moves around the nucleus in circular paths of fixed radius and energy called orbits, stationary states, or allowed energy states (numbered by the Principal Quantum Number \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\)).
  2. Quantized Energy Transitions: The energy of an electron in an orbit does not change with time. Energy is absorbed or emitted only when the electron jumps between stationary states.
  3. Bohr's Frequency Rule: The frequency of radiation absorbed or emitted during a transition between states of energy \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) is: \[ \nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{h} \]
  4. Quantisation of Angular Momentum: An electron can move only in those orbits for which its angular momentum (\(L = I\omega = m_e v r\)) is an integral multiple of \(\frac{h}{2\pi}\): \[ m_e v r = n\frac{h}{2\pi} \qquad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots) \]

Key Quantitative Results of Bohr's Model (for H and One-Electron Ions like \(\mathrm{He^+, Li^{2+}, Be^{3+}}\)):

  • Radius of \(n^{\text{th}}\) Orbit: \[ r_n = \frac{n^2 a_0}{Z} = \frac{52.9\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{pm} = \frac{0.0529\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{nm} = \frac{0.529\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{Å} \] (where first Bohr orbit radius for hydrogen is \(a_0 = 52.9\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Energy of Electron in \(n^{\text{th}}\) Stationary State: \[ E_n = -R_H\left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right) = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right)\,\mathrm{J\,atom^{-1}} = -13.6\left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right)\,\mathrm{eV\,atom^{-1}} \]
  • Meaning of Negative Sign in \(E_n\): A free electron infinitely far from the nucleus (\(n = \infty\), ionized atom) is assigned zero energy (\(E_\infty = 0\)). When attracted by the nucleus into orbit \(n\), energy is emitted and its energy becomes lower than zero (negative). The most negative energy is at \(n = 1\) (ground state, most stable).
  • Velocity of Electron: Increases with increase in nuclear charge (\(Z\)) and decreases with increase in principal quantum number (\(n\)): \(v_n \propto \frac{Z}{n}\) (where \(v_1 = 2.19 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for H).
  • Energy, Frequency & Wavenumber of Transition (\(n_i \rightarrow n_f\)): \[ \Delta E = E_f - E_i = 2.18 \times 10^{-18} Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_i^2} - \frac{1}{n_f^2}\right)\,\mathrm{J}, \qquad \nu = 3.29 \times 10^{15} Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_i^2} - \frac{1}{n_f^2}\right)\,\mathrm{Hz} \]
  • Maximum Number of Emission Lines from Excited State \(n\) to Ground State (\(n=1\)): \[ \text{Total Spectral Lines} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \qquad \left(\text{Between } n_2 \text{ and } n_1: \frac{(n_2 - n_1)(n_2 - n_1 + 1)}{2}\right) \]

Limitations of Bohr's Model:

  • Fails to account for finer details (doublets, closely spaced lines) of the hydrogen spectrum.
  • Unable to explain the spectrum of multi-electron atoms (even helium, which has only \(2\) electrons).
  • Unable to explain the splitting of spectral lines in a magnetic field (Zeeman effect) or in an electric field (Stark effect).
  • Cannot explain the ability of atoms to form molecules by chemical bonds.
  • Ignores the wave character of the electron and contradicts Heisenberg's uncertainty principle by defining exact circular trajectories.

6. Dual Behaviour of Matter & Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

6.1 de Broglie Relation (1924)

French physicist Louis de Broglie proposed that matter, like radiation, exhibits dual behaviour (both particle and wave-like properties). The wavelength (\(\lambda\)) associated with a material particle of mass \(m\) and velocity \(v\) (momentum \(p = mv\)) is:

\[ \lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m(\mathrm{K.E.})}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mqV}} \]
  • Experimental Confirmation: Confirmed when an electron beam was found to undergo diffraction. This principle is used in the electron microscope (capable of \(\sim 15\) million times magnification).
  • Macroscopic vs. Microscopic Objects: Every object in motion has a wave character, but for ordinary macroscopic objects (large mass \(m\)), \(\lambda\) is so extremely short that wave properties cannot be detected. Sub-atomic particles have very small mass, so their wavelengths are experimentally measurable.
  • Connection to Bohr's Quantisation: For a standing electron wave to fit around a circular Bohr orbit of radius \(r\) without destructive interference, the circumference of the Bohr orbit must be an integral multiple of the de Broglie wavelength: \[ 2\pi r = n\lambda = n\left(\frac{h}{m_e v}\right) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad m_e v r = \frac{nh}{2\pi} \]

6.2 Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle (1927)

Werner Heisenberg stated that it is impossible to determine simultaneously, the exact position and exact momentum (or velocity) of an electron (or any microscopic particle).

\[ \Delta x \times \Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi} \quad \text{or} \quad \Delta x \times \Delta(m v_x) \ge \frac{h}{4\pi} \quad \text{or} \quad \Delta x \times \Delta v_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi m} \]

where \(\Delta x\) is the uncertainty in position and \(\Delta p_x\) (or \(\Delta v_x\)) is the uncertainty in momentum (or velocity).

  • Physical Reason: To locate a dimensionless point-like electron accurately, we must illuminate it with electromagnetic radiation of very short wavelength \(\lambda\). Photons of such light have very high momentum (\(p = h/\lambda\)); when they collide with the electron, they drastically alter its velocity/momentum.
  • Significance: It rules out the existence of definite paths or trajectories (orbits) of electrons. Precise statements of position and momentum must be replaced by statements of probability. For macroscopic objects (e.g., \(1\,\mathrm{mg} = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{kg}\)), \(\Delta v \cdot \Delta x \approx 10^{-28}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\), which is negligible; for an electron (\(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)), \(\Delta v \cdot \Delta x \approx 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\), which is very large.

7. Quantum Mechanical Model of Atom & Quantum Numbers

Developed independently in 1926 by Werner Heisenberg and Erwin Schrödinger, quantum mechanics describes microscopic objects exhibiting wave-particle duality. For a system whose energy does not change with time, the fundamental Schrödinger equation is:

\[ \hat{H}\psi = E\psi \]

where \(\hat{H}\) is the mathematical Hamiltonian operator (constructed from total kinetic and attractive/repulsive potential energies of electrons and nuclei), \(E\) is the quantized total energy, and \(\psi\) is the wave function (atomic orbital for one-electron systems).

  • Wave Function (\(\psi\)): A mathematical function of the electron's coordinates containing all information about the electron; \(\psi\) by itself has no physical meaning (and can be positive, negative, or zero).
  • Probability Density (\(|\psi|^2\)): According to Max Born, the square of the wave function \(|\psi|^2\) at a point is always positive (or zero at nodes) and gives the probability density (probability per unit volume) of finding the electron at that point. Boundary surface diagrams enclose a region where the probability of finding the electron is very high (\(\sim 90\%\); a \(100\%\) boundary surface cannot be drawn because \(|\psi|^2\) is never strictly zero at any finite distance, except at nodes).

8. Orbital Energies, Shielding & Electronic Configuration Rules

8.1 Energies of Orbitals: Hydrogen vs. Multi-Electron Atoms

  • Hydrogen & One-Electron Species (\(\mathrm{H, He^+, Li^{2+}}\)): Only electrical attraction between the nucleus and the single electron exists. Orbital energy depends solely on \(n\): \[ 1s < 2s = 2p < 3s = 3p = 3d < 4s = 4p = 4d = 4f < \dots \] Orbitals having the same energy are called degenerate orbitals.
  • Multi-Electron Atoms: In addition to nuclear attraction, electron-electron repulsions exist. Inner shell electrons partially screen the outer electrons from the full nuclear charge (\(Ze\)). This is called shielding (or screening), and the net positive charge experienced by outer electrons is the effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\text{eff}}e\)).
    • Within the same shell, spherical \(s\) electrons spend more time close to the nucleus than \(p\) electrons, which penetrate closer than \(d\) electrons, etc.
    • Therefore, \(Z_{\text{eff}}\) experienced by an electron decreases with increasing \(l\): \(Z_{\text{eff}}(s) > Z_{\text{eff}}(p) > Z_{\text{eff}}(d) > Z_{\text{eff}}(f)\).
    • Consequently, within a shell, orbital energy increases as: \(s < p < d < f\).
    • Also, for the same subshell across different elements, orbital energy decreases (becomes more negative) as atomic number \(Z\) increases: \(E_{2s}(\mathrm{H}) > E_{2s}(\mathrm{Li}) > E_{2s}(\mathrm{Na}) > E_{2s}(\mathrm{K})\).

Bohr-Bury \((n + l)\) Rule for Multi-Electron Atoms:

  1. The lower the value of \((n + l)\) for an orbital, the lower is its energy.
  2. If two orbitals have the same value of \((n + l)\), the orbital with the lower value of \(n\) has the lower energy.

Resulting Aufbau Filling Order:
\(1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p < 7s < 5f < 6d < 7p\)

8.2 Three Fundamental Rules for Filling Orbitals

  1. Aufbau Principle: In the ground state of atoms, orbitals are filled in the order of their increasing energies.
  2. Pauli Exclusion Principle (Wolfgang Pauli, 1926): "No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers." Equivalently: "Only two electrons may exist in the same orbital and these electrons must have opposite spin."
  3. Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity: "Pairing of electrons in the orbitals belonging to the same subshell (\(p, d,\) or \(f\) — degenerate orbitals) does not take place until each orbital belonging to that subshell has got one electron each, i.e., is singly occupied" (with parallel spins). Thus, pairing starts with the \(4^{\text{th}}\) electron in \(p\), the \(6^{\text{th}}\) electron in \(d\), and the \(8^{\text{th}}\) electron in \(f\) subshells.

8.3 Extra Stability of Half-Filled & Completely Filled Subshells

In elements where outer subshells (such as \(4s\) and \(3d\)) differ only slightly in energy, an electron shifts from the lower-energy subshell (\(4s\)) to the higher-energy subshell (\(3d\)) if doing so results in a half-filled (\(d^5\)) or completely filled (\(d^{10}\)) configuration:

  • Chromium (\(\mathrm{Cr}, Z = 24\)): \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1\) (instead of \(3d^4 4s^2\)) — \(6\) unpaired electrons in ground state.
  • Copper (\(\mathrm{Cu}, Z = 29\)): \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1\) (instead of \(3d^9 4s^2\)) — \(1\) unpaired electron in ground state.
  • Other Notable Exceptional Configurations: \(\mathrm{Nb}\,(41): [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^4 5s^1\); \(\mathrm{Mo}\,(42): [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^5 5s^1\); \(\mathrm{Ru}\,(44): [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^7 5s^1\); \(\mathrm{Rh}\,(45): [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^8 5s^1\); \(\mathrm{Pd}\,(46): [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10} 5s^0\); \(\mathrm{Ag}\,(47): [\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10} 5s^1\); \(\mathrm{La}\,(57): [\mathrm{Xe}]\,5d^1 6s^2\); \(\mathrm{Gd}\,(64): [\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^7 5d^1 6s^2\); \(\mathrm{Pt}\,(78): [\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14} 5d^9 6s^1\); \(\mathrm{Au}\,(79): [\mathrm{Xe}]\,4f^{14} 5d^{10} 6s^1\).

Causes of Extra Stability:

  1. Symmetrical Distribution of Electrons: Half-filled and fully filled subshells have symmetrical spatial distribution, resulting in relatively small shielding of one another and stronger attraction by the nucleus, along with smaller coulombic repulsion energy.
  2. Exchange Energy: Two or more electrons with the same spin in degenerate orbitals of a subshell tend to exchange their positions, releasing exchange energy. The number of possible exchanges is maximum when the subshell is half-filled or completely filled (for a \(d^5\) configuration with \(5\) parallel spins, total exchanges \(= 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10\) exchanges).

9. Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Sub-Atomic Particles in Neutral Atoms and Ions

Problem: (a) Calculate the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in \(^{80}_{35}\mathrm{Br}\). (b) An ion has \(18\) electrons, \(16\) protons, and \(16\) neutrons. Assign its proper symbol. (c) An ion with mass number \(56\) contains \(3\) units of positive charge and \(30.4\%\) more neutrons than electrons. Assign the symbol to this ion.

Solution:

(a) For neutral \(^{80}_{35}\mathrm{Br}\), \(Z = 35\) and \(A = 80\).
Number of protons \(= \text{Number of electrons} = Z = 35\).
Number of neutrons \(= A - Z = 80 - 35 = 45\).

(b) Atomic number \(Z = \text{number of protons} = 16\), which corresponds to sulphur (\(\mathrm{S}\)).
Mass number \(A = 16 + 16 = 32\). Since there are \(18 - 16 = 2\) excess electrons, it is an anion with a \(2-\) charge: \(^{32}_{16}\mathrm{S}^{2-}\).

(c) Let the number of electrons in the \(+3\) cation be \(x\).
Then the number of protons \(Z = x + 3\), and the number of neutrons \(n = x + 0.304x = 1.304x\).
Since mass number \(A = Z + n = 56\):
\[ (x + 3) + 1.304x = 56 \implies 2.304x = 53 \implies x = \frac{53}{2.304} = 23\text{ electrons} \] Therefore, number of protons \(Z = 23 + 3 = 26\) (Iron, \(\mathrm{Fe}\)). The symbol of the ion is \(^{56}_{26}\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}\).

Worked Example 2: Mass and Number of Neutrons/Protons in Macroscopic Samples

Problem: (a) Find the total number and total mass of neutrons in \(7\,\mathrm{mg}\) of \(^{14}\mathrm{C}\) (mass of a neutron \(= 1.675 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\)). (b) Find the total number and total mass of protons in \(34\,\mathrm{mg}\) of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) at STP. Will the answer change if temperature and pressure are changed?

Solution:

(a) \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) (\(14\,\mathrm{g} = 14 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{mg}\)) contains \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\) atoms, and each \(^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C}\) atom has \(14 - 6 = 8\) neutrons.
Number of neutrons in \(7\,\mathrm{mg}\): \[ N_n = \frac{7\,\mathrm{mg}}{14,000\,\mathrm{mg\,mol^{-1}}} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \times 8 = 2.4088 \times 10^{21}\text{ neutrons} \] Total mass of neutrons \(= (2.4088 \times 10^{21}) \times (1.675 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}) = 4.035 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{kg}\).

(b) Molar mass of \(\mathrm{NH_3} = 17\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 17,000\,\mathrm{mg\,mol^{-1}}\). Each \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) molecule contains \(7 + 3(1) = 10\) protons.
Moles of \(\mathrm{NH_3} = \frac{34\,\mathrm{mg}}{17,000\,\mathrm{mg\,mol^{-1}}} = 2 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol}\).
Total number of protons \(= (2 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol}) \times (6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}) \times 10 = 1.2044 \times 10^{22}\text{ protons}\).
Total mass of protons \(= (1.2044 \times 10^{22}) \times (1.6726 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}) = 2.0145 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{kg}\).
Effect of \(T\) and \(P\): No, the answer will not change because mass (\(34\,\mathrm{mg}\)) and the number of protons are independent of temperature and pressure.

Worked Example 3: Photoelectric Effect & Photon Energy

Problem: When electromagnetic radiation of wavelength \(300\,\mathrm{nm}\) falls on the surface of sodium, electrons are emitted with a kinetic energy of \(1.68 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\). What is the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from sodium, and what is the maximum wavelength that will cause a photoelectron to be emitted?

Solution:

Energy of one \(300\,\mathrm{nm}\) photon: \[ E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s})(3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})}{300 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}} = 6.626 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \] Energy of one mole of photons \(= (6.626 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J})(6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}) = 3.99 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\).
Minimum energy needed to remove one mole of electrons \(= (3.99 - 1.68) \times 10^5 = 2.31 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\).
Minimum energy per electron (Work function \(W_0\)): \[ W_0 = \frac{2.31 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}} = 3.84 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \] Maximum (threshold) wavelength \(\lambda_0\): \[ \lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{W_0} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s})(3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})}{3.84 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}} = 5.17 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 517\,\mathrm{nm}\text{ (green light)} \]

Worked Example 4: Bohr Orbit Radii & Hydrogen Spectral Transitions

Problem: Calculate the wavelength for the emission transition in the hydrogen atom if it starts from the orbit having radius \(1.3225\,\mathrm{nm}\) and ends at \(211.6\,\mathrm{pm}\). Name the series to which this transition belongs and the region of the spectrum.

Solution:

For the hydrogen atom (\(Z = 1\)), the radius of the \(n^{\text{th}}\) orbit is \(r_n = 52.9\,n^2\,\mathrm{pm}\).
Initial radius \(r_i = 1.3225\,\mathrm{nm} = 1322.5\,\mathrm{pm} \implies n_i^2 = \frac{1322.5}{52.9} = 25 \implies n_i = 5\).
Final radius \(r_f = 211.6\,\mathrm{pm} \implies n_f^2 = \frac{211.6}{52.9} = 4 \implies n_f = 2\).
Since the emission ends at \(n_f = 2\), it belongs to the Balmer series in the visible region.
Wavenumber \(\bar{\nu}\): \[ \bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = 1.09677 \times 10^7\left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{5^2}\right)\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} = 1.09677 \times 10^7 \times \frac{21}{100}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} = 2.303 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} \] \[ \lambda = \frac{1}{2.303 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}} = 4.34 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 434\,\mathrm{nm} \]

Worked Example 5: de Broglie Wavelength & Heisenberg Uncertainty

Problem: (a) The mass of an electron is \(9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\). If its kinetic energy is \(3.0 \times 10^{-25}\,\mathrm{J}\), calculate its wavelength. (b) A microscope using suitable photons is employed to locate an electron in an atom within a distance of \(0.1\,\mathrm{Å}\). What is the uncertainty involved in the measurement of its velocity?

Solution:

(a) From \(\mathrm{K.E.} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\): \[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 3.0 \times 10^{-25}\,\mathrm{J}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}}} = 812\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \] \[ \lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}}{(9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg})(812\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})} = 8.967 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 896.7\,\mathrm{nm} \]

(b) Given \(\Delta x = 0.1\,\mathrm{Å} = 0.1 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m} = 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m}\). Using Heisenberg's relation: \[ \Delta v = \frac{h}{4\pi m \Delta x} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}}{4 \times 3.14 \times (9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}) \times (0.1 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m})} = 5.79 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

  • (2) \(5 < 3 = 6 < 2 = 4 < 1\)

  • (4) \(5 < 2 < 4 < 3 < 6 < 1\)

11. Common Pitfalls & Final Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid in NEET UG:

  • Confusing Canal Rays with Cathode Rays: Remember that \(e/m_e\) of cathode rays is independent of the gas, whereas \(q/m\) of canal rays (positive ions) depends on the gas inside the discharge tube.
  • Photoelectric Intensity vs. Frequency Trap: Increasing the intensity (brightness) of light increases the number of photoelectrons emitted (photocurrent), not their kinetic energy. Kinetic energy depends strictly on the frequency (\(\nu\)) of incident light.
  • Counting Transition Lines vs. Series Lines: "First line" or "longest wavelength line" of a series means \(n_2 = n_1 + 1\) (minimum \(\Delta E\)). "Series limit" or "shortest wavelength line" means \(n_2 = \infty\) (maximum \(\Delta E\)).
  • One-Electron vs. Multi-Electron Orbital Energies: In the \(\mathrm{H}\) atom (or \(\mathrm{He^+, Li^{2+}}\)), \(E(2s) = E(2p)\) and \(E(3s) = E(3p) = E(3d)\) because energy depends only on \(n\). The \((n+l)\) rule applies only to multi-electron atoms!
  • Radial vs. Angular Nodes: Never mix up the formulas: Angular nodes \(= l\) (depends only on subshell), Radial nodes \(= n - l - 1\), and Total nodes \(= n - 1\).
  • Cation Electronic Configurations: When forming cations of transition metals (e.g., \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}, Cu^{2+}, Cr^{3+}}\)), always remove electrons from the outermost shell (highest \(n\), i.e., \(4s\)) first before removing from \(3d\).

Concise Final Formula & Concept Recap:

  • Sub-Atomic Constants: \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\); \(m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\); \(e/m_e = 1.7588 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\); \(m_p = 1.6726 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\); \(m_n = 1.6749 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\).
  • Radiation & Photons: \(c = \nu\lambda = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\); \(\bar{\nu} = 1/\lambda\); \(E = nh\nu = \frac{nhc}{\lambda}\) (\(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\)).
  • Photoelectric Equation: \(h\nu = W_0 + \mathrm{K.E._{\max}} = h\nu_0 + \frac{1}{2}m_e v^2\).
  • Bohr Model (H-like species): \(m_e v r = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\); \(r_n = \frac{52.9\,n^2}{Z}\,\mathrm{pm}\); \(E_n = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\left(\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\right)\,\mathrm{J}\); \(\bar{\nu} = 109,677\,Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\).
  • Matter Waves & Uncertainty: \(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{h}{p}\); \(\Delta x \cdot \Delta p_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}\) or \(\Delta x \cdot \Delta v_x \ge \frac{h}{4\pi m}\).
  • Quantum Capacity Rules: Subshells in shell \(n = n\); Orbitals in subshell \(l = 2l + 1\); Orbitals in shell \(n = n^2\); Max electrons in shell \(n = 2n^2\).
