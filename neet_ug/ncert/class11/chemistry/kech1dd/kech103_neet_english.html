Why Do We Need to Classify Elements?

Elements are the basic units of all types of matter. Historically, the discovery of elements progressed rapidly:

  • In 1800, only 31 elements were known.
  • By 1865, the number of identified elements had more than doubled to 63.
  • At present, 114 to 118 elements are known (with recent elements being man-made through nuclear synthesis).

With such a large number of elements, it is very difficult to study individually the chemistry of all these elements and their innumerable compounds. To ease this problem, scientists searched for a systematic way to organise their knowledge by classifying the elements. Classification not only rationalizes known chemical facts about elements, but also predicts new ones for undertaking further study.

2. Genesis of Periodic Classification

2.1 Dobereiner's Triads (1829)

The German chemist Johann Dobereiner in the early 1800s was the first to consider the idea of trends among properties of elements. By 1829, he noted a similarity among the physical and chemical properties of several groups of three elements called Triads.

Law of Triads: In each triad, the middle element had an atomic weight about half-way between the atomic weights of the other two, and the properties of the middle element were in between those of the other two members. Because this relationship seemed to work only for a few elements, it was dismissed as a coincidence.

2.2 De Chancourtois' Cylindrical Table (1862)

A French geologist, A.E.B. de Chancourtois, arranged the then-known elements in order of increasing atomic weights and made a cylindrical table of elements to display the periodic recurrence of properties. This did not attract much attention.

2.3 Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865)

The English chemist John Alexander Newlands propounded the Law of Octaves. He arranged the elements in increasing order of their atomic weights and noted that every eighth element had properties similar to the first element (just like every eighth note resembles the first in musical octaves).

  • Limitation: Newlands' Law of Octaves seemed to be true only for elements up to calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca}\)).
  • Recognition: Although not widely accepted at that time, he was later awarded the Davy Medal in 1887 by the Royal Society, London.

2.4 Lothar Meyer and Dmitri Mendeleev (1869)

Working independently in 1869, Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev (1834–1907) and German chemist Lothar Meyer (1830–1895) proposed that on arranging elements in increasing order of their atomic weights, similarities appear in physical and chemical properties at regular intervals.

  • Lothar Meyer's Contribution: He plotted physical properties such as atomic volume, melting point, and boiling point against atomic weight and obtained a periodically repeated pattern with changing lengths. By 1868, he had developed a table closely resembling the Modern Periodic Table, but his work was published after Mendeleev's.
  • Mendeleev's Periodic Law: Published for the first time by Mendeleev, it states:
    Mendeleev's Periodic Law: "The properties of the elements are a periodic function of their atomic weights."

Key Features of Mendeleev's Periodic Table:

  1. Groups and Series: Elements were arranged in horizontal rows (called series) and vertical columns (called groups) in order of increasing atomic weights so that elements with similar properties occupied the same vertical group.
  2. Empirical Formulas as Criteria: Mendeleev relied heavily on similarities in the empirical formulas and properties of compounds formed by the elements (such as oxides \(\mathrm{R_2O, RO, R_2O_3, RO_2, R_2O_5, RO_3, R_2O_7, RO_4}\) and hydrides \(\mathrm{RH_4, RH_3, RH_2, RH}\)).
  3. Priority to Similarity in Properties over Atomic Weight Order: Where strict atomic weight order did not fit chemical properties, he ignored the order of atomic weights (assuming atomic weight measurements might be incorrect). For example, Iodine (\(\mathrm{I}\), atomic weight \(126.9\)) has a lower atomic weight than Tellurium (\(\mathrm{Te}\), atomic weight \(127.6\) in Group VI), yet Mendeleev placed Iodine in Group VII along with \(\mathrm{F, Cl, Br}\) because of similarities in chemical properties.
  4. Prediction of Undiscovered Elements: To keep elements with similar properties in the same group, Mendeleev left several gaps for undiscovered elements. He left a gap under aluminium (\(\mathrm{Al}\)) and called it Eka-Aluminium (later discovered as Gallium, \(\mathrm{Ga}\)) and a gap under silicon (\(\mathrm{Si}\)) called Eka-Silicon (later discovered as Germanium, \(\mathrm{Ge}\)), quantitatively predicting their physical and chemical properties with remarkable accuracy.

3. Modern Periodic Law and the Present Form of the Periodic Table

In 1913, the English physicist Henry Moseley observed regularities in the characteristic X-ray spectra of the elements. A plot of \(\sqrt{\nu}\) (where \(\nu\) is the frequency of X-rays emitted) against atomic number (\(Z\)) gave a straight line, whereas the plot of \(\sqrt{\nu}\) versus atomic mass did not.

Moseley thereby demonstrated that atomic number (\(Z\)) is a more fundamental property of an element than its atomic mass. Mendeleev's Periodic Law was accordingly modified into the Modern Periodic Law:

Modern Periodic Law: "The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers."

The atomic number (\(Z\)) is equal to the nuclear charge (number of protons) or the number of electrons in a neutral atom. Thus, the Periodic Law is essentially the consequence of the periodic variation in electronic configurations, which determine the physical and chemical properties of elements and their compounds.

Note on Naturally Occurring Elements: There are 94 naturally occurring elements (neptunium, \(Z=93\), and plutonium, \(Z=94\), like actinium and protactinium, are also found in pitchblende—an ore of uranium).

3.1 Structure of the "Long Form" of the Periodic Table

  • Periods (Horizontal Rows): There are altogether 7 periods. The period number corresponds to the highest principal quantum number (\(n\)) of the elements in that period.
    • 1st Period (\(n = 1\)): 2 elements (\(\mathrm{H}\) to \(\mathrm{He}\))
    • 2nd Period (\(n = 2\)): 8 elements (\(\mathrm{Li}\) to \(\mathrm{Ne}\))
    • 3rd Period (\(n = 3\)): 8 elements (\(\mathrm{Na}\) to \(\mathrm{Ar}\))
    • 4th Period (\(n = 4\)): 18 elements (\(\mathrm{K}\) to \(\mathrm{Kr}\))
    • 5th Period (\(n = 5\)): 18 elements (\(\mathrm{Rb}\) to \(\mathrm{Xe}\))
    • 6th Period (\(n = 6\)): 32 elements (\(\mathrm{Cs}\) to \(\mathrm{Rn}\), including 14 lanthanoids)
    • 7th Period (\(n = 7\)): Theoretical maximum of 32 elements (up to \(Z = 118\), including 14 actinoids)
  • Groups (Vertical Columns): Elements having similar outer electronic configurations in their atoms are arranged in vertical columns called groups or families. According to the 1984 IUPAC recommendation, groups are numbered from 1 to 18, replacing the older notation of \(\mathrm{IA \dots VIIA, VIII, IB \dots VIIB}\) and \(\mathrm{0}\).
  • Separate Bottom Panels: 14 elements of the sixth period (Lanthanoids, \(\mathrm{Ce}\) \(Z=58\) to \(\mathrm{Lu}\) \(Z=71\)) and 14 elements of the seventh period (Actinoids, \(\mathrm{Th}\) \(Z=90\) to \(\mathrm{Lr}\) \(Z=103\)) are placed in separate panels at the bottom to maintain the table's structure and keep elements with similar properties in a single column. 4. IUPAC Nomenclature of Elements with Atomic Numbers \(Z > 100\)

New elements with very high atomic numbers are extremely unstable and synthesized in minute quantities (sometimes only a few atoms), leading to historical naming controversies. For example, both American and Soviet scientists claimed credit for discovering element 104: the Americans named it Rutherfordium whereas the Soviets named it Kurchatovium.

To avoid such problems, IUPAC recommended that until a new element's discovery is proved and its official name is recognized, a systematic three-letter symbol and temporary name be derived directly from the digits of its atomic number:

  1. Combine the numerical roots for each digit of the atomic number in order.
  2. Add the suffix "-ium" at the end (if "enn" precedes "nil", drop one 'n'; if "bi" or "tri" precedes "ium", drop one 'i').
  3. Capitalize the first letter of the three-letter symbol.

5. Electronic Configurations and Types of Elements (\(s, p, d, f\) Blocks)

5.1 Electronic Configurations in Periods

The period number indicates the value of \(n\) for the outermost or valence shell. The number of elements in each period is twice the number of atomic orbitals available in the energy level that is being filled:

  • 1st Period (\(n = 1\)): Fills \(1s\) orbital (1 orbital \(\Rightarrow 2\) elements): \(\mathrm{H}\,(1s^1)\) and \(\mathrm{He}\,(1s^2)\), completing the \(K\) shell.
  • 2nd Period (\(n = 2\)): Fills \(2s\) and \(2p\) orbitals (4 orbitals \(\Rightarrow 8\) elements): starts at \(\mathrm{Li}\,(1s^2 2s^1)\), \(\mathrm{Be}\,(1s^2 2s^2)\), \(\mathrm{B}\) to \(\mathrm{Ne}\,(2s^2 2p^6)\), completing the \(L\) shell.
  • 3rd Period (\(n = 3\)): Fills \(3s\) and \(3p\) orbitals (4 orbitals \(\Rightarrow 8\) elements): from \(\mathrm{Na}\) to \(\mathrm{Ar}\).
  • 4th Period (\(n = 4\)): Fills \(4s, 3d,\) and \(4p\) orbitals (9 orbitals \(\Rightarrow 18\) elements): starts at \(\mathrm{K}\,(4s^1)\) and \(\mathrm{Ca}\,(4s^2)\), followed by the \(3d\) transition series from \(\mathrm{Sc}\,(Z=21, 3d^1 4s^2)\) to \(\mathrm{Zn}\,(Z=30, 3d^{10} 4s^2)\), and ends at \(\mathrm{Kr}\) with the filling of \(4p\) orbitals.
  • 5th Period (\(n = 5\)): Fills \(5s, 4d,\) and \(5p\) orbitals (9 orbitals \(\Rightarrow 18\) elements): starts at \(\mathrm{Rb}\), contains the \(4d\) transition series starting at \(\mathrm{Y}\,(Z=39)\), and ends at \(\mathrm{Xe}\) with the filling of \(5p\) orbitals.
  • 6th Period (\(n = 6\)): Fills \(6s, 4f, 5d,\) and \(6p\) orbitals (16 orbitals \(\Rightarrow 32\) elements): filling of \(4f\) orbitals begins at Cerium (\(\mathrm{Ce}, Z=58\)) and ends at Lutetium (\(\mathrm{Lu}, Z=71\)) to give the \(4f\)-inner transition series (Lanthanoid series).
  • 7th Period (\(n = 7\)): Fills \(7s, 5f, 6d,\) and \(7p\) orbitals (16 orbitals \(\Rightarrow 32\) elements): ends at \(Z = 118\) (noble gas family). Filling of \(5f\) orbitals after Actinium (\(\mathrm{Ac}, Z=89\)) from \(\mathrm{Th}\,(Z=90)\) to \(\mathrm{Lr}\,(Z=103)\) gives the \(5f\)-inner transition series (Actinoid series), which includes most man-made radioactive elements.

5.2 Groupwise Electronic Configurations

Elements in the same vertical column (group) have similar valence shell electronic configurations, the same number of electrons in the outer orbitals, and therefore similar chemical properties. For example, all Group 1 elements (alkali metals) have an \(ns^1\) valence shell configuration:

  • \(\mathrm{Li}\,(Z=3): 1s^2 2s^1\) or \([\mathrm{He}]2s^1\)
  • \(\mathrm{Na}\,(Z=11): 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^1\) or \([\mathrm{Ne}]3s^1\)
  • \(\mathrm{K}\,(Z=19): 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^1\) or \([\mathrm{Ar}]4s^1\)
  • \(\mathrm{Rb}\,(Z=37): 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^{10} 4s^2 4p^6 5s^1\) or \([\mathrm{Kr}]5s^1\)
  • \(\mathrm{Cs}\,(Z=55): 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^{10} 4s^2 4p^6 4d^{10} 5s^2 5p^6 6s^1\) or \([\mathrm{Xe}]6s^1\)
  • \(\mathrm{Fr}\,(Z=87): [\mathrm{Rn}]7s^1\)

5.3 Classification into \(s\text{-}, p\text{-}, d\text{-},\) and \(f\text{-Blocks}\)

Two Important Exceptions in Block Placement:
  1. Helium (\(\mathrm{He}, 1s^2\)): Strictly belongs to the \(s\text{-block}\), but is placed in the \(p\text{-block}\) (Group 18) because it has a completely filled valence shell (\(1s^2\)) and exhibits properties characteristic of other noble gases.
  2. Hydrogen (\(\mathrm{H}, 1s^1\)): Has one \(s\text{-electron}\) and can lose it like Group 1 alkali metals, or gain one electron to achieve a noble gas arrangement like Group 17 halogens. Hence, it is placed separately at the top of the Periodic Table.

5.4 Metals, Non-Metals, and Metalloids

  • Metals (>78% of all known elements): Located on the left side of the Periodic Table. Usually solids at room temperature (exceptions: mercury (\(\mathrm{Hg}\)) is a liquid; gallium (\(\mathrm{Ga}\), m.p. \(303\,\mathrm{K}\)) and caesium (\(\mathrm{Cs}\), m.p. \(302\,\mathrm{K}\)) have very low melting points). Have high melting and boiling points, are good conductors of heat and electricity, and are malleable and ductile.
  • Non-Metals (<20 elements): Located at the top right-hand side of the Periodic Table. Usually solids or gases at room temperature (bromine is a liquid) with low melting and boiling points (exceptions: boron (\(\mathrm{B}\)) and carbon (\(\mathrm{C}\)) have high melting/boiling points). Poor conductors of heat and electricity; brittle solids (neither malleable nor ductile).
  • Metalloids (Semi-metals): Elements bordering the thick zig-zag line running diagonally across the \(p\text{-block}\) (e.g., silicon (\(\mathrm{Si}\)), germanium (\(\mathrm{Ge}\)), arsenic (\(\mathrm{As}\)), antimony (\(\mathrm{Sb}\)), and tellurium (\(\mathrm{Te}\))) show properties characteristic of both metals and non-metals.

6. Periodic Trends in Physical Properties

Summary of periodic variations in atomic radius, ionization enthalpy (IE), electron gain enthalpy (EGE), electronegativity, and metallic/non-metallic character.

6.1 Atomic Radius

Because an atom is extremely small (\(\sim 1.2\,\mathrm{Å}\), i.e., \(1.2 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\) in radius) and its surrounding electron cloud does not have a sharp boundary, the size of an individual isolated atom cannot be measured directly. Instead, it is estimated from internuclear distances in combined states using X-ray or spectroscopic methods:

  • Covalent Radius (for non-metals): Half the internuclear distance between two identical atoms bound together by a single covalent bond in a molecule.
    Example: The bond distance in the chlorine molecule (\(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)) is \(198\,\mathrm{pm}\), so the atomic (covalent) radius of chlorine is \(198 / 2 = 99\,\mathrm{pm}\).
  • Metallic Radius (for metals): Half the internuclear distance separating the metal cores in a metallic crystal.
    Example: The distance between two adjacent copper atoms in solid copper is \(256\,\mathrm{pm}\), so the metallic radius of copper is \(256 / 2 = 128\,\mathrm{pm}\).
  • Van der Waals Radius (for Noble Gases): Because noble gases are monoatomic, their non-bonded radii values are very large and should be compared only with the van der Waals radii of other elements, not covalent radii.

Periodic Trends in Atomic Radius:

  1. Across a Period (Left to Right): Decreases. Within a period, outer electrons are in the same valence shell while the nuclear charge (\(Z\)) increases. Consequently, the effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\)) increases, drawing the electron cloud closer to the nucleus.
  2. Down a Group (Top to Bottom): Increases. As we descend a group, the principal quantum number (\(n\)) increases and inner energy levels filled with core electrons shield (screen) the outer valence electrons from the pull of the nucleus.

6.2 Ionic Radius and Isoelectronic Species

  • Cations are ALWAYS smaller than their parent atoms (\(r_{\mathrm{cation}} < r_{\mathrm{atom}}\)): A cation has fewer electrons while its nuclear charge remains the same, resulting in increased effective nuclear attraction per electron (and often loss of the outermost shell).
    Example: Atomic radius of \(\mathrm{Na} = 186\,\mathrm{pm}\), whereas ionic radius of \(\mathrm{Na^+} = 95\,\mathrm{pm}\).
  • Anions are ALWAYS larger than their parent atoms (\(r_{\mathrm{anion}} > r_{\mathrm{atom}}\)): Addition of one or more electrons increases electron–electron repulsion and decreases effective nuclear charge, causing the electron cloud to expand.
    Example: Atomic radius of \(\mathrm{F} = 64\,\mathrm{pm}\), whereas ionic radius of \(\mathrm{F^-} = 136\,\mathrm{pm}\).
Isoelectronic Species: Atoms and ions that contain the same number of electrons (or, more broadly, two or more species with the same number of atoms, same number of valence electrons, and same structure).
For monoatomic isoelectronic ions (e.g., \(\mathrm{N^{3-}, O^{2-}, F^-, Ne, Na^+, Mg^{2+}, Al^{3+}}\), all having \(10\,\text{electrons}\)), ionic radius is inversely proportional to nuclear charge (\(Z\)): \[ \text{Ionic Radius Order: } \mathrm{Al^{3+}} < \mathrm{Mg^{2+}} < \mathrm{Na^+} < \mathrm{F^-} < \mathrm{O^{2-}} < \mathrm{N^{3-}} \] Rule: Among isoelectronic species, the cation with the greater positive charge has a smaller radius, while the anion with the greater negative charge has a larger radius.

6.3 Ionization Enthalpy (\(\Delta_i H\))

Definition: The energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom (\(\mathrm{X}\)) in its ground state. It is expressed in units of \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

\[ \text{First Ionization Enthalpy } (\Delta_i H_1): \quad \mathrm{X(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{X^+(g)} + \mathrm{e^-} \] \[ \text{Second Ionization Enthalpy } (\Delta_i H_2): \quad \mathrm{X^+(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{X^{2+}(g)} + \mathrm{e^-} \]
  • Always Positive: Energy is always required to remove electrons from an atom, so ionization enthalpies are always positive (\(\Delta_i H > 0\)).
  • Successive Ionization Enthalpies: It is more difficult to remove an electron from a positively charged ion than from a neutral atom. Therefore: \[ \Delta_i H_1 < \Delta_i H_2 < \Delta_i H_3 < \dots \] A massive jump in ionization enthalpy occurs when an electron is removed from a stable, completely filled core noble gas configuration.
  • Maxima and Minima: In a plot of \(\Delta_i H_1\) vs. atomic number \(Z\), maxima occur at the noble gases (closed electron shells) and minima occur at the alkali metals (single \(ns^1\) electron preceded by a noble gas core, correlating with their high reactivity).

Shielding (Screening) Effect and Periodic Trends in \(\Delta_i H\):

The outer valence electron is shielded (screened) from the nucleus by intervening inner-core electrons, so the effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\)) experienced by a valence electron is less than the actual nuclear charge (\(Z\)).

  • Across a Period: Generally Increases. Successive electrons are added to the same principal quantum level (\(n\)), so shielding does not increase enough to compensate for the increasing nuclear charge.
  • Down a Group: Decreases. The outermost electron is farther from the nucleus and increased shielding by inner-shell electrons outweighs the increasing nuclear charge.
    Group 1 values (\(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)): \(\mathrm{Li}\,(520) > \mathrm{Na}\,(496) > \mathrm{K}\,(419) > \mathrm{Rb}\,(403) > \mathrm{Cs}\,(374)\).
Crucial NEET Exceptions in First Ionization Enthalpy (\(\Delta_i H_1\)):
  1. Period 2 Order (\(\mathrm{Be > B}\) and \(\mathrm{N > O}\)): \[ \mathrm{Li}\,(520) < \mathbf{\mathrm{B}\,(801) < \mathrm{Be}\,(899)} < \mathrm{C}\,(1086) < \mathbf{\mathrm{O}\,(1314) < \mathrm{N}\,(1402)} < \mathrm{F}\,(1681) < \mathrm{Ne}\,(2080)\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]
    • Why \(\Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{Be}) > \Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{B})\)? (Penetration Effect): Within the same principal quantum level (\(n=2\)), an \(s\text{-electron}\) penetrates closer to the nucleus than a \(p\text{-electron}\) (\(s > p > d > f\)). In \(\mathrm{Be}\,(1s^2 2s^2)\), a tightly held \(2s\text{-electron}\) is removed, whereas in \(\mathrm{B}\,(1s^2 2s^2 2p^1)\), a more shielded \(2p\text{-electron}\) is removed. (Similarly in Period 3, \(\Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{Mg}) > \Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{Al})\)).
    • Why \(\Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{N}) > \Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{O})\)? (Half-Filled Stability & Pairing Repulsion): In \(\mathrm{N}\,(1s^2 2s^2 2p_x^1 2p_y^1 2p_z^1)\), the three \(2p\text{-electrons}\) reside in different atomic orbitals (Hund's rule, stable half-filled subshell). In \(\mathrm{O}\,(1s^2 2s^2 2p_x^2 2p_y^1 2p_z^1)\), two of the four \(2p\text{-electrons}\) occupy the same \(2p\text{-orbital}\), causing increased electron–electron repulsion, making it easier to remove the fourth \(2p\text{-electron}\). (Similarly in Period 3, \(\Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{P}) > \Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{S})\)).
  2. Group 13 Ionization Enthalpy Anomaly:
    Values (\(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)): \(\mathrm{B}\,(801),\; \mathrm{Al}\,(577),\; \mathrm{Ga}\,(579),\; \mathrm{In}\,(558),\; \mathrm{Tl}\,(589)\).
    Notice the order: \(\mathrm{B > Tl > Ga > Al > In}\).
    Reason: \(\mathrm{Ga}\) follows the \(3d^{10}\) series and \(\mathrm{Tl}\) follows both the \(4f^{14}\) and \(5d^{10}\) series. Because inner \(d\text{-}\) and \(f\text{-electrons}\) shield the nuclear charge very poorly, the outer valence electrons in \(\mathrm{Ga}\) and \(\mathrm{Tl}\) experience a much higher effective nuclear charge (\(Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\)), causing \(\Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{Ga}) > \Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{Al})\) and \(\Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{Tl}) > \Delta_i H_1(\mathrm{In})\).
Notice the two characteristic dips in Period 2: at Boron (2p penetration effect vs Be 2s) and at Oxygen (2p electron-pairing repulsion vs half-filled N 2p3).

6.4 Electron Gain Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{eg}H\))

Definition: When an electron is added to a neutral isolated gaseous atom (\(\mathrm{X}\)) to convert it into a negative ion, the enthalpy change accompanying the process is defined as the Electron Gain Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{eg}H\)):

\[ \mathrm{X(g)} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{X^-(g)} \quad (\Delta H = \Delta_{eg}H) \]
  • Exothermic or Endothermic:
    • For most elements, energy is released when an electron is added (\(\Delta_{eg}H\) is negative). Group 17 (halogens) have very high negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\) because they attain stable noble gas configurations by picking up one electron.
    • Noble gases (Group 18) have large positive \(\Delta_{eg}H\) because the added electron must enter the next higher principal quantum level, leading to a very unstable electronic configuration.
    • Second Electron Gain Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{eg}H_2\)): Adding a second electron to a negatively charged ion (e.g., \(\mathrm{O^-(g) + e^- \rightarrow O^{2-}(g)}\)) is always endothermic (positive) because energy must be supplied to overcome strong electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged anion and the incoming electron.
  • Relation to Electron Affinity (\(A_e\)): In many texts, the negative of the enthalpy change at absolute zero is called Electron Affinity (\(A_e\)). At any temperature \(T\), taking heat capacities into account: \[ \Delta_{eg}H = -A_e - \frac{5}{2}RT \]
Crucial NEET Exception in Electron Gain Enthalpy (\(\mathrm{Cl}\) vs \(\mathrm{F}\), and \(\mathrm{S}\) vs \(\mathrm{O}\)):

Generally, \(\Delta_{eg}H\) becomes more negative across a period and less negative down a group. However, the electron gain enthalpy of \(\mathrm{O}\) or \(\mathrm{F}\) is LESS negative than that of the succeeding element (\(\mathrm{S}\) or \(\mathrm{Cl}\)):

  • Group 17 Magnitude of Negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{Cl}\;(-349) > \mathrm{F}\;(-328)} > \mathrm{Br}\;(-325) > \mathrm{I}\;(-295) > \mathrm{At}\;(-270)\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).
  • Group 16 Magnitude of Negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\): \(\mathrm{S}\;(-200) > \mathrm{Se}\;(-195) > \mathrm{Te}\;(-190) > \mathrm{Po}\;(-174) > \mathbf{\mathrm{O}\;(-141)}\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). (Note: Oxygen has the least negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\) in Group 16!).

Reason: When an electron is added to \(\mathrm{O}\) or \(\mathrm{F}\), it enters the smaller \(n = 2\) quantum level (\(2p\) subshell) and suffers significant inter-electronic repulsion from the other electrons already crowded in this small volume. In \(\mathrm{S}\) or \(\mathrm{Cl}\) (\(n = 3\) quantum level, \(3p\) subshell), the added electron occupies a larger region of space and electron–electron repulsion is much less.

6.5 Electronegativity

Definition: A qualitative measure of the ability of an atom in a chemical compound to attract shared electrons to itself.

  • Unlike ionization enthalpy and electron gain enthalpy (which are properties of isolated gaseous atoms), electronegativity is not a directly measurable quantity and is not constant for a given element—it varies depending on the element to which it is bound (and its oxidation state/hybridization).
  • Scales of Electronegativity: Pauling scale, Mulliken-Jaffe scale, and Allred-Rochow scale. On the widely used Pauling scale (1922), Fluorine (\(\mathrm{F}\)) is arbitrarily assigned a value of \(4.0\) (highest ability to attract electrons).
  • Periodic Trends: Increases across a period from left to right (as atomic radius decreases) and decreases down a group (as atomic radius increases). Directly proportional to non-metallic character and inversely proportional to metallic character.

7.2 Anomalous Properties of Second Period Elements & Diagonal Relationship

The first element of each of Groups 1 (\(\mathrm{Li}\)), 2 (\(\mathrm{Be}\)), and 13–17 (\(\mathrm{B}\) to \(\mathrm{F}\)) differs significantly in chemical behaviour from the subsequent members of its respective group. For instance, \(\mathrm{Li}\) and \(\mathrm{Be}\) form compounds with pronounced covalent character, whereas other members form predominantly ionic compounds.

Diagonal Relationship: The behaviour of \(\mathrm{Li}\) and \(\mathrm{Be}\) is more similar to the second element of the following group—namely, \(\mathrm{Li}\) resembles \(\mathrm{Mg}\), \(\mathrm{Be}\) resembles \(\mathrm{Al}\), and \(\mathrm{B}\) resembles \(\mathrm{Si}\). Metallic radii (\(\mathrm{pm}\)): \(\mathrm{Li}\,(152), \mathrm{Be}\,(111), \mathrm{B}\,(88)\) vs. \(\mathrm{Na}\,(186), \mathrm{Mg}\,(160), \mathrm{Al}\,(143)\). Ionic radii (\(\mathrm{pm}\)): \(\mathrm{Li^+}\,(76), \mathrm{Be^{2+}}\,(31)\) vs. \(\mathrm{Na^+}\,(102), \mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\,(72)\).

Diagonal similarity in charge/radius ratio (polarizing power) and electronegativity between Period 2 and Period 3 elements.

Four Reasons for the Anomalous Behaviour of Second Period Elements:

  1. Small atomic and ionic size.
  2. Large charge/radius ratio (high polarizing power).
  3. High electronegativity.
  4. Absence of \(d\text{-orbitals}\) (Maximum Covalency = 4): The first member of a group has only four valence orbitals (\(2s\) and three \(2p\)) available for bonding, whereas the second member (\(n=3\)) has nine valence orbitals (\(3s\), three \(3p\), and five \(3d\)).
    • Consequently, the maximum covalency of second-period elements is strictly 4 (e.g., Boron can only form \([\mathrm{BF_4}]^-\)), whereas third-period elements can expand their valence shell to accommodate more than four pairs of electrons (e.g., Aluminium forms \([\mathrm{AlF_6}]^{3-}\), covalency = 6).
  5. Strong \(p_\pi\text{–}p_\pi\) Multiple Bonding: First members of \(p\text{-block}\) groups display a much greater ability to form \(p_\pi\text{–}p_\pi\) multiple bonds to themselves (e.g., \(\mathrm{C=C, C\equiv C, N=N, N\equiv N}\)) and to other second-period elements (e.g., \(\mathrm{C=O, C=N, C\equiv N, N=O}\)) compared to heavier members of the same group.

7.3 Periodic Trends, Chemical Reactivity, and Nature of Oxides

  • Reactivity Across a Period: Ionization enthalpy is lowest at the extreme left (alkali metals \(\rightarrow\) easy loss of electron \(\rightarrow\) strong reducing agents) and electron gain enthalpy is most negative at the extreme right before noble gases (halogens \(\rightarrow\) easy gain of electron \(\rightarrow\) strong oxidizing agents). Thus, chemical reactivity is highest at the two extremes of a period and lowest in the centre.
  • Reactivity Down a Group (Main Group Elements):
    • Group 1 (Metals): Reactivity increases down the group (\(\mathrm{Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs}\)) because ionization enthalpy decreases down the group, making electron loss progressively easier.
    • Group 17 (Non-metals): Reactivity decreases down the group (\(\mathrm{F > Cl > Br > I}\)) because tendency to accept electrons (oxidizing power / electrode potential) decreases down the group.
    • Note on Transition Metals: In transition elements, atomic radius changes are much smaller across a period (and even smaller in \(4f\) inner-transition metals), ionization enthalpies are intermediate between \(s\text{-}\) and \(p\text{-blocks}\), and down a group a reverse trend in metallic reactivity is observed.
  • Acid–Base Nature of Normal Oxides Across a Period:
    • Extreme Left Oxides: Strongly Basic (e.g., \(\mathrm{Na_2O}\)). Reacts with water to form a strong base: \[ \mathrm{Na_2O + H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH} \]
    • Extreme Right Oxides: Strongly Acidic (e.g., \(\mathrm{Cl_2O_7}\)). Reacts with water to form a strong acid: \[ \mathrm{Cl_2O_7 + H_2O \rightarrow 2HClO_4} \]
    • Centre of Period Oxides:
      • Amphoteric Oxides (behave as acidic with bases and as basic with acids): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{Al_2O_3,\; As_2O_3}}\) (also \(\mathrm{BeO, ZnO, Ga_2O_3}\)).
      • Neutral Oxides (have neither acidic nor basic properties): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{CO,\; NO,\; N_2O}}\).

8. Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: IUPAC Systematic Nomenclature

Problem: What would be the IUPAC systematic name and symbol for the element with atomic number \(120\)?

Solution:

The digits of atomic number \(120\) are \(1\), \(2\), and \(0\). Their corresponding IUPAC numerical roots are:
\(1 = \text{un}\;(\mathrm{u}), \quad 2 = \text{bi}\;(\mathrm{b}), \quad 0 = \text{nil}\;(\mathrm{n})\).
Putting the roots together in order and adding "-ium" gives the name unbinilium and the three-letter symbol \(\mathrm{Ubn}\).

Worked Example 2: Quantum Numbers and Period Capacity

Problem: How would you justify the presence of \(18\) elements in the 5th period and \(32\) elements in the 6th period of the Periodic Table on the basis of quantum numbers?

Solution:

  • For the 5th Period (\(n = 5\)): The available subshells filled in order of increasing energy (\((n+l)\) rule) between the start and end of the 5th period are \(5s < 4d < 5p\). The total number of orbitals available is \(1\,(5s) + 5\,(4d) + 3\,(5p) = 9\text{ orbitals}\). Since each orbital accommodates at most \(2\) electrons, the maximum number of electrons (and hence elements) is \(2 \times 9 = 18\text{ elements}\).
  • For the 6th Period (\(n = 6\)): The available subshells filled in order of increasing energy are \(6s < 4f < 5d < 6p\). The total number of orbitals available is \(1\,(6s) + 7\,(4f) + 5\,(5d) + 3\,(6p) = 16\text{ orbitals}\). The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated is \(2 \times 16 = 32\text{ elements}\).
Worked Example 3: Predicting Group and Electronic Configuration of Superheavy Elements

Problem: In which family/group would you place the elements with \(Z = 117\) and \(Z = 120\), and what is the ground-state electronic configuration in each case?

Solution:

  • For \(Z = 117\): Element \(Z = 118\) (\(\mathrm{Og}/\mathrm{Uuo}\)) is the noble gas (Group 18) ending the 7th period. Therefore, \(Z = 117\) is one column before Group 18, placing it in the halogen family (Group 17, Period 7). Starting from Radon (\(\mathrm{Rn}, Z = 86\)), its electronic configuration is: \[ [\mathrm{Rn}]\,5f^{14}\,6d^{10}\,7s^2\,7p^5 \]
  • For \(Z = 120\): It has two electrons beyond the 7th-period noble gas (\(Z = 118\), \(\mathrm{Uuo}/\mathrm{Og}\)). Thus, it enters the 8th period (\(8s^2\)) and belongs to Group 2 (alkaline earth metals) with electronic configuration: \[ [\mathrm{Uuo}]\,8s^2 \quad (\text{or } [\mathrm{Og}]\,8s^2) \]
Worked Example 4: Ordering Metallic Character

Problem: Considering their atomic number and position in the periodic table, arrange the following elements in increasing order of metallic character: \(\mathrm{Si, Be, Mg, Na, P}\).

Solution:

Metallic character increases down a group and decreases across a period from left to right.
In Period 3 (left to right): \(\mathrm{Na}\;(\text{Group 1}) > \mathrm{Mg}\;(\text{Group 2}) > \mathrm{Si}\;(\text{Group 14}) > \mathrm{P}\;(\text{Group 15})\).
In Group 2 (down the group): \(\mathrm{Mg}\;(\text{Period 3}) > \mathrm{Be}\;(\text{Period 2})\), while \(\mathrm{Be}\) (Group 2 metal) is more metallic than \(\mathrm{Si}\) (Group 14 metalloid).
Hence, the increasing order of metallic character is: \[ \mathrm{P < Si < Be < Mg < Na} \]

Worked Example 5: Atomic and Ionic Size Comparison

Problem: Which of the following species will have the largest and the smallest size: \(\mathrm{Mg, Mg^{2+}, Al, Al^{3+}}\)?

Solution:

Atomic radii decrease across a period (\(\mathrm{Mg > Al}\)). Cations are smaller than their parent atoms (\(\mathrm{Mg^{2+} < Mg}\) and \(\mathrm{Al^{3+} < Al}\)). Between the isoelectronic cations \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\) (\(Z=12, 10\text{ e}^-\)) and \(\mathrm{Al^{3+}}\) (\(Z=13, 10\text{ e}^-\)), the one with the larger positive nuclear charge has a smaller radius (\(\mathrm{Al^{3+} < Mg^{2+}}\)).
Overall size order: \(\mathrm{Al^{3+} < Mg^{2+} < Al < Mg}\).
Largest species: \(\mathrm{Mg}\); Smallest species: \(\mathrm{Al^{3+}}\).

Worked Example 6: Predicting Ionization Enthalpy of Aluminium

Problem: The first ionization enthalpy (\(\Delta_i H\)) values of the third-period elements \(\mathrm{Na, Mg,}\) and \(\mathrm{Si}\) are respectively \(496, 737,\) and \(786\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Predict whether the first \(\Delta_i H\) value for \(\mathrm{Al}\) will be closer to \(575\) or \(760\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Justify your answer.

Solution:

It will be closer to \(575\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Although \(\mathrm{Al}\,(Z=13, [\mathrm{Ne}]3s^2 3p^1)\) lies between \(\mathrm{Mg}\,(Z=12, [\mathrm{Ne}]3s^2)\) and \(\mathrm{Si}\,(Z=14, [\mathrm{Ne}]3s^2 3p^2)\), the value for \(\mathrm{Al}\) is lower than that of \(\mathrm{Mg}\,(737\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}})\) because the \(3p\text{-electron}\) removed from \(\mathrm{Al}\) is effectively shielded from the nucleus by the completely filled \(3s^2\) electrons and penetrates less than a \(3s\text{-electron}\).

Worked Example 7: Comparing Electron Gain Enthalpies

Problem: Which of the following will have the most negative electron gain enthalpy and which the least negative: \(\mathrm{P, S, Cl, F}\)? Explain your answer.

Solution:

Electron gain enthalpy generally becomes more negative across a period from left to right (\(\mathrm{P < S < Cl}\) in magnitude of negative value) and less negative down a group. However, adding an electron to the compact \(2p\text{-orbital}\) of \(\mathrm{F}\) leads to greater inter-electronic repulsion than adding an electron to the larger \(3p\text{-orbital}\) of \(\mathrm{Cl}\).
Magnitude of negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\): \(\mathrm{Cl}\;(-349) > \mathrm{F}\;(-328) > \mathrm{S}\;(-200) > \mathrm{P}\).
Most negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\): \(\mathrm{Cl}\); Least negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\): \(\mathrm{P}\).

Worked Example 8: Oxidation State vs. Covalency in Coordination Complexes

Problem: Are the oxidation state and covalency of \(\mathrm{Al}\) in \([\mathrm{AlCl(H_2O)_5}]^{2+}\) the same?

Solution:

No. Since \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) is a neutral ligand (\(0\) charge) and \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) has a \(-1\) charge, the oxidation state \(x\) of \(\mathrm{Al}\) satisfies \(x + (-1) + 5(0) = +2 \implies x = \mathbf{+3}\). However, \(\mathrm{Al}\) is bonded to \(1\,\mathrm{Cl^-}\) and \(5\,\mathrm{H_2O}\) ligands, forming \(6\) coordinate/covalent bonds, so its covalency is \(6\).

Worked Example 9: Calculating Ionization Enthalpy of Hydrogen from Ground-State Energy

Problem: The energy of an electron in the ground state of the hydrogen atom is \(-2.18 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J}\). Calculate the ionization enthalpy of atomic hydrogen in terms of \(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Solution:

The energy required to remove an electron from \(1\) hydrogen atom in its ground state (\(n=1\)) to infinity (\(E_\infty = 0\)) is: \[ \Delta E_{\text{atom}} = E_\infty - E_1 = 0 - (-2.18 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J}) = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J\,atom^{-1}} \] For \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of hydrogen atoms (\(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\)): \[ \Delta_i H = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}} = 1.3128 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} = \mathbf{1312.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}} \]

Problem: Predict the formulas of the stable binary compounds formed by the combination of the following pairs of elements:
(a) Silicon and bromine, (b) Aluminium and sulphur, (c) Lithium and oxygen, (d) Magnesium and nitrogen, (e) Phosphorus and fluorine, (f) Element 71 and fluorine.

Solution:

  • (a) Silicon (Group 14, valence 4) & Bromine (Group 17, valence 1): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{SiBr_4}}\)
  • (b) Aluminium (Group 13, valence 3) & Sulphur (Group 16, valence 2): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{Al_2S_3}}\)
  • (c) Lithium (Group 1, valence 1) & Oxygen (Group 16, valence 2): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{Li_2O}}\)
  • (d) Magnesium (Group 2, valence 2) & Nitrogen (Group 15, valence 3): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{Mg_3N_2}}\)
  • (e) Phosphorus (Group 15, valence 3 or 5) & Fluorine (Group 17, valence 1): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{PF_3}}\) or \(\mathbf{\mathrm{PF_5}}\)
  • (f) Element 71 (Lutetium, \(\mathrm{Lu}\), a lanthanoid with common oxidation state/valence \(+3\)) & Fluorine (valence 1): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{LuF_3}}\) (or \(\mathrm{EF_3}\))

  • (B) The \(d\text{-block}\) has 8 columns because a maximum of 8 electrons can occupy all the orbitals in a \(d\text{-subshell}\).

Practice Question 3 [Single-Correct MCQ | Isoelectronic Species & Ionic Radii]

Consider the following isoelectronic species: \(\mathrm{N^{3-}, O^{2-}, F^-, Na^+, Mg^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Al^{3+}}\). Which of the following represents the correct order of their increasing ionic radii?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{N^{3-} < O^{2-} < F^- < Na^+ < Mg^{2+} < Al^{3+}}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{Al^{3+} < Mg^{2+} < Na^+ < F^- < O^{2-} < N^{3-}}\)

  • (D) \(\mathrm{Al^{3+} < Mg^{2+} < Na^+ < N^{3-} < O^{2-} < F^-}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).

  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.

10. Common NEET Pitfalls and Final Revision Checklist

Top 7 Exam Pitfalls to Avoid in NEET UG

  1. Confusing \(\mathrm{F}\) and \(\mathrm{Cl}\) in Electron Gain Enthalpy vs. Electronegativity vs. Oxidizing Power:
    • Electronegativity: \(\mathrm{F}\,(4.0) > \mathrm{Cl}\,(3.0)\)
    • Oxidizing Power: \(\mathrm{F_2 > Cl_2}\)
    • Magnitude of Negative Electron Gain Enthalpy (\(\Delta_{eg}H\)): \(\mathbf{\mathrm{Cl}\;(-349\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}) > \mathrm{F}\;(-328\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}})}\) (due to \(2p\text{–}2p\) inter-electronic repulsion in small \(\mathrm{F}\)).
  2. Oxygen's Position in Group 16 Electron Gain Enthalpy: Oxygen does not merely have a less negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\) than Sulphur; it has the least negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\) in the entire Group 16: \(\mathrm{S}\;(-200) > \mathrm{Se}\;(-195) > \mathrm{Te}\;(-190) > \mathrm{Po}\;(-174) > \mathbf{\mathrm{O}\;(-141)}\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).
  3. Adjacent Ionization Enthalpy Reversals: Never write a strictly monotonic increase across Period 2 or Period 3. Always swap Group 2 with Group 13 (\(\mathrm{Be > B}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg > Al}\)) and Group 15 with Group 16 (\(\mathrm{N > O}\) and \(\mathrm{P > S}\)) for \(\Delta_i H_1\).
  4. Group 13 \(\Delta_i H_1\) Zig-Zag Order: Memorize the exact order \(\mathbf{\mathrm{B > Tl > Ga > Al > In}}\) (values: \(801 > 589 > 579 > 577 > 558\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  5. Palladium's Ground-State Configuration: Unlike other \(d\text{-block}\) elements with \(ns^{1-2}\), Palladium (\(\mathrm{Pd}, Z=46\)) has zero outer \(s\text{-electrons}\): \([\mathrm{Kr}]\,4d^{10}\,5s^0\).
  6. \(\mathrm{Zn, Cd, Hg}\) Classification: They are \(d\text{-block}\) elements (Group 12), not transition elements, because their \(d\text{-orbitals}\) are completely filled (\((n-1)d^{10} ns^2\)) in the ground state as well as in their common oxidation states.
  7. Covalency vs. Oxidation State of Period 2 vs. Period 3 Elements: Period 2 elements (\(\mathrm{Li}\) to \(\mathrm{F}\)) can never exceed a covalency of \(4\) due to the absence of \(2d\) orbitals.

Concise Final Recap

  • Mendeleev's Periodic Law: Properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic weights. (Predicted Eka-aluminium = Gallium, Eka-silicon = Germanium).
  • Modern Periodic Law (Moseley, 1913): Physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers (\(Z\)) (\(\sqrt{\nu} \propto Z\)).
  • Long Form Periodic Table: \(7\) horizontal periods (indicating highest principal quantum number \(n\); sizes: \(2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32\)) and \(18\) vertical groups.
  • Four Blocks:
    • \(s\text{-block}\) (Groups 1–2): \(ns^{1-2}\)
    • \(p\text{-block}\) (Groups 13–18): \(ns^2 np^{1-6}\) (\(s\) and \(p\) together = Representative / Main Group elements)
    • \(d\text{-block}\) (Groups 3–12): \((n-1)d^{1-10} ns^{0-2}\) (Transition elements, except \(\mathrm{Zn, Cd, Hg}\))
    • \(f\text{-block}\) (Bottom two rows, Group 3): \((n-2)f^{1-14} (n-1)d^{0-1} ns^2\) (Inner-transition elements: \(4f\) Lanthanoids \(Z=58\text{–}71\); \(5f\) Actinoids \(Z=90\text{–}103\)).
  • Across a Period (Left \(\rightarrow\) Right): Atomic/ionic radius decreases; \(\Delta_i H\), magnitude of negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\), electronegativity, non-metallic character, and oxide acidity increase.
  • Down a Group (Top \(\rightarrow\) Bottom): Atomic/ionic radius, metallic character, and oxide basicity increase; \(\Delta_i H\), magnitude of negative \(\Delta_{eg}H\), and electronegativity decrease.
