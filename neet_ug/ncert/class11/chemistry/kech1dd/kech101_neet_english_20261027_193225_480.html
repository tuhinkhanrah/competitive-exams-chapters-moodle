1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

"Chemistry is the science of molecules and their transformations. It is the science not so much of the one hundred elements but of the infinite variety of molecules that may be built from them." — Roald Hoffmann

Science is a continuing human effort to systematise knowledge for describing and understanding nature. We observe diverse substances and changes in daily life—such as curd formation from milk, vinegar formation from sugarcane juice kept for a prolonged time, and the rusting of iron. The branch of science that studies the preparation, properties, structure, and reactions of material substances is called chemistry. Because material properties and interactions are best understood in terms of basic constituents—atoms and molecules—chemistry is also called the science of atoms and molecules.

After studying this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Appreciate the historical contribution of ancient India in the development of chemistry and understand the role of chemistry in healthcare, industry, and environmental protection.
  • Explain the particle-level characteristics of the three states of matter and classify substances into elements, compounds, and homogeneous or heterogeneous mixtures.
  • Express quantities in scientific notation, apply rules for significant figures and rounding off, and differentiate between precision and accuracy.
  • Define the seven SI base units, use SI prefixes, and convert physical quantities across unit systems using dimensional analysis (unit factor method).
  • State and apply the five fundamental laws of chemical combination and Dalton's atomic theory.
  • Calculate atomic mass, average atomic mass from isotopic abundances, molecular mass, formula mass, moles, and molar mass.
  • Determine mass percentage composition, empirical formulas, and molecular formulas from experimental analysis data.
  • Balance chemical equations, identify limiting reagents, and perform quantitative stoichiometric calculations involving masses, moles, particle counts, gas volumes, and solution concentrations (w/w %, mole fraction, molarity, and molality).

2. Core Concepts & Explanations

2.1 Historical Development of Chemistry & Contributions of Ancient India

Chemistry did not initially emerge as an isolated academic discipline; rather, it developed from the pursuit of two fascinating goals:

  1. Philosopher's stone (Paras): A substance believed to convert baser metals (such as iron and copper) into gold.
  2. Elixir of life: A potion believed to grant immortality.

Between 1300 and 1600 CE, chemistry developed mainly in the forms of Alchemy and Iatrochemistry (medicinal chemistry). Modern chemistry took shape in 18th-century Europe after centuries of alchemical traditions introduced by the Arabs. However, ancient India and China had rich, independent alchemical traditions much earlier.

In ancient India, chemistry was called Rasayan Shastra, Rastantra, Ras Kriya, or Rasvidya, encompassing metallurgy, medicine, cosmetics, glass, and dyes:

  • Archaeological & Harappan Evidence: Excavations at Mohenjodaro (Sindh) and Harappa (Punjab) reveal baked bricks and mass-produced pottery—the earliest chemical process involving mixing, moulding, and firing materials. Glazed pottery remains were found at Mohenjodaro. Construction used gypsum cement containing lime, sand, and traces of \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\). Harappans made faience (a sort of glass used in ornaments), forged lead, silver, gold, and copper, and improved the hardness of copper artefacts by alloying with tin and arsenic. Glass objects coloured with metal oxides were discovered at Maski in South India (1000–900 BCE) and at Hastinapur and Taxila in North India (1000–200 BCE). Northern Black Polished Ware exhibits a golden gloss and mastery over kiln temperatures that remains a chemical mystery.
  • Vedic & Classical Literature: According to the Rigveda, leather tanning and cotton dyeing were practised during 1000–400 BCE. Kautilya's Arthashastra describes salt production from the sea and various liquors. Atharvaveda (1000 BCE) mentions dyestuffs including turmeric, madder, sunflower, orpiment, cochineal, lac, and tinting substances like kampillaka, pattanga, and jatuka.
  • Ayurvedic & Alchemical Treaties:
    • Sushruta Samhita explains the importance of alkalies.
    • Charaka Samhita (the oldest Ayurvedic epic) mentions the preparation of \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\), oxides of \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Sn}\), and \(\mathrm{Zn}\); sulphates of \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Zn}\), and \(\mathrm{Fe}\); and carbonates of \(\mathrm{Pb}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe}\). It also details fermentation ingredients (barks, stems, flowers, leaves, woods, cereals, fruits, sugarcane for making Asavas) and extreme particle-size reduction of metals (nanotechnology) to prepare bhasmas containing metallic nanoparticles for treating ailments.
    • Rasopanishada describes gunpowder mixture preparation. Tamil texts describe fireworks made using sulphur, charcoal, saltpetre (\(\mathrm{KNO_3}\)), mercury, and camphor.
    • Nagarjuna, a great Indian chemist, alchemist, and metallurgist, authored Rasratnakar, dealing with mercury compounds and extraction of gold, silver, tin, and copper.
    • Rsarnavam (around 800 CE) discusses furnaces, ovens, crucibles, and methods to identify metals by flame colour.
    • Chakrapani discovered mercury sulphide (\(\mathrm{HgS}\)) and is credited with inventing soap using mustard oil and alkalies. In the 18th century CE, Indians made soap from Eranda oil, Mahua plant seeds, and calcium carbonate.
    • Varahamihira's Brihat Samhita (6th century CE) describes glutinous wall/roof material made from concentrated plant extracts treated with resins, as well as perfumes, cosmetics, and hair dyes made from indigo, iron powder/black iron/steel, and acidic extracts of sour rice gruel.
    • Gandhayukti describes recipes for scents, mouth perfumes, bath powders, incense, and talcum powder.
    • Paper was known in the 17th century (as noted by Chinese traveller I-tsing), and Taxila excavations show ink used from the 4th century made from chalk, red lead, and minium.
  • Indian Atomic Theory (Paramānu): Acharya Kanad (born 600 BCE, originally named Kashyap) authored the Vaiseshika Sutras and proposed atomic theory ~2500 years before John Dalton (1766–1844). He taught that all substances are aggregated forms of smaller indivisible units called Paramānu (atoms), which are eternal, indestructible, spherical, suprasensible, and in motion in their original state. He stated that varieties of atoms correspond to different classes of substances and combine into pairs (dvyanuka) or triplets (tryanuka) via unseen forces.

2.2 Importance of Chemistry in Modern Science

Chemistry is central to science and national economic growth. Key exam-relevant applications from the syllabus include:

  • Healthcare & Life-Saving Drugs: Isolation from natural sources and synthesis of vital medicines:
    • Cisplatin and Taxol: Effective in cancer therapy.
    • AZT (Azidothymidine): Used for helping AIDS patients.
  • Advanced Materials: Design and synthesis of materials with specific magnetic, electric, and optical properties, such as superconducting ceramics, conducting polymers, and optical fibres.
  • Environment: Synthesis of safer alternatives to environmentally hazardous refrigerants like CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons), which cause stratospheric ozone depletion, and management of greenhouse gases like methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)) and carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)).

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Macroscopic Classification of Matter

Tutorial Diagram 2: Macroscopic classification of matter into mixtures and pure substances.
  • Mixtures: Contain particles of two or more pure substances in any variable ratio. Components retain their chemical identity and can be separated by physical methods (hand-picking, filtration, crystallisation, distillation).
    • Homogeneous mixture: Uniform composition throughout (e.g., sugar solution in water, air, tea).
    • Heterogeneous mixture: Non-uniform composition with distinct phases (e.g., salt and sugar mixture, grains and pulses with dirt/stone pieces).
  • Pure Substances: Have a fixed chemical composition whose constituents cannot be separated by simple physical methods (e.g., copper, silver, gold, water, glucose).
    • Elements: Consist of only one type of atom. They may exist as monoatomic particles (\(\mathrm{Na}\), \(\mathrm{Cu}\), \(\mathrm{Ag}\)) or diatomic/polyatomic molecules (\(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\)).
    • Compounds: Formed when two or more atoms of different elements combine in a fixed and definite ratio (e.g., \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), sugar, glucose \(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\)). The properties of a compound are completely different from those of its constituent elements (for instance, \(\mathrm{H_2}\) is a combustible gas that burns with a pop sound and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) supports combustion, whereas \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) is a liquid used as a fire extinguisher). Constituents of a compound can be separated only by chemical methods.

  • Physical properties: Measured or observed without changing the identity or composition of the substance (e.g., colour, odour, melting point, boiling point, density).

Important Measured Physical Quantities

  • Mass vs. Weight: Mass is the amount of matter present in a substance and is constant everywhere (measured in the laboratory using an analytical balance). Weight is the gravitational force exerted on an object and varies from place to place with changes in gravity. While the SI unit of mass is \(\mathrm{kg}\), chemists commonly use the gram (\(1\,\mathrm{kg} = 1000\,\mathrm{g}\)). (Historically, since 1889, the kilogram reference standard was a platinum-iridium (\(\mathrm{Pt\text{-}Ir}\)) cylinder stored at Sèvres, France, chosen for its high resistance to chemical attack.)
  • Volume: Space occupied by a substance. The SI unit is \(\mathrm{m^3}\). Common laboratory units include \(\mathrm{cm^3}\), \(\mathrm{dm^3}\), \(\mathrm{mL}\), and litre (\(\mathrm{L}\), not an SI unit): \[ 1\,\mathrm{L} = 1000\,\mathrm{mL} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3} \quad \text{and} \quad 1\,\mathrm{mL} = 1\,\mathrm{cm^3} \] In laboratories, liquid volumes are measured using a graduated cylinder, burette, or pipette, while a volumetric flask prepares a known volume of solution.
  • Density: Mass per unit volume (\(\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}\)). SI unit: \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\); laboratory unit: \(\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) or \(\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)).
  • Temperature: Measured on Celsius (°C), Fahrenheit (°F), and Kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\), the SI unit) scales. Water freezes at \(0\,\text{°C}\) (\(32\,\text{°F}\), \(273.15\,\mathrm{K}\)) and boils at \(100\,\text{°C}\) (\(212\,\text{°F}\), \(373.15\,\mathrm{K}\)). Negative temperatures are possible on the Celsius scale, but negative temperatures are not possible on the Kelvin scale.

2.5 Uncertainty in Measurement: Scientific Notation, Significant Figures, Precision & Accuracy

Scientific Notation

Extremely large or small numbers are written in exponential form as \(N \times 10^n\), where \(N\) is the digit term (\(1.000\dots \le N < 10\)) and \(n\) is a positive or negative integer exponent. Moving the decimal point \(k\) places to the left increases the exponent by \(+k\) (e.g., \(232.508 = 2.32508 \times 10^2\)); moving it \(k\) places to the right decreases the exponent by \(-k\) (e.g., \(0.00016 = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}\)).

Precision vs. Accuracy

  • Precision: Closeness of various measurements to one another for the same quantity.
  • Accuracy: Agreement of a particular measurement (or average value) to the true value of the result.

Consider a sample whose true mass is \(2.00\,\mathrm{g}\) measured twice by three students:

Rules for Determining Significant Figures

Significant figures are the meaningful digits in a measured or calculated quantity that are known with certainty plus one last digit that is estimated or uncertain (an uncertainty of \(\pm 1\) in the last digit is understood unless stated otherwise).

  1. All non-zero digits are significant: \(285\,\mathrm{cm}\) has 3 significant figures; \(0.25\,\mathrm{mL}\) has 2 significant figures.
  2. Leading zeros (zeros preceding the first non-zero digit) are NEVER significant: They only locate the decimal point. \(0.03\) has 1 significant figure; \(0.0052\) has 2 significant figures.
  3. Captive zeros (zeros between two non-zero digits) are ALWAYS significant: \(2.005\) has 4 significant figures.
  4. Trailing zeros (zeros at the end or right of a number):
    • Are significant if they are on the right side of the decimal point: \(0.200\,\mathrm{g}\) has 3 significant figures; \(100.0\) has 4 significant figures.
    • Are not significant if there is no decimal point: \(100\) has 1 significant figure, whereas \(100.\) (with a decimal point) has 3 significant figures.
    • In scientific notation \(N \times 10^n\), all digits in \(N\) are significant: \(1 \times 10^2\) (1 sig fig), \(1.0 \times 10^2\) (2 sig figs), \(1.00 \times 10^2\) (3 sig figs).
  5. Exact / Counted numbers have infinite significant figures: e.g., \(2\) balls or \(20\) eggs (\(20 = 20.000000\dots\)).

Rules for Rounding Off & Arithmetic with Significant Figures

  • Rounding off rules:
    1. If the rightmost digit to be removed is greater than 5, increase the preceding digit by 1 (e.g., \(1.386 \rightarrow 1.39\)).
    2. If the rightmost digit to be removed is less than 5, leave the preceding digit unchanged (e.g., \(4.334 \rightarrow 4.33\)).
    3. If the rightmost digit to be removed is 5, leave the preceding digit unchanged if it is even (\(6.25 \rightarrow 6.2\)), and increase it by 1 if it is odd (\(6.35 \rightarrow 6.4\)).
  • Addition and Subtraction: The final result cannot have more digits to the right of the decimal point than the original number with the fewest decimal places (e.g., \(12.11 + 18.0 + 1.012 = 31.122 \rightarrow \text{reported as } 31.1\) because \(18.0\) has only one decimal place).
  • Multiplication and Division: The final result must be reported with no more significant figures than the measurement with the fewest total significant figures (e.g., \(2.5 \times 1.25 = 3.125 \rightarrow \text{reported as } 3.1\) because \(2.5\) has 2 significant figures).

2.6 Laws of Chemical Combination & Dalton's Atomic Theory

  1. Law of Conservation of Mass (Antoine Lavoisier, 1789): In all physical and chemical changes, there is no net change in mass during the process; matter can neither be created nor destroyed.
  2. Law of Definite Proportions / Definite Composition (Joseph Proust): A given compound always contains the exact same proportion of elements by weight, irrespective of its source or method of preparation. For example, both natural and synthetic samples of cupric carbonate (\(\mathrm{CuCO_3}\)) contain \(51.35\%\) copper, \(9.74\%\) carbon, and \(38.91\%\) oxygen by mass.
  3. Law of Multiple Proportions (John Dalton, 1803): If two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in the ratio of small whole numbers. For example, \(2\,\mathrm{g}\) of hydrogen combines with \(16\,\mathrm{g}\) of oxygen to form \(18\,\mathrm{g}\) of water (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) and with \(32\,\mathrm{g}\) of oxygen to form \(34\,\mathrm{g}\) of hydrogen peroxide (\(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\)). The oxygen masses combining with \(2\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) are in the ratio \(16 : 32 = 1 : 2\).
  4. Gay Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes (1808): When gases combine or are produced in a chemical reaction, they do so in a simple ratio by volume, provided all gases are at the same temperature and pressure. For example, \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of hydrogen combines with \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of oxygen to give \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of water vapour (volume ratio \(2 : 1 : 2\)). This is essentially the law of definite proportions by volume.
  5. Avogadro's Law (1811): Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules (\(V \propto n\) at constant \(T, P\)). Avogadro distinguished between atoms and polyatomic (diatomic) molecules (\(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\)), explaining Gay Lussac's law. In 1860, at the Karlsruhe conference in Germany, Stanislao Cannizzaro emphasised the importance of Avogadro's work.

Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808, A New System of Chemical Philosophy)

  • Postulates:
    1. Matter consists of indivisible atoms.
    2. All atoms of a given element have identical properties, including identical mass. Atoms of different elements differ in mass.
    3. Compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine in a fixed ratio.
    4. Chemical reactions involve the reorganisation of atoms; atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
  • Successes & Limitations: Successfully explained the laws of chemical combination by mass (Conservation of Mass, Definite Proportions, Multiple Proportions), but could not explain the laws of gaseous volumes (Gay Lussac's law) and could not explain why atoms combine to form molecules.

2.7 Atomic Mass, Molecular Mass, Formula Mass & Mole Concept

  • Atomic Mass Unit (\(\mathrm{amu}\) or unified mass \(\mathrm{u}\)): Agreed upon in 1961 using the carbon-12 isotope (\(\mathrm{^{12}C}\)) as the standard, assigned a mass of exactly \(12\,\mathrm{u}\). One atomic mass unit is defined as a mass exactly equal to one-twelfth (\(\frac{1}{12}\)) of the mass of one \(\mathrm{^{12}C}\) atom: \[ \text{Mass of one } \mathrm{^{12}C}\text{ atom} = 1.992648 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{g} \] \[ 1\,\mathrm{u} = 1\,\mathrm{amu} = \frac{1.992648 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{g}}{12} = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g} \] For a hydrogen atom (mass \(= 1.6736 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g}\)), relative atomic mass \(= \frac{1.6736 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g}}{1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g}} = 1.0078\,\mathrm{u} \approx 1.0080\,\mathrm{u}\). Similarly, the mass of an \(\mathrm{^{16}O}\) atom is \(15.995\,\mathrm{u}\).
  • Average Atomic Mass: Computed from the relative natural abundance of an element's isotopes. For carbon:
    • \(\mathrm{^{12}C}\): \(98.892\%\) abundance, mass \(= 12\,\mathrm{u}\)
    • \(\mathrm{^{13}C}\): \(1.108\%\) abundance, mass \(= 13.00335\,\mathrm{u}\)
    • \(\mathrm{^{14}C}\): \(2 \times 10^{-10}\%\) abundance, mass \(= 14.00317\,\mathrm{u}\)
    \[ \text{Average Atomic Mass of C} = (0.98892)(12\,\mathrm{u}) + (0.01108)(13.00335\,\mathrm{u}) + (2 \times 10^{-12})(14.00317\,\mathrm{u}) = 12.011\,\mathrm{u} \]
  • Molecular Mass: Sum of the average atomic masses of the elements present in a discrete molecule (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_4} = 12.011 + 4(1.008) = 16.043\,\mathrm{u}\); \(\mathrm{H_2O} = 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02\,\mathrm{u}\)).
  • Formula Mass: Used for ionic solids (like \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)) that do not exist as discrete molecules in the solid state, where each \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) ion is octahedrally surrounded by six \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) ions and vice-versa in a 3D crystal lattice. For \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), formula mass \(= 23.0\,\mathrm{u} + 35.5\,\mathrm{u} = 58.5\,\mathrm{u}\).
  • The Mole & Avogadro Constant (\(N_{\mathrm{A}}\)): One mole is the amount of substance containing exactly \(6.02214076 \times 10^{23}\) elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, or formula units). In calculations, \(N_{\mathrm{A}} \approx 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\).
  • Molar Mass: The mass of one mole of a substance expressed in grams (\(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)). It is numerically equal to the atomic/molecular/formula mass in \(\mathrm{u}\). Note that while \(\mathrm{u}\) is the mass of a single entity, \(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\) is the mass of \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\) entities.

3. Key Equations & Important Results

1. Temperature Conversions

\[ \text{°F} = \frac{9}{5}(\text{°C}) + 32 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathrm{K} = \text{°C} + 273.15 \]

2. Average Atomic Mass

\[ M_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\sum (\text{Isotopic Mass}_i \times \text{\% Abundance}_i)}{100} = \sum (\text{Isotopic Mass}_i \times \text{Fractional Abundance}_i) \]

3. Fundamental Mole Interconversions

\[ n = \frac{\text{Given Mass } (w)}{\text{Molar Mass } (M)} = \frac{\text{Number of Entities } (N)}{\text{Avogadro Constant } (N_{\mathrm{A}})} = \frac{\text{Volume of Gas at STP in Litres } (V_{\text{STP}})}{\text{Molar Volume at STP}} \]

Note on Molar Volume of Ideal Gas at STP: At standard temperature and pressure (\(273.15\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{bar}\) pressure), the molar volume of an ideal gas is \(22.7\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\) (as used in modern IUPAC/NCERT combustion stoichiometry: \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\,\mathrm{CH_4} = 22.7\,\mathrm{L}\)). If historical \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) STP is assumed, \(22.4\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\) is used.

4. Mass Percentage & Empirical/Molecular Formula

\[ \text{Mass \% of an element} = \frac{\text{Mass of that element in 1 mole of compound}}{\text{Molar mass of the compound}} \times 100 \] \[ \text{Molecular Formula} = n \times (\text{Empirical Formula}) \quad \text{where} \quad n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}} \]

5. Solution Concentration Terms

  • Mass by Mass Percentage (\(\mathrm{w/w\,\%}\)): \[ \text{Mass per cent} = \frac{\text{Mass of solute } (w_{\text{solute}})}{\text{Mass of solution } (w_{\text{solute}} + w_{\text{solvent}})} \times 100 \]
  • Parts Per Million (\(\mathrm{ppm}\) by mass): \[ \mathrm{ppm} = \frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}} \times 10^6 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 1\,\mathrm{ppm} = 10^{-4}\,\text{mass \%} \]
  • Mole Fraction (\(\chi\)): For a binary solution of component A (\(n_{\mathrm{A}}\) moles) and component B (\(n_{\mathrm{B}}\) moles): \[ \chi_{\mathrm{A}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{A}}}{n_{\mathrm{A}} + n_{\mathrm{B}}}, \qquad \chi_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{B}}}{n_{\mathrm{A}} + n_{\mathrm{B}}}, \qquad \chi_{\mathrm{A}} + \chi_{\mathrm{B}} = 1 \]
  • Molarity (\(\mathrm{M}\), in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)): Depends on temperature (since volume changes with temperature). \[ \text{Molarity (M)} = \frac{\text{Number of moles of solute } (n_{\text{solute}})}{\text{Volume of solution in litres } (V_{\mathrm{L}})} = \frac{w_{\text{solute}} \times 1000}{M_{\text{solute}} \times V_{\mathrm{mL}}} \]
  • Dilution Equation & Molarity from Density: \[ M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2 \quad (\text{moles of solute remain constant upon dilution}) \] \[ \mathrm{M} = \frac{(\text{w/w \%}) \times d_{\text{solution}} (\text{in }\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}) \times 10}{M_{\text{solute}} (\text{in }\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}})} \]
  • Molality (\(\mathrm{m}\), in \(\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\)): Independent of temperature (since mass is unaffected by temperature). \[ \text{Molality (m)} = \frac{\text{Number of moles of solute } (n_{\text{solute}})}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg } (w_{\text{solvent, kg}})} = \frac{w_{\text{solute}} \times 1000}{M_{\text{solute}} \times w_{\text{solvent, g}}} \]
  • Relation between Molarity (\(\mathrm{M}\)), Molality (\(\mathrm{m}\)), and Density (\(d\) in \(\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\)): \[ \mathrm{m} = \frac{1000 \times \mathrm{M}}{1000\,d - \mathrm{M} \times M_{\text{solute}}} \]

4. Step-by-Step Problem-Solving Procedures

Tutorial Diagram 6: Stoichiometric interconversion map linking mass, moles, and number of particles.

Procedure A: Determining Empirical and Molecular Formulas

  1. Step 1 (Mass % to Grams): Assume \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of the compound so that the mass percentage of each element equals its mass in grams.
  2. Step 2 (Grams to Moles): Divide the mass of each element by its respective atomic mass to get the relative number of moles.
  3. Step 3 (Simplest Mole Ratio): Divide all mole values obtained in Step 2 by the smallest mole value among them.
  4. Step 4 (Whole Number Ratio): If the ratios are not whole numbers (e.g., \(1.5\) or \(1.33\)), multiply all ratios by the smallest suitable integer (\(2\) or \(3\)) to obtain the simplest whole-number ratio and write the Empirical Formula.
  5. Step 5 (Molecular Formula): Calculate the integer multiplier \(n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}}\) and multiply all subscripts in the empirical formula by \(n\).

Procedure B: Balancing Chemical Equations & Finding the Limiting Reagent

  • Balancing Equations (Trial and Error / Inspection Method): Write correct chemical formulas of reactants and products (never alter subscripts inside a chemical formula to balance an equation!). Balance atoms appearing in the fewest formulas first (e.g., C and H in hydrocarbon combustion) and leave elemental diatomic gases (like \(\mathrm{O_2}\)) for the final step. For example, in the combustion of propane (\(\mathrm{C_3H_8}\)): \[ \mathrm{C_3H_8(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)} \quad \text{(Unbalanced)} \] Balance C (\(3\,\mathrm{CO_2}\)), balance H (\(4\,\mathrm{H_2O}\)), then count O on the right (\(3 \times 2 + 4 \times 1 = 10\,\text{O atoms} = 5\,\mathrm{O_2}\)): \[ \mathrm{C_3H_8(g)} + 5\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow 3\mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 4\mathrm{H_2O(l)} \quad \text{(Balanced)} \]
  • Quantitative Meaning of a Balanced Equation: For methane combustion: \[ \mathrm{CH_4(g)} + 2\mathrm{O_2(g)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO_2(g)} + 2\mathrm{H_2O(g)} \]
    • \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)}\) reacts with \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2(g)}\) to give \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)}\) and \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O(g)}\).
    • \(1\,\text{molecule}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)}\) reacts with \(2\,\text{molecules}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2(g)}\) to give \(1\,\text{molecule}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)}\) and \(2\,\text{molecules}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O(g)}\).
    • At STP (\(1\,\mathrm{bar}, 273.15\,\mathrm{K}\)), \(22.7\,\mathrm{L}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)}\) reacts with \(45.4\,\mathrm{L}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2(g)}\) to give \(22.7\,\mathrm{L}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)}\) and \(45.4\,\mathrm{L}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O(g)}\).
    • \(16\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)}\) reacts with \(2 \times 32 = 64\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2(g)}\) to give \(44\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)}\) and \(2 \times 18 = 36\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O(g)}\).
  • Shortcut to Identify the Limiting Reagent: For a balanced reaction \(a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightarrow c\mathrm{C} + d\mathrm{D}\):
    1. Calculate the initial moles of each reactant: \(n_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{B}}\).
    2. Divide the initial moles of each reactant by its stoichiometric coefficient: compare \(\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{A}}}{a}\right)\) and \(\left(\frac{n_{\mathrm{B}}}{b}\right)\).
    3. The reactant with the smallest ratio \(\left(\frac{\text{Initial Moles}}{\text{Stoichiometric Coefficient}}\right)\) is consumed first and is the Limiting Reagent (LR). All product yields and reacted amounts of excess reagents must be calculated strictly from the initial moles of the limiting reagent.

Worked Example 1: Dimensional Analysis (Unit Factor Method) & Pressure Conversion

Problem: (a) A jug contains \(2\,\mathrm{L}\) of milk. Calculate the volume of the milk in \(\mathrm{m^3}\). (b) Pressure is determined as force per unit area of the surface (\(1\,\mathrm{Pa} = 1\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}} = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\)). If the mass of air at sea level is \(1034\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-2}}\), calculate the pressure in pascals (take \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

Solution:

(a) We know \(1\,\mathrm{L} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{m} = 100\,\mathrm{cm}\). Cubing the unit factor \(\frac{1\,\mathrm{m}}{100\,\mathrm{cm}} = 1\):

\[ \left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{m}}{100\,\mathrm{cm}}\right)^3 = \frac{1\,\mathrm{m^3}}{10^6\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 1 \] \[ \text{Volume} = 2\,\mathrm{L} = 2 \times 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3} \times \frac{1\,\mathrm{m^3}}{10^6\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 2 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3} \]

(b) Pressure \(P = \frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Area}} = \frac{\text{Mass} \times g}{\text{Area}}\). Using unit factors to convert \(\mathrm{g}\) to \(\mathrm{kg}\) and \(\mathrm{cm^2}\) to \(\mathrm{m^2}\):

\[ P = \frac{1034\,\mathrm{g}}{1\,\mathrm{cm^2}} \times 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \times \frac{1\,\mathrm{kg}}{1000\,\mathrm{g}} \times \left(\frac{100\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{m}}\right)^2 \] \[ P = 1034 \times 9.8 \times 10^{-3} \times 10^4\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}} = 101332\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}} = 1.01332 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa} \]

Worked Example 2: Significant Figures in Multi-Step Arithmetic

Problem: How many significant figures should be present in the answers of the following calculations, and what are the properly rounded results?
(i) \(\frac{0.02856 \times 298.15 \times 0.112}{0.5785}\)   (ii) \(5 \times 5.364\) (where 5 is an exact number)   (iii) \(0.0125 + 0.7864 + 0.0215\)

Solution:

  • (i) Multiplication/Division: Count significant figures in each term: \(0.02856\) has 4, \(298.15\) has 5, \(0.112\) has 3, and \(0.5785\) has 4. The least number of significant figures is 3 (in \(0.112\)). Thus, the answer must have 3 significant figures (exact calculation \(= 1.6485 \rightarrow \text{rounded to } 1.65\)).
  • (ii) Exact Number Multiplication: The integer \(5\) has infinite significant figures, while \(5.364\) has 4 significant figures. Thus, the answer must have 4 significant figures (\(5 \times 5.364 = 26.82\)).
  • (iii) Addition: All three numbers have 4 digits after the decimal point: \(0.0125 + 0.7864 + 0.0215 = 0.8204\). Keeping 4 decimal places gives \(0.8204\), which contains 4 significant figures.

Worked Example 3: Empirical and Molecular Formula from Mass Percentage

Problem: A compound contains \(4.07\%\) hydrogen, \(24.27\%\) carbon, and \(71.65\%\) chlorine by mass. Its molar mass is \(98.96\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). Determine its empirical and molecular formulas.

Solution: In \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of compound, mass of \(\mathrm{H} = 4.07\,\mathrm{g}\), \(\mathrm{C} = 24.27\,\mathrm{g}\), and \(\mathrm{Cl} = 71.65\,\mathrm{g}\).

  • \(\text{Moles of H} = \frac{4.07\,\mathrm{g}}{1.008\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 4.04\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • \(\text{Moles of C} = \frac{24.27\,\mathrm{g}}{12.01\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 2.021\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • \(\text{Moles of Cl} = \frac{71.65\,\mathrm{g}}{35.453\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 2.021\,\mathrm{mol}\)

Dividing by the smallest mole value (\(2.021\)): \(\mathrm{C} : \mathrm{H} : \mathrm{Cl} = \frac{2.021}{2.021} : \frac{4.04}{2.021} : \frac{2.021}{2.021} = 1 : 2 : 1\).

Therefore, the Empirical Formula is \(\mathrm{CH_2Cl}\).

Empirical formula mass \(= 12.01 + 2(1.008) + 35.453 = 49.48\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).

\[ n = \frac{\text{Molar mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}} = \frac{98.96}{49.48} = 2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{Molecular Formula} = 2 \times (\mathrm{CH_2Cl}) = \mathbf{\mathrm{C_2H_4Cl_2}} \]

Worked Example 4: Combustion Analysis of a Hydrocarbon

Problem: A welding fuel gas contains carbon and hydrogen only. Burning a small sample of it in oxygen gives \(3.38\,\mathrm{g}\) carbon dioxide, \(0.690\,\mathrm{g}\) of water, and no other products. A volume of \(10.0\,\mathrm{L}\) (measured at STP, where \(1\,\mathrm{mol} = 22.4\,\mathrm{L}\)) of this welding gas is found to weigh \(11.6\,\mathrm{g}\). Calculate (i) the empirical formula, (ii) molar mass of the gas, and (iii) the molecular formula.

Solution:

(i) Empirical Formula: All C in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and all H in \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) come from the fuel gas:

\[ \text{Moles of C} = \text{Moles of }\mathrm{CO_2} = \frac{3.38\,\mathrm{g}}{44.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 0.0768\,\mathrm{mol} \] \[ \text{Moles of H} = 2 \times (\text{Moles of }\mathrm{H_2O}) = 2 \times \frac{0.690\,\mathrm{g}}{18.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 2 \times 0.0383\,\mathrm{mol} = 0.0767\,\mathrm{mol} \]

Since \(\text{Moles of C} : \text{Moles of H} = 0.0768 : 0.0767 = 1 : 1\), the Empirical Formula is \(\mathrm{CH}\) (Empirical formula mass \(= 12 + 1 = 13\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)).

(ii) Molar Mass: Since \(10.0\,\mathrm{L}\) at STP weighs \(11.6\,\mathrm{g}\), the mass of \(22.4\,\mathrm{L}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{mol}\)) is:

\[ \text{Molar Mass} = \frac{11.6\,\mathrm{g}}{10.0\,\mathrm{L}} \times 22.4\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}} = 25.984\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} \approx \mathbf{26\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} \]

(iii) Molecular Formula: \(n = \frac{26}{13} = 2 \implies \text{Molecular Formula} = \mathbf{\mathrm{C_2H_2}}\) (ethyne/acetylene).

Worked Example 5: Limiting Reagent & Unreacted Excess Reactant

Problem: Dinitrogen and dihydrogen react to produce ammonia according to: \(\mathrm{N_2(g)} + 3\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NH_3(g)}\).
(i) Calculate the mass of ammonia produced if \(2.00 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{g}\) dinitrogen reacts with \(1.00 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{g}\) of dihydrogen.
(ii) Will any of the two reactants remain unreacted? If yes, which one and what would be its mass?

Solution:

\[ n_{\mathrm{N_2}} = \frac{2000\,\mathrm{g}}{28.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 71.43\,\mathrm{mol}, \qquad n_{\mathrm{H_2}} = \frac{1000\,\mathrm{g}}{2.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}} = 500\,\mathrm{mol} \]

Compare stoichiometric ratios \(\left(\frac{\text{Initial moles}}{\text{Coefficient}}\right)\):

\[ \text{For }\mathrm{N_2}: \frac{71.43}{1} = 71.43 \qquad \text{For }\mathrm{H_2}: \frac{500}{3} = 166.67 \]

Since \(71.43 < 166.67\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\) is the limiting reagent and \(\mathrm{H_2}\) is the excess reagent.

  • (i) Mass of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) produced: \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\,\mathrm{N_2}\) produces \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\,\mathrm{NH_3}\). Thus, \(71.43\,\mathrm{mol}\,\mathrm{N_2}\) produces \(2 \times 71.43 = 142.86\,\mathrm{mol}\,\mathrm{NH_3}\). \[ \text{Mass of }\mathrm{NH_3} = 142.86\,\mathrm{mol} \times 17.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = \mathbf{2428.6\,\mathrm{g}} \approx 2.43 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{g} \]
  • (ii) Unreacted excess reactant: Yes, dihydrogen (\(\mathrm{H_2}\)) remains unreacted. \[ \text{Moles of }\mathrm{H_2}\text{ reacted} = 3 \times n_{\mathrm{N_2}} = 3 \times 71.43 = 214.29\,\mathrm{mol} \implies \text{Mass reacted} = 214.29 \times 2.0 = 428.6\,\mathrm{g} \] \[ \text{Mass of unreacted }\mathrm{H_2} = 1000\,\mathrm{g} - 428.6\,\mathrm{g} = \mathbf{571.4\,\mathrm{g}} \]

Worked Example 6: Interconverting Molarity, Molality, and Mass Percentage

Problem: (a) Calculate the concentration of nitric acid (\(\mathrm{HNO_3}\), molar mass \(= 63\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) in moles per litre in a sample which has a density of \(1.41\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\) and a mass per cent of \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) of \(69\%\).
(b) The density of a \(3\,\mathrm{M}\) solution of \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (molar mass \(= 58.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) is \(1.25\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\). Calculate the molality of the solution.

Solution:

(a) Consider \(1\,\mathrm{L}\) (\(1000\,\mathrm{mL}\)) of the nitric acid solution:

\[ \text{Mass of } 1\,\mathrm{L}\text{ solution} = 1000\,\mathrm{mL} \times 1.41\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}} = 1410\,\mathrm{g} \] \[ \text{Mass of }\mathrm{HNO_3}\text{ in } 1\,\mathrm{L}\text{ solution} = \frac{69}{100} \times 1410\,\mathrm{g} = 972.9\,\mathrm{g} \] \[ \text{Molarity (M)} = \frac{972.9\,\mathrm{g}}{63.0\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} \times 1\,\mathrm{L}} = \mathbf{15.44\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}} \]

(b) Consider \(1\,\mathrm{L}\) (\(1000\,\mathrm{mL}\)) of the \(3\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) solution, which contains \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{NaCl}\):

  • \(\text{Mass of }\mathrm{NaCl}\text{ (solute)} = 3\,\mathrm{mol} \times 58.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 175.5\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • \(\text{Mass of } 1\,\mathrm{L}\text{ solution} = 1000\,\mathrm{mL} \times 1.25\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}} = 1250\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • \(\text{Mass of water (solvent)} = 1250\,\mathrm{g} - 175.5\,\mathrm{g} = 1074.5\,\mathrm{g} = 1.0745\,\mathrm{kg}\)
\[ \text{Molality (m)} = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}} = \frac{3\,\mathrm{mol}}{1.0745\,\mathrm{kg}} = \mathbf{2.79\,\mathrm{m}} \]

7. Common Exam Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Top 6 NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid

  1. Confusing \(52\,\mathrm{u}\) with \(52\,\mathrm{g}\): \(52\,\mathrm{u}\) of \(\mathrm{He}\) is a microscopic mass corresponding to just \(\frac{52}{4} = 13\text{ atoms}\), whereas \(52\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{He}\) is a macroscopic mass corresponding to \(13\,\mathrm{mol} = 13 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms}\).
  2. Forgetting Atomicity When Counting Atoms: In \(1\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) or \(\mathrm{O_2}\), the number of molecules is \(\frac{w}{M}N_{\mathrm{A}}\), while the number of atoms is \(\text{Atomicity} \times \frac{w}{M}N_{\mathrm{A}}\).
  3. Using Solution Mass Instead of Solvent Mass in Molality: Molality requires the mass of the solvent in \(\mathrm{kg}\) in the denominator: \(w_{\text{solvent}} = w_{\text{solution}} - w_{\text{solute}}\). Never divide by total solution mass!
  4. Picking the Reactant with Smaller Mass as the Limiting Reagent: Always convert masses to moles and divide by the stoichiometric coefficient \(\left(\frac{n_i}{\nu_i}\right)\) before comparing. A reactant with a larger mass can still be the limiting reagent if its molar mass or stoichiometric coefficient is large.
  5. Miscounting Significant Figures in Decimal vs. Non-Decimal Zeros: \(0.0025\) has 2 sig figs (leading zeros never count), \(500\) has 1 sig fig (trailing zeros without a decimal point do not count), and \(500.0\) has 4 sig figs.
  6. Mixing Up Addition/Subtraction and Multiplication/Division Sig-Fig Rules: Addition/subtraction is governed by the least number of decimal places, whereas multiplication/division is governed by the least total number of significant figures.

8. Concise Final Recap

  • Classification of Matter: Pure substances (elements & compounds, fixed composition) vs. Mixtures (homogeneous & heterogeneous, variable composition, separable by physical methods).
  • SI Base Units & Scales: \(\mathrm{m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd}\). \(1\,\mathrm{L} = 1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\). \(\text{°F} = \frac{9}{5}(\text{°C}) + 32\); \(\mathrm{K} = \text{°C} + 273.15\).
  • Five Laws of Chemical Combination: Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier), Definite Proportions (Proust), Multiple Proportions (Dalton), Gaseous Volumes (Gay Lussac), and Avogadro's Law (\(V \propto n\) at constant \(T, P\)).
  • Atomic Mass & Mole: \(1\,\mathrm{u} = \frac{1}{12}\text{ mass of }\mathrm{^{12}C}\text{ atom} = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g}\). \(1\,\mathrm{mol} = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ entities}\).
  • Concentration Terms: Mass % (\(\mathrm{w/w}\)), Mole fraction (\(\chi_i\)), and Molality (\(\mathrm{m}\), \(\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\)) are temperature-independent; Molarity (\(\mathrm{M}\), \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) is temperature-dependent.
