2. Historical Roots & Modern Scope of Chemistry

Chemistry investigates the composition, structure, properties, and transformations of matter. Long before modern European chemistry emerged in the eighteenth century, ancient Indian traditions—known as Rasayan Shastra, Rastantra, and Rasvidya—developed sophisticated practical knowledge in metallurgy (copper, bronze, iron, and zinc extraction), glazed ceramics, natural dyes, soap-making, glass coloration, and medicinal preparations.

Notably, around 600 BCE, Acharya Kanad proposed in his Vaiseshika Sutras that all matter is composed of eternal, indivisible, sub-microscopic particles called Paramānu, which combine in pairs or triplets to form observable substances. Similarly, the Charaka Samhita described metallic bhasmas prepared by extreme particle-size reduction—an early empirical precursor to modern nanotechnology.

Modern Relevance: Today, chemical principles drive agriculture (fertilisers and crop protection), healthcare (life-saving anticancer agents such as cisplatin and taxol, and antiretroviral drugs such as AZT), advanced materials (superconducting ceramics, conducting polymers, optical fibres), and environmental stewardship (synthesising ozone-safe alternatives to chlorofluorocarbons, CFCs).

3. Nature and Classification of Matter

Matter is anything that possesses mass and occupies space. At the macroscopic level, matter is examined both by its physical state and by its chemical composition.

3.1 Physical States of Matter

  • Solid: Constituent particles are tightly packed in an ordered arrangement with minimal freedom of motion (only vibration about fixed positions). Solids have both a definite shape and a definite volume.
  • Liquid: Particles remain close together but can slide past one another. Liquids have a definite volume but no definite shape, adopting the contour of their container.
  • Gas: Particles are widely separated and move rapidly in all directions. Gases have neither definite shape nor definite volume, expanding to fill the entire container.

These three states are interconvertible by altering temperature and pressure: heating drives Solid → Liquid → Gas, while cooling reverses the sequence.

3.2 Chemical Classification of Matter

  • Pure Substances: Have a fixed, uniform composition throughout and cannot be separated into simpler components by physical methods.
    • Elements: Made of only one kind of atom (existing either as free atoms like \(\mathrm{Na}, \mathrm{Cu}\) or as homonuclear molecules like \(\mathrm{H_2}, \mathrm{O_2}, \mathrm{N_2}\)).
    • Compounds: Formed when atoms of two or more different elements combine in a fixed mass and mole ratio (e.g., \(\mathrm{H_2O}, \mathrm{CO_2}, \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\)). A compound's properties differ markedly from those of its constituent elements (for example, flammable \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and combustion-supporting \(\mathrm{O_2}\) form \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), which extinguishes fire). Components of a compound can only be separated by chemical methods.
  • Mixtures: Contain two or more pure substances in variable proportions; their components retain their individual identities and can be separated by physical techniques (filtration, crystallisation, distillation, hand-picking).
    • Homogeneous Mixtures: Uniform composition throughout (e.g., aqueous sugar solution, clean air).
    • Heterogeneous Mixtures: Non-uniform composition with visibly distinct phases or regions (e.g., salt mixed with sand, pulses with stone pieces).

4. Properties, SI Units & Uncertainty in Measurement

4.1 Physical vs. Chemical Properties

Physical properties (colour, odour, melting point, boiling point, density) are observed or measured without altering a substance's chemical identity. Chemical properties (combustibility, acidity/basicity, reactivity) can only be observed during a chemical change.

4.3 Key Derived Quantities: Mass vs. Weight, Volume, Density, and Temperature

  • Mass vs. Weight: Mass is the amount of matter in a body and remains constant everywhere. Weight is the gravitational force acting on that mass and varies with local gravity.
  • Volume: The SI unit is \(\mathrm{m^3}\), though laboratory chemistry commonly uses \(\mathrm{cm^3}\), \(\mathrm{dm^3}\), and litres (\(\mathrm{L}\)): \[ 1\,\mathrm{L} = 1000\,\mathrm{mL} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3} \]
  • Density: Mass per unit volume, \(\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}\). Its SI unit is \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), often expressed as \(\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) or \(\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\) in laboratories.
  • Temperature Scales: Measured in °C (Celsius), °F (Fahrenheit), or K (Kelvin, SI unit). Negative values are possible on the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales, but never on the Kelvin scale (0 K is absolute zero): \[ ^{\circ}\mathrm{F} = \frac{9}{5}(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) + 32 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathrm{K} = ^{\circ}\mathrm{C} + 273.15 \]

4.4 Scientific Notation, Precision, Accuracy & Significant Figures

Scientific Notation expresses any number as \(N \times 10^n\), where \(1.000\dots \le N < 10\) and \(n\) is an integer exponent. During addition or subtraction, exponents must be equalised first; during multiplication or division, exponents are added or subtracted respectively.

Precision vs. Accuracy: Precision measures how closely repeated measurements agree with each other, whereas accuracy measures how close a measurement (or average) is to the true accepted value.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Visual distinction between precision (clustering) and accuracy (closeness to true centre).

Rules for Counting Significant Figures:

  1. All non-zero digits are significant (e.g., \(285\) has 3).
  2. Leading zeros before the first non-zero digit are not significant (e.g., \(0.0052\) has 2).
  3. Zeros trapped between non-zero digits are significant (e.g., \(2.005\) has 4).
  4. Trailing zeros to the right of a decimal point are significant (e.g., \(0.200\) has 3; \(500.0\) has 4). Trailing zeros in an integer without a decimal point (e.g., \(100\)) are ambiguous and should be written in scientific notation (\(1.0 \times 10^2\) for 2 sig figs).
  5. Exact counted numbers (e.g., 20 atoms, 2 molecules) have infinite significant figures.

Arithmetic & Rounding Rules: In addition/subtraction, round the result to the fewest decimal places among the operands. In multiplication/division, round to the fewest significant figures among the operands. When rounding off a digit equal to 5 (with no non-zero digits after it), leave the preceding digit unchanged if it is even, and increase it by 1 if it is odd.

Dimensional Analysis (Unit Factor Method): Multiply any given quantity by a ratio equal to 1 (a unit factor such as \(\frac{2.54\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{in}}\)) arranged so unwanted units cancel and desired units remain.

5. Laws of Chemical Combination & Dalton's Atomic Theory

Law & Proposer Core Statement Illustrative Example
Law of Conservation of Mass
(Antoine Lavoisier, 1789)		 Matter is neither created nor destroyed in a physical or chemical change; total mass of reactants equals total mass of products. Burning \(12\,\mathrm{g}\) of carbon in \(32\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) yields exactly \(44\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).
Law of Definite Proportions
(Joseph Proust)		 A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements in the exact same proportion by mass, regardless of source or preparation method. Pure \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) from any source always contains \(11.18\%\) H and \(88.82\%\) O by mass.
Law of Multiple Proportions
(John Dalton, 1803)		 When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other bear a simple whole-number ratio. In \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O_2}\), \(2\,\mathrm{g}\) of H combines with \(16\,\mathrm{g}\) and \(32\,\mathrm{g}\) of O, a \(1:2\) ratio.
Gay-Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes
(1808)		 At constant temperature and pressure, gases combine or are produced in simple whole-number ratios by volume. \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) + \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) → \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) vapour (\(2:1:2\)).
Avogadro's Law
(Amedeo Avogadro, 1811)		 Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. Distinguished atoms from polyatomic molecules (\(\mathrm{H_2}, \mathrm{O_2}\)), explaining Gay-Lussac's ratios.
Tutorial Diagram 5: Avogadro's Law explains why 2 volumes of diatomic H₂ and 1 volume of O₂ yield 2 volumes of H₂O vapour.

Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

Dalton proposed that: (1) matter consists of indivisible atoms; (2) all atoms of a given element share identical properties and mass, while atoms of different elements differ in mass; (3) compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in fixed whole-number ratios; and (4) chemical reactions merely reorganise atoms without creating or destroying them. While Dalton's theory explained the mass laws, it could not explain Gay-Lussac's gaseous volume laws or why atoms combine.

6. Atomic, Molecular, Formula Masses & The Mole Concept

6.1 Unified Atomic Mass & Average Atomic Mass

One unified atomic mass unit (\(\mathrm{u}\), formerly \(\mathrm{amu}\)) is defined as exactly \(\frac{1}{12}\) the mass of a single \(^{12}\mathrm{C}\) atom:

Atomic Mass Unit \[ 1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g} = \frac{1}{N_A}\,\mathrm{g} \]

Because most elements occur as mixtures of isotopes, the periodic table reports the average atomic mass, weighted by fractional natural abundance:

Average Atomic Mass \[ M_{\text{avg}} = \sum_{i} f_i M_i = \frac{\sum_{i} (\%\,\text{abundance}_i \times M_i)}{100} \]

6.2 Molecular Mass vs. Formula Mass

Molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a discrete molecule (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_4} = 12.011 + 4 \times 1.008 = 16.043\,\mathrm{u}\)). Ionic compounds such as \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) exist as three-dimensional crystal lattices rather than isolated molecules; therefore, we use formula mass (\(\mathrm{NaCl} = 23.0 + 35.5 = 58.5\,\mathrm{u}\)).

6.3 The Mole & Molar Mass

One mole (\(\mathrm{mol}\)) is the SI amount of substance containing exactly \(N_A = 6.02214076 \times 10^{23}\) elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, or electrons). For NEET calculations, \(N_A \approx 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\) is standard. The mass of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a substance in grams is its molar mass (\(\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)), which is numerically equal to its atomic, molecular, or formula mass in \(\mathrm{u}\).

7. Percentage Composition, Empirical & Molecular Formula

The mass percentage of an element in a compound is:

\[ \text{Mass \% of element} = \frac{\text{Mass of that element in } 1\,\mathrm{mol}\text{ compound}}{\text{Molar mass of compound}} \times 100 \]

An empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound, whereas the molecular formula gives the exact number of each type of atom in one molecule.

Step-by-Step Procedure to Determine Empirical & Molecular Formulas

  1. Assume a \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) sample so mass percentages convert directly into grams.
  2. Divide each element's mass by its atomic mass to obtain moles of each element.
  3. Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value among them to get a relative ratio.
  4. If any ratio is fractional (e.g., \(1.5\) or \(1.33\)), multiply all ratios by the smallest integer (\(2, 3,\dots\)) to obtain whole numbers—this gives the empirical formula.
  5. Compute the integer factor \(n = \frac{\text{Molar mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}}\), then multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by \(n\) to get the molecular formula.

8. Stoichiometry, Limiting Reagent & Solution Concentrations

8.1 Balanced Equations & Limiting Reagent

A balanced chemical equation obeys the conservation of mass: the number of atoms of each element is identical on both sides (never alter chemical subscripts while balancing—adjust only stoichiometric coefficients). When reactants are mixed in non-stoichiometric proportions, the reactant that is completely consumed first is the limiting reagent; it dictates the maximum amount of product formed.

NEET Shortcut to Identify the Limiting Reagent: Divide the given moles of each reactant by its stoichiometric coefficient in the balanced equation. The reactant yielding the smallest value is the limiting reagent.

8.2 Concentration Terms for Solutions

Concentration Term Definition & Formula Temperature Dependence
Mass Per Cent (w/w %) \(\frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}} \times 100\) Independent
Mole Fraction (\(x\)) \(x_A = \frac{n_A}{n_A + n_B},\quad x_A + x_B = 1\) Independent
Molarity (\(M\)) \(\frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution in litres}}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) Dependent (volume changes with \(T\))
Molality (\(m\)) \(\frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}\)) Independent

9. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Dimensional Analysis & Significant Figures

Problem: A hypothetical metal alloy has a density of \(8.45\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\). Express this density in SI units (\(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)), and calculate the mass of a \(2.5\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) block of this alloy to the correct number of significant figures.

Solution:

\[ 8.45\,\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{cm^3}} \times \frac{1\,\mathrm{kg}}{1000\,\mathrm{g}} \times \left(\frac{100\,\mathrm{cm}}{1\,\mathrm{m}}\right)^3 = 8.45 \times 10^{-3} \times 10^6\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} = 8.45 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} \]

Mass of the block: \(m = \text{density} \times \text{volume} = 8.45\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} \times 2.5\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 21.125\,\mathrm{g}\).

Since \(2.5\) has 2 significant figures, rounding \(21.125\,\mathrm{g}\) to 2 significant figures gives \(21\,\mathrm{g}\).

Worked Example 2: Average Isotopic Atomic Mass

Problem: Suppose a hypothetical element \(X\) consists of two naturally occurring isotopes: \(^{63}X\) (atomic mass \(62.93\,\mathrm{u}\), abundance \(70.0\%\)) and \(^{65}X\) (atomic mass \(64.93\,\mathrm{u}\), abundance \(30.0\%\)). Calculate the average atomic mass of \(X\).

Solution:

\[ M_{\text{avg}} = (0.700 \times 62.93\,\mathrm{u}) + (0.300 \times 64.93\,\mathrm{u}) = 44.051\,\mathrm{u} + 19.479\,\mathrm{u} = \mathbf{63.53\,\mathrm{u}} \]
Worked Example 3: Empirical and Molecular Formula

Problem: A hypothetical organic compound contains \(40.0\%\) carbon, \(6.67\%\) hydrogen, and \(53.33\%\) oxygen by mass, and its molar mass is \(180\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). Determine its empirical and molecular formulas.

Solution: In \(100\,\mathrm{g}\) of compound:

  • Moles of C = \(\frac{40.0}{12.0} = 3.33\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • Moles of H = \(\frac{6.67}{1.0} = 6.67\,\mathrm{mol}\)
  • Moles of O = \(\frac{53.33}{16.0} = 3.33\,\mathrm{mol}\)

Dividing by \(3.33\) gives the simplest ratio \(\mathrm{C : H : O} = 1 : 2 : 1\), so the empirical formula is \(\mathrm{CH_2O}\) (empirical mass = \(12 + 2 + 16 = 30\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Factor \(n = \frac{180}{30} = 6\). Therefore, the molecular formula is \(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\).

Worked Example 4: Limiting Reagent & Product Yield

Problem: If \(14\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{N_2(g)}\) is mixed with \(4.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2(g)}\) to form \(\mathrm{NH_3(g)}\), identify the limiting reagent and calculate the mass of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) formed.

Solution: Balanced equation: \(\mathrm{N_2(g)} + 3\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightarrow 2\mathrm{NH_3(g)}\).

Moles of \(\mathrm{N_2} = \frac{14}{28} = 0.50\,\mathrm{mol}\); moles of \(\mathrm{H_2} = \frac{4.0}{2.0} = 2.0\,\mathrm{mol}\).

Ratio of moles to stoichiometric coefficient: \(\frac{0.50}{1} = 0.50\) for \(\mathrm{N_2}\), and \(\frac{2.0}{3} = 0.67\) for \(\mathrm{H_2}\). Since \(0.50 < 0.67\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\) is the limiting reagent.

Moles of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) formed = \(2 \times 0.50 = 1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\), which has a mass of \(1.0 \times 17.0 = \mathbf{17.0\,\mathrm{g}}\).

Worked Example 5: Molarity to Molality Conversion

Problem: An aqueous solution of urea (\(\mathrm{NH_2CONH_2}\), molar mass = \(60\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) is \(2.0\,\mathrm{M}\) and has a density of \(1.12\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\). Calculate its molality.

Solution: Consider \(1\,\mathrm{L}\) (\(1000\,\mathrm{mL}\)) of solution:

  • Moles of urea = \(2.0\,\mathrm{mol}\); mass of urea = \(2.0 \times 60 = 120\,\mathrm{g}\).
  • Mass of \(1\,\mathrm{L}\) solution = \(1000\,\mathrm{mL} \times 1.12\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}} = 1120\,\mathrm{g}\).
  • Mass of water (solvent) = \(1120 - 120 = 1000\,\mathrm{g} = 1.00\,\mathrm{kg}\).

Molality \(m = \frac{2.0\,\mathrm{mol}}{1.00\,\mathrm{kg}} = \mathbf{2.0\,\mathrm{m}}\).

Practice Question 1 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: A chemical compound always has a fixed composition by mass, and its properties differ from those of its constituent elements.
Statement II: The constituent elements of a compound can be separated by simple physical methods such as filtration or distillation.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (C)

Solution: Compounds have a definite composition and unique properties (Statement I is true). However, their constituents are chemically bonded and can only be separated by chemical or electrochemical methods, not physical methods (Statement II is false).

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

What is the value of \(\frac{3.42 \times 1.2}{0.500}\) expressed to the correct number of significant figures?

  • (A) \(8.208\)
  • (B) \(8.21\)
  • (C) \(8.2\)
  • (D) \(8\)

Answer: (C)

Solution: \(\frac{3.42 \times 1.2}{0.500} = 8.208\). In multiplication and division, the final answer must have no more significant figures than the operand with the fewest significant figures. Here, \(1.2\) has 2 significant figures, so \(8.208\) rounds to \(8.2\).

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

At what temperature do the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales record the exact same numerical value?

  • (A) \(-273.15\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)
  • (B) \(-40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)
  • (C) \(+40\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) \(0\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: Setting \(^{\circ}\mathrm{F} = ^{\circ}\mathrm{C} = x\) in \(^{\circ}\mathrm{F} = \frac{9}{5}(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) + 32\) gives \(x = \frac{9}{5}x + 32 \Rightarrow -\frac{4}{5}x = 32 \Rightarrow x = -40\).

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Nitrogen and oxygen form a series of oxides: \(\mathrm{N_2O}\), \(\mathrm{NO}\), \(\mathrm{N_2O_3}\), and \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\). For a fixed mass of \(28\,\mathrm{g}\) of nitrogen, the masses of oxygen combining with it are in the ratio:

  • (A) \(1 : 2 : 3 : 4\)
  • (B) \(1 : 2 : 3 : 5\)
  • (C) \(2 : 3 : 4 : 5\)
  • (D) \(1 : 3 : 4 : 5\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: In \(\mathrm{N_2O}\), \(\mathrm{N_2O_2}\) (\(\mathrm{NO}\) doubled), \(\mathrm{N_2O_3}\), and \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\), \(28\,\mathrm{g}\) of nitrogen combines with \(16\,\mathrm{g}\), \(32\,\mathrm{g}\), \(48\,\mathrm{g}\), and \(80\,\mathrm{g}\) of oxygen, respectively. Dividing by 16 yields \(1 : 2 : 3 : 5\), illustrating the Law of Multiple Proportions.

Practice Question 5 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following samples contains the maximum total number of atoms?

  • (A) \(16\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2(g)}\)
  • (B) \(16\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4(g)}\)
  • (C) \(22\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)}\)
  • (D) \(18\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: Total atoms = \(n \times \text{atomicity} \times N_A\):
(A) \(\frac{16}{32} \times 2 N_A = 1.0 N_A\); (B) \(\frac{16}{16} \times 5 N_A = 5.0 N_A\); (C) \(\frac{22}{44} \times 3 N_A = 1.5 N_A\); (D) \(\frac{18}{18} \times 3 N_A = 3.0 N_A\). Thus, \(16\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) has the most atoms.

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

Naturally occurring boron consists of two isotopes, \(^{10}\mathrm{B}\) (mass \(10.0\,\mathrm{u}\)) and \(^{11}\mathrm{B}\) (mass \(11.0\,\mathrm{u}\)). If the average atomic mass of boron is \(10.8\,\mathrm{u}\), what is the percentage abundance of \(^{11}\mathrm{B}\)?

  • (A) \(20\%\)
  • (B) \(50\%\)
  • (C) \(80\%\)
  • (D) \(90\%\)

Answer: (C)

Solution: Let the fractional abundance of \(^{11}\mathrm{B}\) be \(x\), so that of \(^{10}\mathrm{B}\) is \(1 - x\). Then \(10.8 = 10.0(1 - x) + 11.0x = 10.0 + x \Rightarrow x = 0.80\), or \(80\%\).

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

Complete combustion of \(0.44\,\mathrm{g}\) of a hydrocarbon yields \(1.32\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.72\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). What is the empirical formula of the hydrocarbon?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{CH_2}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{C_2H_5}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{C_3H_8}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{C_3H_6}\)

Answer: (C)

Solution: Moles of \(\mathrm{C} = \text{moles of }\mathrm{CO_2} = \frac{1.32}{44} = 0.03\,\mathrm{mol}\). Moles of \(\mathrm{H} = 2 \times \text{moles of }\mathrm{H_2O} = 2 \times \frac{0.72}{18} = 0.08\,\mathrm{mol}\). The simplest whole-number ratio \(\mathrm{C : H} = 0.03 : 0.08 = 3 : 8\), giving \(\mathrm{C_3H_8}\).

Practice Question 8 Numerical MCQ

When \(20\,\mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\) (molar mass \(100\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) is treated with \(100\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(2.0\,\mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\) according to \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)} + 2\mathrm{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \mathrm{CaCl_2(aq)} + \mathrm{H_2O(l)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\), what volume of \(\mathrm{CO_2(g)}\) is evolved at STP (taking molar volume at STP as \(22.4\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}}\))?

  • (A) \(4.48\,\mathrm{L}\)
  • (B) \(2.24\,\mathrm{L}\)
  • (C) \(1.12\,\mathrm{L}\)
  • (D) \(6.72\,\mathrm{L}\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: Moles of \(\mathrm{CaCO_3} = \frac{20}{100} = 0.20\,\mathrm{mol}\). Moles of \(\mathrm{HCl} = 2.0\,\mathrm{M} \times 0.100\,\mathrm{L} = 0.20\,\mathrm{mol}\). Since \(0.20\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\) requires \(0.40\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\), \(\mathrm{HCl}\) is the limiting reagent. From stoichiometry, \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\) produces \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), so \(0.20\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\) produces \(0.10\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), occupying \(0.10 \times 22.4 = 2.24\,\mathrm{L}\) at STP.

Practice Question 9 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): The molality of an aqueous solution remains constant with a change in temperature, whereas its molarity changes.
Reason (R): Molality depends on the mass of the solvent, while molarity depends on the volume of the solution, and volume varies with temperature.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.

Answer: (A)

Solution: Mass is invariant with temperature, so mass-based concentration terms (molality, mole fraction, mass %) are temperature-independent. Volume expands or contracts with temperature, making molarity temperature-dependent.

Practice Question 10 Numerical MCQ

An aqueous solution of glucose has a mole fraction of glucose equal to \(0.05\). What is the molality of this solution? (Molar mass of water = \(18\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\))

  • (A) \(2.50\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(2.92\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(3.15\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(1.85\,\mathrm{m}\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: Using the mole-fraction-to-molality relation: \[ m = \frac{x_{\text{solute}} \times 1000}{x_{\text{solvent}} \times M_{\text{solvent}}} = \frac{0.05 \times 1000}{0.95 \times 18} = \frac{50}{17.1} \approx 2.92\,\mathrm{m} \]

11. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing Molarity and Molality Denominators: Molarity divides by volume of solution (\(\mathrm{L}\)); molality divides by mass of solvent only (\(\mathrm{kg}\)), not total solution mass.
  • Skipping the Limiting Reagent Check: Whenever amounts of two or more reactants are given, always identify the limiting reagent before calculating product mass or volume.
  • Ignoring Atomicity: \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) contains \(N_A\) molecules, which equals \(2N_A\) oxygen atoms. Read whether a question asks for atoms or molecules.
  • Unit Mismatches in Dilution & Density: In \(M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2\), keep \(V_1\) and \(V_2\) in the same unit. When using density in \(\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}\), convert solution volume to \(\mathrm{mL}\) before multiplying.

Final Quick-Recap Checklist

  • \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\); \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\,\mathrm{g}\).
  • \(1\,\mathrm{L} = 1000\,\mathrm{mL} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\).
  • \(\mathrm{K} = ^{\circ}\mathrm{C} + 273.15\); \(^{\circ}\mathrm{F} = \frac{9}{5}(^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) + 32\).
  • Empirical formula = simplest whole-number atomic ratio; Molecular formula = \(n \times \text{Empirical formula}\).
  • Temperature-independent concentration units: Mass %, Mole Fraction, Molality. Temperature-dependent: Molarity.
