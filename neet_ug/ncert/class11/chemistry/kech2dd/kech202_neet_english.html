  • Write chemical equations for qualitative elemental analysis (Lassaigne's test) and perform stoichiometric calculations for quantitative estimations (Liebig's, Dumas, Kjeldahl's, and Carius methods).

2. Tetravalence of Carbon & Shapes of Organic Molecules

2.1 Catenation and Historical Background

Carbon possesses the unique property of catenation—the ability to form stable covalent bonds with other carbon atoms, creating long chains, branched chains, and rings. It also forms strong covalent bonds with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, phosphorus, and halogens.

Around 1780, Swedish chemist Berzelius proposed the 'vital force theory', claiming that organic compounds could only be produced within living organisms. In 1828, F. Wöhler disproved this notion by synthesising an organic compound, urea, from an inorganic compound, ammonium cyanate, in the laboratory:

\[\mathrm{NH_4CNO} \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} \mathrm{NH_2CONH_2}\]

Ammonium cyanate (Inorganic) \(\longrightarrow\) Urea (Organic)

Subsequent pioneering laboratory syntheses of acetic acid by Kolbe (1845) and methane by Berthelot (1856) conclusively proved that organic compounds can be synthesised from inorganic sources.

2.2 Hybridisation, Bond Length, Bond Enthalpy, and Electronegativity

The tetravalence of carbon and the geometries of organic molecules are governed by the hybridisation of its valence \(2s\) and \(2p\) orbitals:

  • \(sp^3\) Hybridisation (e.g., Methane, \(\mathrm{CH_4}\)): Four \(sp^3\) hybrid orbitals (\(25\%\) \(s\)-character, \(75\%\) \(p\)-character) form four \(\sigma\) bonds directed towards the corners of a regular tetrahedron (bond angle \(109.5^\circ\)).
  • \(sp^2\) Hybridisation (e.g., Ethene, \(\mathrm{C_2H_4}\)): Three \(sp^2\) hybrid orbitals (\(33.3\%\) \(s\)-character) lie in one plane forming a trigonal planar geometry (bond angle \(\approx 120^\circ\)), while one unhybridised \(p\) orbital lies perpendicular to the plane.
  • \(sp\) Hybridisation (e.g., Ethyne, \(\mathrm{C_2H_2}\)): Two \(sp\) hybrid orbitals (\(50\%\) \(s\)-character) orient at \(180^\circ\) in a linear geometry, with two mutually perpendicular unhybridised \(p\) orbitals.

Influence of Percentage \(s\)-Character

Because an \(s\) orbital is spherical and closer to the nucleus than a \(p\) orbital, increasing the \(s\)-character pulls the bonded electron pair closer to the carbon nucleus. Therefore, as \(s\)-character increases (\(sp^3 < sp^2 < sp\)):

  • Bond Length Decreases: \(\mathrm{C}(sp^3)\text{—}\mathrm{C}(sp^3)\ (154\,\mathrm{pm}) > \mathrm{C}(sp^2)\text{=}\mathrm{C}(sp^2)\ (134\,\mathrm{pm}) > \mathrm{C}(sp)\equiv\mathrm{C}(sp)\)
  • Bond Enthalpy (Strength) Increases: \(sp\text{ bonds} > sp^2\text{ bonds} > sp^3\text{ bonds}\)
  • Electronegativity of Carbon Increases: \(\mathrm{C}(sp)\ (50\%\ s) > \mathrm{C}(sp^2)\ (33.3\%\ s) > \mathrm{C}(sp^3)\ (25\%\ s)\)

2.3 Characteristic Features of \(\pi\) Bonds

In a \(\pi\) (pi) bond formation, parallel orientation of two unhybridised \(p\) orbitals on adjacent atoms is essential for proper sideways (lateral) overlap. In ethene (\(\mathrm{H_2C=CH_2}\)), all six atoms lie in the same plane, and the two unhybridised \(p\) orbitals are mutually parallel and perpendicular to the molecular plane.

  • Restricted Rotation: Rotation of one \(\mathrm{CH_2}\) fragment relative to the other disrupts the parallel overlap of the \(p\) orbitals. Hence, rotation about a carbon-carbon double bond (\(\mathrm{C=C}\)) is restricted.
  • Reactive Centres: The electron charge cloud of a \(\pi\) bond is located above and below the plane of the bonding atoms. These loosely held \(\pi\) electrons are easily exposed and available to attacking electrophilic reagents, making multiple bonds the most reactive centres in unsaturated molecules.

3. Structural Representations of Organic Compounds

3.1 Complete, Condensed, and Bond-Line Formulas

  • Complete Structural Formula (Dash Structure): Every two-electron covalent bond is shown explicitly by a dash (single dash \(-\) for single bond, double dash \(=\) for double bond, triple dash \(\equiv\) for triple bond). Lone pairs on heteroatoms (O, N, S, halogens) may or may not be shown.
  • Condensed Structural Formula: Covalent dashes are omitted, and identical groups attached to an atom are indicated by a subscript. For example, octane is written as \(\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_6CH_3}\), ethane as \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_3}\), ethene as \(\mathrm{H_2C=CH_2}\), ethyne as \(\mathrm{HC\equiv CH}\), and methanol as \(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\).
  • Bond-Line Structural Formula: Carbon and hydrogen atoms bonded to carbon are not written explicitly. Carbon-carbon bonds are drawn as zig-zag lines. Line terminals represent methyl (\(-\mathrm{CH_3}\)) groups (unless a functional group is shown), and line junctions represent carbon atoms bonded to the appropriate number of hydrogens needed to satisfy carbon's tetravalency. Heteroatoms (e.g., O, N, Cl, Br) and hydrogens attached to heteroatoms (e.g., \(-\mathrm{OH}\), \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\)) are written explicitly.

3.2 Three-Dimensional (Wedge-and-Dash) Representation and Molecular Models

To perceive the 3-D structure of an organic molecule on 2-D paper, wedge-and-dash conventions are used:

  • Normal line (—): Bond lying in the plane of the paper.
  • Solid wedge (◀): Bond projecting out of the plane of the paper, towards the observer (broad end towards observer).
  • Dashed wedge (⋯⋯): Bond projecting out of the plane of the paper, away from the observer.
Tutorial Diagram 1: Left — Zig-zag bond-line representation of 3-methyloctane. Right — 3-D wedge-and-dash representation of methane.

Molecular Models: Physical devices used to visualise 3-D shapes include:

  1. Framework model: Shows only the bonds connecting the atoms while ignoring atomic sizes.
  2. Ball-and-stick model: Shows both atoms (balls) and bonds (sticks). For \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds (like ethene), springs replace sticks (ball-and-spring model).
  3. Space-filling model: Emphasises the relative size of each atom based on its van der Waals radius and conveys the volume occupied by each atom; bonds are not shown separately.

4. Classification of Organic Compounds

Tutorial Diagram 2: Structural classification of organic compounds.
  • Acyclic (Aliphatic) Compounds: Straight or branched open-chain compounds such as ethane (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_3}\)), isobutane (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH_3}\)), acetaldehyde (\(\mathrm{CH_3CHO}\)), and acetic acid (\(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\)).
  • Alicyclic Compounds: Aliphatic cyclic compounds. They may be homocyclic (only carbon atoms in the ring, e.g., cyclopropane, cyclohexane, cyclohexene) or heterocyclic non-aromatic (containing a heteroatom like O, N, or S in the ring, e.g., tetrahydrofuran, a 5-membered saturated ring containing four \(\mathrm{CH_2}\) units and one \(\mathrm{O}\) atom).
  • Aromatic Compounds:
    • Benzenoid: Contain at least one benzene ring (e.g., benzene, aniline, naphthalene).
    • Non-benzenoid: Aromatic rings without a benzene ring, such as tropone (a 7-membered ring with three conjugated \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds and a \(\mathrm{C=O}\) group).
    • Heterocyclic aromatic: Aromatic rings containing heteroatoms, such as furan (5-membered ring with O), thiophene (5-membered ring with S), and pyridine (6-membered aromatic ring with N).
  • Functional Group & Homologous Series: A functional group is an atom or group of atoms joined to the carbon chain responsible for characteristic chemical properties (e.g., \(-\mathrm{OH}\), \(-\mathrm{CHO}\), \(-\mathrm{COOH}\)). A series of compounds with the same functional group and general molecular formula where successive members differ by a \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) unit (molecular mass \(14\,\mathrm{u}\)) is called a homologous series.

5.2 Unbranched Alkanes and Branched Alkyl Groups

Straight-chain saturated hydrocarbons end with the suffix '-ane': Methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)), Ethane (\(\mathrm{C_2H_6}\)), Propane (\(\mathrm{C_3H_8}\)), Butane (\(\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}\)), Pentane (\(\mathrm{C_5H_{12}}\)), Hexane (\(\mathrm{C_6H_{14}}\)), Heptane (\(\mathrm{C_7H_{16}}\)), Octane (\(\mathrm{C_8H_{18}}\)), Nonane (\(\mathrm{C_9H_{20}}\)), Decane (\(\mathrm{C_{10}H_{22}}\)), Icosane (\(\mathrm{C_{20}H_{42}}\)), and Triacontane (\(\mathrm{C_{30}H_{62}}\)).

Removing one hydrogen atom from an alkane gives an alkyl group (methyl \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\), ethyl \(-\mathrm{CH_2CH_3}\), propyl \(-\mathrm{CH_2CH_2CH_3}\), butyl \(-\mathrm{CH_2CH_2CH_2CH_3}\), decyl \(-\mathrm{CH_2(CH_2)_8CH_3}\)). Important branched alkyl groups are:

  • Isopropyl: \(\mathrm{-CH(CH_3)_2}\)
  • sec-Butyl: \(\mathrm{-CH(CH_3)CH_2CH_3}\)
  • Isobutyl: \(\mathrm{-CH_2CH(CH_3)_2}\)
  • tert-Butyl: \(\mathrm{-C(CH_3)_3}\)
  • Neopentyl: \(\mathrm{-CH_2C(CH_3)_3}\)

5.3 Rules for Naming Branched-Chain Alkanes and Cyclic Hydrocarbons

  1. Longest Chain Rule: Identify the longest continuous carbon chain as the parent chain. If two chains of equal length compete, select the chain with the greater number of side chains (substituents).
  2. Lowest Locant Rule: Number the parent chain from the end that gives the substituted carbons the lowest possible locants (e.g., 2,5,6-trimethyloctane, not 3,4,7-trimethyloctane).
  3. Alphabetical Order of Prefixes: List different alkyl substituents alphabetically before the parent alkane name. Separate numbers by commas and numbers from words by hyphens.
  4. Multiplying Prefixes & Trivial Alkyl Prefixes:
    • Prefixes di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa- are NOT considered when alphabetising (e.g., 3-ethyl-4,4-dimethylheptane: 'e' of ethyl precedes 'm' of methyl).
    • Prefixes iso- and neo- ARE considered part of the fundamental alkyl name for alphabetisation, whereas italicized sec- and tert- are NOT considered (e.g., in 5-sec-butyl-4-isopropyldecane, 'b' of sec-butyl comes before 'i' of isopropyl).
  5. Equivalent Positions: If two different substituents are at equivalent positions from either end, assign the lower number to the one coming first alphabetically (e.g., 3-ethyl-6-methyloctane, not 6-ethyl-3-methyloctane).
  6. Complex (Branched) Substituents: Number the side chain starting with carbon-1 as the carbon directly attached to the parent root chain, and enclose the complex branch name in parentheses, e.g., 5-(2-ethylbutyl)-3,3-dimethyldecane or 5-(2,2-dimethylpropyl)nonane.
  7. Cyclic Compounds: Prefix 'cyclo' to the alkane name. A more branched ring carbon gets the lower number (e.g., 3-ethyl-1,1-dimethylcyclohexane, not 1-ethyl-3,3-dimethylcyclohexane). If substituents have equal locants, number alphabetically (e.g., 1-methyl-3-propylcyclohexane).

5.4 Nomenclature of Compounds Containing Functional Groups

In polyfunctional compounds, select the principal functional group (which determines the main suffix and gets the lowest possible locant) according to the decreasing priority order:

\[\mathrm{-COOH > -SO_3H > -COOR > -COCl > -CONH_2 > -CN > -HC=O > \; >C=O \; > -OH > -NH_2 > \; >C=C< \; > -C\equiv C-}\]

Groups such as \(-\mathrm{R}\), \(-\mathrm{C_6H_5}\) (phenyl), halogens (\(-\mathrm{F, -Cl, -Br, -I}\)), \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) (nitro), and \(-\mathrm{OR}\) (alkoxy) are always treated as prefix substituents. If multiple double or triple bonds occur, drop the terminal '-ne' of the alkane and add 'a' (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-CH=CH_2}\) is buta-1,3-diene). When an alkene and alkyne are both present, the chain is named as alken-yne (e.g., \(\mathrm{CH\equiv C-CH=CH-CH=CH_2}\) is hexa-1,3-dien-5-yne).

5.5 Substituted Benzene Compounds

  • Monosubstituted: Prefix the substituent to benzene (e.g., methylbenzene [toluene], methoxybenzene [anisole], aminobenzene [aniline], nitrobenzene, bromobenzene).
  • Disubstituted: Number positions to give the lowest locants: 1,2- (ortho or o-), 1,3- (meta or m-), and 1,4- (para or p-).
  • Tri- or Higher Substituted: Prefixes o-, m-, p- cannot be used. Follow the lowest locant rule (e.g., 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene). If an accepted trivial base name (like phenol, aniline, anisole, or toluene) is used, the group defining the base name is assigned C-1, and the ring is numbered in the direction that gives the next substituent the lowest locant (e.g., 2-chloro-4-methylanisole, 4-ethyl-2-methylaniline, 3,4-dimethylphenol).

6. Isomerism

Compounds having the same molecular formula but different physical or chemical properties are called isomers.

  1. Structural Isomerism (Constitutional Isomerism): Isomers differ in the connectivity (linking sequence) of atoms:
    • Chain Isomerism: Different carbon skeletons. Example: \(\mathrm{C_5H_{12}}\) exists as pentane (straight chain), isopentane (2-methylbutane), and neopentane (2,2-dimethylpropane).
    • Position Isomerism: Same carbon skeleton and functional group, but different position of the substituent or functional group. Example: \(\mathrm{C_3H_8O}\) gives propan-1-ol (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2OH}\)) and propan-2-ol (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH(OH)CH_3}\)).
    • Functional Group Isomerism: Different functional groups. Example: \(\mathrm{C_3H_6O}\) represents propanone (ketone, \(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3}\)) and propanal (aldehyde, \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CHO}\)).
    • Metamerism: Arises due to different alkyl chains on either side of a divalent/polyvalent functional group. Example: \(\mathrm{C_4H_{10}O}\) represents methoxypropane (\(\mathrm{CH_3OC_3H_7}\)) and ethoxyethane (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5OC_2H_5}\)).
  2. Stereoisomerism: Same constitution and covalent bond sequence, but different relative spatial arrangement of atoms or groups in 3-D space. Subdivided into geometrical isomerism (e.g., cis/trans across \(\mathrm{C=C}\)) and optical isomerism.

7. Fundamental Concepts in Organic Reaction Mechanism

An organic reaction involves an organic molecule (substrate, which supplies carbon to the new bond) reacting with an attacking reagent to form one or more reactive intermediates and finally products (plus byproducts). A step-by-step description of electron movement, energetics of bond cleavage/formation, and reaction kinetics is called the reaction mechanism.

7.2 Nucleophiles and Electrophiles

  • Nucleophile (\(\mathrm{Nu:}\), "nucleus-seeking"): An electron-rich reagent that brings an electron pair to an electron-deficient site (electrophilic centre) of the substrate.
    • Negatively charged nucleophiles: \(\mathrm{HO^-, NC^-, HS^-, C_2H_5O^-}\), carbanions (\(\mathrm{R_3C:-}\)).
    • Neutral nucleophiles (with lone pairs): \(\mathrm{H_2\ddot{O}:, R_3N:, R_2NH, H_2N:^-}\).
  • Electrophile (\(\mathrm{E^+}\), "electron-seeking"): An electron-deficient reagent that takes away an electron pair from an electron-rich site (nucleophilic centre) of the substrate.
    • Positively charged electrophiles: Carbocations (\(\mathrm{\stackrel{+}{C}H_3}\)), nitronium ion (\(\mathrm{\stackrel{+}{N}O_2}\)), \(\mathrm{Cl^+}\), acylium ion (\(\mathrm{CH_3\stackrel{+}{C}=O}\)).
    • Neutral electrophiles: Incomplete octet molecules like \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), or molecules with polar functional groups having an electrophilic carbon (\(\delta^+\)) such as carbonyls (\(\mathrm{>C=O}\)) and alkyl halides (\(\mathrm{R_3C-X}\)).

7.3 Electron Displacement Effects in Covalent Bonds

(A) Inductive Effect (\(I\) Effect) — Permanent \(\sigma\)-Bond Polarisation

When a polar covalent bond (like \(\mathrm{C-Cl}\) in \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-Cl}\)) is present, carbon-1 acquires a partial positive charge (\(\delta^+\)) and induces a smaller positive charge (\(\delta\delta^+\)) on carbon-2. This permanent polarisation of a \(\sigma\) bond caused by the polarisation of an adjacent \(\sigma\) bond is called the inductive effect. It diminishes rapidly with distance and becomes negligible after three carbon-carbon bonds.

  • Electron-Withdrawing Groups (\(-I\) relative to H): Halogens (\(-\mathrm{F, -Cl, -Br, -I}\)), nitro (\(-\mathrm{NO_2}\)), cyano (\(-\mathrm{CN}\)), carboxy (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)), ester (\(-\mathrm{COOR}\)), aryloxy (\(-\mathrm{OAr}\), e.g., \(-\mathrm{OC_6H_5}\)).
  • Electron-Donating Groups (\(+I\) relative to H): Alkyl groups such as methyl (\(-\mathrm{CH_3}\)), ethyl (\(-\mathrm{CH_2CH_3}\)), isopropyl, and tert-butyl.

(B) Resonance and Resonance Effect (\(R\) or \(M\) Effect) — Permanent \(\pi\)-Delocalisation

When a single Lewis structure cannot explain the properties of a molecule, the actual molecule is a resonance hybrid of two or more hypothetical canonical (contributing) structures. For instance, experimental \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond lengths in benzene are all uniform at \(139\,\mathrm{pm}\), intermediate between a \(\mathrm{C-C}\) single bond (\(154\,\mathrm{pm}\)) and a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (\(134\,\mathrm{pm}\)). The difference in energy between the actual resonance hybrid and the most stable canonical structure is the resonance stabilisation energy.

Rules for Evaluating Relative Stability of Resonance Structures

Canonical structures must have the same positions of nuclei and the same number of unpaired electrons. A canonical structure is more stable if it has:

  1. More covalent bonds.
  2. Complete octets on all atoms (duplet for H).
  3. Less separation of opposite charges.
  4. Any negative charge on a more electronegative atom and any positive charge on a more electropositive atom.
  5. Greater dispersal of charge.

Resonance Effect (\(R\) or \(M\)): Polarity produced in a conjugated molecule by the interaction of two \(\pi\) bonds or between a \(\pi\) bond and a lone pair of electrons on an adjacent atom:

  • \(+R\) (Positive Resonance) Effect: Electron transfer is away from the substituent towards the conjugated system, increasing electron density at ortho and para positions (e.g., in aniline).
    \(+R\) Groups: \(-\mathrm{X}\) (halogens), \(-\mathrm{OH}\), \(-\mathrm{OR}\), \(-\mathrm{OCOR}\), \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\), \(-\mathrm{NHR}\), \(-\mathrm{NR_2}\), \(-\mathrm{NHCOR}\).
  • \(-R\) (Negative Resonance) Effect: Electron transfer is towards the substituent away from the conjugated system, creating positive charges (\(\delta^+\)) at ortho and para positions (e.g., in nitrobenzene).
    \(-R\) Groups: \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), \(-\mathrm{CHO}\), \(\mathrm{>C=O}\), \(-\mathrm{CN}\), \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\).

(C) Electromeric Effect (\(E\) Effect) — Temporary Effect

Observed only in organic compounds having a multiple bond in the presence of an attacking reagent. It is the complete transfer of a shared pair of \(\pi\) electrons to one of the bonded atoms on the demand of an attacking reagent. When inductive and electromeric effects act in opposite directions, the electromeric effect predominates.

  • \(+E\) Effect: \(\pi\) electrons transfer to the atom to which the reagent attaches (e.g., addition of \(\mathrm{H^+}\) to \(\mathrm{>C=C<}\)).
  • \(-E\) Effect: \(\pi\) electrons transfer to the atom to which the reagent does not attach (e.g., attack of \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) on a multiple bond).

(D) Hyperconjugation — Permanent \(\sigma\text{–}\pi\) or \(\sigma\text{–}p\) Delocalisation ("No-Bond Resonance")

Hyperconjugation involves the delocalisation of \(\sigma\) electrons of a \(\mathrm{C_{\alpha}-H}\) bond of an alkyl group directly attached to an atom of an unsaturated system (alkenes, alkylarenes) or to an atom with an unshared empty/partially filled \(p\) orbital (carbocations, free radicals). The greater the number of \(\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{C-H}\) bonds attached to the positively charged carbon or double bond, the greater the hyperconjugative stabilisation:

\[\mathrm{(CH_3)_3\stackrel{+}{C}}\ (9\ \alpha\text{-H}) > \mathrm{(CH_3)_2\stackrel{+}{C}H}\ (6\ \alpha\text{-H}) > \mathrm{CH_3\stackrel{+}{C}H_2}\ (3\ \alpha\text{-H}) > \mathrm{\stackrel{+}{C}H_3}\ (0\ \alpha\text{-H})\]

7.4 Types of Organic Reactions

Organic reactions are classified into four fundamental types: (i) Substitution reactions (an atom/group is replaced by another), (ii) Addition reactions (reagent adds across a multiple bond), (iii) Elimination reactions (loss of two atoms/groups to form a multiple bond), and (iv) Rearrangement reactions (reorganisation of the carbon skeleton or functional group position).

8. Methods of Purification of Organic Compounds

After extraction or synthesis, organic compounds are purified based on differences in physical properties. Purity is verified by sharp melting or boiling points, or via chromatographic/spectroscopic methods.

Purification Technique Underlying Principle Key Applications & Textbook Examples
1. Sublimation Direct transition of a solid to vapour on heating without passing through the liquid state. Separating sublimable solids (e.g., camphor, naphthalene, benzoic acid) from non-sublimable impurities (e.g., \(\mathrm{CaSO_4}\)).
2. Crystallisation Difference in solubilities of the compound and impurities in a suitable solvent (sparingly soluble at room temp, appreciably soluble at higher temp). Purifying solid organic compounds. Coloured impurities are removed by adsorption over activated charcoal.
3. Simple Distillation Liquids vaporise at different temperatures when they have a sufficient difference in boiling points, or to separate volatile liquids from non-volatile impurities. Mixture of chloroform (b.p. \(334\,\mathrm{K}\)) and aniline (b.p. \(457\,\mathrm{K}\)).
4. Fractional Distillation Used when the difference in boiling points of two liquids is small. Repeated condensation and vaporisation occur in a fractionating column (each unit is a theoretical plate). Separating different fractions of crude oil in the petroleum industry.
5. Distillation under Reduced Pressure (Vacuum Distillation) A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals external pressure. Lowering external pressure makes high-boiling liquids boil below their decomposition temperature. Separating glycerol from spent-lye in the soap industry.
6. Steam Distillation Used for substances that are steam volatile and immiscible with water. Mixture boils below \(373\,\mathrm{K}\) when \(p = p_1\text{ (organic)} + p_2\text{ (water)}\). Separating aniline from an aniline–water mixture.
7. Differential Extraction Shaking an aqueous solution with an immiscible organic solvent in which the organic compound is much more soluble. Separating organic compounds from aqueous media using a separatory funnel (or continuous extraction if solubility is low).

8.1 Chromatography

From the Greek chroma (colour). A mixture is applied to a stationary phase (solid or liquid) while a mobile phase (solvent, solvent mixture, or gas) moves over it:

  1. Adsorption Chromatography: Based on differential adsorption of components onto a solid adsorbent (stationary phase: silica gel or alumina).
    • Column Chromatography: Adsorbent is packed in a vertical glass tube with a stopcock. The most strongly adsorbed component stays near the top; weakly adsorbed components elute first at the bottom.
    • Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC): Glass plate coated with a \(0.2\,\mathrm{mm}\) layer of silica gel/alumina (chromaplate). Sample is spotted \(2\,\mathrm{cm}\) above the base. As the eluant rises, components separate. Relative adsorption is measured by the Retardation Factor (\(R_f\)):
    \[R_f = \frac{\text{Distance moved by the substance from base line }(x)}{\text{Distance moved by the solvent from base line }(y)}\]

    Detection of Colourless Spots on TLC: UV fluorescence, iodine crystals (brown spots), or spraying reagents (e.g., ninhydrin solution to detect amino acids).

  2. Partition Chromatography (Paper Chromatography): Based on continuous differential partitioning of components between two liquid phases. Water trapped in chromatography paper acts as the stationary phase, while the ascending solvent is the mobile phase.

9.2 Quantitative Analysis (Estimation Formulas)

Let \(m\) (in grams) be the mass of the organic compound taken in each estimation:

1. Carbon & Hydrogen (Liebig's Combustion Method)

Compound burnt with excess \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CuO}\). Water (\(m_1\,\mathrm{g}\)) is absorbed in weighed anhydrous \(\mathrm{CaCl_2}\), and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (\(m_2\,\mathrm{g}\)) is absorbed in weighed conc. \(\mathrm{KOH}\) solution:

\[\text{Percentage of Carbon} = \frac{12 \times m_2 \times 100}{44 \times m} \qquad;\qquad \text{Percentage of Hydrogen} = \frac{2 \times m_1 \times 100}{18 \times m}\]

2. Nitrogen — (i) Dumas Method

Heated with \(\mathrm{CuO}\) in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) atmosphere to yield free \(\mathrm{N_2}\) gas (collected over aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\)). Any nitrogen oxides formed are reduced back to \(\mathrm{N_2}\) over heated copper gauze. If \(V_1\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{N_2}\) is collected at temperature \(T_1\,\mathrm{K}\) and atmospheric pressure \(P\), then dry gas pressure \(p_1 = P - \text{aqueous tension}\):

\[V\text{ (at STP)} = \frac{p_1 V_1 \times 273}{760 \times T_1}\,\mathrm{mL} \qquad;\qquad \text{Percentage of Nitrogen} = \frac{28 \times V \times 100}{22400 \times m}\]

3. Nitrogen — (ii) Kjeldahl's Method

Heated with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) (in presence of \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) catalyst and \(\mathrm{K_2SO_4}\)) to convert N into \(\mathrm{(NH_4)_2SO_4}\), then distilled with excess \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to liberate \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) into a known volume of standard acid:

\[\text{Percentage of Nitrogen} = \frac{1.4 \times M \times 2(V - V_1/2)}{m} = \frac{1.4 \times \text{Normality of acid neutralized} \times \text{Volume of acid (mL)}}{m}\]

Crucial Limitation: Kjeldahl's method is NOT applicable to nitro (\(-\mathrm{NO_2}\)), azo (\(-\mathrm{N=N-}\)), and ring-nitrogen compounds (e.g., pyridine, quinoline) because their nitrogen is not quantitatively converted to \(\mathrm{(NH_4)_2SO_4}\).

4. Halogens, Sulphur, Phosphorus (Carius Method) & Oxygen

  • Halogens (as \(\mathrm{AgX}\), mass \(m_1\,\mathrm{g}\)): \(\%\,\mathrm{X} = \dfrac{\text{Atomic mass of X} \times m_1 \times 100}{\text{Molar mass of AgX} \times m}\)   (\(\mathrm{AgCl}=143.5,\ \mathrm{AgBr}=188,\ \mathrm{AgI}=235\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\))
  • Sulphur (as \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\), mass \(m_1\,\mathrm{g}\)): \(\%\,\mathrm{S} = \dfrac{32 \times m_1 \times 100}{233 \times m}\)
  • Phosphorus: As ammonium phosphomolybdate \(\mathrm{(NH_4)_3PO_4\cdot 12MoO_3}\) (\(M = 1877\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)): \(\%\,\mathrm{P} = \dfrac{31 \times m_1 \times 100}{1877 \times m}\).
    As magnesium pyrophosphate \(\mathrm{Mg_2P_2O_7}\) (\(M = 222\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)): \(\%\,\mathrm{P} = \dfrac{62 \times m_1 \times 100}{222 \times m}\).
  • Oxygen (Aluise's Method): \(\mathrm{O_2}\) converted to \(\mathrm{CO}\) over red-hot coke at \(1373\,\mathrm{K}\), then oxidised by warm \(\mathrm{I_2O_5}\) to \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (\(m_1\,\mathrm{g}\)): \(\%\,\mathrm{O} = \dfrac{32 \times m_1 \times 100}{88 \times m}\).

10. Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Sigma (\(\sigma\)) and Pi (\(\pi\)) Bond Counting & Hybridisation

Problem: Determine the number of \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) bonds and the hybridisation of each carbon atom in: (a) \(\mathrm{HC\equiv CCH=CHCH_3}\) (pent-3-en-1-yne) and (b) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH=CHCN}\) (but-2-enenitrile).

Solution:

  • (a) \(\mathrm{\stackrel{1}{H}C\equiv \stackrel{2}{C}-\stackrel{3}{C}H=\stackrel{4}{C}H-\stackrel{5}{C}H_3}\):
    • \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\sigma\) bonds \(= 4\); \(\mathrm{C-H}\) \(\sigma\) bonds \(= 6\) (total \(10\ \sigma\) bonds).
    • \(\pi\) bonds: One \(\pi_{\mathrm{C=C}}\) bond (between C3 and C4) + Two \(\pi_{\mathrm{C\equiv C}}\) bonds (between C1 and C2) \(= 3\ \pi\) bonds.
    • Hybridisation: C1 and C2 are \(sp\); C3 and C4 are \(sp^2\); C5 is \(sp^3\).
  • (b) \(\mathrm{\stackrel{4}{C}H_3-\stackrel{3}{C}H=\stackrel{2}{C}H-\stackrel{1}{C}\equiv N}\):
    • Hybridisation from left to right: \(\mathrm{CH_3}\) carbon is \(sp^3\); the two \(\mathrm{CH=CH}\) carbons are \(sp^2\); the \(-\mathrm{CN}\) carbon is \(sp\).

Worked Example 2: Polyfunctional IUPAC Nomenclature

Problem: Write the systematic IUPAC names for the following compounds:
(a) \(\mathrm{CH_3-CO-CH_2-CH_2-CH_2-COOH}\)
(b) \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-CH_2-CH(OH)-CH_3}\)
(c) A benzene ring having \(-\mathrm{F}\) at position 1, \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) at position 2, and \(-\mathrm{CH_2CH_3}\) at position 4.

Solution:

  • (a) Contains a carboxylic acid (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)) and a ketone (\(\mathrm{>C=O}\)). Since \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) has higher priority than \(\mathrm{>C=O}\), the suffix is '-oic acid' and the ketone is named as a prefix ('oxo'). Numbering from \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) as C1 puts the keto group at C5 on a 6-carbon chain. IUPAC Name: 5-Oxohexanoic acid.
  • (b) Contains an alcohol (\(-\mathrm{OH}\)) and an alkene (\(\mathrm{>C=C<}\)). Alcohol has higher priority than alkene, so number the 5-carbon chain from the right end to give \(-\mathrm{OH}\) locant 2 and the double bond locant 4. IUPAC Name: Pent-4-en-2-ol.
  • (c) All three groups (\(-\mathrm{F}\), \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\), \(-\mathrm{C_2H_5}\)) are prefix substituents. The locant set (1,2,4) is the lowest possible. Listing substituents alphabetically: 4-Ethyl-1-fluoro-2-nitrobenzene.

Worked Example 3: Acidity and Alkoxide Stability via Electronic Effects

Problem: (a) Which ion is more stable: \(\mathrm{O_2NCH_2CH_2O^-}\) or \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2O^-}\)?
(b) Account for the acidity order: \(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH > Cl_2CHCOOH > ClCH_2COOH}\).

Solution:

  • (a) \(\mathrm{O_2NCH_2CH_2O^-}\) is more stable than \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2O^-}\). The \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) group is a strong electron-withdrawing (\(-I\)) group that disperses the negative charge on the oxygen atom, thereby stabilising the alkoxide ion. Conversely, the \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\) group exerts a \(+I\) (electron-donating) effect that intensifies the negative charge and destabilises \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2O^-}\).
  • (b) Chlorine exerts a \(-I\) effect. As the number of chlorine atoms increases from one to three, the \(-I\) inductive withdrawal increases, which disperses the negative charge of the conjugate carboxylate anion (\(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOO^-}\)) most effectively and facilitates \(\mathrm{H^+}\) release.

Worked Example 4: Quantitative Estimation by Dumas and Kjeldahl's Methods

Problem:
(a) In Dumas' method, \(0.30\,\mathrm{g}\) of an organic compound gave \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of nitrogen collected at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(715\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\) pressure (aqueous tension at \(300\,\mathrm{K} = 15\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\)). Find \(\%\,\mathrm{N}\).
(b) \(0.50\,\mathrm{g}\) of an organic compound was analysed by Kjeldahl's method. The evolved \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) was absorbed in \(50\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.5\,\mathrm{M}\ \mathrm{H_2SO_4}\). The residual acid required \(60\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.5\,\mathrm{M}\ \mathrm{NaOH}\) for neutralisation. Find \(\%\,\mathrm{N}\).

Solution:

  • (a) Dumas Method: Pressure of dry \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(p_1 = 715 - 15 = 700\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\). \[V_{\mathrm{STP}} = \frac{p_1 V_1 \times 273}{760 \times T_1} = \frac{700 \times 50 \times 273}{760 \times 300} = 41.9\,\mathrm{mL}\] \[\%\,\mathrm{N} = \frac{28 \times V_{\mathrm{STP}} \times 100}{22400 \times m} = \frac{28 \times 41.9 \times 100}{22400 \times 0.30} = 17.46\%\]
  • (b) Kjeldahl's Method:
    • Initial millimoles of \(\mathrm{H^ Available}\) from \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4} = 2 \times M \times V = 2 \times 0.5 \times 50 = 50\,\mathrm{mmol}\).
    • Millimoles of \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) used to neutralise residual acid \(= 0.5 \times 60 = 30\,\mathrm{mmol}\).
    • Millimoles of \(\mathrm{H^+}\) consumed by \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) (\(=\) millimoles of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)) \(= 50 - 30 = 20\,\mathrm{mmol}\).
    • Mass of \(\mathrm{N} = 20 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol} \times 14\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 0.28\,\mathrm{g}\).
    • \(\%\,\mathrm{N} = \dfrac{0.28}{0.50} \times 100 = 56.0\%\).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Choice MCQ)

Correct Answer: (2)

Worked Solution: According to IUPAC numbering, a double bond takes precedence over a triple bond only when both receive the same locant from either end. Thus, for the explicitly numbered \(\mathrm{C_2-C_3}\) bond shown, the overlap is \(sp - sp^3\). (If unnumbered, numbering from left to right gives \(\mathrm{C_2}\) as \(sp^2\) and \(\mathrm{C_3}\) as \(sp^3\), i.e., \(sp^2-sp^3\)).

• (A) Chloroform (\(334\,\mathrm{K}\)) and aniline (\(457\,\mathrm{K}\)) have a large boiling point difference (\(123\,\mathrm{K}\)) and are separated by simple distillation (iii).

Practice Question 11 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): In Lassaigne's test for halogens, the sodium fusion extract is boiled with concentrated nitric acid before adding silver nitrate solution.

Reason (R): Concentrated nitric acid decomposes \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) and \(\mathrm{Na_2S}\) (if nitrogen and sulphur are present) into volatile \(\mathrm{HCN}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) gases; otherwise, \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) and \(\mathrm{S^{2-}}\) ions would form precipitates (\(\mathrm{AgCN}\) and \(\mathrm{Ag_2S}\)) with \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\) and interfere with the halogen test.

  • (1) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (2) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (3) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (4) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (1)

Worked Solution: If nitrogen or sulphur is also present in the organic compound, the sodium fusion extract contains \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) and \(\mathrm{Na_2S}\). Boiling with conc. \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) decomposes them prior to adding \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\).

Practice Question 12 (Single-Choice MCQ)

In Lassaigne's test, when both nitrogen and sulphur are present in an organic compound and sodium is not in excess, treating the sodium fusion extract with \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\) ions produces a blood-red colour due to the formation of:

  • (1) \(\mathrm{Fe_4[Fe(CN)_6]_3\cdot xH_2O}\)
  • (2) \(\mathrm{[Fe(CN)_5NOS]^{4-}}\)
  • (3) \(\mathrm{[Fe(SCN)]^{2+}}\)
  • (4) \(\mathrm{Na_4[Fe(CN)_6]}\)
Correct Answer: (3)

Worked Solution: When both N and S are present, fusion with sodium forms sodium thiocyanate (\(\mathrm{Na + C + N + S \rightarrow NaSCN}\)). Kjeldahl's method cannot be used for the quantitative estimation of nitrogen in which of the following compounds?

  • (2) Aniline (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2}\))
  • (3) Pyridine (\(\mathrm{C_5H_5N}\))

Practice Question 14 (Numerical MCQ)

An organic compound contains \(69\%\) carbon and \(4.8\%\) hydrogen, the remainder being oxygen. What are the masses of carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) and water (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) produced when \(0.20\,\mathrm{g}\) of this substance is subjected to complete combustion?

  • (1) \(0.506\,\mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.0864\,\mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{H_2O}\)
  • (2) \(0.253\,\mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.0432\,\mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{H_2O}\)
  • (3) \(0.506\,\mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.1728\,\mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{H_2O}\)
  • (4) \(0.376\,\mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.0864\,\mathrm{g}\ \mathrm{H_2O}\)
Correct Answer: (1)

Worked Solution:
• From the carbon percentage formula: \(\%\,\mathrm{C} = \dfrac{12 \times m_{\mathrm{CO_2}} \times 100}{44 \times m}\)
\[m_{\mathrm{CO_2}} = \frac{\%\,\mathrm{C} \times 44 \times m}{12 \times 100} = \frac{69 \times 44 \times 0.20}{1200} = 0.506\,\mathrm{g}\] • From the hydrogen percentage formula: \(\%\,\mathrm{H} = \dfrac{2 \times m_{\mathrm{H_2O}} \times 100}{18 \times m}\)
\[m_{\mathrm{H_2O}} = \frac{\%\,\mathrm{H} \times 18 \times m}{2 \times 100} = \frac{4.8 \times 18 \times 0.20}{200} = 0.0864\,\mathrm{g}\]

Practice Question 15 (Numerical MCQ)

In Carius estimation of halogens, \(0.3780\,\mathrm{g}\) of an organic chloro compound gave \(0.5740\,\mathrm{g}\) of silver chloride (\(\mathrm{AgCl}\)). What is the percentage of chlorine present in the compound? (Given: Molar mass of \(\mathrm{AgCl} = 143.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), Atomic mass of \(\mathrm{Cl} = 35.5\,\mathrm{u}\))

  • (2) \(25.40\%\)
  • (3) \(37.57\%\)
  • (4) \(42.10\%\)
Correct Answer: (3)

Worked Solution: Using the Carius formula for chlorine:
\[\text{Percentage of Chlorine} = \frac{35.5 \times m_{\mathrm{AgCl}} \times 100}{143.5 \times m} = \frac{35.5 \times 0.5740 \times 100}{143.5 \times 0.3780}\] \[\text{Notice that } \frac{0.5740}{143.5} = 0.0040\,\mathrm{mol} \implies \%\mathrm{Cl} = \frac{35.5 \times 0.0040 \times 100}{0.3780} = \frac{14.2}{0.3780} = 37.57\%\]

12. Common Pitfalls & NEET Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid

  • Trap 1 — Alphabetising Alkyl Prefixes: Never ignore iso- and neo- during alphabetisation (they ARE part of the fundamental name), and never include sec-, tert-, di-, or tri- in alphabetisation.
  • Trap 2 — Alkene vs. Halide/Nitro Priority: Halogens (\(-\mathrm{X}\)) and nitro (\(-\mathrm{NO_2}\)) groups are always prefixes; double and triple bonds take suffix priority over them. Thus, \(\mathrm{BrCH_2CH=CH_2}\) is 3-bromoprop-1-ene (NOT 1-bromoprop-2-ene), and a cyclohexene ring with \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) adjacent to the double bond is 3-nitrocyclohexene.
  • Trap 3 — Acidification in Lead Acetate Test for Sulphur: Always acidify the sodium fusion extract with acetic acid (and NOT \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)) before adding lead acetate, because \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) would precipitate white \(\mathrm{PbSO_4}\) even in the absence of sulphur!
  • Trap 4 — Direct \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\) Test on \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\): Heating \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) directly with \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\) does not give a white precipitate of \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) because \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) is a covalent compound and does not ionise to release \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) ions (sodium fusion is required first).
  • Trap 5 — Subtracting Aqueous Tension in Dumas Method: Always subtract the aqueous tension of water vapour from the recorded atmospheric pressure (\(p_1 = P_{\mathrm{atm}} - p_{\mathrm{water}}\)) before converting the collected \(\mathrm{N_2}\) volume to STP.

Concise Final Recap

  • Electronegativity & Bond Strength: \(sp\ (50\%\ s) > sp^2\ (33.3\%\ s) > sp^3\ (25\%\ s)\).
  • Benzene \(\mathrm{C-C}\) Bond Length: \(139\,\mathrm{pm}\) (between \(\mathrm{C-C}\ 154\,\mathrm{pm}\) and \(\mathrm{C=C}\ 134\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Principal Functional Group Priority: \(\mathrm{-COOH > -SO_3H > -COOR > -COCl > -CONH_2 > -CN > -CHO > \; >C=O \; > -OH > -NH_2 > \; >C=C< \; > -C\equiv C-}\).
  • Carbocation & Free Radical Stability: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \text{methyl}\) (governed by hyperconjugation and \(+I\) effect).
  • Permanent vs. Temporary Effects: Inductive (\(I\)), Resonance (\(R\)), and Hyperconjugation are permanent ground-state effects; Electromeric (\(E\)) is a temporary effect requiring an attacking reagent.
  • Key Lassaigne Colours: Prussian blue \(=\ \mathrm{Fe_4[Fe(CN)_6]_3\cdot xH_2O}\) (N); Violet \(=\ \mathrm{[Fe(CN)_5NOS]^{4-}}\) (S); Blood red \(=\ \mathrm{[Fe(SCN)]^{2+}}\) (N + S); Canary yellow \(=\ \mathrm{(NH_4)_3PO_4\cdot 12MoO_3}\) (P).
