Hydrocarbons: Structure, Synthesis, Reactions, and Aromaticity

Concept-first mastery of alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, and arenes with reaction mechanisms, IUPAC nomenclature, and NEET UG practice assessments.

1. Learning Objectives and Big-Picture Overview

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds composed exclusively of carbon and hydrogen atoms. Because carbon is tetravalent and readily undergoes catenation, hydrocarbons form the structural backbone of organic chemistry and serve as primary energy sources (such as liquefied petroleum gas, compressed natural gas, liquefied natural gas, petrol, diesel, and kerosene) as well as industrial feedstocks for polymers, solvents, dyes, and pharmaceuticals.

Learning Objectives
  • Classify hydrocarbons into saturated, unsaturated, and aromatic families and apply IUPAC nomenclature rules accurately.
  • Identify structural, conformational (ethane projections), and geometrical (cis–trans) isomers and predict their physical trends.
  • Master standard laboratory and industrial syntheses of alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, and benzene.
  • Predict products and regioselectivity using free-radical substitution, electrophilic addition (Markovnikov and peroxide effects), and electrophilic aromatic substitution mechanisms.
  • Evaluate aromaticity using Hückel's \((4n + 2)\pi\) rule and determine the activating, deactivating, and directive effects of substituents on a benzene ring.

2. Alkanes: Saturated Hydrocarbons

Alkanes were historically called paraffins (Latin: parum affinis, meaning little affinity) because their strong, non-polar \(\mathrm{C-C}\) and \(\mathrm{C-H}\) \(\sigma\) bonds make them relatively unreactive toward acids, bases, and common oxidising or reducing agents at room temperature. Each carbon atom is \(sp^3\) hybridised with a tetrahedral geometry, an \(\mathrm{H-C-H}\) bond angle of \(109.5^\circ\), a \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond length of \(154\text{ pm}\), and a \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond length of \(112\text{ pm}\).

2.1 Carbon Classification, Isomerism, and IUPAC Naming

  • Degree of Carbon: A carbon atom attached to one (or zero, as in methane) other carbon is primary (\(1^\circ\)); attached to two carbons is secondary (\(2^\circ\)); to three is tertiary (\(3^\circ\)); and to four is quaternary (\(4^\circ\)).
  • Chain Isomerism: Methane, ethane, and propane have only one structural arrangement. From butane (\(\mathrm{C}_4\mathrm{H}_{10}\)) onward, branching produces chain isomers: \(\mathrm{C}_4\mathrm{H}_{10}\) has 2 isomers (butane, 2-methylpropane); \(\mathrm{C}_5\mathrm{H}_{12}\) has 3 isomers (pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane); \(\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{14}\) has 5; \(\mathrm{C}_7\mathrm{H}_{16}\) has 9; and \(\mathrm{C}_{10}\mathrm{H}_{22}\) has 75.
  • Alkyl Groups (\(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+1}\)): Formed by removing one hydrogen atom from an alkane.
  • IUPAC Rules Recap: Select the longest continuous carbon chain; number from the end giving the lowest set of locants (lowest sum rule); list substituents alphabetically (ignoring multiplying prefixes such as di-, tri-, and hyphenated prefixes such as sec- or tert-, while treating iso- and neo- as part of the fundamental name).

2.3 Physical and Chemical Properties

Because alkanes are non-polar, intermolecular attractions are weak van der Waals dispersion forces. At \(298\text{ K}\), \(\mathrm{C_1\text{--}C_4}\) are gases, \(\mathrm{C_5\text{--}C_{17}}\) are liquids, and \(\ge \mathrm{C_{18}}\) are solids. They are hydrophobic (insoluble in water, soluble in non-polar solvents). Boiling points rise steadily with molar mass. Among isomers, increased branching lowers the boiling point because the molecule becomes more spherical, reducing surface area of contact and weakening van der Waals forces (for example, pentane boils at \(309.1\text{ K}\), whereas 2,2-dimethylpropane boils at \(282.5\text{ K}\)).

Key Reactions of Alkanes

  • Free-Radical Halogenation: Occurs at \(573\text{--}773\text{ K}\) or under UV light (\(h\nu\)) via three steps:
    1. Initiation: Homolytic cleavage \(\mathrm{Cl-Cl} \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2\dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{l}\).
    2. Propagation: \(\mathrm{CH_4} + \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{l} \to \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H_3} + \mathrm{HCl}\), followed by \(\dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H_3} + \mathrm{Cl_2} \to \mathrm{CH_3Cl} + \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{l}\).
    3. Termination: Radical recombination (\(\dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{l}+\dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{l}\), \(\dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H_3}+\dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H_3}\) forming trace ethane, or \(\dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H_3}+\dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{l}\)).
    Halogen reactivity follows \(\mathrm{F_2} > \mathrm{Cl_2} > \mathrm{Br_2} > \mathrm{I_2}\). Ease of hydrogen abstraction is \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\). Fluorination is explosively violent, while iodination is slow and reversible unless an oxidising agent such as \(\mathrm{HIO_3}\) or \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) is added to consume \(\mathrm{HI}\): \(\mathrm{HIO_3} + 5\mathrm{HI} \to 3\mathrm{I_2} + 3\mathrm{H_2O}\).
  • Combustion: Complete combustion is strongly exothermic: \[ \mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2} + \left(\frac{3n+1}{2}\right)\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow n\mathrm{CO_2} + (n+1)\mathrm{H_2O} \] Incomplete combustion yields carbon black (\(\mathrm{C(s)}\)), used in inks and pigments.
  • Controlled Oxidation:
    • \(2\mathrm{CH_4} + \mathrm{O_2} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Cu},\ 523\text{ K},\ 100\text{ atm}} 2\mathrm{CH_3OH}\) (Methanol)
    • \(\mathrm{CH_4} + \mathrm{O_2} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Mo_2O_3},\ \Delta} \mathrm{HCHO} + \mathrm{H_2O}\) (Methanal)
    • \(2\mathrm{CH_3CH_3} + 3\mathrm{O_2} \xrightarrow{(\mathrm{CH_3COO})_2\mathrm{Mn},\ \Delta} 2\mathrm{CH_3COOH} + 2\mathrm{H_2O}\) (Ethanoic acid)
    • Alkanes with a tertiary hydrogen are oxidised by \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) to tertiary alcohols: \((\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{CH} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{KMnO_4}} (\mathrm{CH_3})_3\mathrm{COH}\).
  • Isomerisation: Straight-chain alkanes heated with anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3/HCl}\) rearrange into branched isomers (e.g., n-hexane \(\to\) 2-methylpentane + 3-methylpentane).
  • Aromatisation (Reforming): n-Alkanes with \(\ge 6\) carbons heated at \(773\text{ K}\) and \(10\text{--}20\text{ atm}\) over \(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}\), \(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\), or \(\mathrm{Mo_2O_3}\) on alumina undergo dehydrogenation and cyclisation to form benzene or its homologues.

2.4 Conformations of Ethane

Because the \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\sigma\) orbital is cylindrically symmetrical around the internuclear axis, rotation about the single bond generates infinite spatial arrangements called conformations (conformers or rotamers). However, rotation is slightly hindered by a torsional barrier of \(\approx 12.5\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\) caused by repulsion between adjacent \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond electron clouds (torsional strain). At room temperature, thermal collisions easily overcome this small barrier, so individual conformers cannot be isolated.

  • Staggered Conformation: Dihedral (torsional) angle is \(60^\circ\). Bonding electron pairs are maximally separated, giving minimum torsional strain and maximum stability.
  • Eclipsed Conformation: Dihedral angle is \(0^\circ\). Front and rear \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bonds align directly, causing maximum repulsion and minimum stability.
  • Skew Conformation: Any intermediate dihedral angle between \(0^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\).

3. Alkenes: Unsaturated Hydrocarbons with Double Bonds

Alkenes (olefins) contain at least one \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond and follow the general formula \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n}\) for mono-alkenes. Each doubly bonded carbon is \(sp^2\) hybridised, forming a planar trigonal geometry. The \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond (\(134\text{ pm}\), total bond enthalpy \(681\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) consists of one strong \(\sigma\) bond (\(\approx 397\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\), head-on \(sp^2\text{--}sp^2\) overlap) and one weaker \(\pi\) bond (\(\approx 284\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\), sideways \(2p\text{--}2p\) overlap). Because the \(\pi\) electrons are loosely held above and below the molecular plane, alkenes act as electron-rich nucleophiles toward electrophilic reagents.

3.1 Geometrical (cis–trans) Isomerism

Lateral overlap of the \(2p\) orbitals restricts rotation around the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond. When each doubly bonded carbon carries two different groups (types \(\mathrm{XYC=CXY}\), \(\mathrm{XYC=CXZ}\), or \(\mathrm{XYC=CZW}\)), two non-interconvertible stereoisomers exist:

  • cis Isomer: Identical groups lie on the same side of the double bond. Because bond dipoles reinforce, cis-alkenes generally have a higher dipole moment (e.g., cis-but-2-ene has \(\mu = 0.33\text{ D}\)) and a higher boiling point (\(277\text{ K}\) for cis-but-2-ene).
  • trans Isomer: Identical groups lie on opposite sides. Bond dipoles cancel partially or completely (trans-but-2-ene has \(\mu = 0\text{ D}\), boiling point \(274\text{ K}\)). However, symmetrical trans isomers pack more efficiently in a crystal lattice, giving them a higher melting point than their cis counterparts.

3.2 Preparation of Alkenes

  1. Partial Reduction of Alkynes:
    • Using Lindlar's catalyst (palladised charcoal partially deactivated with sulphur compounds or quinoline): yields cis-alkene selectively.
    • Using sodium in liquid ammonia (\(\mathrm{Na/liq.\ NH_3}\)): yields trans-alkene selectively.
  2. Dehydrohalogenation of Alkyl Halides (\(\beta\)-Elimination): Heating an alkyl halide with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) eliminates \(\mathrm{HX}\). Reactivity order for halogens: \(\mathrm{R-I > R-Br > R-Cl}\); for alkyl groups: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\).
  3. Dehalogenation of Vicinal Dihalides: Heating a 1,2-dihalide with zinc dust removes \(\mathrm{ZnX_2}\) to form an alkene.
  4. Acidic Dehydration of Alcohols: Heating an alcohol with concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) eliminates \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) via \(\beta\)-elimination.

3.3 Chemical Properties of Alkenes

  • Test for Unsaturation (\(\mathrm{Br_2/CCl_4}\)): Reddish-orange bromine in \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) adds across \(\mathrm{C=C}\) via a cyclic halonium ion to form a colourless vicinal dibromide. (Iodine does not add under normal conditions.)
  • Addition of Hydrogen Halides (\(\mathrm{HI > HBr > HCl}\)):
    • Markovnikov's Rule (Ionic Mechanism): In unsymmetrical alkenes, the proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) adds first to give the more stable carbocation (\(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\)), and \(\mathrm{X^-}\) attacks that carbocation. Thus, the negative part of the addendum attaches to the doubly bonded carbon carrying fewer hydrogen atoms.
    • Anti-Markovnikov / Peroxide Effect (Kharasch Effect): In the presence of organic peroxides such as benzoyl peroxide, \(\mathrm{HBr}\) adds to unsymmetrical alkenes via a free-radical chain mechanism, forming the more stable secondary radical intermediate and yielding the 1-bromoalkane. Critical exam point: This effect works only with \(\mathrm{HBr}\), never with \(\mathrm{HCl}\) (the \(\mathrm{H-Cl}\) bond, \(430.5\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\), is too strong to be cleaved by radicals) or \(\mathrm{HI}\) (the \(\mathrm{H-I}\) bond, \(296.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\), is weak, so iodine radicals dimerise to \(\mathrm{I_2}\) rather than adding to the double bond).
  • Addition of \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) and Water: Cold conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) adds by Markovnikov's rule to give alkyl hydrogen sulphates; dilute acid hydration (\(\mathrm{H_2O/H^+}\)) yields Markovnikov alcohols.
  • Oxidation:
    • Baeyer's Reagent (cold, dilute, alkaline \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) at \(273\text{ K}\)): converts alkenes to colourless vicinal glycols (1,2-diols) and discharges the purple colour—a classic test for unsaturation.
    • Hot Acidic \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) or \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\): cleaves the double bond completely to ketones and/or carboxylic acids (terminal \(=\mathrm{CH_2}\) oxidises to \(\mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O}\)).
  • Reductive Ozonolysis (\(\mathrm{O_3}\), then \(\mathrm{Zn + H_2O}\)): Cleaves the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond into two carbonyl fragments (aldehydes and/or ketones), pinpointing the exact position of the double bond.
  • Polymerisation: High temperature, high pressure, and catalysts convert ethene to polythene and propene to polypropene.

4. Alkynes: Unsaturated Hydrocarbons with Triple Bonds

Alkynes contain at least one \(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\) triple bond (general formula \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}\)). In ethyne, each carbon is \(sp\) hybridised with a linear geometry (\(\mathrm{H-C-C}\) angle \(180^\circ\)). The \(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\) bond (\(120\text{ pm}\), bond enthalpy \(823\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) consists of one \(\sigma\) bond and two mutually perpendicular \(\pi\) bonds, creating a cylindrical \(\pi\)-electron cloud around the internuclear axis.

4.1 Preparation of Alkynes

  1. From Calcium Carbide (Industrial): Limestone is decomposed to quicklime (\(\mathrm{CaO}\)), heated with coke to form calcium carbide (\(\mathrm{CaC_2}\)), and hydrolysed: \[ \mathrm{CaC_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} \longrightarrow \mathrm{Ca(OH)_2} + \mathrm{C_2H_2} \]
  2. From Vicinal Dihalides: Two-step dehydrohalogenation—first with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) to form a vinyl halide, then with a stronger base, sodamide (\(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\)), to generate the triple bond.

4.2 Acidic Character and Chemical Reactions

Why Are Terminal Alkynes Acidic?

An \(sp\) hybrid orbital has \(50\%\) s-character (compared with \(33.3\%\) in \(sp^2\) and \(25\%\) in \(sp^3\)). Because s-electrons lie closer to the nucleus, an \(sp\) carbon is significantly more electronegative and pulls the \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bonding pair toward itself, allowing terminal protons (\(\equiv \mathrm{C-H}\)) to be abstracted by strong bases such as \(\mathrm{Na}\) metal or \(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\) with evolution of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) or \(\mathrm{NH_3}\). Internal alkynes (e.g., but-2-yne) lack a terminal \(\equiv \mathrm{C-H}\) proton and do not react.

Acidity Order: \(\mathrm{HC\equiv CH} > \mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv CH} \gg \mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv C-CH_3}\)  |  \(\mathrm{HC\equiv CH} > \mathrm{H_2C=CH_2} > \mathrm{CH_3-CH_3}\)
  • Electrophilic Additions: Alkynes add two equivalents of \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) (decolourising \(\mathrm{Br_2/CCl_4}\) to a tetrabromoalkane), or \(\mathrm{HX}\) (forming gem-dihalides via Markovnikov addition, e.g., propyne + \(2\mathrm{HBr} \to\) 2,2-dibromopropane).
  • Hydration (\(\mathrm{Hg^{2+}/H^+}\) at \(333\text{ K}\)): Addition of one mole of water forms an unstable enol that tautomerises (isomerises) to a carbonyl compound: ethyne gives ethanal, whereas higher alkynes give ketones (e.g., propyne gives propanone).
  • Polymerisation: Linear polymerisation of ethyne produces conductive polyacetylene; cyclic polymerisation through a red-hot iron tube at \(873\text{ K}\) converts 3 moles of ethyne into benzene (\(\mathrm{C_6H_6}\)).

5. Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Arenes) and Benzene

Benzene (\(\mathrm{C_6H_6}\)) is a planar, cyclic molecule in which all six carbons are \(sp^2\) hybridised. Every \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond has an identical length of \(139\text{ pm}\)—intermediate between a \(\mathrm{C-C}\) single bond (\(154\text{ pm}\)) and a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (\(133\text{ pm}\)). Its six unhybridised \(p\) orbitals overlap laterally around the entire ring to form a delocalised \(\pi\)-electron cloud above and below the hexagonal plane. This resonance stabilisation explains why benzene resists addition reactions under ordinary conditions and instead undergoes electrophilic substitution, preserving its aromatic sextet.

5.1 Hückel's Rule of Aromaticity

A cyclic system (benzenoid or non-benzenoid) is aromatic if it satisfies all three criteria:

  1. Planarity (all ring atoms lie in one plane).
  2. Complete delocalisation of \(\pi\) electrons around the entire ring (continuous \(p\)-orbital overlap).
  3. \((4n + 2)\pi\) electrons in the ring, where \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\) (e.g., \(2, 6, 10, 14\ \pi\) electrons).

Examples: Benzene (\(6\pi, n=1\)), cyclopentadienyl anion (\(6\pi, n=1\)), cycloheptatrienyl/tropylium cation (\(6\pi, n=1\)), naphthalene (\(10\pi, n=2\)), anthracene and phenanthrene (\(14\pi, n=3\)).

5.2 Preparation and Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (\(S_E\))

Benzene is prepared by cyclic polymerisation of ethyne (\(873\text{ K}\), red-hot Fe), soda-lime decarboxylation of sodium benzoate, or reduction of phenol vapours over heated zinc dust.

5.3 Directive Influence, Addition, and Carcinogenicity

Substituent Category Examples Electronic Effect & Orientation
ortho/para-Directing & Activating \(-\mathrm{OH}\), \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\), \(-\mathrm{NHR}\), \(-\mathrm{NHCOCH_3}\), \(-\mathrm{OCH_3}\), \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\), \(-\mathrm{C_2H_5}\) Increase electron density at o- and p-positions via \(+R\) (or hyperconjugation); ring reacts faster than benzene.
ortho/para-Directing & Deactivating \(-\mathrm{F}\), \(-\mathrm{Cl}\), \(-\mathrm{Br}\), \(-\mathrm{I}\) Strong \(-I\) effect lowers overall ring electron density (slower than benzene), but \(+R\) enriches o- and p-positions relative to m-.
meta-Directing & Deactivating \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\), \(-\mathrm{CN}\), \(-\mathrm{CHO}\), \(-\mathrm{COR}\), \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), \(-\mathrm{COOR}\), \(-\mathrm{SO_3H}\) Withdraw electrons via \(-R\) and \(-I\), depleting o- and p-positions strongly; incoming \(\mathrm{E^+}\) attacks the comparatively less-depleted meta position.

Special Addition & Toxicity Notes: Under vigorous conditions (\(\mathrm{Ni},\ \Delta\)), benzene adds \(3\mathrm{H_2}\) to form cyclohexane. Under UV light at \(500\text{ K}\), benzene adds \(3\mathrm{Cl_2}\) to form benzene hexachloride (\(\mathrm{C_6H_6Cl_6}\), gammaxane). Polynuclear hydrocarbons containing more than two fused benzene rings (such as 1,2-benzanthracene, 1,2-benzpyrene, 3-methylcholanthrene, and 1,2,5,6-dibenzanthracene), formed by incomplete combustion of tobacco, coal, and petroleum, are toxic and carcinogenic because they damage DNA.

6. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Backtracking Structure from Ozonolysis

Problem: An unsymmetrical alkene X (assumed molar mass \(84\text{ g mol}^{-1}\)) on reductive ozonolysis (\(\mathrm{O_3}\), followed by \(\mathrm{Zn/H_2O}\)) yields an equimolar mixture of ethanal and butan-2-one. Determine the structure and IUPAC name of X.

Solution:

  1. Write the structures of the two carbonyl products: \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=O}\) (ethanal, 2 carbons) and \(\mathrm{CH_3-C(=O)-CH_2-CH_3}\) (butan-2-one, 4 carbons).
  2. Remove the two oxygen atoms and join the carbonyl carbons with a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond: \[ \mathrm{CH_3-CH=C(CH_3)-CH_2-CH_3} \]
  3. Verify the molecular formula: \(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}}\) (molar mass \(= 6 \times 12 + 12 \times 1 = 84\text{ g mol}^{-1}\)), matching the assumption.
  4. Number the longest chain containing the double bond from the end giving the lower locant to \(\mathrm{C=C}\): \(\mathrm{C^1H_3-C^2H=C^3(CH_3)-C^4H_2-C^5H_3}\).
  5. IUPAC Name: 3-Methylpent-2-ene.
Worked Example 2: Regioselectivity with and without Peroxide

Problem: Predict the major organic product when 3-methylbut-1-ene reacts with: (a) \(\mathrm{HBr}\) in the absence of peroxide, and (b) \(\mathrm{HBr}\) in the presence of benzoyl peroxide.

Solution:

  • (a) Absence of peroxide (Ionic Markovnikov addition): Protonation at \(\mathrm{C1}\) initially forms a \(2^\circ\) carbocation, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CH-\overset{+}{C}H-CH_3}\). However, a rapid 1,2-hydride shift converts it into the more stable \(3^\circ\) carbocation, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2\overset{+}{C}-CH_2-CH_3}\), which is attacked by \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) to give 2-bromo-2-methylbutane as the rearranged major product.
  • (b) Presence of peroxide (Free-radical anti-Markovnikov addition): \(\dot{\mathrm{B}}\mathrm{r}\) attacks \(\mathrm{C1}\) to form the more stable \(2^\circ\) carbon radical \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CH-\dot{C}H-CH_2Br}\) (radicals do not undergo 1,2-hydride shifts). Hydrogen abstraction from \(\mathrm{HBr}\) yields 1-bromo-3-methylbutane.
Worked Example 3: Planning Disubstituted Benzene Synthesis

Problem: Suggest a two-step reaction sequence to convert benzene into m-bromonitrobenzene without forming significant o/p-isomers.

Solution:

Order of group introduction is crucial because \(-\mathrm{Br}\) is o,p-directing whereas \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) is m-directing:

  1. Step 1 (Nitration): Treat benzene with \(\mathrm{conc.\ HNO_3 + conc.\ H_2SO_4}\) at \(323\text{--}333\text{ K}\) to obtain nitrobenzene.
  2. Step 2 (Bromination): Treat nitrobenzene with \(\mathrm{Br_2 / anhyd.\ FeBr_3}\). Since \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) directs incoming electrophiles to the meta position, the major product is m-bromonitrobenzene.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following alkanes cannot be prepared in good yield as the sole organic product by either Kolbe's electrolysis or the Wurtz reaction using a single alkyl halide or carboxylate salt?

  1. (A) Ethane
  2. (B) Butane
  3. (C) Propane
  4. (D) Hexane
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Both Kolbe's electrolysis (\(2\mathrm{RCOO^-} \to \mathrm{R-R}\)) and the Wurtz reaction (\(2\mathrm{R-X} \to \mathrm{R-R}\)) couple two identical alkyl fragments \(\mathrm{R}\), so they produce symmetrical alkanes with an even number of carbon atoms (ethane, butane, hexane). Propane has an odd number of carbons (3) and would require a mixture of two different precursors, yielding a mixture of three alkanes.

Practice Question 2 Boiling Point Trend

Arrange the following isomeric pentanes in decreasing order of their boiling points:
I. Pentane    II. 2-Methylbutane    III. 2,2-Dimethylpropane

  1. (A) \(\mathrm{I > II > III}\)
  2. (B) \(\mathrm{III > II > I}\)
  3. (C) \(\mathrm{II > I > III}\)
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{I > III > II}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: All three isomers have the same molar mass (\(\mathrm{C_5H_{12}}\)). As branching increases, the molecular shape becomes more compact and spherical, decreasing the surface area of contact and weakening intermolecular van der Waals forces. Thus, boiling point decreases with branching: Pentane (\(309.1\text{ K}\)) \(>\) 2-Methylbutane (\(300.9\text{ K}\)) \(>\) 2,2-Dimethylpropane (\(282.5\text{ K}\)).

Practice Question 3 Statement-Based MCQ

Given below are two statements:
Statement I: The peroxide effect (Kharasch effect) is observed during the addition of \(\mathrm{HBr}\) to propene, but not with \(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{HI}\).
Statement II: The \(\mathrm{H-Cl}\) bond is too strong to be cleaved homolytically by free radicals, whereas iodine free radicals formed from \(\mathrm{HI}\) combine to form \(\mathrm{I_2}\) instead of adding to the double bond.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Peroxide-initiated radical addition is thermodynamically favourable only for \(\mathrm{HBr}\). The \(\mathrm{H-Cl}\) bond (\(430.5\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)) resists homolytic cleavage in propagation, and for \(\mathrm{HI}\) (\(296.8\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\)), the addition of \(\dot{\mathrm{I}}\) to the alkene is endothermic, so \(\dot{\mathrm{I}}\) radicals preferentially dimerise to \(\mathrm{I_2}\).

Practice Question 4 Stereochemistry

When but-2-yne is treated with \(\mathrm{H_2}\) in the presence of Lindlar's catalyst (partially deactivated \(\mathrm{Pd/C}\)), the major product is:

  1. (A) Butane
  2. (B) trans-But-2-ene
  3. (C) cis-But-2-ene
  4. (D) But-1-ene
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Partial hydrogenation of an internal alkyne over Lindlar's catalyst delivers two hydrogen atoms to the same face (syn-addition), selectively forming the cis-alkene (cis-but-2-ene). By contrast, \(\mathrm{Na/liq.\ NH_3}\) gives trans-but-2-ene.

Practice Question 5 Acidic Strength

Which of the following hydrocarbons will liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) gas on reaction with metallic sodium, and what is the correct decreasing order of acidity among ethane, ethene, and ethyne?

  1. (A) Ethyne; \(\mathrm{HC\equiv CH > H_2C=CH_2 > CH_3-CH_3}\)
  2. (B) Ethene; \(\mathrm{H_2C=CH_2 > HC\equiv CH > CH_3-CH_3}\)
  3. (C) Ethane; \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH_3 > H_2C=CH_2 > HC\equiv CH}\)
  4. (D) Ethyne; \(\mathrm{HC\equiv CH > CH_3-CH_3 > H_2C=CH_2}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Ethyne has \(sp\)-hybridised carbons (\(50\%\) s-character), making the carbon more electronegative than \(sp^2\) (\(33.3\%\) s) or \(sp^3\) (\(25\%\) s) carbons. Consequently, ethyne readily donates a proton to \(\mathrm{Na}\) to form sodium ethynide and \(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{H_2}\).

Practice Question 6 Alkyne Hydration

Propyne on warming with dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) in the presence of \(\mathrm{HgSO_4}\) at \(333\text{ K}\) yields:

  1. (A) Propanal
  2. (B) Propan-2-one
  3. (C) Propanoic acid
  4. (D) Propan-1-ol
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Markovnikov hydration of propyne (\(\mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv CH}\)) with \(\mathrm{Hg^{2+}/H^+}\) attaches \(-\mathrm{OH}\) to the internal carbon, forming the enol \(\mathrm{CH_3-C(OH)=CH_2}\), which immediately tautomerises to the ketone propan-2-one (\(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_3}\)).

Practice Question 7 Aromaticity

Which of the following species is aromatic according to Hückel's rule?

  1. (A) Cyclopentadienyl cation (\(4\pi\) electrons)
  2. (B) Cycloheptatrienyl (tropylium) cation (\(6\pi\) electrons)
  3. (C) Cyclobutadiene (\(4\pi\) electrons)
  4. (D) Cyclohexene (\(2\pi\) electrons, non-conjugated)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: The cycloheptatrienyl cation is planar, completely conjugated (all seven carbons are \(sp^2\) hybridised with an empty \(p\) orbital on the cationic carbon), and contains \(6\pi\) electrons, satisfying \((4n+2)\pi\) for \(n=1\). Options (A) and (C) have \(4\pi\) electrons, and (D) has an \(sp^3\) \(\mathrm{CH_2}\) interruption preventing complete delocalisation.

Practice Question 8 Friedel–Crafts Rearrangement

Treatment of benzene with 1-chloropropane in the presence of anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) gives which major product?

  1. (A) n-Propylbenzene
  2. (B) Isopropylbenzene (Cumene)
  3. (C) 1,2-Dipropylbenzene
  4. (D) Chlorobenzene
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Reaction of 1-chloropropane with \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) generates the primary propyl carbocation (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2^+}\)), which undergoes a rapid 1,2-hydride shift to the more stable secondary isopropyl carbocation (\(\mathrm{CH_3\overset{+}{C}HCH_3}\)). Electrophilic attack on benzene therefore yields isopropylbenzene as the major product.

Practice Question 9 Reactivity Toward Electrophiles

Arrange the following aromatic compounds in decreasing order of reactivity toward nitration (electrophilic substitution):
I. Toluene    II. Benzene    III. Chlorobenzene    IV. Nitrobenzene

  1. (A) \(\mathrm{I > II > III > IV}\)
  2. (B) \(\mathrm{IV > III > II > I}\)
  3. (C) \(\mathrm{I > III > II > IV}\)
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{II > I > III > IV}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: The methyl group in toluene is electron-donating (activating), increasing ring electron density relative to benzene. Both \(-\mathrm{Cl}\) and \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) are deactivating, with \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) being far more strongly electron-withdrawing than \(-\mathrm{Cl}\). Thus, the reactivity order is Toluene \(>\) Benzene \(>\) Chlorobenzene \(>\) Nitrobenzene.

Practice Question 10 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): trans-But-2-ene has a higher melting point than cis-but-2-ene.
Reason (R): The trans isomer is more symmetrical and packs more closely into a crystal lattice than the cis isomer.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  4. (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Symmetrical trans-alkenes fit more efficiently into a solid crystal lattice, increasing lattice energy and raising the melting point relative to the less symmetrical cis isomer.

Practice Question 11 Match the Following

Match the reagent/reaction in Column I with its characteristic product in Column II:

Column I (Reaction) Column II (Product)
(p) Ethene + cold dil. \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) (\(273\text{ K}\)) (i) Cyclohexane
(q) Ethyne + red-hot Fe tube (\(873\text{ K}\)) (ii) Ethane-1,2-diol
(r) Benzene + \(3\mathrm{H_2}\) (\(\mathrm{Ni},\ \Delta\)) (iii) Benzene
(s) Benzene + \(3\mathrm{Cl_2}\) (UV, \(500\text{ K}\)) (iv) Benzene hexachloride
  1. (A) (p)–(ii), (q)–(iii), (r)–(i), (s)–(iv)
  2. (B) (p)–(iii), (q)–(ii), (r)–(i), (s)–(iv)
  3. (C) (p)–(ii), (q)–(iii), (r)–(iv), (s)–(i)
  4. (D) (p)–(i), (q)–(iv), (r)–(iii), (s)–(ii)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Cold dilute \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) (Baeyer's reagent) converts ethene to ethane-1,2-diol; cyclic polymerisation of ethyne at \(873\text{ K}\) forms benzene; catalytic hydrogenation of benzene yields cyclohexane; and UV chlorination of benzene yields \(\mathrm{C_6H_6Cl_6}\) (BHC).

Practice Question 12 Numerical Stoichiometry

How many moles of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) are required for the complete combustion of \(2\text{ moles}\) of gaseous butane (\(\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}\))?

  1. (A) \(6.5\text{ mol}\)
  2. (B) \(13\text{ mol}\)
  3. (C) \(10\text{ mol}\)
  4. (D) \(8\text{ mol}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Using \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2} + \left(\frac{3n+1}{2}\right)\mathrm{O_2} \to n\mathrm{CO_2} + (n+1)\mathrm{H_2O}\) with \(n=4\): 1 mole of \(\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}\) requires \(\frac{3(4)+1}{2} = \frac{13}{2} = 6.5\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\). Therefore, \(2\text{ moles}\) of butane require \(2 \times 6.5 = 13\text{ mol}\) of \(\mathrm{O_2}\).

8. Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Halogen Directive vs. Reactivity Paradox: Halogens (\(-\mathrm{Cl}\), \(-\mathrm{Br}\)) are ortho/para-directing yet deactivating. Do not confuse orientation (governed by \(+R\)) with reaction rate (governed by \(-I\)).
  • Carbocation Rearrangement: Both Markovnikov addition and Friedel–Crafts alkylation involve carbocation intermediates—always check whether a 1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shift can form a more stable carbocation.
  • Peroxide Effect Scope: Anti-Markovnikov radical addition applies exclusively to \(\mathrm{HBr}\), never to \(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{HI}\).
  • Internal vs. Terminal Alkynes: Only terminal alkynes (\(\mathrm{R-C\equiv C-H}\)) react with \(\mathrm{Na}\) or \(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\) to show acidic behaviour; internal alkynes do not.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Alkane b.p. decreases with branching; staggered ethane is \(\approx 12.5\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}\) more stable than eclipsed.
  • Lindlar's catalyst \(\to\) cis-alkene; \(\mathrm{Na/liq.\ NH_3} \to\) trans-alkene.
  • Baeyer's reagent (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)) and \(\mathrm{Br_2/CCl_4}\) decolourisation are standard unsaturation tests.
  • Ozonolysis (\(\mathrm{O_3},\ \mathrm{Zn/H_2O}\)) cleaves \(\mathrm{C=C}\) into aldehydes/ketones; join carbonyl carbons to reconstruct the alkene.
  • Aromaticity requires: planar ring + complete \(\pi\)-delocalisation + \((4n+2)\pi\) electrons.
Final Recap

Hydrocarbon reactivity is governed by orbital hybridisation and electron availability: \(sp^3\) alkanes undergo free-radical substitution; \(sp^2\) alkenes and \(sp\) alkynes undergo electrophilic addition (with terminal alkynes also showing acidity due to \(50\%\) s-character); and \((4n+2)\pi\) aromatic rings undergo electrophilic substitution so that their resonance-stabilised aromatic sextet is preserved.
