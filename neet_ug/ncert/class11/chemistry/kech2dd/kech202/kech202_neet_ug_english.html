1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Roadmap

General Organic Chemistry forms the conceptual backbone of the entire organic chemistry syllabus for NEET UG. Mastering how electrons are distributed, how molecules are named, and how compounds are purified and analysed enables you to predict reaction outcomes across hydrocarbons, functional-group derivatives, and biomolecules.

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Determine carbon hybridisation (\(sp^3\), \(sp^2\), \(sp\)), molecular geometry, and \(\sigma\)/\(\pi\) bond counts in organic molecules.
  • Translate between complete, condensed, bond-line, and 3D wedge-and-dash structural formulas.
  • Classify organic compounds and apply IUPAC nomenclature rules to branched, cyclic, polyfunctional, and substituted aromatic systems.
  • Distinguish structural isomerism subtypes (chain, position, functional, metamerism) from stereoisomerism.
  • Predict intermediate stability and reactivity using bond fission, nucleophiles/electrophiles, and electronic effects (inductive, resonance, electromeric, and hyperconjugation).
  • Select appropriate purification methods (crystallisation, distillation variants, extraction, chromatography) and solve qualitative (Lassaigne's test) and quantitative (Dumas, Kjeldahl, Carius) problems.

2. Carbon's Tetravalence, Hybridisation & Molecular Representation

2.1 Origin of Organic Chemistry & Catenation

Carbon possesses a unique ability called catenation—linking with other carbon atoms via strong covalent bonds to form chains, branched skeletons, and rings, alongside covalent bonding with H, O, N, S, P, and halogens. Early 19th-century chemists believed a mysterious "vital force" was required to produce organic substances. This vitalism theory was overturned in 1828 when Friedrich Wöhler synthesised urea (an organic compound) by heating inorganic ammonium cyanate:

\[\mathrm{NH_4CNO} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{NH_2CONH_2}\]

Subsequent laboratory syntheses of acetic acid (Kolbe, 1845) and methane (Berthelot, 1856) established that organic molecules obey the exact same chemical laws as inorganic matter.

2.2 Hybridisation, Bond Parameters, and \(\pi\)-Bond Characteristics

Carbon's tetravalence and molecular shapes are governed by mixing its \(2s\) and \(2p\) orbitals. As the percentage of \(s\)-character increases, the hybrid orbital is held closer to the nucleus, making the carbon atom more electronegative and its bonds shorter and stronger.

Hybridisation % \(s\)-Character Geometry & Bond Angle Example Relative Bond Strength & Electronegativity
\(sp^3\) 25% Tetrahedral, 109.5° \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) (Methane) Longest C–C bond, lowest electronegativity
\(sp^2\) 33.3% Trigonal planar, 120° \(\mathrm{C_2H_4}\) (Ethene) Intermediate bond length and electronegativity
\(sp\) 50% Linear, 180° \(\mathrm{C_2H_2}\) (Ethyne) Shortest, strongest C≡C bond; highest electronegativity

Key Features of \(\pi\) Bonds: A \(\pi\) bond forms by sideways (lateral) overlap of two mutually parallel unhybridised \(p\) orbitals on adjacent atoms. Consequently:

  • All atoms directly attached to a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond lie in a single plane, with the \(p\) orbitals perpendicular to that plane.
  • Restricted Rotation: Twisting one \(\mathrm{CH_2}\) unit relative to the other breaks the parallel \(p\)-orbital overlap; hence free rotation around \(\mathrm{C=C}\) is prevented.
  • High Reactivity: The \(\pi\)-electron cloud lies above and below the internuclear axis, making \(\pi\) electrons loosely held and readily accessible to attacking electrophilic reagents.

2.3 Structural Representations (2D and 3D)

  • Complete Structural Formula: Shows every atom and every covalent dash (single, double, triple).
  • Condensed Formula: Omits most bond dashes and groups identical units with subscripts, e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_4CH_3}\).
  • Bond-Line (Zig-Zag) Formula: Omits C and H atoms attached to carbon; vertices and line terminals represent carbons (terminals = \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\) unless a heteroatom is shown). Heteroatoms (O, N, Cl, Br) and their attached hydrogens are written explicitly.
  • 3D Wedge-and-Dash Formula: Represents tetrahedral geometry on paper. A solid wedge points toward the observer, a dashed wedge points away from the observer, and normal lines lie in the plane of the paper.

Worked Example 1: Counting \(\sigma\)/\(\pi\) Bonds & Identifying Hybridisation

Problem: Consider the molecule \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CO-C\equiv N}\) (hypothetical exam exercise). Determine (a) the total number of \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) bonds, and (b) the hybridisation state of each carbon atom from left to right.

Solution:

  • Expand all bonds: \(\mathrm{H_3C-CH=CH-C(=O)-C\equiv N}\).
  • C–H \(\sigma\) bonds: \(3 + 1 + 1 = 5\).
  • C–C \(\sigma\) bonds: \(4\) single-bond linkages between the five carbons.
  • C=O and C≡N \(\sigma\) bonds: \(1\) (\(\mathrm{C-O}\)) + \(1\) (\(\mathrm{C-N}\)) = \(2\).
  • Total \(\sigma\) bonds = \(5 + 4 + 2 = 11\).
  • \(\pi\) bonds: \(1\) in \(\mathrm{C=C}\), \(1\) in \(\mathrm{C=O}\), and \(2\) in \(\mathrm{C\equiv N}\) \(\rightarrow\) Total \(\pi\) bonds = \(1 + 1 + 2 = 4\).
  • Hybridisation (left to right): \(\mathrm{C_1}\) (\(-\mathrm{CH_3}\), 4 \(\sigma\)) \(\rightarrow\) \(sp^3\); \(\mathrm{C_2}\) (\(-\mathrm{CH=}\), 3 \(\sigma\)) \(\rightarrow\) \(sp^2\); \(\mathrm{C_3}\) (\(=\mathrm{CH-}\), 3 \(\sigma\)) \(\rightarrow\) \(sp^2\); \(\mathrm{C_4}\) (\(\mathrm{C=O}\), 3 \(\sigma\)) \(\rightarrow\) \(sp^2\); \(\mathrm{C_5}\) (\(-\mathrm{C\equiv N}\), 2 \(\sigma\)) \(\rightarrow\) \(sp\).

3. Classification & Systematic IUPAC Nomenclature

3.1 Structural Classification & Homologous Series

Organic compounds are divided into acyclic (aliphatic) open-chain molecules and cyclic ring molecules. Cyclic systems are further split into alicyclic (homocyclic rings like cyclopropane/cyclohexane, or heterocyclic non-aromatic rings like tetrahydrofuran) and aromatic (benzenoid like benzene/aniline/naphthalene, non-benzenoid like tropone, and heterocyclic aromatic like furan, thiophene, and pyridine).

A functional group is an atom or group of atoms that dictates a molecule's characteristic chemical reactivity. Compounds sharing the same functional group and differing successively by a \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) unit (14 u) form a homologous series.

3.2 Core IUPAC Rules for Branched, Polyfunctional & Aromatic Compounds

  1. Longest Chain Rule: Identify the longest continuous carbon chain containing the principal functional group (and maximum multiple bonds if applicable). If two chains have equal length, select the one with more side chains.
  2. Lowest Locant Rule: Number the parent chain from the end that gives the principal functional group the lowest number; for alkanes, give the first point of branching the lowest locant set.
  3. Alphabetical Order of Substituents: List prefixes alphabetically. Multiplicative prefixes (di-, tri-, tetra-) and hyphenated prefixes (sec-, tert-) are ignored during alphabetisation, whereas iso- and neo- are treated as part of the fundamental alkyl name.
  4. Equivalent Positions: When two different substituents occupy equivalent positions from either end, assign the lower locant to the substituent that comes first alphabetically.
  5. Polyfunctional Compounds: Choose one principal functional group (suffix) according to the IUPAC priority order; all other groups become prefixed substituents.
  6. Substituted Benzenes: For disubstituted rings, 1,2- is ortho (o-), 1,3- is meta (m-), and 1,4- is para (p-). For three or more substituents, use lowest locants (never o/m/p). If a benzene ring is attached to an alkane bearing a functional group, the ring is named as a phenyl (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5-}\)) substituent.

5. Organic Reaction Mechanisms & Electronic Effects

5.1 Covalent Bond Fission & Reactive Intermediates

  • Homolytic Cleavage: Each bonded atom takes one electron from the shared pair (shown by half-headed fish-hook arrows), forming neutral free radicals with an unpaired electron. Favoured by heat, light, or peroxides. Stability order of alkyl radicals: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \dot{\mathrm{C}}\mathrm{H}_3\).
  • Heterolytic Cleavage: Both shared electrons stay with one fragment (shown by full curved arrows), generating ions:
    • Carbocation: Positively charged carbon with a sextet of electrons, \(sp^2\) hybridised, trigonal planar geometry with an empty perpendicular \(p\) orbital. Stability order: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > \mathrm{CH_3^+}\).
    • Carbanion: Negatively charged carbon with an octet (including a lone pair), generally \(sp^3\) hybridised, distorted tetrahedral (pyramidal) shape.

5.2 Nucleophiles and Electrophiles

  • Nucleophile (\(\mathrm{Nu:}\)): "Nucleus-seeking" electron-rich species that donates an electron pair to an electron-deficient centre. Examples: \(\mathrm{HO^-}\), \(\mathrm{CN^-}\), \(\mathrm{HS^-}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{R_3N}\).
  • Electrophile (\(\mathrm{E^+}\)): "Electron-seeking" electron-deficient species that accepts an electron pair. Examples: \(\mathrm{CH_3^+}\), \(\mathrm{NO_2^+}\), \(\mathrm{BF_3}\), \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\), carbonyl carbon (\(\mathrm{>C^{\delta+}=O^{\delta-}}\)).

5.3 Electron Displacement Effects

  1. Inductive Effect (\(I\)) [Permanent]: Polarisation of a \(\sigma\) bond caused by an adjacent polar \(\sigma\) bond due to electronegativity difference. It diminishes rapidly with distance and becomes negligible after three bonds.
    • \(-I\) (Electron-withdrawing): \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\), \(-\mathrm{CN}\), \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), \(-\mathrm{F, -Cl, -Br, -I}\), \(-\mathrm{OAr}\). Increases acidity of carboxylic acids (e.g., \(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH > Cl_2CHCOOH > ClCH_2COOH}\)).
    • \(+I\) (Electron-donating): Alkyl groups (\(-\mathrm{C(CH_3)_3 > -CH(CH_3)_2 > -CH_2CH_3 > -CH_3}\)). Decreases acidity of carboxylic acids and destabilises carbanions.
  2. Resonance / Mesomeric Effect (\(R\) or \(M\)) [Permanent]: Delocalisation of \(\pi\) electrons or lone pairs across a conjugated system (alternating single and multiple bonds). Canonical structures are hypothetical; the real molecule is a lower-energy resonance hybrid.
    • \(+R\) Groups (donate electrons into conjugated ring): \(-\mathrm{OH, -OR, -NH_2, -NHR, -NR_2, -NHCOR, -X}\) (halogens). Increase electron density at ortho and para positions.
    • \(-R\) Groups (withdraw electrons from conjugated ring): \(-\mathrm{NO_2, -CN, -CHO, >C=O, -COOH}\). Create positive charge at ortho and para positions.
    • Canonical Stability Rules: Structures with (i) more covalent bonds, (ii) complete octets, (iii) minimal charge separation, and (iv) negative charge on the more electronegative atom contribute most to the hybrid.
  3. Hyperconjugation [Permanent]: Often called no-bond resonance, it involves delocalisation of \(\sigma\) electrons of an \(\alpha\)-\(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond into an adjacent empty \(p\) orbital (carbocation) or \(\pi\) orbital (alkene/alkylarene). Greater the number of \(\alpha\)-hydrogens, greater the stability.
  4. Electromeric Effect (\(E\)) [Temporary]: Complete transfer of a \(\pi\)-electron pair to one of the doubly/triply bonded atoms only in the presence of an attacking reagent. If the \(\pi\) electrons shift toward the atom where the reagent attaches (e.g., \(\mathrm{H^+}\)), it is \(+E\); if away from the attacking atom (e.g., \(\mathrm{CN^-}\)), it is \(-E\).
Diagram 3: Parallel alignment of an α C–H σ bond with the empty 2p orbital in ethyl carbocation.

5.4 Major Classes of Organic Reactions

Organic reactions are broadly grouped into four types: Substitution (one group replaces another, e.g., \(\mathrm{R-Br + HS^- \rightarrow R-SH + Br^-}\)), Addition (reagent adds across a multiple bond), Elimination (loss of atoms/groups to form a multiple bond), and Rearrangement (migration of a group/atom within the molecule to yield a more stable isomer).

Worked Example 3: Comparing Carbocation & Carbanion Stability

Problem: Arrange (i) \(\mathrm{CH_3\dot{C}H_2}\), \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C^+}\), \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CH_2^+}\) in increasing order of carbocation stability, and (ii) explain why \(\mathrm{O_2N-CH_2-CH_2-O^-}\) is more stable than \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-O^-}\).

Solution:

  • (i) \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CH_2^+}\) is a \(1^\circ\) carbocation with 0 \(\alpha\)-hydrogens (least stable). \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2^+}\) is a \(1^\circ\) carbocation with 3 \(\alpha\)-hydrogens. \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C^+}\) is a \(3^\circ\) carbocation with 9 \(\alpha\)-hydrogens and strong \(+I\) stabilisation. Order: \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CH_2^+ < CH_3CH_2^+ < (CH_3)_3C^+}\).
  • (ii) In \(\mathrm{O_2N-CH_2-CH_2-O^-}\), the strongly electron-withdrawing \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) group (\(-I\) effect) disperses the negative charge on oxygen, stabilising the anion. Conversely, the \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\) group in \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2O^-}\) exerts a \(+I\) effect, intensifying negative charge and destabilising the alkoxide.

6. Methods of Purification of Organic Compounds

Pure organic compounds exhibit sharp melting and boiling points. Selecting a purification method depends on physical state, volatility, thermal stability, and solubility differences.

Technique Underlying Principle Classic Application
Sublimation Solid passes directly to vapour on heating without melting Separating camphor from non-sublimable \(\mathrm{CaSO_4}\)
Crystallisation Difference in solubility of compound and impurities in a hot vs. cold solvent (activated charcoal removes colour) Purifying impure solid benzoic acid or sugar
Simple Distillation Large difference in boiling points of miscible liquids Chloroform (334 K) + Aniline (457 K)
Fractional Distillation Close boiling points; repeated condensation–vaporisation in a fractionating column (theoretical plates) Separating crude oil fractions
Reduced Pressure Distillation Lowering external pressure lowers boiling point below decomposition temperature Recovering glycerol from spent-lye in soap industry
Steam Distillation Steam-volatile, water-immiscible liquid boils below 373 K when \(p = p_1 + p_2\) Aniline–water mixture
Differential Extraction Higher solubility in an immiscible organic solvent using a separatory funnel (or continuous extraction) Extracting organic acids/amines from aqueous layer

6.1 Chromatography: Adsorption vs. Partition

  • Adsorption Chromatography: Components travel at different rates over a solid adsorbent (silica gel or alumina) based on differential adsorption. Includes Column Chromatography and Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC) (0.2 mm adsorbent layer on glass). Colourless TLC spots are visualised under UV light, in an iodine chamber (brown spots), or with ninhydrin spray (for amino acids).
  • Partition Chromatography: Continuous differential partitioning between a liquid stationary phase (water trapped in cellulose paper) and a moving solvent. Paper Chromatography is a classic example.
\[\text{Retardation Factor } (R_f) = \frac{\text{Distance moved by the substance from baseline } (x)}{\text{Distance moved by the solvent front from baseline } (y)}\]

7. Qualitative & Quantitative Elemental Analysis

7.1 Qualitative Detection (CuO Test & Lassaigne's Test)

  • Carbon & Hydrogen: Heat the compound with dry \(\mathrm{CuO}\). Carbon oxidises to \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (turns lime-water milky), and hydrogen oxidises to \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (turns white anhydrous \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) blue).
  • Lassaigne's Test (Sodium Fusion Extract): Covalent N, S, and halogens are converted into water-soluble ionic salts by fusing with metallic sodium: \(\mathrm{Na + C + N \rightarrow NaCN}\); \(\mathrm{2Na + S \rightarrow Na_2S}\); \(\mathrm{Na + X \rightarrow NaX}\).

7.2 Quantitative Estimation Formulas

Let \(m\) be the mass of the organic compound taken (in g):

  • Carbon & Hydrogen (Liebig's Method): \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (\(m_1\) g) is absorbed in anhydrous \(\mathrm{CaCl_2}\), and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (\(m_2\) g) in \(\mathrm{KOH}\) solution. \[\%\mathrm{C} = \frac{12 \times m_2 \times 100}{44 \times m}, \qquad \%\mathrm{H} = \frac{2 \times m_1 \times 100}{18 \times m}\]
  • Nitrogen — Dumas Method: Compound heated with \(\mathrm{CuO}\) in \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) yields \(\mathrm{N_2}\) collected over \(\mathrm{KOH}\). Correct pressure for aqueous tension: \(p_1 = p_{\text{atm}} - \text{aqueous tension}\). Convert volume to STP (\(V\text{ mL}\)): \[V = \frac{p_1 V_1 \times 273}{760 \times T_1}, \qquad \%\mathrm{N} = \frac{28 \times V \times 100}{22400 \times m}\]
  • Nitrogen — Kjeldahl's Method: Compound digested with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) \(\rightarrow\) \(\mathrm{(NH_4)_2SO_4}\), then distilled with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) into \(V\text{ mL}\) of \(M\) molar \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\); excess acid requires \(V_1\text{ mL}\) of \(M\) molar \(\mathrm{NaOH}\): \[\%\mathrm{N} = \frac{1.4 \times M \times 2(V - V_1/2)}{m} = \frac{1.4 \times \text{mEq of }\mathrm{NH_3}}{m}\] Limitation: Not applicable to nitro (\(-\mathrm{NO_2}\)), azo (\(-\mathrm{N=N}-\)), or ring-nitrogen compounds (e.g., pyridine).
  • Halogens (Carius Method): Heated with fuming \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) + \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\) to form \(m_1\text{ g}\) of \(\mathrm{AgX}\): \[\%\mathrm{X} = \frac{\text{Atomic mass of X} \times m_1 \times 100}{\text{Molar mass of AgX} \times m}\]
  • Sulphur (Carius Method): Oxidised to \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) and precipitated as \(m_1\text{ g}\) of \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\) (molar mass 233 g mol\(^{-1}\)): \[\%\mathrm{S} = \frac{32 \times m_1 \times 100}{233 \times m}\]
  • Phosphorus: Precipitated as \(\mathrm{(NH_4)_3PO_4\cdot 12MoO_3}\) (molar mass 1877 g mol\(^{-1}\)) or ignited to \(\mathrm{Mg_2P_2O_7}\) (molar mass 222 g mol\(^{-1}\)): \[\%\mathrm{P} = \frac{31 \times m_1 \times 100}{1877 \times m} \quad \text{or} \quad \%\mathrm{P} = \frac{62 \times m_1 \times 100}{222 \times m}\]
  • Oxygen: Usually by difference (\(100 - \text{sum of }\%\text{ of other elements}\)), or directly via red-hot coke + \(\mathrm{I_2O_5}\) giving \(m_1\text{ g}\) \(\mathrm{CO_2}\): \(\%\mathrm{O} = \frac{32 \times m_1 \times 100}{88 \times m}\).
Worked Example 4: Dumas Method Calculation

Problem: In a hypothetical Dumas estimation, \(0.25\text{ g}\) of an organic compound yielded \(40\text{ mL}\) of moist nitrogen gas at \(27^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(300\text{ K}\)) and \(735\text{ mmHg}\) pressure. If the aqueous tension at \(27^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is \(15\text{ mmHg}\), calculate the percentage of nitrogen.

Solution:

  • Dry \(\mathrm{N_2}\) pressure: \(p_1 = 735 - 15 = 720\text{ mmHg}\).
  • Volume at STP: \(V = \frac{720 \times 40 \times 273}{760 \times 300} = 34.48\text{ mL}\).
  • Percentage of nitrogen: \(\%\mathrm{N} = \frac{28 \times 34.48 \times 100}{22400 \times 0.25} = 17.24\%\).
Worked Example 5: Kjeldahl's Back-Titration Calculation

Problem: Ammonia evolved from \(0.40\text{ g}\) of an organic sample (assumed data) in Kjeldahl's method was absorbed in \(40\text{ mL}\) of \(0.25\text{ M}\) \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\). The residual acid required \(30\text{ mL}\) of \(0.25\text{ M}\) \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) for complete neutralisation. Find the percentage of nitrogen.

Solution:

  • Since molarity \(M = 0.25\text{ M}\) for both acid and alkali, volume of \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) unused = \(V_1/2 = 30/2 = 15\text{ mL}\).
  • Volume of \(0.25\text{ M}\) \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) neutralised by \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) = \(40 - 15 = 25\text{ mL}\).
  • Applying the Kjeldahl formula: \[\%\mathrm{N} = \frac{1.4 \times 0.25 \times 2 \times 25}{0.40} = \frac{17.5}{0.40} = 43.75\%.\]

8. NEET UG Practice Questions & Worked Solutions

The following original practice questions are designed to match the NEET UG pattern (single-correct MCQs, statement-based MCQs, assertion–reason, and matching). Attempt each before checking the worked solution.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ — Hybridisation & \(\sigma\)/\(\pi\) Bonds)

In the molecule \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-CH_2-C\equiv CH}\), the hybridisation of \(\mathrm{C_2}\) and \(\mathrm{C_3}\) (numbering from the triple-bond end) and the total number of \(\pi\) bonds are respectively:

  1. (A) \(sp\) and \(sp^3\); 3 \(\pi\) bonds
  2. (B) \(sp^2\) and \(sp^3\); 3 \(\pi\) bonds
  3. (C) \(sp\) and \(sp^2\); 2 \(\pi\) bonds
  4. (D) \(sp^3\) and \(sp^2\); 3 \(\pi\) bonds
Answer: (A)

Solution: Numbering from the terminal alkyne end gives \(\mathrm{C_1\equiv C_2-C_3H_2-C_4H=C_5H_2}\) (alkene gets priority over alkyne in IUPAC numbering only when equidistant; wait—let us follow the question's explicit instruction "numbering from the triple-bond end": \(\mathrm{C_1\equiv C_2-C_3H_2-C_4H=C_5H_2}\)). Here, \(\mathrm{C_2}\) is triply bonded (\(sp\)), and \(\mathrm{C_3}\) has four single bonds (\(sp^3\)). Total \(\pi\) bonds = 2 (from \(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\)) + 1 (from \(\mathrm{C=C}\)) = 3.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ — IUPAC Nomenclature)

What is the correct IUPAC name of \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH(Cl)-CH_2-CH(OH)-CH_3}\)?

  1. (A) 2-Chloropentan-4-ol
  2. (B) 4-Chloropentan-2-ol
  3. (C) 4-Hydroxypentan-2-chloride
  4. (D) 2-Hydroxypentan-4-ol
Answer: (B)

Solution: The \(-\mathrm{OH}\) group is the principal functional group and must receive the lowest possible locant. Numbering from the right gives \(-\mathrm{OH}\) at C-2 and \(-\mathrm{Cl}\) (always a prefix) at C-4. Thus, the name is 4-Chloropentan-2-ol.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ — Substituted Benzene Naming)

The correct IUPAC name of 1-bromo-4-chloro-2-methylbenzene is applied to which substituent arrangement on the benzene ring?

  1. (A) \(\mathrm{Br}\) at C-1, \(\mathrm{CH_3}\) at C-2, \(\mathrm{Cl}\) at C-4
  2. (B) \(\mathrm{Cl}\) at C-1, \(\mathrm{CH_3}\) at C-3, \(\mathrm{Br}\) at C-4
  3. (C) \(\mathrm{CH_3}\) at C-1, \(\mathrm{Br}\) at C-2, \(\mathrm{Cl}\) at C-5
  4. (D) \(\mathrm{Br}\) at C-1, \(\mathrm{Cl}\) at C-2, \(\mathrm{CH_3}\) at C-4
Answer: (A)

Solution: For trisubstituted benzenes with no special base name, the lowest locant set is {1, 2, 4}. Numbering to give substituents at 1, 2, 4 places \(\mathrm{Br}\) at 1, \(\mathrm{CH_3}\) at 2, and \(\mathrm{Cl}\) at 4, and listing them alphabetically yields 1-bromo-4-chloro-2-methylbenzene.

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ — Isomerism)

Diethyl ether (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2-O-CH_2CH_3}\)) and methyl propyl ether (\(\mathrm{CH_3-O-CH_2CH_2CH_3}\)) are examples of:

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ — Carbocation Stability)

Practice Question 12 (Numerical MCQ — Quantitative Analysis)

In a Carius estimation, \(0.20\text{ g}\) of an organic bromo compound (hypothetical value) yielded \(0.188\text{ g}\) of \(\mathrm{AgBr}\). What is the percentage of bromine in the compound? (Atomic masses: \(\mathrm{Ag} = 108\), \(\mathrm{Br} = 80\))

  1. (A) 20%
  2. (B) 40%
  3. (C) 60%
  4. (D) 80%
Answer: (B)

Solution: Molar mass of \(\mathrm{AgBr} = 108 + 80 = 188\text{ g mol}^{-1}\). \[\%\mathrm{Br} = \frac{80 \times 0.188 \times 100}{188 \times 0.20} = \frac{80 \times 0.1}{0.20} = 40\%.\]

9. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Alphabetisation Error: Never include di-, tri-, sec-, or tert- when alphabetising substituents, but do include iso- and neo- (e.g., isopropyl is alphabetised under i, whereas tert-butyl is under b).
  • Acidity vs. Alkyl Substituents: Alkyl groups destabilise carboxylate anions via \(+I\) effect, so \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CCOOH}\) is weaker than \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2COOH}\).
  • Lassaigne's Halogen Interference: Always boil the sodium fusion extract with conc. \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) before adding \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\) if N or S is present; otherwise \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) and \(\mathrm{S^{2-}}\) form interfering precipitates (\(\mathrm{AgCN}\), \(\mathrm{Ag_2S}\)).
  • Acidification for Lead Acetate Test: Use acetic acid (not \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)) to acidify the extract before adding lead acetate, because \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) would precipitate white \(\mathrm{PbSO_4}\).
  • CCl4 with AgNO3: Covalent \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) does not ionise to give \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) ions, so it gives no precipitate on heating with \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\) unless first fused with sodium.

Pre-Exam Revision Checklist

  • Can determine \(sp^3\)/\(sp^2\)/\(sp\) hybridisation and count \(\sigma\)/\(\pi\) bonds in under 30 seconds.
  • Memorised the IUPAC functional-group priority table from \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) down to alkynes.
  • Can rank carbocations and free radicals using \(\alpha\)-hydrogen counts (hyperconjugation) and \(\pm I\) / \(\pm R\) effects.
  • Know the exact conditions for steam distillation, reduced-pressure distillation, and TLC \(R_f\) calculation.
  • Memorised the formulas and limitations for Dumas, Kjeldahl, and Carius quantitative estimations.

10. Concise Final Recap

Structure & Bonding: Carbon forms \(sp^3\) (tetrahedral, 25% \(s\)), \(sp^2\) (trigonal planar, 33.3% \(s\)), and \(sp\) (linear, 50% \(s\)) hybrids; higher \(s\)-character increases electronegativity and bond strength while shortening bond length. \(\pi\) bonds restrict rotation and serve as reactive centres.

Mechanisms & Effects: Homolysis yields radicals; heterolysis yields planar \(sp^2\) carbocations or pyramidal \(sp^3\) carbanions. Permanent effects (\(I\), \(R/M\), hyperconjugation) govern ground-state stability and acidity/basicity, while the temporary \(E\) effect operates on reagent approach.

Purification & Analysis: Physical separation relies on volatility and phase partitioning (distillation variants, extraction, chromatography). Lassaigne's sodium fusion converts covalent N, S, X into ionic \(\mathrm{CN^-}\), \(\mathrm{S^{2-}}\), \(\mathrm{X^-}\) for qualitative detection, while Liebig, Dumas, Kjeldahl, and Carius methods provide exact elemental mass percentages.
