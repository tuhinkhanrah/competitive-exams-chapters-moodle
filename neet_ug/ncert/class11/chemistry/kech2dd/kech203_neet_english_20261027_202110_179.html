1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

After studying this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Name hydrocarbons systematically according to the IUPAC system of nomenclature and recognise structural, chain, position, geometrical, and conformational isomers.
  • Understand laboratory and industrial methods of preparation of alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, and benzene.
  • Distinguish between alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, and aromatic hydrocarbons on the basis of physical and chemical properties.
  • Draw and differentiate between eclipsed, staggered, and skew conformations of ethane using Sawhorse and Newman projections.
  • Predict addition products of unsymmetrical alkenes and alkynes using Markovnikov's rule and the anti-Markovnikov peroxide (Kharash) effect based on electronic mechanisms.
  • Comprehend the resonance structure of benzene, apply Hückel's \((4n + 2)\,\pi\) rule for aromaticity, and explain the mechanism of electrophilic aromatic substitution (\(S_E\)).
  • Predict the directive influence and activating/deactivating nature of substituents in monosubstituted benzene rings, and understand the carcinogenicity of polynuclear hydrocarbons.

Industrial & Everyday Importance of Hydrocarbons

The term hydrocarbon means compounds composed of carbon and hydrogen only. They are the principal sources of energy and industrial raw materials:

  • LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas): Domestic fuel causing the least pollution.
  • CNG (Compressed Natural Gas) & LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas): Natural gas found in upper strata during drilling of oil wells is compressed to form CNG or liquefied to form LNG.
  • Fractional Distillation of Petroleum: Yields petrol, diesel, and kerosene oil. Petrol and CNG vehicles cause less pollution. Lower petroleum fractions are used for dry cleaning clothes to remove non-polar grease stains.
  • Destructive Distillation of Coal: Yields coal gas and coal tar (commercial source of benzene).
  • Polymers & Chemicals: Starting materials for polymers (polythene, polypropene, polystyrene), solvents for paints, and synthesis of dyes and drugs.

2. Classification of Hydrocarbons

Depending upon the types of carbon-carbon bonds present, hydrocarbons are classified into three main categories:

  1. Saturated Hydrocarbons: Contain only carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen single (\(\sigma\)) bonds.
    • Alkanes (Paraffins): Open-chain saturated hydrocarbons with general formula \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2}\).
    • Cycloalkanes (Alicyclic): Carbon atoms form a closed chain or ring.
  2. Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Contain carbon-carbon multiple bonds — double bonds (alkenes, \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n}\)), triple bonds (alkynes, \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}\)), or both.
  3. Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Arenes): Special cyclic compounds containing delocalised \(\pi\)-electron clouds (benzenoids and non-benzenoids).

3. Alkanes (Saturated Open-Chain Hydrocarbons)

3.1 Structure, Bonding, and Homologous Series

Alkanes are inert under normal conditions towards acids, bases, and other reagents; hence they were earlier called paraffins (Latin: parum = little, affinis = affinity). Their general formula is \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2}\).

  • Methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)): Found in coal mines and marshy places. According to VSEPR theory, methane has a regular tetrahedral structure with carbon at the centre and four hydrogens at the four corners. All \(\mathrm{H-C-H}\) bond angles are \(109.5^\circ\).
  • Orbital Overlap & Bond Lengths: \(\mathrm{C-C}\) and \(\mathrm{C-H}\) \(\sigma\) bonds are formed by head-on overlapping of \(sp^3\) hybrid orbitals of carbon and \(1s\) orbitals of hydrogen. In alkanes, the \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond length is \(154\,\mathrm{pm}\) and the \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond length is \(112\,\mathrm{pm}\).
  • Classification of Carbon Atoms:
    • Primary (\(1^\circ\)): Attached to no other carbon (as in \(\mathrm{CH_4}\)) or to only 1 carbon atom (terminal carbons are always \(1^\circ\)).
    • Secondary (\(2^\circ\)): Attached to 2 carbon atoms.
    • Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)): Attached to 3 carbon atoms.
    • Quaternary (\(4^\circ\) or neo): Attached to 4 carbon atoms.
  • Alkyl Groups: Derived by removing one hydrogen atom from an alkane; general formula \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+1}\) (e.g., \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\) methyl, \(-\mathrm{C_2H_5}\) ethyl, \(-\mathrm{C_3H_7}\) propyl).

3.2 Nomenclature & Chain Isomerism in Alkanes

Methane, ethane, and propane have only one structure each. Higher alkanes exhibit structural (chain) isomerism due to differences in the carbon chain skeleton:

  • Butane (\(\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}\)) has 2 chain isomers:
    1. \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-CH_2-CH_3}\): Butane (n-butane), b.p. \(273\,\mathrm{K}\) (or \(272.4\,\mathrm{K}\)).
    2. \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH(CH_3)-CH_3}\): 2-Methylpropane (isobutane), b.p. \(261\,\mathrm{K}\).
  • Pentane (\(\mathrm{C_5H_{12}}\)) has 3 chain isomers:
    1. \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-CH_2-CH_2-CH_3}\): Pentane (n-pentane), b.p. \(309\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(309.1\,\mathrm{K}\)).
    2. \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH(CH_3)-CH_2-CH_3}\): 2-Methylbutane (isopentane), b.p. \(301\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(300.9\,\mathrm{K}\)).
    3. \(\mathrm{CH_3-C(CH_3)_2-CH_3}\): 2,2-Dimethylpropane (neopentane), b.p. \(282.5\,\mathrm{K}\).
  • Number of Chain Isomers in Higher Alkanes: \(\mathrm{C_6H_{14}}\) has 5 isomers, \(\mathrm{C_7H_{16}}\) has 9 isomers, and \(\mathrm{C_{10}H_{22}}\) has 75 possible isomers.

3.4 Methods of Preparation of Alkanes

  1. Hydrogenation of Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Dihydrogen gas adds to alkenes and alkynes in the presence of finely divided catalysts like \(\mathrm{Pt}\), \(\mathrm{Pd}\), or \(\mathrm{Ni}\). \(\mathrm{Pt}\) and \(\mathrm{Pd}\) catalyse the reaction at room temperature, whereas \(\mathrm{Ni}\) requires relatively higher temperature and pressure (Sabatier-Senderens reaction).
    \[\mathrm{CH_2=CH_2 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} CH_3-CH_3}\] \[\mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv C-H + 2H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} CH_3-CH_2-CH_3}\]
  2. From Alkyl Halides:
    • Reduction with Zinc and Dilute Hydrochloric Acid: Alkyl halides (except fluorides, as \(\mathrm{C-F}\) bond is very strong) undergo reduction to alkanes: \[\mathrm{R-Cl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Zn, H^+} R-H + HCl}\]
    • Wurtz Reaction: Alkyl halides on treatment with metallic sodium in dry ether (free from moisture, as sodium reacts violently with water) give higher alkanes containing an even number of carbon atoms: \[\mathrm{2R-Br + 2Na \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} R-R + 2NaBr}\] Limitation: If two different alkyl halides (\(\mathrm{R-X}\) and \(\mathrm{R'-X}\)) are used to prepare an alkane with an odd number of carbon atoms, a mixture of three alkanes (\(\mathrm{R-R}\), \(\mathrm{R'-R'}\), and \(\mathrm{R-R'}\)) is formed, which have close boiling points and are difficult to separate. Methane cannot be prepared by Wurtz reaction.
  3. From Carboxylic Acids:
    • Decarboxylation with Soda Lime: Heating sodium salts of carboxylic acids with soda lime (a mixture of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) and \(\mathrm{CaO}\)) yields an alkane with one carbon atom less than the parent acid: \[\mathrm{R-COO^-Na^+ + NaOH \xrightarrow[\Delta]{CaO} R-H + Na_2CO_3}\] For example, preparing propane (\(\mathrm{C_3H_8}\)) requires the sodium salt of butanoic acid (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2COO^-Na^+}\)).
    • Kolbe's Electrolytic Method: Electrolysis of a concentrated aqueous solution of sodium or potassium salt of a carboxylic acid gives an alkane containing an even number of carbon atoms at the anode: \[\mathrm{2CH_3COO^-Na^+ + 2H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{Electrolysis}} CH_3-CH_3\,(anode) + 2CO_2\,(anode) + H_2\,(cathode) + 2NaOH}\] Mechanism:
      • At Anode (Oxidation via free radicals): \(\mathrm{2CH_3COO^- \xrightarrow{-2e^-} 2CH_3COO^\bullet \longrightarrow 2^\bullet CH_3 + 2CO_2\uparrow}\); then \(\mathrm{^\bullet CH_3 + ^\bullet CH_3 \longrightarrow CH_3-CH_3\uparrow}\).
      • At Cathode (Reduction): \(\mathrm{2H_2O + 2e^- \longrightarrow 2OH^- + 2H^\bullet \longrightarrow H_2\uparrow}\).
      • Note: Methane cannot be prepared by this method because the radical coupling step requires at least two \(\mathrm{^\bullet CH_3}\) radicals, giving a minimum of two carbons (ethane).

3.5 Physical Properties of Alkanes

  • Nature & Solubility: Alkanes are non-polar molecules possessing weak intermolecular van der Waals forces. They are hydrophobic (insoluble in water) and soluble in non-polar organic solvents ("like dissolves like").
  • Physical State at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): \(\mathrm{C_1}\) to \(\mathrm{C_4}\) are colourless, odourless gases; \(\mathrm{C_5}\) to \(\mathrm{C_{17}}\) are liquids; \(\mathrm{C_{18}}\) onwards are solids.
  • Boiling Point Trends: Boiling point increases steadily with molecular mass due to increased surface area and stronger van der Waals forces. Among isomeric alkanes, branching decreases the boiling point because branched molecules become more spherical, reducing surface area of contact and weakening van der Waals forces.
  • Melting Point Trends: Symmetrical isomers pack more tightly in the crystal lattice and have unusually high melting points (e.g., 2,2-dimethylpropane melts at \(256.4\,\mathrm{K}\) compared to \(143.3\,\mathrm{K}\) for n-pentane and \(113.1\,\mathrm{K}\) for isopentane).

3.6 Chemical Properties of Alkanes

  1. Free Radical Substitution (Halogenation): Takes place at high temperature (\(573\text{–}773\,\mathrm{K}\)) or in diffused sunlight / UV light (\(h\nu\)).
    • Successive chlorination of methane yields \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\) → \(\mathrm{CH_2Cl_2}\) → \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) → \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\).
    • Reactivity of Halogens: \(\mathrm{F_2 > Cl_2 > Br_2 > I_2}\). Fluorination is too violent to control. Iodination is slow and reversible (\(\mathrm{CH_4 + I_2 \rightleftharpoons CH_3I + HI}\)), so it must be carried out in the presence of oxidising agents like \(\mathrm{HIO_3}\) or \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) which destroy \(\mathrm{HI}\) (\(\mathrm{HIO_3 + 5HI \rightarrow 3I_2 + 3H_2O}\)) and shift the equilibrium forward.
    • Rate of Replacement of Hydrogen Atoms: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\) (due to stability order of alkyl free radicals).
    • Free Radical Chain Mechanism:
      • Initiation: Homolysis of \(\mathrm{Cl-Cl}\) bond (weaker than \(\mathrm{C-C}\) and \(\mathrm{C-H}\)): \(\mathrm{Cl_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2Cl^\bullet}\).
      • Propagation: \(\mathrm{CH_4 + Cl^\bullet \xrightarrow{h\nu} ^\bullet CH_3 + HCl}\) followed by \(\mathrm{^\bullet CH_3 + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} CH_3Cl + Cl^\bullet}\).
      • Termination: Combination of free radicals: \(\mathrm{Cl^\bullet + Cl^\bullet \rightarrow Cl_2}\); \(\mathrm{^\bullet CH_3 + Cl^\bullet \rightarrow CH_3Cl}\); and \(\mathrm{^\bullet CH_3 + ^\bullet CH_3 \rightarrow CH_3-CH_3}\). This last step explains why ethane is formed as a minor byproduct during the chlorination of methane.
  2. Combustion: Complete oxidation in air/dioxygen gives \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), and heat:
    \[\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2} + \left(\frac{3n+1}{2}\right)\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow n\,\mathrm{CO_2} + (n+1)\,\mathrm{H_2O}\] \[\mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l)}; \quad \Delta_c H^\circ = -890\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\] \[\mathrm{C_4H_{10}(g) + \frac{13}{2}O_2(g) \rightarrow 4CO_2(g) + 5H_2O(l)}; \quad \Delta_c H^\circ = -2875.84\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\]
    Incomplete Combustion: In insufficient air, alkanes form carbon black (\(\mathrm{CH_4 + O_2 \rightarrow C(s) + 2H_2O}\)), used in ink, printer ink, black pigments, and filters.
  3. Controlled Catalytic Oxidation:
    • \(\mathrm{2CH_4 + O_2 \xrightarrow{Cu / 523\,K / 100\,atm} 2CH_3OH}\) (Methanol)
    • \(\mathrm{CH_4 + O_2 \xrightarrow{Mo_2O_3, \Delta} HCHO + H_2O}\) (Methanal)
    • \(\mathrm{2CH_3CH_3 + 3O_2 \xrightarrow{(CH_3COO)_2Mn, \Delta} 2CH_3COOH + 2H_2O}\) (Ethanoic acid)
    • Alkanes ordinarily resist oxidation, but those having a tertiary (\(\mathrm{3^\circ}\)) \(\mathrm{H}\) atom are oxidised by \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) to corresponding \(3^\circ\) alcohols: \[\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CH \xrightarrow{KMnO_4\text{ (Oxidation)}} (CH_3)_3COH}\quad(\text{2-Methylpropan-2-ol})\]
  4. Isomerisation: Heating n-alkanes with anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\) gas converts them into branched-chain isomers (e.g., n-hexane isomerises into 2-methylpentane and 3-methylpentane).
  5. Aromatization (Reforming): Heating n-alkanes with six or more carbons at \(773\,\mathrm{K}\) under \(10\text{–}20\,\mathrm{atm}\) pressure in the presence of oxides of vanadium (\(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\)), molybdenum (\(\mathrm{Mo_2O_3}\)), or chromium (\(\mathrm{Cr_2O_3}\)) supported over alumina (\(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\)) causes dehydrogenation and cyclisation to benzene and its homologues (e.g., n-hexane → benzene + \(4\mathrm{H_2}\)).
  6. Reaction with Steam: At \(1273\,\mathrm{K}\) in the presence of \(\mathrm{Ni}\) catalyst, methane reacts with steam to form carbon monoxide and dihydrogen (industrial preparation of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) gas): \[\mathrm{CH_4 + H_2O \xrightarrow[1273\,K]{Ni} CO + 3H_2}\]
  7. Pyrolysis (Cracking): Thermal decomposition of higher alkanes into lower alkanes and alkenes on heating to \(773\,\mathrm{K}\) (or in the presence of \(\mathrm{Pd/Pt/Ni}\) catalysts). For example,hexane cracks at \(773\,\mathrm{K}\) to give \(\mathrm{C_6H_{12} + H_2}\), \(\mathrm{C_4H_8 + C_2H_6}\), and \(\mathrm{C_3H_6 + C_2H_4 + CH_4}\). Dodecane (\(\mathrm{C_{12}H_{26}}\), a constituent of kerosene oil) heated to \(973\,\mathrm{K}\) over \(\mathrm{Pt/Pd/Ni}\) gives heptane (\(\mathrm{C_7H_{16}}\)), pentene (\(\mathrm{C_5H_{10}}\)), and other products.

3.7 Conformations of Ethane

Because the electron distribution of a \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\sigma\) bond is cylindrically symmetrical around the internuclear axis, rotation around the \(\mathrm{C-C}\) single bond is permissible. Different spatial arrangements of atoms that can be converted into one another by rotation around a \(\mathrm{C-C}\) single bond are called conformations, conformers, or rotamers.

  • Torsional Strain: Rotation is not completely free; it is hindered by a small energy barrier of \(1\text{–}20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) (\(12.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) in ethane) due to weak repulsive interactions between adjacent bond electron clouds. This repulsion is called torsional strain.
  • Eclipsed Conformation: Hydrogen atoms on the two carbons are as close together as possible (dihedral angle \(\theta = 0^\circ\)). Maximum torsional strain, highest energy, least stable.
  • Staggered Conformation: Hydrogen atoms are as far apart as possible (dihedral angle \(\theta = 60^\circ\)). Minimum torsional strain, lowest energy, most stable.
  • Skew Conformations: All infinite intermediate conformations between eclipsed and staggered.
  • Note: Bond angles and bond lengths remain identical in all conformations. Because the \(12.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) barrier is easily overcome by thermal collisions at room temperature, conformers of ethane cannot be isolated separately.

4. Alkenes (Olefins)

4.1 Structure of the Double Bond

Alkenes have the general formula \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n}\) and are known as olefins (oil-forming) because ethene (\(\mathrm{C_2H_4}\)) forms an oily liquid on reaction with chlorine. Methene (\(\mathrm{CH_2}\)) has a very short life; ethene is the first stable member.

  • Bonding & Enthalpy: The \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (total bond enthalpy \(\approx 681\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), compared to \(348\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) for \(\mathrm{C-C}\) in ethane) consists of:
    • One strong sigma (\(\sigma\)) bond (bond enthalpy \(\approx 397\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) formed by head-on overlapping of \(sp^2\) hybridised orbitals.
    • One weak pi (\(\pi\)) bond (bond enthalpy \(\approx 284\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) formed by lateral (sideways) overlapping of unhybridised \(2p\) orbitals.
  • Geometry of Ethene: Planar molecule; \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond length = \(134\,\mathrm{pm}\), \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond length = \(110\,\mathrm{pm}\), \(\mathrm{H-C-H}\) bond angle = \(116.6^\circ\), and \(\mathrm{H-C=C}\) bond angle = \(121.7^\circ\).
  • Reactivity: Loosely held mobile \(\pi\) electrons make alkenes rich sources of electrons that are readily attacked by electron-deficient electrophilic reagents.

4.2 Structural & Geometrical Isomerism in Alkenes

  • Structural Isomerism in \(\mathrm{C_4H_8}\):
    1. \(\mathrm{^1CH_2=^2CH-^3CH_2-^4CH_3}\) (But-1-ene)
    2. \(\mathrm{^1CH_3-^2CH=^3CH-^4CH_3}\) (But-2-ene)
    3. \(\mathrm{^1CH_2=^2C(CH_3)-^3CH_3}\) (2-Methylprop-1-ene)
    Here, (1) and (2) are position isomers, while (1) & (3) and (2) & (3) are chain isomers.
  • Geometrical (cis-trans) Isomerism: Restricted rotation around the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond gives rise to stereoisomers when each doubly bonded carbon is attached to two different atoms or groups (\(\mathrm{YXC=CXY}\), \(\mathrm{XYC=CXZ}\), or \(\mathrm{XYC=CZW}\)).
    • cis-Isomer: Identical atoms/groups lie on the same side of the double bond. In cis-but-2-ene, the two \(\mathrm{C-CH_3}\) bond dipoles reinforce each other, giving a net dipole moment \(\mu = 0.33\,\mathrm{D}\) and a higher boiling point (\(277\,\mathrm{K}\)) due to dipole-dipole interactions.
    • trans-Isomer: Identical atoms/groups lie on opposite sides of the double bond. In trans-but-2-ene, the two \(\mathrm{C-CH_3}\) bond dipoles cancel out (\(\mu = 0\)), giving a lower boiling point (\(274\,\mathrm{K}\)) but a higher melting point due to better crystal packing in the solid state.

4.3 Preparation of Alkenes

  1. Partial Reduction of Alkynes (Stereospecific):
    • Using Lindlar's Catalyst: Reduction of an internal alkyne (\(\mathrm{RC\equiv CR'}\)) with \(\mathrm{H_2}\) in the presence of palladised charcoal (\(\mathrm{Pd/C}\)) partially deactivated with poisons like sulphur compounds or quinoline gives a cis-alkene (syn-addition).
    • Using Sodium in Liquid Ammonia (Birch Reduction): Reduction of an internal alkyne with \(\mathrm{Na}\) in liquid \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) gives a trans-alkene (anti-addition).
  2. Dehydrohalogenation of Alkyl Halides (\(\beta\)-Elimination): Heating an alkyl halide with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) eliminates \(\mathrm{HX}\) (\(-\mathrm{X}\) from \(\alpha\)-carbon and \(-\mathrm{H}\) from \(\beta\)-carbon): \[\mathrm{R-CH_2-CH_2-X \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{alc. KOH}} R-CH=CH_2 + HX}\]
    • Reactivity order of halogens: \(\mathrm{I > Br > Cl}\)
    • Reactivity order of alkyl groups: \(\mathrm{Tertiary\,(3^\circ) > Secondary\,(2^\circ) > Primary\,(1^\circ)}\)
  3. Dehalogenation of Vicinal Dihalides: Dihalides having two halogen atoms on adjacent carbon atoms (vicinal dihalides) react with \(\mathrm{Zn}\) metal to lose \(\mathrm{ZnX_2}\) and form an alkene: \[\mathrm{CH_3-CHBr-CH_2Br + Zn \longrightarrow CH_3-CH=CH_2 + ZnBr_2}\]
  4. Acidic Dehydration of Alcohols (\(\beta\)-Elimination): Heating alcohols with concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) eliminates a water molecule: \[\mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-OH \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{Conc. }H_2SO_4} CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O}\]

4.4 Physical & Chemical Properties of Alkenes

Physical Properties: \(\mathrm{C_2}\) to \(\mathrm{C_4}\) are gases, the next fourteen (\(\mathrm{C_5\text{–}C_{18}}\)) are liquids, and higher ones are solids. Ethene has a faint sweet smell; all other alkenes are colourless and odourless. Every added \(-\mathrm{CH_2}-\) group increases the boiling point by \(20\text{–}30\,\mathrm{K}\).

Chemical Reactions of Alkenes:

  1. Addition of Halogens (Test for Unsaturation): \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) adds across the double bond via a cyclic halonium ion intermediate to give vicinal dihalides. (Iodine does not react under normal conditions). The discharge of the reddish-orange colour of bromine solution in \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) serves as a laboratory test for unsaturation: \[\mathrm{CH_2=CH_2 + Br_2 \xrightarrow{CCl_4} BrCH_2-CH_2Br}\quad(\text{1,2-Dibromoethane})\]
  2. Addition of Hydrogen Halides (\(\mathrm{HI > HBr > HCl}\)):
    • Markovnikov's Rule (Electrophilic Addition): When an unsymmetrical reagent adds to an unsymmetrical alkene in the absence of peroxide, the negative part of the addendum gets attached to the carbon atom possessing the lesser number of hydrogen atoms.
      Mechanism: \(\mathrm{H^+}\) attacks propene (\(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH_2}\)) to form the more stable secondary carbocation (\(\mathrm{CH_3-\overset{+}{C}H-CH_3}\)) rather than the less stable primary carbocation (\(\mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-\overset{+}{C}H_2}\)). Attack of \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) on the \(2^\circ\) carbocation gives 2-bromopropane as the major product.
    • Anti-Markovnikov Addition / Peroxide Effect / Kharash Effect (Free Radical Mechanism): In the presence of benzoyl peroxide \(\mathrm{(C_6H_5CO)_2O_2}\), addition of \(\mathrm{HBr}\) only to unsymmetrical alkenes occurs contrary to Markovnikov's rule, yielding 1-bromopropane from propene.
      Mechanism:
      1. \(\mathrm{(C_6H_5CO)_2O_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Homolysis}} 2C_6H_5COO^\bullet \longrightarrow 2^\bullet C_6H_5 + 2CO_2}\)
      2. \(\mathrm{^\bullet C_6H_5 + H-Br \xrightarrow{\text{Homolysis}} C_6H_6 + Br^\bullet}\)
      3. \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH_2 + Br^\bullet \longrightarrow CH_3-\overset{\bullet}{C}H-CH_2Br}\) (more stable \(2^\circ\) free radical, major pathway) vs \(\mathrm{CH_3-CHBr-\overset{\bullet}{C}H_2}\) (less stable \(1^\circ\) radical).
      4. \(\mathrm{CH_3-\overset{\bullet}{C}H-CH_2Br + H-Br \longrightarrow CH_3-CH_2-CH_2Br + Br^\bullet}\) (1-Bromopropane, major product).
      Why only \(\mathrm{HBr}\)? The \(\mathrm{H-Cl}\) bond (\(430.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is stronger than the \(\mathrm{H-Br}\) bond (\(363.7\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) and cannot be cleaved by the phenyl radical. The \(\mathrm{H-I}\) bond (\(296.8\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) is weak, but the resulting iodine free radicals (\(\mathrm{I^\bullet}\)) rapidly combine to form \(\mathrm{I_2}\) molecules instead of adding to the double bond.
  3. Addition of Cold Concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\): Follows Markovnikov's rule to form alkyl hydrogen sulphates (\(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH_2 + HOSO_2OH \rightarrow CH_3-CH(OSO_2OH)-CH_3}\)).
  4. Acid-Catalysed Hydration: Addition of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) in the presence of a few drops of conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) follows Markovnikov's rule (e.g., 2-methylpropene gives 2-methylpropan-2-ol).
  5. Oxidation of Alkenes:
    • Cold, dilute, aqueous \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) (Baeyer's Reagent at \(273\,\mathrm{K}\)): Oxidises alkenes to vicinal glycols (1,2-diols). Decolourisation of the purple \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) solution is a test for unsaturation: \[\mathrm{CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O + (O) \xrightarrow[273\,K]{\text{dil. }KMnO_4} HO-CH_2-CH_2-OH}\quad(\text{Ethane-1,2-diol})\]
    • Acidic \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) or Acidic \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7}\): Cleaves the double bond to give ketones and/or carboxylic acids (or \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) for terminal \(=\mathrm{CH_2}\)): \[\mathrm{(CH_3)_2C=CH_2 \xrightarrow{KMnO_4/H^+} (CH_3)_2C=O + CO_2 + H_2O}\] \[\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CH_3 \xrightarrow{KMnO_4/H^+} 2CH_3COOH}\]
  6. Reductive Ozonolysis (\(\mathrm{O_3}\) followed by \(\mathrm{Zn + H_2O}\)): Ozone adds across the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) bond to form an ozonide, which is cleaved by \(\mathrm{Zn/H_2O}\) into aldehydes and/or ketones. Shortcut: Replace the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond with two \(\mathrm{C=O}\) double bonds (\(\mathrm{>C=C< \xrightarrow{1.\,O_3\;\;2.\,Zn/H_2O} >C=O + O=C<}\)). Used to locate the position of double bonds.
  7. Polymerisation: At high temperature, high pressure, and in the presence of a catalyst, ethene polymerises to polythene \(\mathrm{-(CH_2-CH_2)}_n-\) and propene polymerises to polypropene \(\mathrm{-(CH(CH_3)-CH_2)}_n-\).

5. Alkynes

5.1 Structure, Nomenclature, and Isomerism

Alkynes contain at least one carbon-carbon triple bond (\(\mathrm{-C\equiv C-}\)) and have the general formula \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}\). The first stable member is ethyne (\(\mathrm{C_2H_2}\)), commonly called acetylene (used in oxy-acetylene flames for welding).

  • Structure of the Triple Bond in Ethyne: Each carbon atom is \(sp\) hybridised (\(50\%\) \(s\)-character). Ethyne is a linear molecule with an \(\mathrm{H-C-C}\) bond angle of \(180^\circ\).
    • Contains one \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\sigma\) bond (\(sp\text{–}sp\) head-on overlap), two \(\mathrm{C-H}\) \(\sigma\) bonds (\(sp\text{–}1s\) overlap), and two mutually perpendicular \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\pi\) bonds (\(2p_x\text{–}2p_x\) and \(2p_y\text{–}2p_y\) lateral overlap), forming a cylindrically symmetrical \(\pi\)-electron cloud around the internuclear axis.
    • Bond Enthalpy: \(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\) (\(823\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) > \(\mathrm{C=C}\) (\(681\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) > \(\mathrm{C-C}\) (\(348\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)).
    • Bond Length: \(\mathrm{C\equiv C}\) (\(120\,\mathrm{pm}\)) < \(\mathrm{C=C}\) (\(133\,\mathrm{pm}\)) < \(\mathrm{C-C}\) (\(154\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Isomerism: Ethyne and propyne have only one structure. Butyne (\(\mathrm{C_4H_6}\)) has two position isomers: but-1-yne (\(\mathrm{HC\equiv C-CH_2CH_3}\)) and but-2-yne (\(\mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv C-CH_3}\)).

5.2 Preparation of Alkynes

  1. From Calcium Carbide (Industrial Preparation of Ethyne): Limestone is heated to quicklime, which is heated with coke to give calcium carbide (\(\mathrm{CaC_2}\)). Hydrolysis of \(\mathrm{CaC_2}\) yields ethyne:
    \[\mathrm{CaCO_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} CaO + CO_2}\] \[\mathrm{CaO + 3C \longrightarrow CaC_2 + CO}\] \[\mathrm{CaC_2 + 2H_2O \longrightarrow Ca(OH)_2 + C_2H_2}\]
  2. From Vicinal Dihalides (Double Dehydrohalogenation): Treatment of 1,2-dibromoethane with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) removes one molecule of \(\mathrm{HBr}\) to form a vinylic halide (bromoethene, \(\mathrm{CH_2=CHBr}\)), which requires the stronger base sodamide (\(\mathrm{Na^+NH_2^-}\)) to eliminate the second \(\mathrm{HBr}\) molecule and yield ethyne: \[\mathrm{BrCH_2-CH_2Br \xrightarrow[-KBr, -H_2O]{\text{alc. KOH}} CH_2=CHBr \xrightarrow[-NaBr, -NH_3]{NaNH_2} HC\equiv CH}\]

5.3 Acidic Character & Chemical Properties of Alkynes

  1. Acidic Nature of Terminal Alkynes: Hydrogen atoms attached directly to \(sp\) hybridised triply bonded carbon atoms (\(\mathrm{-C\equiv C-H}\)) are acidic because \(sp\) hybridised carbon has the highest percentage of \(s\)-character (\(50\%\)) and therefore the highest electronegativity, attracting the shared \(\mathrm{C-H}\) electron pair strongly and facilitating proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) release.
    • Terminal alkynes react with sodium metal or sodamide (\(\mathrm{NaNH_2}\)) to form sodium acetylides/alkynides and liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) or \(\mathrm{NH_3}\): \[\mathrm{HC\equiv CH + Na \longrightarrow HC\equiv C^-Na^+ + \frac{1}{2}H_2\uparrow \xrightarrow{+Na} Na^+C^-\equiv C^-Na^+ + \frac{1}{2}H_2\uparrow}\] \[\mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv C-H + Na^+NH_2^- \longrightarrow CH_3-C\equiv C^-Na^+ + NH_3}\]
    • Distinction Test: Alkenes, alkanes, and non-terminal internal alkynes (like but-2-yne) do not show this reaction.
    • Acidic Strength Order:
      • \(\mathrm{HC\equiv CH\;(sp, 50\%\,s) > H_2C=CH_2\;(sp^2, 33.3\%\,s) > CH_3-CH_3\;(sp^3, 25\%\,s)}\)
      • \(\mathrm{HC\equiv CH > CH_3-C\equiv CH \gg CH_3-C\equiv C-CH_3}\)
  2. Electrophilic Addition Reactions (via Vinylic Cation):
    • Addition of \(\mathrm{H_2}\): \(\mathrm{RC\equiv CH \xrightarrow{H_2, Pt/Pd/Ni} [RCH=CH_2] \xrightarrow{H_2} RCH_2CH_3}\).
    • Addition of Halogens (\(\mathrm{Br_2/CCl_4}\)): Decolourises reddish-orange \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) to give a 1,1,2,2-tetrabromoalkane (\(\mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv CH + 2Br_2 \rightarrow CH_3-CBr_2-CHBr_2}\)).
    • Addition of Hydrogen Halides (\(\mathrm{HX}\)): Two equivalents of \(\mathrm{HX}\) add according to Markovnikov's rule to form geminal dihalides (both halogens on the same carbon): \[\mathrm{HC\equiv CH \xrightarrow{HBr} [CH_2=CHBr] \xrightarrow{HBr} CH_3-CHBr_2}\quad(\text{1,1-Dibromoethane})\] \[\mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv CH \xrightarrow{HBr} [CH_3-CBr=CH_2] \xrightarrow{HBr} CH_3-CBr_2-CH_3}\quad(\text{2,2-Dibromopropane})\]
    • Addition of Water (Kucherov Hydration): Warming alkynes with mercuric sulphate (\(\mathrm{Hg^{2+}}\)) and dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) at \(333\,\mathrm{K}\) adds one molecule of water to form an unstable enol that tautomerises (isomerises) into a carbonyl compound. Only ethyne gives an aldehyde (ethanal); all other alkynes give ketones: \[\mathrm{HC\equiv CH + H-OH \xrightarrow[333\,K]{Hg^{2+}/H^+} [CH_2=CH-OH] \xrightarrow{\text{Isomerisation}} CH_3-CHO}\quad(\text{Ethanal})\] \[\mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv CH + H-OH \xrightarrow[333\,K]{Hg^{2+}/H^+} [CH_3-C(OH)=CH_2] \xrightarrow{\text{Isomerisation}} CH_3-CO-CH_3}\quad(\text{Propanone})\]
  3. Polymerisation of Ethyne:
    • Linear Polymerisation: Produces polyacetylene \(\mathrm{-(CH=CH-CH=CH)}_n-\), a conducting polymer used as lightweight, inexpensive electrodes in batteries.
    • Cyclic Polymerisation: Passing ethyne through a red-hot iron (\(\mathrm{Fe}\)) tube at \(873\,\mathrm{K}\) trimerises three molecules of ethyne into benzene (\(\mathrm{3C_2H_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Red hot Fe tube, }873\,K} C_6H_6}\)).

6. Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Arenes)

6.1 Structure of Benzene, Resonance, and Aromaticity

Benzene (\(\mathrm{C_6H_6}\)) was isolated by Michael Faraday in 1825, and its cyclic structure with alternating single and double bonds was proposed by Friedrich August Kekulé in 1865.

  • Evidence for Kekulé Structure & Resonance:
    • Benzene forms a triozonide, confirming three double bonds.
    • It yields only one monosubstituted derivative (all 6 carbons and 6 hydrogens are equivalent) and only one ortho-disubstituted derivative (1,2-dibromobenzene), explained by resonance between the two equivalent Kekulé structures.
    • Orbital Picture & X-Ray Data: All six carbon atoms are \(sp^2\) hybridised in a planar hexagon. Each carbon has an unhybridised \(2p\) orbital perpendicular to the ring plane, overlapping equally with both neighbours to form two continuous doughnut-shaped \(\pi\)-electron clouds above and below the ring plane. All six \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond lengths are identical at \(139\,\mathrm{pm}\), intermediate between a \(\mathrm{C-C}\) single bond (\(154\,\mathrm{pm}\)) and a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (\(133\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Conditions for Aromaticity (Hückel Rule): A cyclic system (benzenoid or non-benzenoid) is aromatic if it satisfies three criteria:
    1. Planarity (all ring atoms \(sp^2\) or \(sp\) hybridised).
    2. Complete delocalisation of \(\pi\) electrons in the ring.
    3. Presence of \((4n + 2)\,\pi\) electrons in the ring, where \(n\) is a non-negative integer (\(n = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\), corresponding to \(2, 6, 10, 14, \dots\) \(\pi\) electrons).
    Examples of Aromatic Species:
    • \(n = 1\) (\(6\,\pi\) electrons): Benzene, cyclopentadienyl anion (\(\mathrm{C_5H_5^-}\)), cycloheptatrienyl cation (\(\mathrm{C_7H_7^+}\), tropylium ion).
    • \(n = 2\) (\(10\,\pi\) electrons): Naphthalene.
    • \(n = 3\) (\(14\,\pi\) electrons): Anthracene, Phenanthrene.
  • Disubstituted Benzene Isomers: 1,2- (or 1,6-) is ortho (\(o\text{-}\)); 1,3- (or 1,5-) is meta (\(m\text{-}\)); and 1,4- is para (\(p\text{-}\)). Dimethylbenzenes are called xylenes (\(o\text{-}\), \(m\text{-}\), and \(p\text{-xylene}\)).

6.2 Preparation of Benzene

  1. Cyclic Polymerisation of Ethyne: \(\mathrm{3HC\equiv CH \xrightarrow{\text{Red hot Fe tube, }873\,K} C_6H_6}\).
  2. Decarboxylation of Aromatic Acids: Heating sodium benzoate with soda lime (\(\mathrm{NaOH + CaO}\)): \[\mathrm{C_6H_5COONa + NaOH \xrightarrow[\Delta]{CaO} C_6H_6 + Na_2CO_3}\]
  3. Reduction of Phenol: Passing phenol vapours over heated zinc dust: \[\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + Zn \xrightarrow{\Delta} C_6H_6 + ZnO}\]

6.3 Chemical Properties of Benzene

Aromatic hydrocarbons are non-polar, immiscible with water, and burn with a sooty flame due to high carbon content. General combustion equation for any hydrocarbon \(\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y\):

\[\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y + \left(x + \frac{y}{4}\right)\mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow x\,\mathrm{CO_2} + \frac{y}{2}\,\mathrm{H_2O}\]

6.5 Carcinogenicity and Toxicity

Benzene and polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) containing more than two benzene rings fused together are toxic and carcinogenic (cancer-producing). They are formed during incomplete combustion of organic materials like tobacco, coal, and petroleum. In the human body, they undergo biochemical reactions that damage DNA and cause cancer. Key examples include: 1,2-benzanthracene, 3-methylcholanthrene, 1,2-benzpyrene, 1,2,5,6-dibenzanthracene, and 9,10-dimethyl-1,2-benzanthracene.

Worked Example 3: Structure Determination via Ozonolysis

Problem: (a) An alkene 'A' on ozonolysis gives a mixture of ethanal and pentan-3-one. Write the structure and IUPAC name of 'A'.
(b) An alkene 'A' contains three \(\mathrm{C-C}\), eight \(\mathrm{C-H}\) \(\sigma\) bonds, and one \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\pi\) bond. 'A' on ozonolysis gives two moles of an aldehyde of molar mass \(44\,\mathrm{u}\). Write the IUPAC name of 'A'.
(c) Write down the ozonolysis products of 1,2-dimethylbenzene (o-xylene) and explain how this result supports Kekulé's oscillating structure for benzene.

Solution:

  • (a) Align the carbonyl carbons of ethanal (\(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=O}\)) and pentan-3-one (\(\mathrm{O=C(CH_2CH_3)_2}\)) and replace the two \(=\mathrm{O}\) atoms with a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond: \[\mathrm{CH_3-CH=C(CH_2CH_3)_2} \implies \text{IUPAC Name: } \mathbf{3\text{-Ethylpent-2-ene}}\]
  • (b) An aldehyde of molar mass \(44\,\mathrm{u}\) is ethanal (\(\mathrm{CH_3CHO}\), \(12\times 2 + 4\times 1 + 16 = 44\,\mathrm{u}\)). Joining two \(\mathrm{CH_3CH=O}\) units gives \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CH_3}\) (But-2-ene), which indeed has three \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\sigma\) bonds, eight \(\mathrm{C-H}\) \(\sigma\) bonds, and one \(\mathrm{C-C}\) \(\pi\) bond.
  • (c) Ozonolysis of o-xylene gives a mixture of three dicarbonyl products:
    1. Glyoxal (Ethanedial, \(\mathrm{OHC-CHO}\))
    2. Methylglyoxal (2-Oxopropanal, \(\mathrm{CH_3-CO-CHO}\))
    3. Dimethylglyoxal (Butane-2,3-dione, \(\mathrm{CH_3-CO-CO-CH_3}\))
    A single static Kekulé structure could only give two of these products; obtaining all three proves that both Kekulé structures contribute to the real benzene hybrid.
Worked Example 4: Multi-Step Conversions to Benzene & Disubstituted Benzenes

Problem: How will you convert: (a) Ethanoic acid into benzene, (b) Hexane into benzene, and (c) Benzene into (i) p-nitrobromobenzene, (ii) m-nitrochlorobenzene, (iii) p-nitrotoluene, and (iv) acetophenone?

Solution:

  • (a) Ethanoic acid to Benzene: \[\mathrm{CH_3COOH \xrightarrow{NaOH} CH_3COONa \xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{Soda lime}} CH_4 \xrightarrow{Cl_2/h\nu} CH_3Cl \xrightarrow[\text{Wurtz}]{Na/\text{dry ether}} C_2H_6 \xrightarrow{Cl_2/h\nu} C_2H_5Cl}\] \[\mathrm{C_2H_5Cl \xrightarrow{\text{alc. KOH}} CH_2=CH_2 \xrightarrow{Br_2/CCl_4} BrCH_2-CH_2Br \xrightarrow{\text{alc. KOH}} CH_2=CHBr \xrightarrow{NaNH_2} HC\equiv CH \xrightarrow[873\,K]{\text{Red hot Fe tube}} C_6H_6}\]
  • (b) Hexane to Benzene (Aromatization): \[\mathrm{CH_3(CH_2)_4CH_3 \xrightarrow{Cr_2O_3\text{ or }V_2O_5\text{ or }Mo_2O_3,\;773\,K,\;10\text{–}20\,\text{atm}} C_6H_6 + 4H_2}\]
  • (c) Conversions from Benzene (Order of Introducing Substituents):
    • (i) p-Nitrobromobenzene: Since \(-\mathrm{Br}\) is ortho/para-directing and \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) is meta-directing, brominate first, then nitrate: \[\mathrm{C_6H_6 \xrightarrow{Br_2/\text{Anhyd. }FeBr_3} C_6H_5Br \xrightarrow{\text{Conc. }HNO_3 + \text{Conc. }H_2SO_4,\;\Delta} \mathit{p}\text{-}O_2N-C_6H_4-Br\;\;(\text{+ }\mathit{o}\text{-isomer, separated})}\]
    • (ii) m-Nitrochlorobenzene: Nitrate first (since \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) is meta-directing), then chlorinate: \[\mathrm{C_6H_6 \xrightarrow{\text{Conc. }HNO_3 + \text{Conc. }H_2SO_4} C_6H_5NO_2 \xrightarrow{Cl_2/\text{Anhyd. }AlCl_3} \mathit{m}\text{-}O_2N-C_6H_4-Cl}\]
    • (iii) p-Nitrotoluene: Friedel-Crafts methylation first (\(-\mathrm{CH_3}\) is o/p-directing), then nitration: \[\mathrm{C_6H_6 \xrightarrow{CH_3Cl/\text{Anhyd. }AlCl_3} C_6H_5CH_3 \xrightarrow{\text{Conc. }HNO_3 + \text{Conc. }H_2SO_4} \mathit{p}\text{-}O_2N-C_6H_4-CH_3}\]
    • (iv) Acetophenone: Friedel-Crafts acylation with acetyl chloride: \[\mathrm{C_6H_6 + CH_3COCl \xrightarrow{\text{Anhyd. }AlCl_3,\;\Delta} C_6H_5COCH_3 + HCl}\]

9. Common Pitfalls & NEET Revision Checklist

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in NEET UG:
  • Pitfall 1 (Soda-lime Decarboxylation Stoichiometry): Remember that soda lime decarboxylation removes the \(-\mathrm{COONa}\) group as \(\mathrm{Na_2CO_3}\), giving an alkane with one carbon less. To prepare propane (\(\mathrm{C_3H_8}\)), you must start with sodium butanoate (\(\mathrm{C_3H_7COONa}\)), not sodium propanoate!
  • Pitfall 2 (\(\mathrm{C_6Cl_6}\) vs \(\mathrm{C_6H_6Cl_6}\)): Do not confuse hexachlorobenzene (\(\mathrm{C_6Cl_6}\), formed by electrophilic substitution with excess \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\)/anhyd. \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) in the dark) with benzene hexachloride (\(\mathrm{C_6H_6Cl_6}\), formed by free-radical addition of \(3\mathrm{Cl_2}\) under UV light at \(500\,\mathrm{K}\)).
  • Pitfall 3 (Halogens on Benzene Ring): Halogens (\(\mathrm{-F, -Cl, -Br, -I}\)) are deactivating due to their strong \(-I\) effect, yet they are ortho/para-directing due to \(+R\) resonance. Every other deactivating group (\(\mathrm{-NO_2, -CN, -CHO, -COOH, -SO_3H}\)) is meta-directing.
  • Pitfall 4 (Alphabetising Alkyl Prefixes): In IUPAC naming, hyphenated prefixes like sec- and tert- are ignored when alphabetising (so sec-butyl is alphabetised under 'b'), whereas non-hyphenated iso and neo are considered part of the group name (isopropyl is alphabetised under 'i').
  • Pitfall 5 (Nitration Acid-Base Role): During generation of the nitronium ion (\(\mathrm{\overset{+}{N}O_2}\)), concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) acts as the acid (proton donor) and concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) acts as the base (proton acceptor).
