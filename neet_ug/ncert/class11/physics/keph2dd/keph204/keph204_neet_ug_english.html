NEET UG Physics Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics: Principles, Processes, and Heat Engines

A complete, standalone tutorial with conceptual derivations, indicator-diagram analysis, original worked examples, and NEET UG-aligned practice questions.

1. Learning Objectives & Syllabus Scope

Thermodynamics governs how thermal energy converts into mechanical work and vice versa. Unlike mechanics, which tracks the motion of a body as a whole, thermodynamics examines the internal macroscopic state of bulk matter without requiring detailed molecular trajectories.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish thermodynamic equilibrium from mechanical equilibrium and apply the Zeroth Law to define temperature.
  • Separate state variables (such as internal energy \(U\)) from path-dependent transfers (heat \(\Delta Q\) and work \(\Delta W\)).
  • Apply the First Law of Thermodynamics with consistent sign conventions across phase changes and gas processes.
  • Use molar specific heats (\(C_p\), \(C_v\)), Mayer's relation, and the equipartition prediction for solids (\(C = 3R\)).
  • Analyze isothermal, adiabatic, isobaric, isochoric, cyclic, and free-expansion processes using equations and \(P\)-\(V\) diagrams.
  • State the Second Law of Thermodynamics, evaluate reversibility, and calculate the maximum efficiency of a Carnot engine.

2. Thermal Equilibrium & The Zeroth Law

In mechanics, equilibrium requires zero net external force and zero net torque. In thermodynamics, a system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when its macroscopic state variables—pressure (\(P\)), volume (\(V\)), temperature (\(T\)), mass, and chemical composition—remain constant over time.

2.1 Adiabatic vs. Diathermic Walls

  • Adiabatic Wall: An ideal insulating barrier that blocks all heat transfer between two systems. Any equilibrium values \((P_A, V_A)\) of system A can coexist with any \((P_B, V_B)\) of system B.
  • Diathermic Wall: A conducting barrier that permits thermal energy (heat) to flow until both systems settle into a shared state of thermal equilibrium, after which macroscopic variables stop changing.

2.2 Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

Formulated by R. H. Fowler in 1931, the Zeroth Law states: If two systems A and B are separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

This transitivity proves the existence of a single scalar property shared by all systems in thermal equilibrium: temperature (\(T\)). If \(T_A = T_C\) and \(T_B = T_C\), then \(T_A = T_B\). Heat spontaneously flows from higher temperature to lower temperature until temperatures equalize.

3. Heat, Internal Energy, Work & The First Law

3.1 Internal Energy (\(U\))

The internal energy (\(U\)) of a system is the sum of the disordered kinetic energies (translational, rotational, and vibrational) and intermolecular potential energies of all its molecules, measured in the frame where the system's centre of mass is at rest.

  • Bulk motion excluded: Firing a bullet at high speed increases its macroscopic mechanical kinetic energy, not its temperature or internal energy.
  • State variable: \(U\) depends strictly on the current equilibrium state \((P, V, T)\), not on the history or path used to reach that state.
  • Ideal gas simplification: Because intermolecular forces are neglected in an ideal gas, \(U\) depends only on absolute temperature \(T\).

3.2 Heat (\(\Delta Q\)) and Work (\(\Delta W\)): Modes of Energy Transfer

Historically, Count Rumford's 1798 cannon-boring experiments disproved the "caloric fluid" theory by showing that mechanical work generates heat continuously without exhausting any fluid. Today, we recognize both heat and work as energy in transit—not properties stored inside a body:

  • Heat (\(\Delta Q\)): Energy transferred across a boundary purely due to a temperature difference.
  • Work (\(\Delta W\)): Energy transferred by mechanical or non-thermal means (such as moving a piston).
Tutorial Diagram 2: Both heat flow and mechanical work alter the internal energy \(U\) of a thermodynamic system.

3.3 First Law of Thermodynamics

The First Law is the universal conservation of energy applied to thermal systems:

First Law of Thermodynamics
\[ \Delta Q = \Delta U + \Delta W \]

For a gas expanding quasi-statically against external pressure \(P\), \(\Delta W = P\,\Delta V\), giving:

\[ \Delta Q = \Delta U + P\,\Delta V \]

Notice that although \(\Delta Q\) and \(\Delta W\) individually depend on the process path, their difference \(\Delta Q - \Delta W = \Delta U\) is completely path-independent.

Quantity Positive (\(+\)) Meaning Negative (\(-\)) Meaning Path or State?
\(\Delta Q\) Heat added to the system Heat removed from the system Path-dependent
\(\Delta W\) Work done by the system (expansion) Work done on the system (compression) Path-dependent
\(\Delta U\) Internal energy increases Internal energy decreases State variable (path-independent)

4. Specific Heat Capacity & Ideal Gas Relations

When heat \(\Delta Q\) raises the temperature of a body by \(\Delta T\), its heat capacity is \(S = \Delta Q / \Delta T\). Dividing by mass \(m\) (in \(\mathrm{kg}\)) or number of moles \(\mu\) yields substance-specific constants:

  • Specific Heat Capacity (\(s\)): \( s = \dfrac{1}{m}\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T} \)  (SI unit: \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).
  • Molar Specific Heat Capacity (\(C\)): \( C = \dfrac{1}{\mu}\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T} \)  (SI unit: \(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).
Key Facts for NEET UG: Water & Solids
  • Calorie Definition: \(1\,\mathrm{cal}\) is the heat needed to raise \(1\,\mathrm{g}\) of water from \(14.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(15.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), where \(1\,\mathrm{cal} = 4.186\,\mathrm{J}\). Water's specific heat is \(4186\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), which explains why coastal climates are more temperate than desert climates and why water makes an effective coolant.
  • Solids (Law of Equipartition): Each atom in a solid oscillates in 3 dimensions, having average energy \(3 k_B T\). For \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\), \(U = 3 N_A k_B T = 3RT\). Since \(\Delta V\) is negligible for solids, \( C \approx \dfrac{\Delta U}{\Delta T} = 3R \approx 24.9\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \). This agrees well with metals like aluminium, copper, and tungsten at room temperature, though carbon is a notable exception (\(C \approx 6.1\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)) and all solids deviate at very low temperatures.

4.1 Mayer's Relation for an Ideal Gas

For gases, heat capacity depends on the constraint during heating. At constant volume (\(\Delta V = 0\)), all supplied heat changes internal energy:

\[ C_v = \left(\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T}\right)_v = \frac{\Delta U}{\Delta T} \]

At constant pressure, supplied heat both raises internal energy and performs expansion work \(P\,\Delta V\). Using \(PV = RT\) for \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\), we have \(P(\Delta V / \Delta T)_p = R\), which leads directly to Mayer's Relation:

Mayer's Relation & Adiabatic Index
\[ C_p - C_v = R \qquad \text{and} \qquad \gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v} \]

5. State Variables & Thermodynamic Processes

State variables classify into two categories using a simple mental test—imagine splitting an equilibrium system into two identical halves:

  • Extensive Variables (halved upon division, scale with size): Mass \(M\), Volume \(V\), Internal Energy \(U\), Entropy \(S\).
  • Intensive Variables (unchanged upon division, independent of size): Pressure \(P\), Temperature \(T\), Density \(\rho\).

For \(\mu\) moles of an ideal gas, the equation of state is \(PV = \mu RT\). Note that non-equilibrium events—such as sudden free expansion into vacuum or an explosive chemical reaction—do not have uniform \(P\) and \(T\), so intermediate states cannot be plotted on a \(P\)-\(V\)-\(T\) surface.

5.1 Quasi-Static Processes

A quasi-static process is an idealized, infinitely slow transformation in which the system stays in thermal and mechanical equilibrium with its surroundings at every step (\(\Delta P \to 0\), \(\Delta T \to 0\)).

\(TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}\)		 \(\dfrac{P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2}{\gamma - 1} = \dfrac{\mu R(T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1}\) \(\Delta U = -W\)

6. Second Law, Reversibility & Carnot Engine

The First Law permits processes that never occur in nature—such as a resting book spontaneously cooling down and jumping upward using its own thermal energy. The Second Law of Thermodynamics establishes the directional arrow of natural processes:

  • Kelvin–Planck Statement: No cyclic process can absorb heat from a single reservoir and convert it completely into work (100% heat-engine efficiency \(\eta = 1\) is impossible).
  • Clausius Statement: No process can transfer heat spontaneously from a colder body to a hotter body without external work input (infinite refrigerator coefficient of performance is impossible).

6.1 Reversible vs. Irreversible Processes

A process is reversible if both the system and its surroundings can be restored to their exact initial states with zero net change anywhere else in the universe. Reversibility requires two strict conditions: (1) the process must be quasi-static, and (2) there must be no dissipative forces (no friction, viscosity, or inelasticity). All spontaneous natural processes—free expansion, diffusion, and combustion—are irreversible.

6.2 The Carnot Cycle & Carnot's Theorem

A Carnot engine is an idealized reversible heat engine operating between a hot source at absolute temperature \(T_1\) and a cold sink at \(T_2\). It executes four reversible steps:

  1. Isothermal Expansion (\(1 \to 2\)) at \(T_1\): Absorbs heat \( Q_1 = \mu R T_1 \ln(V_2/V_1) \).
  2. Adiabatic Expansion (\(2 \to 3\)): Temperature drops from \(T_1\) to \(T_2\) with no heat exchange.
  3. Isothermal Compression (\(3 \to 4\)) at \(T_2\): Rejects heat \( Q_2 = \mu R T_2 \ln(V_3/V_4) \).
  4. Adiabatic Compression (\(4 \to 1\)): Temperature rises back from \(T_2\) to \(T_1\).
Tutorial Diagram 4: The clockwise Carnot cycle on a \(P\)-\(V\) diagram encloses net positive work \(W = Q_1 - Q_2\).

From the two adiabatic relations, \(V_3 / V_4 = V_2 / V_1\), which simplifies the efficiency \(\eta\) to a universal formula:

Carnot Efficiency & Universal Temperature Relation
\[ \eta = \frac{W}{Q_1} = 1 - \frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = 1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1} \qquad \text{with} \qquad \frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{T_1}{T_2} \]

Carnot's Theorem: (a) No engine operating between two temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) can exceed the efficiency of a reversible Carnot engine, and (b) Carnot efficiency depends only on \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) (in Kelvin), never on the working substance.

7. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: First Law During Vaporisation

Hypothetical scenario: Assume \(2.0\,\mathrm{g}\) of a liquid boils at constant atmospheric pressure \(P = 1.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\) with latent heat \(2000\,\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}}\). Its volume changes from \(2.5\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) (liquid) to \(3002.5\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) (vapour).

Task: Find the heat supplied \(\Delta Q\), work done \(\Delta W\), and change in internal energy \(\Delta U\).

Solution:

  • Heat supplied: \(\Delta Q = mL = 2.0\,\mathrm{g} \times 2000\,\mathrm{J\,g^{-1}} = 4000\,\mathrm{J}\).
  • Volume change: \(\Delta V = (3002.5 - 2.5)\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 3000\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 3.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\).
  • Work done by vapour: \(\Delta W = P\,\Delta V = (1.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa})(3.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}) = 300\,\mathrm{J}\).
  • By the First Law: \(\Delta U = \Delta Q - \Delta W = 4000 - 300 = 3700\,\mathrm{J}\).
Worked Example 2: Isobaric Expansion of a Monatomic Gas

Hypothetical scenario: Assume \(2.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal monatomic gas (\(C_v = \frac{3}{2}R\)) expands isobarically at \(P = 2.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\) from \(V_1 = 0.01\,\mathrm{m^3}\) to \(V_2 = 0.03\,\mathrm{m^3}\).

Task: Calculate (a) work done by the gas, (b) \(\Delta U\), and (c) heat absorbed \(\Delta Q\).

Solution:

  • (a) \(W = P(V_2 - V_1) = 2.0 \times 10^5 \times (0.03 - 0.01) = 4000\,\mathrm{J}\).
  • (b) From \(PV = \mu RT\), we have \(\mu R\,\Delta T = P\,\Delta V = 4000\,\mathrm{J}\). Thus: \[ \Delta U = \mu C_v \Delta T = \frac{3}{2}(\mu R\,\Delta T) = 1.5 \times 4000 = 6000\,\mathrm{J} \]
  • (c) \(\Delta Q = \Delta U + W = 6000 + 4000 = 10000\,\mathrm{J}\).
Worked Example 3: Adiabatic Compression

Hypothetical scenario: Assume \(1.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal monatomic gas (\(\gamma = 5/3\), \(R = 8.3\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)) at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) is compressed adiabatically to \(\frac{1}{8}\) of its initial volume.

Task: Find the final temperature and the work done on the gas.

Solution:

  • Using \(T_1 V_1^{\gamma - 1} = T_2 V_2^{\gamma - 1}\) with \(\gamma - 1 = \frac{2}{3}\): \[ T_2 = 300 \times \left(\frac{V_1}{V_1/8}\right)^{2/3} = 300 \times 8^{2/3} = 300 \times 4 = 1200\,\mathrm{K} \]
  • Work done by the gas: \[ W = \frac{\mu R(T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{1.0 \times 8.3 \times (300 - 1200)}{2/3} = -11205\,\mathrm{J} \] Hence, \(11.205\,\mathrm{kJ}\) of work is done on the gas.
Worked Example 4: Carnot Engine Efficiency

Hypothetical scenario: A Carnot engine operates between \(T_1 = 600\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(T_2 = 400\,\mathrm{K}\), absorbing \(1500\,\mathrm{J}\) per cycle.

Task: Find the work output per cycle and the new efficiency if the sink is cooled to \(300\,\mathrm{K}\).

Solution:

  • Initial efficiency: \(\eta = 1 - \dfrac{400}{600} = \dfrac{1}{3} \approx 33.3\%\).
  • Work per cycle: \(W = \eta Q_1 = \dfrac{1}{3} \times 1500 = 500\,\mathrm{J}\).
  • With \(T_2' = 300\,\mathrm{K}\): \(\eta' = 1 - \dfrac{300}{600} = 0.50 = 50\%\).

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Two thermally insulated systems A and B are each placed in diathermic contact with a third system C until equilibrium is reached. Which macroscopic property is guaranteed to be identical for A and B?

  • (A) Internal energy
  • (B) Pressure
  • (C) Temperature
  • (D) Volume
Answer: (C)
Explanation: By the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics, systems in thermal equilibrium with a third system are in thermal equilibrium with one another, which means they share the same temperature \(T\).
Practice Question 2 Numerical MCQ

A gas moves from equilibrium state X to state Y along path 1 by absorbing \(80\,\mathrm{J}\) of heat and doing \(30\,\mathrm{J}\) of work. When taken from X to Y along path 2, it performs \(12\,\mathrm{J}\) of work. How much heat does the gas absorb along path 2?

  • (A) \(38\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(50\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(62\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(92\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Internal energy change is path-independent: \(\Delta U = Q_1 - W_1 = 80 - 30 = 50\,\mathrm{J}\). Along path 2, \(Q_2 = \Delta U + W_2 = 50 + 12 = 62\,\mathrm{J}\).
Practice Question 3 Cyclic Process MCQ

An ideal gas executes a triangular cycle \(A \to B \to C \to A\) on a \(P\)-\(V\) plot with vertices \(A(2\,\mathrm{L}, 100\,\mathrm{kPa})\), \(B(2\,\mathrm{L}, 300\,\mathrm{kPa})\), and \(C(6\,\mathrm{L}, 100\,\mathrm{kPa})\). What is the net heat absorbed by the gas in one cycle?

  • (A) \(200\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(400\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(600\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(800\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (B)
Explanation: The cycle \(A \to B \to C \to A\) is clockwise, so \(W_{\text{net}} > 0\). Since \(1\,\mathrm{L\cdot kPa} = 1\,\mathrm{J}\), the enclosed triangle area is \(\frac{1}{2} \times (6 - 2)\,\mathrm{L} \times (300 - 100)\,\mathrm{kPa} = 400\,\mathrm{J}\). For a cycle, \(\Delta U = 0\), so \(Q_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{net}} = 400\,\mathrm{J}\).
Practice Question 4 Conceptual MCQ

Which pair consists exclusively of intensive thermodynamic state variables?

  • (A) Volume and internal energy
  • (B) Pressure and density
  • (C) Temperature and mass
  • (D) Internal energy and temperature
Answer: (B)
Explanation: If an equilibrium system is halved, pressure and density remain unchanged (intensive), whereas volume, mass, and internal energy are halved (extensive).
Practice Question 5 Numerical MCQ

Supplying \(9.0\,\mathrm{kJ}\) of heat to a \(0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) metal block at constant volume raises its temperature from \(25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(65\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). What is the specific heat capacity of the metal?

  • (A) \(225\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(360\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(450\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(900\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)
Answer: (C)
Explanation: \(\Delta T = 65 - 25 = 40\,\mathrm{K}\). Thus \(s = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{m\,\Delta T} = \dfrac{9000}{0.5 \times 40} = 450\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\).
Practice Question 6 Numerical MCQ

An ideal gas with adiabatic index \(\gamma = 1.5\), initially at pressure \(P_0\) and volume \(V_0\), is compressed adiabatically to volume \(V_0 / 4\). What is its final pressure?

  • (A) \(4 P_0\)
  • (B) \(6 P_0\)
  • (C) \(8 P_0\)
  • (D) \(16 P_0\)
Answer: (C)
Explanation: Using \(P_1 V_1^\gamma = P_2 V_2^\gamma\), we get \(P_2 = P_0 \left(\dfrac{V_0}{V_0/4}\right)^{3/2} = P_0 \times 4^{3/2} = 8 P_0\).
Practice Question 7 Statement I & II

Given below are two statements:
Statement I: During free expansion of an ideal gas into vacuum inside a thermally insulated container, its temperature remains constant.
Statement II: In free expansion against vacuum, both \(\Delta Q\) and \(\Delta W\) are zero, making \(\Delta U = 0\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)
Explanation: Thermal insulation gives \(\Delta Q = 0\) and vacuum offers zero resisting pressure so \(\Delta W = 0\). By the First Law, \(\Delta U = 0\). Since \(U\) of an ideal gas depends only on \(T\), temperature stays constant.
Practice Question 8 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): A Carnot engine operating between \(400\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) has a higher efficiency than any irreversible engine operating between the same two reservoirs.
Reason (R): The efficiency of a Carnot engine depends on the nature of the working substance used.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Answer: (C)
Explanation: By Carnot's theorem, no engine can outperform a Carnot engine between two fixed temperatures (A is true), and Carnot efficiency \(\eta = 1 - T_2/T_1\) is completely independent of the working substance (R is false).
Practice Question 9 Numerical MCQ

A Carnot engine absorbs \(2400\,\mathrm{J}\) of heat from a hot reservoir at \(527\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and rejects \(1500\,\mathrm{J}\) to a cold sink. What is the temperature of the cold sink?

  • (A) \(127\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (B) \(227\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (C) \(330\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) \(500\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
Answer: (B)
Explanation: Convert \(T_1\) to Kelvin: \(T_1 = 527 + 273 = 800\,\mathrm{K}\). Using \(\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = \dfrac{Q_2}{Q_1}\), we get \(T_2 = 800 \times \dfrac{1500}{2400} = 500\,\mathrm{K}\). In Celsius, \(T_2 = 500 - 273 = 227\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
Practice Question 10 Match the Following

Match List I (Process) with List II (Defining Condition for an Ideal Gas):

List I List II
(a) Isothermal (i) \(\Delta Q = 0\)
(b) Isochoric (ii) \(\Delta W = P\,\Delta V\)
(c) Adiabatic (iii) \(\Delta U = 0\)
(d) Isobaric (iv) \(\Delta W = 0\)
  • (A) (a)–(iii), (b)–(iv), (c)–(i), (d)–(ii)
  • (B) (a)–(i), (b)–(iv), (c)–(iii), (d)–(ii)

9. Common Pitfalls & Concise Final Recap

High-Frequency NEET Traps to Avoid
  • Forgetting Kelvin Conversion: In \(PV = \mu RT\), \(TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}\), and \(\eta = 1 - T_2/T_1\), temperatures must be in Kelvin (\(\mathrm{K} = ^\circ\mathrm{C} + 273\)). Only temperature differences (\(\Delta T\)) have the same numerical value in \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(\mathrm{K}\).
  • Confusing \(Q\) and \(U\): Never say a system "contains heat." Heat and work exist only during transfer; internal energy \(U\) belongs to the state.
  • Sign of Work in Cyclic Plots: A clockwise \(P\)-\(V\) loop yields positive net work (heat engine); a counterclockwise loop yields negative net work (refrigerator).
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Zeroth Law: Defines temperature \(T\) and thermal equilibrium.
  • First Law: \(\Delta Q = \Delta U + \Delta W\), with \(\Delta U\) path-independent.
  • Heat Capacities: \(C_p - C_v = R\); solids obey \(C \approx 3R\) (except carbon and low-\(T\) regimes).
  • Processes: Isothermal (\(PV = \text{const}\)); Adiabatic (\(PV^\gamma = \text{const}\), steeper on \(P\)-\(V\) plot); Isochoric (\(W = 0\)); Isobaric (\(W = P\,\Delta V\)).
  • Second Law & Carnot: \(\eta_{\max} = 1 - T_2/T_1 < 1\), independent of the working substance.
