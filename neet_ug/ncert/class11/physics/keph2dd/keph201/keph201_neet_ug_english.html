NEET UG Physics Class 11 Mechanics

Mechanical Properties of Solids

Complete Concept Guide, Elastic Moduli, Engineering Applications & NEET UG Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Overview

In elementary mechanics, objects are often idealized as perfectly rigid bodies whose dimensions never change. Real solids, however, deform whenever external forces act on them. Understanding how solids stretch, shear, compress, store energy, and eventually fracture is essential for NEET UG Physics.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between elastic and plastic deformation using interatomic restoring forces.
  • Calculate tensile, compressive, shearing, and hydraulic stress and their corresponding strains.
  • Interpret metal and elastomer stress–strain curves, including proportional limit, yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and permanent set.
  • Apply Hooke's law using Young's modulus (\(Y\)), shear modulus (\(G\)), bulk modulus (\(B\)), compressibility (\(k\)), and Poisson's ratio.
  • Compute elastic potential energy density and solve series/parallel wire and self-weight elongation problems.
  • Analyze structural applications such as I-beams, crane cables, pillars, and the maximum height of mountains.

2. Elasticity, Plasticity, Stress & Strain

2.1 Microscopic Origin of Elasticity

Atoms in a crystalline solid occupy equilibrium positions where net interatomic forces vanish. When an external deforming force shifts atoms slightly from equilibrium, electromagnetic restoring forces pull them back—much like stiff microscopic springs. When the external load is removed, the solid snaps back to its original geometry.

  • Elasticity: The property of a body by which it tends to recover its original size and shape once the deforming force is withdrawn. Quartz and phosphor bronze are nearly ideal elastic materials.
  • Plasticity: The inability of a body to regain its original configuration after the deforming force is removed, resulting in permanent deformation. Wet clay and putty behave nearly as ideal plastics.

2.2 Stress and Strain

When a solid remains in static equilibrium under balanced external forces, an internal restoring force develops across every cross-section to oppose the deformation. Stress is defined as the internal restoring force acting per unit cross-sectional area:

Definition of Stress
\[ \sigma = \frac{F_{\text{restoring}}}{A} = \frac{F}{A} \]

SI unit: \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\) or pascal (\(\mathrm{Pa}\)). Dimensional formula: \([M^1 L^{-1} T^{-2}]\). Note that stress is not a vector quantity because it cannot be assigned a single direction independent of the plane across which it acts.

Strain quantifies the relative deformation produced by stress. Because it is the ratio of a change in dimension to the original dimension, strain is a pure dimensionless number with no units.

2.3 The Three Fundamental Modes of Deformation

Depending on how external forces are directed relative to a solid's surface, deformation falls into three distinct categories:

  1. Longitudinal (Tensile or Compressive) Stress & Strain: Equal and opposite forces act perpendicular (normal) to opposite faces of a rod or wire of original length \(L\) and area \(A\). Stretching produces tensile stress and an elongation \(+\Delta L\); pushing inward produces compressive stress and a contraction \(-\Delta L\). \[ \text{Longitudinal strain} = \frac{\Delta L}{L} \]
  2. Shearing (Tangential) Stress & Strain: Equal and opposite forces act parallel (tangential) to opposite faces, keeping the volume constant while distorting the shape. If the top face shifts sideways by \(\Delta x\) relative to the fixed bottom face separated by height \(L\), the angular distortion is \(\theta\). \[ \text{Shearing stress } (\sigma_s) = \frac{F}{A}, \qquad \text{Shearing strain} = \frac{\Delta x}{L} = \tan\theta \approx \theta \text{ (for small }\theta\text{ in radians)} \]
  3. Hydraulic (Volumetric) Stress & Volume Strain: A solid immersed in a high-pressure fluid experiences uniform inward force perpendicular to every surface element. The restoring force per unit area equals the fluid pressure \(p\), changing the volume from \(V\) to \(V + \Delta V\) without altering the body's shape. \[ \text{Volume strain} = \frac{\Delta V}{V} \]

3. Hooke's Law & Stress–Strain Curves

3.1 Hooke's Law

For sufficiently small deformations, stress is directly proportional to strain:

Hooke's Law
\[ \text{Stress} = k \times \text{Strain} \]

Here \(k\) is the modulus of elasticity, a material constant that depends on the nature of the solid and temperature, but not on the object's dimensions. Hooke's law is an empirical law valid only within the linear elastic region.

3.2 Stress–Strain Curve for a Ductile Metal

When a metallic wire is stretched under gradually increasing tensile load, plotting stress against strain reveals five key features:

  • Proportional Region (O to A): The graph is a straight line; Hooke's law holds strictly, and the slope gives Young's modulus \(Y\). Point A is the proportional limit.
  • Elastic Region (O to B): Between A and B, stress and strain are no longer strictly proportional, yet the wire still returns completely to its original length upon unloading. Point B is the yield point (elastic limit), and the corresponding stress is the yield strength (\(\sigma_y\)).
  • Plastic Flow & Permanent Set (B to D): Beyond \(\sigma_y\), strain increases rapidly for small increases in stress. If unloaded at a point C, the metal follows a path parallel to OA and retains a residual strain called a permanent set.
  • Ultimate Tensile Strength (D): The peak stress \(\sigma_u\) the material can sustain before necking begins.
  • Fracture Point (E): Beyond D, the wire necks down and breaks at E. If D and E are close together, the material is brittle (e.g., glass, cast iron); if D and E are far apart, the material is ductile (e.g., copper, mild steel).

3.3 Elastomers

Materials such as vulcanized rubber and the elastic tissue of the aorta can be stretched to several times their original length and still snap back when released. Their stress–strain curve is strongly non-linear (violating Hooke's law over most of the range) and exhibits no well-defined plastic region.

4. Elastic Moduli, Poisson's Ratio & Elastic Energy

4.1 The Three Elastic Moduli

Modulus Formula Applicable States Key Physical Meaning
Young's Modulus (\(Y\)) \(Y = \dfrac{F / A}{\Delta L / L} = \dfrac{F L}{A \Delta L}\) Solids only Resistance to change in length under tension or compression.
Shear Modulus (\(G\)) \(G = \dfrac{F / A}{\Delta x / L} = \dfrac{F}{A \theta}\) Solids only Resistance to change in shape (rigidity). Typically \(G \approx Y/3\) for metals.
Bulk Modulus (\(B\)) \(B = -\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta V / V}\) Solids, liquids, gases Resistance to uniform volume compression. Negative sign keeps \(B > 0\).

The reciprocal of bulk modulus is called compressibility (\(k\)):

Compressibility
\[ k = \frac{1}{B} = -\frac{1}{\Delta p}\left(\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right) \quad (\text{SI unit: }\mathrm{Pa^{-1}}\text{ or }\mathrm{m^2\,N^{-1}}) \]

Because interatomic coupling is tightest in solids and weakest in gases, \(B_{\text{solid}} \gg B_{\text{liquid}} \gg B_{\text{gas}}\). Conversely, gases are roughly a million times more compressible than solids. For example, typical values are: Steel (\(Y \approx 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}\), \(G \approx 8.4 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{Pa}\), \(B \approx 1.6 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)), Copper (\(Y \approx 1.1 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)), Water (\(B \approx 2.2 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)), and Air at STP (\(B \approx 1.0 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)).

4.2 Poisson's Ratio

Stretching a wire longitudinally causes a slight contraction in its lateral diameter \(d\) by \(\Delta d\). Within the elastic limit, the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain is a dimensionless material constant called Poisson's ratio (\(\nu\)):

Poisson's Ratio
\[ \nu = -\frac{\Delta d / d}{\Delta L / L} \]

For steel, \(\nu \approx 0.28\text{–}0.30\); for aluminium alloys, \(\nu \approx 0.33\). Theoretically, for isotropic materials, \(-1 \le \nu \le 0.5\), and practically \(0 < \nu < 0.5\).

4.3 Elastic Potential Energy in a Stretched Wire

Stretching a wire by \(\Delta L\) under a gradually increasing force from \(0\) to \(F\) stores work as elastic potential energy \(U\):

Elastic Potential Energy & Energy Density
\[ U = \frac{1}{2} F \Delta L = \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{Y A}{L}\right) (\Delta L)^2 = \frac{F^2 L}{2 A Y} \] \[ u = \frac{U}{A L} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma \varepsilon = \frac{1}{2} Y \varepsilon^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{2Y} \]

Geometrically, the energy density \(u\) (energy per unit volume) equals the area under the stress–strain curve up to the given strain.

4.4 High-Yield NEET Connections: Wire Combinations & Self-Weight

  • Analogy with Springs: A wire of length \(L\), area \(A\), and Young's modulus \(Y\) acts like a spring of force constant \(k_{\text{eff}} = \dfrac{YA}{L}\).
  • Wires in Series (end-to-end): Both wires carry the same tension \(F\). Total elongation is \(\Delta L = \Delta L_1 + \Delta L_2\).
  • Wires in Parallel (side-by-side, equal elongation): Total load splits as \(F = F_1 + F_2\). For equal length \(L\), \(Y_{\text{eq}} = \dfrac{Y_1 A_1 + Y_2 A_2}{A_1 + A_2}\).
  • Elongation Under Own Weight: A uniform vertical rod of length \(L\), density \(\rho\), and cross-sectional area \(A\) suspended from one end experiences tension varying linearly from \(0\) at the bottom to \(Mg\) at the top (average force \(Mg/2\)). Thus: \[ \Delta L_{\text{self}} = \frac{MgL}{2AY} = \frac{\rho g L^2}{2Y} \]

5. Engineering & Geological Applications

  • Crane Ropes & Safety Factor: To lift a \(10\text{ tonne}\) (\(10^4\,\mathrm{kg}\)) load without permanent deformation, the tensile stress must stay below the yield strength of mild steel (\(\sigma_y \approx 300 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}\)), giving a minimum area \(A \ge \frac{Mg}{\sigma_y} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) (radius \(\approx 1\,\mathrm{cm}\)). Engineers apply a safety factor of ~10 (radius \(\approx 3\,\mathrm{cm}\)) and braid many thin wires together for flexibility and strength.
  • Bending of Beams & I-Section Girders: A rectangular bar of length \(l\), breadth \(b\), and depth \(d\) supported at its ends and carrying a central load \(W\) sags by: \[ \delta = \frac{W l^3}{4 b d^3 Y} \] Because \(\delta \propto d^{-3}\), increasing depth \(d\) is far more effective than increasing breadth \(b\). However, a very thin deep bar can buckle sideways. An I-shaped cross-section provides large depth and wide top/bottom flanges to resist bending and buckling while saving material and weight.
  • Pillars with Distributed Ends: Columns with flared capitals and bases distribute compressive load over a larger area, reducing stress concentration compared to rounded ends.
  • Maximum Height of Mountains on Earth: At the base of a mountain of height \(h\) and rock density \(\rho \approx 3 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), the vertical stress \(h\rho g\) induces a comparable shearing stress because the sides are free. Equating this to the critical shearing limit of rock (\(\sim 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)) yields: \[ h_{\max} \approx \frac{3 \times 10^8}{3 \times 10^3 \times 10} = 10\,\mathrm{km} \] which explains why Earth's tallest peaks (like Mt. Everest at ~8.85 km) remain below \(10\,\mathrm{km}\).

6. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Composite Wire in Series

Problem: An aluminium wire of length \(1.4\,\mathrm{m}\) and a steel wire of length \(2.0\,\mathrm{m}\), each having a uniform cross-sectional area of \(2.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\), are joined end to end and suspended vertically. A mass of \(8.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) is hung from the lower end. Assuming \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(Y_{\text{Al}} = 7.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\), and \(Y_{\text{steel}} = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\) (neglecting wire mass), find the total elongation.

Solution:

Both wires carry the same tensile force \(F = mg = 8.0 \times 10 = 80\,\mathrm{N}\). Thus, the tensile stress in each wire is:

\[ \sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{80}{2.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 4.0 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}} \]

Elongation of the aluminium wire:

\[ \Delta L_{\text{Al}} = \frac{\sigma L_{\text{Al}}}{Y_{\text{Al}}} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{7} \times 1.4}{7.0 \times 10^{10}} = 0.80 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.80\,\mathrm{mm} \]

Elongation of the steel wire:

\[ \Delta L_{\text{steel}} = \frac{\sigma L_{\text{steel}}}{Y_{\text{steel}}} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{7} \times 2.0}{2.0 \times 10^{11}} = 0.40 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.40\,\mathrm{mm} \]

Total elongation: \(\Delta L = 0.80 + 0.40 = 1.20\,\mathrm{mm}\).

Worked Example 2: Shearing Deformation of a Brass Block

Problem: A cubical brass block of edge length \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) has its bottom face firmly bolted to the floor. A tangential force of \(7.2 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N}\) acts uniformly on its top face. Taking the shear modulus of brass as \(G = 3.6 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\), calculate the shear strain and the horizontal displacement of the top face.

Solution:

Area of the top face: \(A = (0.20\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 0.04\,\mathrm{m^2}\).

\[ \sigma_s = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{7.2 \times 10^{4}}{0.04} = 1.8 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}} \] \[ \text{Shear strain } (\theta) = \frac{\sigma_s}{G} = \frac{1.8 \times 10^{6}}{3.6 \times 10^{10}} = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad} \]

Horizontal displacement: \(\Delta x = L \theta = 0.20 \times 5.0 \times 10^{-5} = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m} = 10\,\mathrm{µm}\).

Worked Example 3: Bulk Modulus & Density Change at Depth

Problem: A deep-water sensor descends into a lake to a depth where the pressure increases by \(\Delta p = 2.2 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). Given the bulk modulus of water \(B = 2.2 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{Pa}\) and surface density \(\rho_0 = 1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), find (a) the fractional change in volume and (b) the percentage increase in water density.

Solution:

(a) Using \(B = -\dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta V / V}\):

\[ \frac{\Delta V}{V} = -\frac{2.2 \times 10^{7}}{2.2 \times 10^{9}} = -1.0 \times 10^{-2} \quad (1.0\%\text{ volume contraction}) \]

(b) Since mass \(m = \rho V\) is conserved, for small changes \(\dfrac{\Delta \rho}{\rho_0} \approx -\dfrac{\Delta V}{V} = +1.0 \times 10^{-2}\). Hence, the water density increases by \(1.0\%\) (to \(\approx 1010\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)).

Worked Example 4: Elastic Energy Density & Work Done

Problem: A copper wire of length \(2.5\,\mathrm{m}\) and cross-sectional area \(1.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) is stretched within its elastic limit by a load of \(110\,\mathrm{N}\). Given \(Y = 1.1 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\), determine (a) the elastic potential energy stored per unit volume and (b) the total work done.

Solution:

Tensile stress \(\sigma = \dfrac{110}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 1.1 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\). Strain \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{\sigma}{Y} = \dfrac{1.1 \times 10^{8}}{1.1 \times 10^{11}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\).

(a) Energy density: \(u = \dfrac{1}{2}\sigma\varepsilon = \dfrac{1}{2}(1.1 \times 10^{8})(1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 5.5 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\).

(b) Volume \(V = A L = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 2.5 = 2.5 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3}\). Total work \(U = u V = 5.5 \times 10^{4} \times 2.5 \times 10^{-6} = 0.1375\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 1: Which of the following physical quantities is dimensionless?
  • (A) Modulus of rigidity
  • (B) Compressibility
  • (C) Poisson's ratio
  • (D) Yield strength
Answer: (C)

Solution: Poisson's ratio is the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain. Since both strains are dimensionless ratios, Poisson's ratio is a pure dimensionless number. Modulus of rigidity and yield strength have units of \(\mathrm{Pa}\), while compressibility has units of \(\mathrm{Pa^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 2: Two wires P and Q of the same material have lengths in the ratio \(2 : 3\) and radii in the ratio \(1 : 2\). If both are stretched by the same tensile force, the ratio of elongation of P to that of Q (\(\Delta L_P : \Delta L_Q\)) is:
  • (A) \(2 : 3\)
  • (B) \(4 : 3\)
  • (C) \(8 : 3\)
  • (D) \(3 : 8\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Using \(\Delta L = \dfrac{F L}{\pi r^2 Y}\), for the same \(F\) and \(Y\): \[ \frac{\Delta L_P}{\Delta L_Q} = \left(\frac{L_P}{L_Q}\right) \left(\frac{r_Q}{r_P}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\left(\frac{2}{1}\right)^2 = \frac{8}{3} \]

Practice Question 3: On a stress–strain graph (stress on the vertical axis, strain on the horizontal axis) for a ductile metal within the proportional limit, what do the slope of the line and the area under the line represent respectively?
  • (A) Young's modulus; total elastic potential energy
  • (B) Young's modulus; elastic potential energy per unit volume
  • (C) Yield strength; compressibility
  • (D) Bulk modulus; work done per unit cross-sectional area
Answer: (B)

Solution: The slope \(\dfrac{\text{Stress}}{\text{Strain}} = Y\) (Young's modulus). The area of the triangle under the linear graph is \(\dfrac{1}{2} \times \text{stress} \times \text{strain} = u\), which is the elastic potential energy per unit volume.

Practice Question 4: A metal cube of side \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) has its bottom face fixed. A tangential force of \(5.0 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N}\) applied to its top face produces a horizontal shift of \(0.05\,\mathrm{mm}\). The shear modulus of the metal is:
  • (A) \(1.0 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)
  • (B) \(1.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)
  • (C) \(2.5 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)
  • (D) \(5.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: \(A = (0.10\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}\), so \(\sigma_s = \dfrac{5.0 \times 10^{4}}{10^{-2}} = 5.0 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). Shear strain \(= \dfrac{\Delta x}{L} = \dfrac{0.05 \times 10^{-3}}{0.10} = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}\). Thus, \(G = \dfrac{5.0 \times 10^{6}}{5.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 1.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\).

Practice Question 5: The bulk modulus of a liquid is \(2.5 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\). What increase in pressure is required to compress a sample of this liquid by \(0.2\%\) in volume?
  • (A) \(5.0 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • (B) \(5.0 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • (C) \(1.25 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
  • (D) \(5.0 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{Pa}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Fractional volume change \(\left|\dfrac{\Delta V}{V}\right| = \dfrac{0.2}{100} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\). Pressure needed: \(\Delta p = B \left|\dfrac{\Delta V}{V}\right| = (2.5 \times 10^{9})(2.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 5.0 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{Pa}\).

Practice Question 6: Given below are two statements—one labeled Assertion (A) and the other labeled Reason (R):
Assertion (A): Steel is considered more elastic than rubber in physics.
Reason (R): For the same applied tensile stress, steel undergoes a much smaller longitudinal strain than rubber, resulting in a larger Young's modulus.
  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: In physics, elasticity measures resistance to deformation (quantified by elastic modulus). Since \(Y = \sigma / \varepsilon\), a smaller strain \(\varepsilon\) for a given stress \(\sigma\) yields a larger \(Y\), making steel more elastic than rubber.

Practice Question 7: Two wires X and Y of identical length and cross-sectional area have Young's moduli in the ratio \(Y_X : Y_Y = 1 : 3\). If both wires are stretched by the same tensile force, the ratio of elastic potential energy stored in X to that in Y (\(U_X : U_Y\)) is:
  • (A) \(1 : 3\)
  • (B) \(3 : 1\)
  • (C) \(1 : 9\)
  • (D) \(9 : 1\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: Under a constant force \(F\), \(U = \dfrac{F^2 L}{2 A Y}\). Since \(F, L,\) and \(A\) are identical, \(U \propto \dfrac{1}{Y}\). Therefore, \(\dfrac{U_X}{U_Y} = \dfrac{Y_Y}{Y_X} = \dfrac{3}{1}\).

Practice Question 8: A uniform steel wire can withstand a maximum tensile stress (breaking stress) of \(4.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\) before snapping. If the density of steel is \(8.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), what is the maximum length of this wire that can hang vertically under its own weight without breaking?
  • (A) \(2.5\,\mathrm{km}\)
  • (B) \(5.0\,\mathrm{km}\)
  • (C) \(10.0\,\mathrm{km}\)
  • (D) \(20.0\,\mathrm{km}\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: The maximum tensile stress occurs at the top support due to the total weight \(Mg = \rho A L_{\max} g\): \[ \sigma_{\max} = \frac{\rho A L_{\max} g}{A} = \rho g L_{\max} \implies L_{\max} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{8}}{8.0 \times 10^{3} \times 10} = 5.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m} = 5.0\,\mathrm{km} \]

Practice Question 9: A horizontal rectangular beam of length \(l\), breadth \(b\), and depth \(d\) is supported at both ends and loaded at its midpoint with a mass \(M\). If the depth \(d\) is doubled while \(l\), \(b\), and \(M\) remain unchanged, the central sag \(\delta\) becomes:
  • (A) \(\delta / 2\)
  • (B) \(\delta / 4\)
  • (C) \(\delta / 8\)
  • (D) \(8\delta\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: Central sag is given by \(\delta = \dfrac{Wl^3}{4bd^3Y}\). Since \(\delta \propto \dfrac{1}{d^3}\), doubling \(d\) reduces \(\delta\) by a factor of \(2^3 = 8\).

Practice Question 10: Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Shear modulus is applicable to solids, liquids, and gases because all three states of matter resist changes in shape.
Statement II: Elastomers such as vulcanized rubber do not obey Hooke's law over most of their stress–strain curve and lack a well-defined plastic region.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
  • (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  • (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
Answer: (D)

Solution: Statement I is false because only solids possess a definite shape and can sustain shearing stress; liquids and gases flow under shear, so their shear modulus is zero. Statement II is true for elastomers.

8. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Avoid These Common NEET Mistakes:
  • Doubling the Tension: A wire fixed to a ceiling and pulled by weight \(F\) at the bottom has tension \(F\) (not \(2F\)) at every cross-section.
  • Confusing Stretching with Elasticity: Stretching easily means low Young's modulus (less elastic). Steel is more elastic than rubber.
  • Unit Conversions: Always convert \(\mathrm{cm^2}\) to \(\mathrm{m^2}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{cm^2} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\)) and \(\mathrm{mm}\) to \(\mathrm{m}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{mm} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}\)). Note that \(1\,\mathrm{GPa} = 10^{9}\,\mathrm{Pa}\).
  • Energy Formula Selection: Use \(U = \frac{1}{2}Y(\Delta L)^2 A/L\) when \(\Delta L\) is fixed, and \(U = \frac{F^2 L}{2AY}\) when \(F\) is fixed!
