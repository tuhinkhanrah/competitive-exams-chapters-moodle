1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

In everyday experience, we use the terms hot and cold as relative indicators of the thermal state of an object. A kettle of boiling water is hotter than a box containing ice. In physics, however, sensory perception by touch is too limited and unreliable for quantitative work, requiring rigorous definitions of temperature and heat. This tutorial develops the complete framework of thermal physics required for the NEET UG examination:

  • Distinguish clearly between temperature (a measure of hotness) and heat (energy transferred solely due to a temperature difference).
  • Calibrate and convert between Celsius (°C), Fahrenheit (°F), Kelvin (K), and custom thermometric scales using fixed reference points and the triple point of water.
  • Apply the ideal gas laws to constant-volume gas thermometry and understand the physical origin of absolute zero (\(-273.15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) or \(0\,\mathrm{K}\)).
  • Analyze linear (\(\alpha_l\)), area (\(\alpha_A = 2\alpha_l\)), and volume (\(\alpha_v = 3\alpha_l\)) thermal expansion of solids, liquids, and gases, including the anomalous expansion of water between \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and thermal stress in rigidly fixed rods.
  • Master specific heat capacity, molar heat capacities (\(C_p\) and \(C_v\)), calorimetry, phase changes, regelation, and latent heats of fusion (\(L_f\)) and vaporisation (\(L_v\)).
  • Solve quantitative problems involving steady-state heat conduction across single and composite slabs, natural and forced convection, blackbody radiation (Wien's displacement law and Stefan-Boltzmann law), and Newton's law of cooling.

2. Temperature, Heat, and Thermometry

2.1 Temperature vs. Heat

Temperature is a relative measure or quantitative indication of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body. When a glass of ice-cold water is left on a table on a hot summer day, it eventually warms up, whereas a cup of hot tea on the same table cools down. Whenever a temperature difference exists between a system and its surrounding medium, energy flows until both attain the same temperature (thermal equilibrium).

Heat is defined as the form of energy transferred between two (or more) systems, or between a system and its surroundings, by virtue of a temperature difference. For ice-cold water, heat flows from the environment into the glass; for hot tea, heat flows from the cup to the environment.

  • SI Unit of Heat Energy (\(\Delta Q\)): joule (\(\mathrm{J}\)); dimensional formula \([\mathrm{M L^2 T^{-2}}]\). Another common non-SI unit is the calorie (\(\mathrm{cal}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{cal} \approx 4.186\,\mathrm{J}\). Note that heat \(Q\) is a path-dependent energy transfer, not a state variable.
  • SI Unit of Temperature (\(T\)): kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\)); dimensional formula \([\mathrm{K}]\). A commonly used practical unit is degree Celsius (\(^\circ\mathrm{C}\)).

2.2 Measurement of Temperature & Temperature Scales

A thermometer utilizes a measurable physical property of matter—called a thermometric property—that varies sufficiently and predictably with temperature. Common liquid-in-glass thermometers use mercury or alcohol, whose volume varies linearly with temperature over a wide range. Electrical resistance thermometers use the linear variation of resistance \(R\) with temperature.

To construct any standard temperature scale, two fixed reference points correlated to reproducible physical phenomena are chosen. Historically, the ice point (freezing point of pure water under standard atmospheric pressure) and the steam point (boiling point of pure water under standard atmospheric pressure) were selected:

  • Celsius Scale (\(t_C\)): Ice point \(= 0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), Steam point \(= 100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), divided into \(100\) equal intervals.
  • Fahrenheit Scale (\(t_F\)): Ice point \(= 32\,^\circ\mathrm{F}\), Steam point \(= 212\,^\circ\mathrm{F}\), divided into \(180\) equal intervals.
Celsius–Fahrenheit Conversion Relation: \[ \frac{t_F - 32}{180} = \frac{t_C}{100} \implies t_F = \frac{9}{5}\,t_C + 32 \] For a small or finite change in temperature: \[ \Delta t_F = \frac{9}{5}\,\Delta t_C = 1.8\,\Delta t_C \]

2.3 Ideal-Gas Equation, Constant-Volume Gas Thermometer & Absolute Temperature

Liquid-in-glass thermometers constructed with different liquids (e.g., mercury vs. alcohol) agree at the fixed points but show slightly different readings at intermediate temperatures because expansion properties differ from liquid to liquid. Experiments show, however, that all gases at low densities exhibit the exact same expansion behaviour:

  • Boyle's Law (constant \(T\)): \(PV = \text{constant}\)
  • Charles' Law (constant \(P\)): \(\dfrac{V}{T} = \text{constant}\)

Combining these two laws for \(\mu\) moles of any low-density gas yields the Ideal-Gas Equation:

\[ \frac{PV}{T} = \mu R \implies PV = \mu R T \] where \(\mu\) is the number of moles in the gas sample and \(R = 8.31\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) is the universal gas constant.

In a constant-volume gas thermometer, the volume \(V\) of a low-density gas is held constant, so \(P \propto T\). Temperature is read directly in terms of pressure. When pressure \(P\) is plotted against temperature \(t_C\) for different low-density gases, straight lines are obtained over a wide temperature range. Extrapolating these straight lines to the zero-pressure axis reveals that all low-density gases converge to the exact same minimum temperature: \(-273.15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

This extrapolated zero-pressure temperature is designated as absolute zero (\(0\,\mathrm{K}\))—the foundation of the Kelvin (absolute) temperature scale, where molecular activity reaches its theoretical minimum. Choosing the unit interval size on the Kelvin scale to be identical to \(1\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) gives the exact conversion:

\[ T = t_C + 273.15 \quad \text{or} \quad t_C = T - 273.15 \]

Modern Thermometry & The Triple Point of Water:
Why is the triple point of water preferred over the melting point of ice and boiling point of water as the standard fixed point in modern thermometry? Because the melting and boiling points depend significantly on external atmospheric pressure. In contrast, the triple point of water—the unique state at which solid ice, liquid water, and water vapour coexist in thermal equilibrium—occurs at a single, unique pressure (\(6.11 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{atm}\) or \(611\,\mathrm{Pa}\), also noted in the chapter box as \(0.006\,\mathrm{atm}\)) and a unique temperature assigned the exact value: \[ T_{\mathrm{tr}} = 273.16\,\mathrm{K} \quad (\text{which corresponds to } t_C = 0.01\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) \] The two fixed points of the modern Kelvin scale are therefore absolute zero (\(0\,\mathrm{K}\)) and the triple point of water (\(273.16\,\mathrm{K}\)). Since the ice point at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) is \(0.01\,\mathrm{K}\) below the triple point of water, \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) corresponds to \(273.16 - 0.01 = 273.15\,\mathrm{K}\), which explains why \(273.15\) (and not \(273.16\)) appears in \(t_C = T - 273.15\).For any constant-volume gas thermometer calibrated at the triple point: \[ T = \frac{P}{P_{\mathrm{tr}}} \times 273.16\,\mathrm{K} \]

3. Thermal Expansion of Solids, Liquids, and Gases

Most substances expand when heated and contract when cooled. A tightly screwed metallic lid on a sealed glass bottle can be loosened by placing the lid in hot water because the metal expands more than the glass. The increase in dimensions of a body due to an increase in its temperature is called thermal expansion.

3.1 Linear, Area, and Volume Expansion

  • Linear Expansion: For a long rod of initial length \(l\) subjected to a small temperature change \(\Delta T\), the fractional change in length \(\dfrac{\Delta l}{l}\) is directly proportional to \(\Delta T\): \[ \frac{\Delta l}{l} = \alpha_l\,\Delta T \implies l' = l(1 + \alpha_l\,\Delta T) \] where \(\alpha_l\) is the coefficient of linear expansion (or linear expansivity), with SI unit \(\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) or \(^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1}\).
  • Area (Superficial) Expansion: For a solid sheet of area \(A\), the fractional change in area for a small temperature change \(\Delta T\) is: \[ \frac{\Delta A}{A} = \alpha_A\,\Delta T = 2\alpha_l\,\Delta T \]
  • Volume (Cubical) Expansion: For a 3D body or fluid of volume \(V\), the fractional change in volume for a temperature change \(\Delta T\) defines the coefficient of volume expansion \(\alpha_v\): \[ \alpha_v = \left(\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right)\frac{1}{\Delta T} \implies \frac{\Delta V}{V} = \alpha_v\,\Delta T = 3\alpha_l\,\Delta T \] Note: \(\alpha_v\) is not strictly constant at all temperatures; for a metal like copper, \(\alpha_v\) increases with temperature at low temperatures and becomes constant only at high temperatures (above roughly \(500\,\mathrm{K}\)).

Proof of \(\alpha_v = 3\alpha_l\) for an Isotropic Solid

Consider a solid cube of side length \(l\) and initial volume \(V = l^3\) that expands equally in all three mutually perpendicular directions when its temperature increases by \(\Delta T\). Since \(\Delta l = \alpha_l l \Delta T\), the new volume is:

\[ V + \Delta V = (l + \Delta l)^3 = l^3 + 3l^2\Delta l + 3l(\Delta l)^2 + (\Delta l)^3 \]

Because \(\Delta l \ll l\) for typical temperature changes (since \(\alpha_l \sim 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\)), terms containing \((\Delta l)^2\) and \((\Delta l)^3\) are negligible and may be dropped:

\[ \Delta V \simeq 3l^2\Delta l = 3l^2(\alpha_l l \Delta T) = 3V\alpha_l\Delta T \implies \alpha_v = \left(\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right)\frac{1}{\Delta T} = 3\alpha_l \]

Thus, the ratio of the three expansion coefficients for an isotropic solid is \(\alpha_l : \alpha_A : \alpha_v = 1 : 2 : 3\). Note also that when a solid containing a cavity or a drilled hole is heated, the dimensions of the hole or cavity expand at the exact same rate as the solid material itself.

3.2 Variation of Density with Temperature & Anomalous Expansion of Water

For a fixed mass \(m\) of a substance, density is \(\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}\). Since mass remains constant during heating, \(\dfrac{\Delta \rho}{\rho} = -\dfrac{\Delta V}{V} = -\alpha_v\,\Delta T\), or \(\rho' \simeq \rho(1 - \alpha_v\,\Delta T)\).

Anomalous Behaviour of Water: Unlike normal liquids, water contracts on heating between \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). When water is cooled from room temperature, its volume decreases until it reaches \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Below \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (from \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) down to \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)), its volume increases and therefore its density decreases. Consequently, water has its minimum volume and maximum density (\(1.00000 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\)) at \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Environmental Significance: Because of this anomalous property, lakes and ponds in cold climates freeze at the top surface first. As a lake cools toward \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), surface water loses heat to the atmosphere, becomes denser, and sinks, while warmer water rises. Once the water cools below \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), however, colder water becomes less dense and stays at the top surface, where it eventually freezes into a protective layer of ice while aquatic animal and plant life survive in the \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) liquid water below.

3.3 Volume Expansion of an Ideal Gas & Thermal Stress

Ideal Gas Expansion at Constant Pressure: From \(PV = \mu RT\), at constant pressure \(P\,\Delta V = \mu R\,\Delta T\). Dividing the two relations gives:

\[ \frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{\Delta T}{T} \implies \alpha_v = \left(\frac{\Delta V}{V}\right)\frac{1}{\Delta T} = \frac{1}{T} \quad (\text{for an ideal gas at constant pressure}) \]

At \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(T = 273.15\,\mathrm{K}\)), \(\alpha_v = 3.7 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), and at room temperature (\(\sim 300\,\mathrm{K}\)), \(\alpha_v \approx 3300 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), which is orders of magnitude larger than for solids and liquids and decreases with increasing temperature.

Thermal Stress in a Rigidly Clamped Rod: If a rod of length \(l\), cross-sectional area \(A\), Young's modulus \(Y\), and linear expansivity \(\alpha_l\) is rigidly clamped at both ends so that expansion or contraction is prevented when temperature changes by \(\Delta T\), a compressive or tensile strain \(\dfrac{\Delta l}{l} = \alpha_l\,\Delta T\) is developed. The resulting thermal stress and thermal force \(\Delta F\) are:

\[ \text{Thermal Stress} = \frac{\Delta F}{A} = Y\left(\frac{\Delta l}{l}\right) = Y\,\alpha_l\,\Delta T, \qquad \Delta F = Y A \alpha_l\,\Delta T \]

For example, a steel rail of length \(5\,\mathrm{m}\) and cross-section \(A = 40\,\mathrm{cm^2} = 40 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) (\(\alpha_l = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\), \(Y = 2 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)) heated by \(\Delta T = 10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) develops a strain of \(1.2 \times 10^{-4}\), a thermal stress of \(2.4 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\), and a force \(\Delta F \simeq 10^5\,\mathrm{N}\) capable of bending the rails if no expansion gaps are provided.

4. Specific Heat Capacity, Calorimetry, and Change of State

4.1 Heat Capacity, Specific Heat Capacity, and Molar Specific Heat

Experiments show that the quantity of heat \(\Delta Q\) required to raise the temperature of a body by \(\Delta T\) (without phase change) is proportional to its mass \(m\) and to \(\Delta T\), and depends on the nature of the substance:

  • Heat Capacity (\(S\)): \(S = \dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T}\)   [SI Unit: \(\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\)]
  • Specific Heat Capacity (\(s\)): \(s = \dfrac{S}{m} = \dfrac{1}{m}\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T} \implies \Delta Q = m s \Delta T\)   [SI Unit: \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), Dimensions: \([\mathrm{L^2 T^{-2} K^{-1}}]\)]
  • Molar Specific Heat Capacity (\(C\)): For \(\mu\) moles, \(C = \dfrac{S}{\mu} = \dfrac{1}{\mu}\dfrac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T} \implies \Delta Q = \mu C \Delta T\)   [SI Unit: \(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)]

For gases, heat transfer conditions must be specified: \(C_p\) is the molar specific heat capacity at constant pressure, and \(C_v\) is the molar specific heat capacity at constant volume.

Applications of Water's High Specific Heat Capacity: Water is used as a coolant in automobile radiators and as a heating medium in hot water bags. Because water warms up and cools down much more slowly than land, coastal regions experience moderate climates while desert areas heat up rapidly by day and cool rapidly at night.

4.2 Calorimetry

Calorimetry is the measurement of heat. A calorimeter consists of a metallic vessel and stirrer (usually copper or aluminium) placed inside a wooden jacket packed with heat-insulating material like glass wool, with a thermometer inserted through an opening in the lid. For an isolated system (no heat exchange with the surroundings), when bodies at different temperatures are mixed:

Principle of Calorimetry (Conservation of Heat Energy): \[ \text{Heat Lost by Hotter Part(s)} = \text{Heat Gained by Colder Part(s)} \]

4.3 Change of State (Phase Transitions), Triple Point & Latent Heat

During a phase transition (change of state), temperature remains constant even though heat is continuously supplied or removed, and both phases coexist in thermal equilibrium:

  • Melting (Fusion) & Freezing: Solid \(\leftrightarrow\) Liquid transition at the melting point. The melting point at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\) is the normal melting point.
  • Regelation: Melting of ice under increased pressure at a temperature below \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), followed by refreezing once the pressure is removed. If a metallic wire with heavy weights attached to its ends is placed across an ice slab, the high pressure beneath the wire lowers the melting point of ice, causing the ice directly below the wire to melt; as the wire passes through, the water above it refreezes, so the wire cuts completely through the ice slab without splitting it into two pieces. Skating on snow is possible because pressure under the skates forms a thin lubricating film of water.
  • Vaporisation & Boiling: Liquid \(\leftrightarrow\) Vapour (gas) transition at the boiling point. Increasing pressure raises the boiling point (e.g., inside a pressure cooker, making cooking faster). Decreasing pressure lowers the boiling point (e.g., at high hill altitudes where atmospheric pressure is low, making cooking difficult).
  • Sublimation: Direct transition between solid and vapour states without passing through the liquid state (e.g., dry ice [solid \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)] and iodine).

Phase Diagrams (\(P\text{–}T\) Diagrams) and Triple Point

A pressure–temperature (\(P\text{–}T\)) phase diagram divides the plane into solid, liquid, and vapour regions separated by three coexistence curves:

  1. Fusion Curve (AO): Solid and liquid coexist in equilibrium. (For water, the fusion curve slopes leftward, showing that melting point decreases with pressure; for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), it slopes rightward.)
  2. Sublimation Curve (BO): Solid and vapour coexist in equilibrium.
  3. Vaporisation Curve (CO): Liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium (terminating at the critical point C).

The unique point O where all three curves meet and all three phases (solid, liquid, vapour) coexist in equilibrium is the triple point:

  • Water (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)): Triple point at \(T = 273.16\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(0.01\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)) and \(P = 0.006\,\mathrm{atm}\) (\(6.11 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{atm}\) or \(611\,\mathrm{Pa}\)). Critical point at \(374\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(218\,\mathrm{atm}\).
  • Carbon Dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)): Triple point at \(T = -56.6\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (\(216.55\,\mathrm{K}\)) and \(P = 5.11\,\mathrm{atm}\). Because liquid \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) cannot exist at \(1.0\,\mathrm{atm}\) (which lies below \(5.11\,\mathrm{atm}\)), solid \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) sublimes directly to vapour at \(-78.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) at \(1.0\,\mathrm{atm}\).

Latent Heat (\(L\))

The amount of heat energy transferred per unit mass during a change of state of a substance at constant temperature and pressure is called its latent heat \(L\):

\[ Q = m L \implies L = \frac{Q}{m} \qquad [\text{SI Unit: }\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}},\text{ Dimensions: }[\mathrm{L^2 T^{-2}}]] \]
  • Latent heat of fusion of water (\(L_f\)): \(L_f = 3.33 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) (or \(3.35 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}} \approx 80\,\mathrm{kcal\,kg^{-1}}\)) at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\).
  • Latent heat of vaporisation of water (\(L_v\)): \(L_v = 22.6 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) (or \(2.256 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}} \approx 540\,\mathrm{kcal\,kg^{-1}}\)) at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\).

Why steam burns are more severe than boiling water burns: Steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) carries \(22.6 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) more heat energy than liquid water at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) due to its large latent heat of vaporisation.

5. Modes of Heat Transfer & Newton's Law of Cooling

Heat energy is transferred from regions of higher temperature to regions of lower temperature by three distinct mechanisms: conduction, convection, and radiation.

5.1 Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of heat between adjacent parts of a body through molecular collisions without any bulk flow of matter. Solids (especially metals) are the best thermal conductors, gases are poor conductors, and liquids have intermediate conductivities.

Consider a uniform bar of length \(L\) and cross-sectional area \(A\) whose sides are thermally insulated and whose ends are maintained at steady temperatures \(T_C\) and \(T_D\) (\(T_C > T_D\)). In the steady state, the temperature decreases uniformly along the bar, and the time rate of heat flow (heat current \(H\)) through any cross-section is:

\[ H = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = K A \frac{T_C - T_D}{L} = -K A \frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}x} \] where \(K\) is the thermal conductivity of the material [SI unit: \(\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\); dimensions: \([\mathrm{M L T^{-3} K^{-1}}]\)].

Thermal Resistance & Composite Bars in Series: By analogy with Ohm's law (\(I = \Delta V / R\)), we can define thermal resistance \(R_{\mathrm{th}} = \dfrac{L}{K A}\) so that \(H = \dfrac{\Delta T}{R_{\mathrm{th}}}\). When two insulated bars of lengths \(L_1, L_2\), areas \(A_1, A_2\), and conductivities \(K_1, K_2\) are joined end-to-end in series between temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), the steady-state heat current \(H\) is identical through both bars:

\[ H = \frac{K_1 A_1 (T_1 - T_0)}{L_1} = \frac{K_2 A_2 (T_0 - T_2)}{L_2} \]

If \(A_1 = A_2 = A\) and \(L_1 = L_2 = L\), the junction temperature \(T_0\) and equivalent thermal conductivity \(K'\) of the compound bar of total length \(2L\) are:

\[ T_0 = \frac{K_1 T_1 + K_2 T_2}{K_1 + K_2}, \qquad K' = \frac{2 K_1 K_2}{K_1 + K_2} \]

5.2 Convection

Convection is heat transfer by the actual bulk motion of matter and is possible only in fluids (liquids and gases):

  • Natural Convection: Driven by gravity and buoyancy due to density differences between heated (less dense, rising) and cooler (more dense, sinking) fluid layers. Examples include sea breeze during the day (land heats faster than water; warm air over land rises and cooler air from the sea blows in), land breeze at night (land cools faster than water, reversing the wind cycle), and global trade winds (modified by the Earth's rotation, where equatorial air has an eastward speed of \(1600\,\mathrm{km/h}\) and descends near \(30^\circ\mathrm{N}\) latitude).
  • Forced Convection: Fluid is forced to circulate by an external pump or fan (e.g., human circulatory system where the heart pumps blood to maintain uniform body temperature, automobile engine cooling systems, forced-air home heating).

5.3 Radiation & Blackbody Radiation Laws

Radiation requires no material medium; energy is transferred as electromagnetic waves (thermal radiation) travelling in vacuum at the speed of light \(c = 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Black surfaces absorb and emit radiant energy much better than light-coloured or polished silvery surfaces. A Dewar flask (thermos bottle) minimizes all three modes of heat transfer: a vacuum between double glass walls prevents conduction and convection, silvered inner and outer walls reflect radiation, and a cork support prevents conduction.

1. Wien's Displacement Law:
Blackbody radiation has a continuous wavelength spectrum. The wavelength \(\lambda_m\) corresponding to maximum emitted radiation intensity is inversely proportional to absolute temperature \(T\): \[ \lambda_m T = b = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K} \] Applications: As iron is heated, \(\lambda_m\) shifts from infrared to dull red, reddish-yellow, and white-hot. Moon's peak radiation at \(\lambda_m = 14\,\mathrm{µm}\) gives \(T \approx 200\,\mathrm{K}\); solar radiation peaking at \(\lambda_m = 4753\,\mathrm{Å}\) gives the Sun's surface temperature \(T \approx 6060\,\mathrm{K}\).

2. Stefan–Boltzmann Law:
The total electromagnetic energy radiated per unit time (\(H\)) by a body of surface area \(A\), emissivity \(e\) (\(e = 1\) for an ideal blackbody), and absolute temperature \(T\) is: \[ H_{\mathrm{emitted}} = A e \sigma T^4 \qquad \left(\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}\right) \] When surrounded by an enclosure at absolute temperature \(T_s\), the net rate of radiant energy loss is: \[ H_{\mathrm{net}} = e \sigma A \left(T^4 - T_s^4\right) \]

5.4 Newton's Law of Cooling

For small temperature differences between a hot body at temperature \(T_2\) and its surroundings at temperature \(T_1\), the rate of loss of heat \(-\dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t}\) is directly proportional to the temperature excess \(\Delta T = (T_2 - T_1)\):

\[ -\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = k(T_2 - T_1) \] Since \(\mathrm{d}Q = m s\,\mathrm{d}T_2\), where \(m\) is the mass and \(s\) is the specific heat capacity of the cooling body: \[ -m s \frac{\mathrm{d}T_2}{\mathrm{d}t} = k(T_2 - T_1) \implies \frac{\mathrm{d}T_2}{\mathrm{d}t} = -K(T_2 - T_1) \quad \left(\text{where } K = \frac{k}{m s}\right) \] Integrating with respect to time \(t\) gives the exact exponential decay law: \[ \log_e(T_2 - T_1) = -K t + c \implies T_2(t) = T_1 + C' e^{-K t} \] Average-Temperature Approximation (Standard NEET Method):
When a body cools from \(T_i\) to \(T_f\) in time \(t\) in surroundings at \(T_1\), we approximate \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}T_2}{\mathrm{d}t}\) by \(\dfrac{T_i - T_f}{t}\) and \(T_2\) by the arithmetic mean \(T_{\mathrm{avg}} = \dfrac{T_i + T_f}{2}\): \[ \frac{T_i - T_f}{t} = K\left(\frac{T_i + T_f}{2} - T_1\right) \]

Worked Example 1: Thermal Expansion — Fitting an Iron Ring on a Wooden Wheel

Problem: A blacksmith fixes an iron ring on the rim of the wooden wheel of a horse cart. The diameters of the wooden rim and the iron ring are \(5.243\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(5.231\,\mathrm{m}\), respectively, at \(27\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). To what temperature should the iron ring be heated so as to fit the rim of the wheel? (Coefficient of linear expansion of iron \(\alpha_l = 1.20 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\)).

Solution:
Given initial temperature \(T_1 = 27\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), initial ring diameter \(L_{T_1} = 5.231\,\mathrm{m}\), and target diameter \(L_{T_2} = 5.243\,\mathrm{m}\). Using the linear expansion relation:

\[ L_{T_2} = L_{T_1}\left[1 + \alpha_l(T_2 - T_1)\right] \] \[ 5.243 = 5.231\left[1 + 1.20 \times 10^{-5}(T_2 - 27)\right] \implies T_2 - 27 = \frac{5.243 - 5.231}{5.231 \times 1.20 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{0.012}{6.2772 \times 10^{-5}} \approx 191.2\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \] \[ T_2 = 27 + 191.2 \approx 218\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \]
Worked Example 2: Calorimetry — Specific Heat Capacity of Aluminium

Problem: A sphere of \(0.047\,\mathrm{kg}\) aluminium is placed for sufficient time in a vessel containing boiling water so that the sphere is at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). It is then immediately transferred to a \(0.14\,\mathrm{kg}\) copper calorimeter containing \(0.25\,\mathrm{kg}\) water at \(20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The temperature of water rises and attains a steady state at \(23\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Calculate the specific heat capacity of aluminium. (\(s_{\mathrm{w}} = 4.18 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), \(s_{\mathrm{Cu}} = 0.386 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).

Solution:
Heat lost by aluminium sphere \(= m_1 s_{\mathrm{Al}} \Delta T_1 = 0.047 \times s_{\mathrm{Al}} \times (100 - 23) = 3.619\,s_{\mathrm{Al}}\).
Heat gained by water and copper calorimeter:

\[ Q_{\mathrm{gained}} = (m_2 s_{\mathrm{w}} + m_3 s_{\mathrm{Cu}})\Delta T_2 = (0.25 \times 4.18 \times 10^3 + 0.14 \times 0.386 \times 10^3)(23 - 20) \] \[ Q_{\mathrm{gained}} = (1045 + 54.04) \times 3 = 1099.04 \times 3 = 3297.12\,\mathrm{J} \] Equating heat lost to heat gained: \[ 3.619\,s_{\mathrm{Al}} = 3297.12 \implies s_{\mathrm{Al}} = \frac{3297.12}{3.619} \approx 911\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}} = 0.911\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \]
Worked Example 3: Multi-Stage Phase Transition — Ice at \(-12\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to Steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)

Problem: Calculate the total heat required to convert \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\) of ice at \(-12\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) kept in a calorimeter to steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) at atmospheric pressure. Given: \(s_{\mathrm{ice}} = 2100\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), \(s_{\mathrm{water}} = 4186\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), \(L_{f,\mathrm{ice}} = 3.35 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\), \(L_{\mathrm{steam}} = 2.256 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\).

Solution:
The conversion occurs in four sequential steps for \(m = 3\,\mathrm{kg}\):

  1. Heat ice from \(-12\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\): \(Q_1 = m s_{\mathrm{ice}}\Delta T_1 = 3 \times 2100 \times 12 = 75,600\,\mathrm{J}\).
  2. Melt ice at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to water at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\): \(Q_2 = m L_{f,\mathrm{ice}} = 3 \times 3.35 \times 10^5 = 1,005,000\,\mathrm{J}\).
  3. Heat water from \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\): \(Q_3 = m s_{\mathrm{water}}\Delta T_2 = 3 \times 4186 \times 100 = 1,255,800\,\mathrm{J}\).
  4. Vaporise water at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\): \(Q_4 = m L_{\mathrm{steam}} = 3 \times 2.256 \times 10^6 = 6,768,000\,\mathrm{J}\).

Total heat required: \(Q = Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3 + Q_4 = 9,104,400\,\mathrm{J} \approx 9.1 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J}\).

Worked Example 4: Steady-State Conduction in a Composite Rod

Problem: A steel rod of length \(L_1 = 15.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) is joined in series with a copper rod of length \(L_2 = 10.0\,\mathrm{cm}\). Their sides are thermally insulated. The free end of the steel rod is maintained in a furnace at \(300\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and the free end of the copper rod is in an ice box at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The cross-sectional area of the steel rod is twice that of the copper rod (\(A_1 = 2A_2\)). Find the steady-state temperature of the steel–copper junction. (\(K_{\mathrm{steel}} = 50.2\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), \(K_{\mathrm{Cu}} = 385\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).

Solution:
In the steady state, the heat current through the steel rod equals the heat current through the copper rod:

\[ \frac{K_1 A_1 (300 - T)}{L_1} = \frac{K_2 A_2 (T - 0)}{L_2} \] Substituting \(A_1 = 2A_2\), \(L_1 = 15.0\,\mathrm{cm}\), \(L_2 = 10.0\,\mathrm{cm}\), \(K_1 = 50.2\), and \(K_2 = 385\): \[ \frac{50.2 \times 2 (300 - T)}{15} = \frac{385\,T}{10} \implies 6.693(300 - T) = 38.5\,T \implies 2008 = 45.193\,T \implies T \approx 44.4\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \]
Worked Example 5: Newton's Law of Cooling

Problem: A pan filled with hot food cools from \(94\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(86\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in \(2\,\mathrm{minutes}\) when the room temperature is \(20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). How long will it take to cool from \(71\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(69\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)?

Solution:
Case 1 (\(94\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \to 86\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): Average temperature \(T_{\mathrm{avg,1}} = \dfrac{94 + 86}{2} = 90\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Excess temperature above room temperature \(\Delta T_1 = 90 - 20 = 70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Using the average form of Newton's law of cooling:

\[ \frac{94 - 86}{2\,\mathrm{min}} = K(70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) \implies \frac{8}{2} = 70 K \quad \text{--- (1)} \]

Case 2 (\(71\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \to 69\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): Average temperature \(T_{\mathrm{avg,2}} = \dfrac{71 + 69}{2} = 70\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Excess temperature \(\Delta T_2 = 70 - 20 = 50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Let the required time be \(t\):

\[ \frac{71 - 69}{t} = K(50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) \implies \frac{2}{t} = 50 K \quad \text{--- (2)} \] Dividing equation (1) by equation (2): \[ \frac{4}{2/t} = \frac{70}{50} \implies 2t = 1.4 \implies t = 0.7\,\mathrm{min} = 42\,\mathrm{s} \]

First convert the temperature drop from Fahrenheit to Celsius: \[ \Delta t_F = 101\,^\circ\mathrm{F} - 98\,^\circ\mathrm{F} = 3\,^\circ\mathrm{F} \implies \Delta t_C = \frac{5}{9}\Delta t_F = \frac{5}{9} \times 3 = \frac{5}{3}\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \] Total heat that must be removed from the child (\(m = 30\,\mathrm{kg} = 30,000\,\mathrm{g}\)): \[ Q = m s \Delta t_C = 30,000\,\mathrm{g} \times 1\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}\,^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1}} \times \frac{5}{3}\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 50,000\,\mathrm{cal} \] If mass \(m_{\mathrm{sweat}}\) evaporates with latent heat \(L_v = 580\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}\): \[ m_{\mathrm{sweat}} = \frac{Q}{L_v} = \frac{50,000\,\mathrm{cal}}{580\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}} \approx 86.21\,\mathrm{g} \] Over \(t = 20\,\mathrm{min}\), the average rate of extra evaporation is: \[ \frac{m_{\mathrm{sweat}}}{t} = \frac{86.21\,\mathrm{g}}{20\,\mathrm{min}} \approx 4.31\,\mathrm{g\,min^{-1}} \]

Practice Question 10 — Steady-State Conduction in a Cubical Icebox and a Brass Boiler

Part I: A cubical thermacole icebox of side \(30\,\mathrm{cm}\) has a wall thickness of \(5.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) (\(K = 0.01\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)). If \(4.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) of ice at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is placed inside when the outside temperature is \(45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), how much ice remains unmelted after \(6\,\mathrm{hours}\)? (\(L_f = 335 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\)).
Part II: A brass boiler has a base area of \(0.15\,\mathrm{m^2}\) and thickness \(1.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) (\(K_{\mathrm{brass}} = 109\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)). It boils water at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) at the rate of \(6.0\,\mathrm{kg/min}\) (\(L_v = 2256 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\)). What is the temperature of the part of the flame in contact with the boiler?

  • (A) Ice remaining \(= 3.69\,\mathrm{kg}\); Flame temperature \(= 238\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (B) Ice remaining \(= 0.31\,\mathrm{kg}\); Flame temperature \(= 238\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (C) Ice remaining \(= 3.69\,\mathrm{kg}\); Flame temperature \(= 138\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) Ice remaining \(= 3.13\,\mathrm{kg}\); Flame temperature \(= 338\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution:
Part I (Cubical Icebox): Total surface area of the \(6\) faces of the cube of side \(s = 0.30\,\mathrm{m}\) is \(A = 6s^2 = 6(0.30)^2 = 0.54\,\mathrm{m^2}\). Thickness \(L = 0.05\,\mathrm{m}\), \(\Delta T = 45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), time \(t = 6\,\mathrm{h} = 21,600\,\mathrm{s}\). Total heat entering the box: \[ Q = \frac{K A \Delta T}{L}t = \frac{0.01 \times 0.54 \times 45}{0.05} \times 21,600 = 4.86\,\mathrm{W} \times 21,600\,\mathrm{s} = 104,976\,\mathrm{J} \] Mass of ice melted: \(m_{\mathrm{melted}} = \dfrac{Q}{L_f} = \dfrac{104,976}{335 \times 10^3} \approx 0.313\,\mathrm{kg}\).
Mass of ice remaining \(= 4.0 - 0.313 = 3.687\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 3.69\,\mathrm{kg}\).

Part II (Brass Boiler): Rate of heat required to boil \(6.0\,\mathrm{kg/min} = 0.10\,\mathrm{kg/s}\) of water: \[ H = \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t}L_v = 0.10\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}} \times 2256 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}} = 2.256 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{W} \] Using \(H = \dfrac{K A (T_1 - 100)}{L}\) with \(A = 0.15\,\mathrm{m^2}\) and \(L = 0.01\,\mathrm{m}\): \[ T_1 - 100 = \frac{H L}{K A} = \frac{2.256 \times 10^5 \times 0.01}{109 \times 0.15} = \frac{2256}{16.35} \approx 138\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \implies T_1 = 238\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \]

Practice Question 11 — Conceptual Reasoning on Thermal Radiation & Heat Transfer

Consider the following four physical statements:
1. A body with a large reflectivity is a poor emitter of thermal radiation.
2. A brass tumbler feels much colder than a wooden tray on a chilly day because brass has a much higher specific heat capacity than wood.
3. An optical pyrometer calibrated for ideal blackbody radiation gives too low a value for the temperature of a red-hot iron piece in the open, but gives a correct value when the same piece is inside a furnace.
4. Heating systems based on circulation of steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) are more efficient in warming a building than those based on circulation of hot water at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
Which of the above statements are correct?

  • (A) 1, 3, and 4 only
  • (B) 1, 2, and 3 only
  • (C) 2 and 4 only
  • (D) 1, 2, 3, and 4
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution:

  • Statement 1 is correct: A body with large reflectivity is a poor absorber of radiation, and good absorbers are good emitters while poor absorbers are poor emitters.
  • Statement 2 is incorrect: A brass tumbler feels much colder than a wooden tray on a chilly day because brass has a much higher thermal conductivity (not specific heat capacity) than wood, so it conducts heat away from the hand rapidly.
  • Statement 3 is correct: In the open, a red-hot iron piece radiates at a lower rate \(H = e\sigma A(T^4 - T_0^4)\) with \(e < 1\), whereas inside a uniform-temperature furnace it reflects as well as emits radiation and behaves like a full blackbody radiator at temperature \(T\).
  • Statement 4 is correct: Steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) carries an extra \(22.6 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) of latent heat of vaporisation compared to liquid water at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Practice Question 12 — Newton's Law of Cooling Across Two Intervals

A body cools from \(80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in \(5\,\mathrm{minutes}\). Calculate the time it takes to cool from \(60\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) when the temperature of the surroundings is \(20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (using the standard average-temperature method).

  • (A) \(9\,\mathrm{minutes}\)
  • (B) \(7.5\,\mathrm{minutes}\)
  • (C) \(10\,\mathrm{minutes}\)
  • (D) \(12\,\mathrm{minutes}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution:
First interval (\(80\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \to 50\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in \(t_1 = 5\,\mathrm{min}\)):
Average temperature \(T_{\mathrm{avg,1}} = \dfrac{80 + 50}{2} = 65\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Excess over surroundings (\(T_s = 20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)) is \(65 - 20 = 45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\): \[ \frac{80 - 50}{5\,\mathrm{min}} = K(45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) \implies 6 = 45 K \quad \text{--- (1)} \] Second interval (\(60\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \to 30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in time \(t_2\)):
Average temperature \(T_{\mathrm{avg,2}} = \dfrac{60 + 30}{2} = 45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Excess over surroundings is \(45 - 20 = 25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\): \[ \frac{60 - 30}{t_2} = K(25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) \implies \frac{30}{t_2} = 25 K \quad \text{--- (2)} \] Dividing equation (1) by equation (2): \[ \frac{6}{30/t_2} = \frac{45}{25} \implies \frac{t_2}{5} = \frac{9}{5} \implies t_2 = 9\,\mathrm{minutes} \]

8. Common Pitfalls & Quick Revision Checklist

  • Pitfall 1 — Temperature vs. Temperature Interval: A specific temperature reading converts via \(T = t_C + 273.15\) and \(t_F = \frac{9}{5}t_C + 32\), whereas a temperature difference converts via \(\Delta T = \Delta t_C\) and \(\Delta t_F = 1.8\,\Delta t_C\). Never add \(273.15\) or \(32\) to a temperature difference \(\Delta T\)!
  • Pitfall 2 — Expansion of Cavities and Holes: When a metal plate with a circular hole is heated, the hole increases in diameter by \(\Delta D = D\alpha_l\Delta T\), exactly as if it were filled with the same metal.
  • Pitfall 3 — Incomplete Melting or Vaporisation in Calorimetry: Always check whether the available heat from the warmer substance is sufficient to melt all the ice before assuming a final temperature above \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). If available heat \(Q_{\mathrm{avail}} < m_{\mathrm{ice}}L_f\), the final mixture remains at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) with a fraction of ice unmelted.
  • Pitfall 4 — Conduction vs. Convection in Fluids: A hot metallic bar placed under a running tap loses heat at its surface by conduction between the bar surface and the touching water layer, and then the moving water carries that heat away.
  • Pitfall 5 — Absolute Temperature in Radiation Laws: Always convert Celsius temperatures to Kelvin (\(T = t_C + 273.15\)) before applying Wien's law (\(\lambda_m T = b\)) or the Stefan–Boltzmann law (\(H = e\sigma A(T^4 - T_s^4)\)), and convert wavelengths from ångströms (\(1\,\mathrm{Å} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)) or micrometres (\(1\,\mathrm{µm} = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}\)) to metres.
