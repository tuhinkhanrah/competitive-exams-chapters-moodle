NEET UG Physics Thermal Physics & Gases

Kinetic Theory of Gases

A complete conceptual and problem-solving tutorial connecting microscopic molecular motion to macroscopic gas laws, internal energy, specific heats, and mean free path.

Learning Objectives & Exam Scope

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Explain why real gases approach ideal gas behaviour at low pressures and high temperatures.
  • Apply the ideal gas equation in molar, molecular, and density forms, along with Dalton's law of partial pressures.
  • Derive and interpret the kinetic pressure formula \(P = \frac{1}{3}nm\overline{v^2}\) and the kinetic meaning of absolute temperature.
  • Calculate root-mean-square (rms) speeds and compare molecular speeds across different gases and temperatures.
  • Use the law of equipartition of energy to determine \(C_v\), \(C_p\), and \(\gamma = C_p / C_v\) for monatomic, diatomic, and polyatomic gases as well as solids.
  • Compute and scale the mean free path \(\lambda\) and collision frequency with number density, pressure, temperature, and molecular diameter.

1. Molecular Picture of Matter & Ideal Gas Behaviour

1.1 Atomic Scale and States of Matter

All matter is composed of atoms and molecules in continuous motion. Typical atomic diameters are on the order of \(1\,\mathrm{Å} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\). In solids and liquids, neighbouring molecules sit only a few ångströms apart (roughly \(2\,\mathrm{Å}\)), so short-range repulsive forces and intermediate-range attractive forces strongly govern their behaviour. Solids maintain rigid lattices, whereas liquids allow molecules to slide past one another.

In a gas at ordinary temperature and pressure, the average intermolecular separation is roughly ten times larger (tens of ångströms), and the average distance travelled between collisions—the mean free path—is on the order of thousands of ångströms. Because molecules spend almost all their time far apart, intermolecular forces are negligible except during brief collisions. Even when a gas appears macroscopically calm, it is in dynamic equilibrium: individual molecules incessantly collide and exchange speeds, while macroscopic averages such as pressure and temperature remain steady.

1.2 Equation of State of an Ideal Gas

An ideal gas is a theoretical model in which point-like molecules exert no forces on one another except during instantaneous elastic collisions. Experimentally, real gases follow this model closely when intermolecular spacing is large—specifically at low pressures and high temperatures (well above their liquefaction point).

Forms of the Ideal Gas Equation \[ PV = \mu R T = N k_{\mathrm{B}} T \] \[ P = n k_{\mathrm{B}} T \qquad \text{and} \qquad P = \frac{\rho R T}{M_0} \]

Here, \(P\) is absolute pressure, \(V\) is volume, \(T\) is absolute temperature in kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\)), \(\mu = \frac{M}{M_0} = \frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{A}}}\) is the number of moles, \(N\) is the total number of molecules, \(n = \frac{N}{V}\) is the number density, \(\rho = \frac{M}{V}\) is the mass density, and \(M_0\) is the molar mass. The fundamental constants are:

  • Universal gas constant: \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)
  • Avogadro's number: \(N_{\mathrm{A}} = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\)
  • Boltzmann constant: \(k_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{R}{N_{\mathrm{A}}} = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\)

At Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP: \(T = 273\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(P = 1\,\mathrm{atm} = 1.01 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}\)), \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of any ideal gas occupies a molar volume of \(22.4\,\mathrm{L}\) (\(22.4 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\)).

1.3 Classical Gas Laws and Partial Pressures

The ideal gas equation unifies the empirical gas laws:

  • Boyle's Law (constant \(\mu, T\)): \(PV = \text{constant}\), so \(P \propto \frac{1}{V}\).
  • Charles' Law (constant \(\mu, P\)): \(V \propto T\) (using absolute kelvin temperature).
  • Avogadro's Hypothesis (fixed \(P, V, T\)): \(N = \frac{PV}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\) is identical for all ideal gases—equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.
  • Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures: In a non-reacting mixture of ideal gases occupying volume \(V\) at temperature \(T\), each component \(i\) exerts a partial pressure \(P_i = \frac{\mu_i R T}{V}\), and the total pressure is the sum: \[ P = P_1 + P_2 + \dots = (\mu_1 + \mu_2 + \dots)\frac{RT}{V} \]

2. Kinetic Pressure & Interpretation of Temperature

2.1 Microscopic Origin of Gas Pressure

Pressure on the walls of a container arises from the continuous bombardment of gas molecules. Consider a molecule of mass \(m\) moving with velocity components \((v_x, v_y, v_z)\) inside a vessel, approaching a flat wall perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis. In an elastic collision with the rigid wall, the tangential components \(v_y\) and \(v_z\) remain unchanged, while the normal component reverses from \(+v_x\) to \(-v_x\).

The change in momentum of the molecule is \(-mv_x - mv_x = -2mv_x\), so by momentum conservation, the wall receives an impulse of \(+2mv_x\).

In a short time \(\Delta t\), molecules within a distance \(v_x \Delta t\) of a wall patch of area \(A\) can reach it. Because molecular motion is random, half move toward the wall and half move away, so the number of collisions in time \(\Delta t\) is \(\frac{1}{2} n A v_x \Delta t\). Multiplying by the impulse \(2mv_x\) and dividing by \(A \Delta t\) gives the pressure contribution \(n m v_x^2\). Averaging over all molecules gives \(P = n m \overline{v_x^2}\).

Since the gas is isotropic (no preferred direction), \(\overline{v_x^2} = \overline{v_y^2} = \overline{v_z^2} = \frac{1}{3}\overline{v^2}\), where \(\overline{v^2} = \overline{v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2}\). This yields the fundamental kinetic pressure relation:

Kinetic Pressure Equation \[ P = \frac{1}{3} n m \overline{v^2} = \frac{1}{3} \rho v_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 \]

2.2 Kinetic Interpretation of Temperature & Molecular Speeds

Multiplying both sides by volume \(V\) (with \(N = nV\)) gives:

\[ PV = \frac{2}{3} N \left(\frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2}\right) = \frac{2}{3} E_{\mathrm{trans}} \]

Comparing this with \(PV = N k_{\mathrm{B}} T\) reveals the microscopic meaning of absolute temperature:

Average Translational Kinetic Energy & rms Speed \[ \langle \varepsilon_{\mathrm{trans}} \rangle = \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \frac{3}{2} k_{\mathrm{B}} T \] \[ v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\overline{v^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 k_{\mathrm{B}} T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 R T}{M_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 P}{\rho}} \]

Key NEET Takeaways:

  • The average translational kinetic energy per molecule depends only on absolute temperature \(T\)—it is completely independent of pressure, volume, or the chemical identity of the ideal gas.
  • At the same temperature, lighter molecules move faster: \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{M_0}}\).
  • The mean of the squares \(\overline{v^2}\) is strictly greater than or equal to the square of the mean \((\bar{v})^2\) whenever speeds vary. Thus \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} > \bar{v}\) for any gas in thermal equilibrium.

3. Degrees of Freedom, Equipartition & Specific Heats

3.1 Degrees of Freedom and the Law of Equipartition

The number of degrees of freedom (\(f\)) of a molecule is the number of independent coordinates (or independent quadratic energy terms) needed to specify its motion and configuration:

  • Translational motion: Every molecule moving in 3D space has \(3\) translational degrees of freedom (\(\frac{1}{2}mv_x^2\), \(\frac{1}{2}mv_y^2\), \(\frac{1}{2}mv_z^2\)).
  • Rotational motion: A monatomic gas atom is treated as a point mass with negligible moment of inertia, so it has \(0\) rotational degrees of freedom. A linear (diatomic) molecule can rotate about two axes perpendicular to the bond axis (\(2\) rotational degrees of freedom; rotation along the bond axis has negligible moment of inertia). A non-linear polyatomic molecule rotates about all \(3\) mutually perpendicular axes (\(3\) rotational degrees of freedom).
  • Vibrational motion: At high temperatures (or in flexible molecules), atoms oscillate along the bond. Each vibrational mode has two quadratic energy terms—kinetic energy \(\frac{1}{2}m\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^2\) and potential energy \(\frac{1}{2}ky^2\)—so each active vibrational mode contributes \(2\) quadratic degrees of freedom.
Law of Equipartition of Energy

In thermal equilibrium at absolute temperature \(T\), the total energy of a system is distributed equally among all quadratic energy modes, with each quadratic degree of freedom carrying an average energy of \(\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\).

\[ \langle \varepsilon_{\text{per quadratic term}} \rangle = \frac{1}{2} k_{\mathrm{B}} T \qquad \text{and} \qquad \langle \varepsilon_{\text{vibrational mode}} \rangle = 2 \times \frac{1}{2} k_{\mathrm{B}} T = k_{\mathrm{B}} T \]

3.2 Molar Specific Heats of Gases and Solids

For \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ideal gas with \(f_{\mathrm{eff}}\) effective quadratic degrees of freedom (counting each active vibrational mode as \(2\)), the internal energy is \(U = \frac{f_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2} R T\). Therefore:

\[ C_v = \frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{f_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2} R, \qquad C_p = C_v + R = \left(\frac{f_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2} + 1\right) R, \qquad \gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v} = 1 + \frac{2}{f_{\mathrm{eff}}} \]

Note that Mayer's relation \(C_p - C_v = R\) holds for every ideal gas regardless of atomicity.

Gas / System Type Degrees of Freedom \(U\) (per mol) \(C_v\) \(C_p\) \(\gamma = C_p / C_v\)
Monatomic (e.g., He, Ne, Ar) \(3\) trans \(\frac{3}{2}RT\) \(\frac{3}{2}R\) \(\frac{5}{2}R\) \(\frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67\)
Rigid Diatomic (e.g., \(\mathrm{N}_2, \mathrm{O}_2\) at moderate \(T\)) \(3\) trans \(+\) \(2\) rot \(\frac{5}{2}RT\) \(\frac{5}{2}R\) \(\frac{7}{2}R\) \(\frac{7}{5} = 1.40\)
Non-rigid Diatomic (with \(1\) vib mode) \(3\) trans \(+\) \(2\) rot \(+\) \(2\) vib \(\frac{7}{2}RT\) \(\frac{7}{2}R\) \(\frac{9}{2}R\) \(\frac{9}{7} \approx 1.29\)
Non-linear Polyatomic (with \(v\) vib modes) \(3\) trans \(+\) \(3\) rot \(+\) \(2v\) vib \((3 + v)RT\) \((3 + v)R\) \((4 + v)R\) \(\frac{4 + v}{3 + v}\)
Crystalline Solid (3D lattice oscillators) \(3\) vib modes (\(6\) quadratic terms) \(3RT\) \(3R\) \(\approx 3R\) \(\approx 1\)

Specific Heat of Solids: Each atom in a solid oscillates about its equilibrium site in three dimensions, giving \(3\) vibrational modes (\(6\) quadratic terms). Thus, \(U = 3 N_{\mathrm{A}} k_{\mathrm{B}} T = 3RT\) per mole. Since volume expansion \(\Delta V\) is negligible for solids, \(C_p \approx C_v = C = 3R \approx 24.9\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\). Most metals (e.g., Al, Cu, Pb, Ag, W) follow this rule closely at room temperature, though light, stiff solids like carbon (diamond) have lower molar specific heat at room temperature because quantum effects freeze out high-frequency vibrations.

4. Molecular Collisions & Mean Free Path

Although gas molecules travel at hundreds of metres per second, a scent or gas leak spreads slowly across a room because molecules undergo billions of collisions per second, following zig-zag paths.

Model molecules as hard spheres of diameter \(d\). A moving molecule travelling with average speed \(\bar{v}\) will collide with any other molecule whose centre lies within a distance \(d\) of its path. In time \(\Delta t\), it sweeps an effective collision cylinder of cross-sectional area \(\pi d^2\) and length \(\bar{v}\Delta t\).

Accounting for the relative motion of all moving molecules introduces a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\) in the collision rate. This gives the standard expressions for mean free path (\(\lambda\)), mean collision time (\(\tau\)), and collision frequency (\(\nu\)):

Mean Free Path & Collision Frequency \[ \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\, n \pi d^2} = \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}} T}{\sqrt{2}\, \pi d^2 P} \] \[ \tau = \frac{\lambda}{\bar{v}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\, n \pi d^2 \bar{v}}, \qquad \nu = \frac{1}{\tau} = \sqrt{2}\, n \pi d^2 \bar{v} \]

Notice the key proportionalities frequently tested in NEET UG: \(\lambda \propto \frac{1}{n}\), \(\lambda \propto \frac{1}{d^2}\), \(\lambda \propto \frac{T}{P}\). At constant volume (so \(n\) is fixed), changing temperature does not change \(\lambda\), whereas at constant pressure, \(\lambda \propto T\).

5. Worked Examples (NEET UG Standard)

Note: Numerical values in these original examples are chosen for clear conceptual practice.

Worked Example 1: Gas Mixture, Partial Pressures, and Density Ratio

A rigid container holds a non-reacting mixture of Helium (\(\mathrm{He}\), molar mass \(4\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) and Nitrogen (\(\mathrm{N_2}\), molar mass \(28\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) in thermal equilibrium. If the ratio of their partial pressures is \(P_{\mathrm{He}} : P_{\mathrm{N_2}} = 4 : 3\), find: (a) the ratio of the number of molecules \(N_{\mathrm{He}} : N_{\mathrm{N_2}}\), and (b) the ratio of their mass densities \(\rho_{\mathrm{He}} : \rho_{\mathrm{N_2}}\).

Solution:

(a) Both gases share the same volume \(V\) and temperature \(T\). From \(PV = N k_{\mathrm{B}} T\), partial pressure is directly proportional to the number of molecules: \[ \frac{N_{\mathrm{He}}}{N_{\mathrm{N_2}}} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{He}}}{P_{\mathrm{N_2}}} = \frac{4}{3} \]

(b) Using \(P = \frac{\rho R T}{M_0}\), we have \(\rho = \frac{P M_0}{RT}\). Since \(T\) is common to both gases: \[ \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{He}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{N_2}}} = \left(\frac{P_{\mathrm{He}}}{P_{\mathrm{N_2}}}\right)\left(\frac{M_{\mathrm{He}}}{M_{\mathrm{N_2}}}\right) = \frac{4}{3} \times \frac{4}{28} = \frac{4}{21} \]

Worked Example 2: Comparing rms Speeds Across Temperatures

At what temperature (in °C) will the root-mean-square speed of methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(M_0 = 16\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) molecules be equal to the rms speed of oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(M_0 = 32\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) molecules at \(127\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)?

Solution:

Convert the oxygen temperature to kelvin: \(T_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 127 + 273 = 400\,\mathrm{K}\).

Using \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M_0}}\), equating the two speeds gives: \[ \frac{T_{\mathrm{CH_4}}}{M_{\mathrm{CH_4}}} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{O_2}}}{M_{\mathrm{O_2}}} \] \[ T_{\mathrm{CH_4}} = 400\,\mathrm{K} \times \frac{16}{32} = 200\,\mathrm{K} \]

Converting back to Celsius: \(t = 200 - 273 = -73\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Worked Example 3: Specific Heat Ratio of an Ideal Gas Mixture

Assume \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a monatomic ideal gas (\(\gamma_1 = 5/3\)) is mixed with \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of a rigid diatomic ideal gas (\(\gamma_2 = 7/5\)). Calculate the effective adiabatic exponent \(\gamma_{\mathrm{mix}}\) of the mixture.

Solution:

The molar internal energy gives \(C_{v1} = \frac{3}{2}R\) and \(C_{v2} = \frac{5}{2}R\). The effective \(C_{v,\mathrm{mix}}\) is the mole-weighted average: \[ C_{v,\mathrm{mix}} = \frac{\mu_1 C_{v1} + \mu_2 C_{v2}}{\mu_1 + \mu_2} = \frac{2\left(\frac{3}{2}R\right) + 1\left(\frac{5}{2}R\right)}{2 + 1} = \frac{3R + 2.5R}{3} = \frac{11}{6}R \] By Mayer's relation, \(C_{p,\mathrm{mix}} = C_{v,\mathrm{mix}} + R = \frac{17}{6}R\). Therefore: \[ \gamma_{\mathrm{mix}} = \frac{C_{p,\mathrm{mix}}}{C_{v,\mathrm{mix}}} = \frac{17/6}{11/6} = \frac{17}{11} \approx 1.55 \]

Worked Example 4: Mean Free Path Scaling

A gas sample has a mean free path of \(1.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}\) at pressure \(1.0\,\mathrm{atm}\) and temperature \(300\,\mathrm{K}\). If the gas is compressed isothermally to \(3.0\,\mathrm{atm}\) and then heated at constant pressure to \(450\,\mathrm{K}\), what is the new mean free path?

Solution:

Since \(\lambda = \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\sqrt{2}\pi d^2 P}\), we have \(\lambda \propto \frac{T}{P}\) for a given gas (fixed \(d\)).

\[ \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} = \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)\left(\frac{P_1}{P_2}\right) = \left(\frac{450}{300}\right)\left(\frac{1.0}{3.0}\right) = 1.5 \times \frac{1}{3} = 0.5 \]

Hence, \(\lambda_2 = 0.5 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m}\).

6. NEET UG Practice Questions

All items below are original practice questions aligned to the NEET UG syllabus and difficulty level.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two closed vessels of equal volume contain \(\mathrm{H_2}\) gas and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) gas at the same temperature \(T\) and pressure \(P\). Which of the following statements is correct?

  1. (A) Both vessels contain the same total mass of gas.
  2. (B) The average translational kinetic energy per molecule is greater for \(\mathrm{H_2}\) than for \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  3. (C) The rms speed of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) molecules is \(4\) times the rms speed of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) molecules.
  4. (D) The number of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) molecules is \(16\) times the number of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) molecules.
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Since \(P, V, T\) are identical, \(N = \frac{PV}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\) is the same in both vessels (ruling out D), and since \(M_{\mathrm{O_2}} \neq M_{\mathrm{H_2}}\), the masses differ (ruling out A). Average translational KE per molecule is \(\frac{3}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\), which is equal for both gases (ruling out B). For rms speed, \(\frac{v_{\mathrm{H_2}}}{v_{\mathrm{O_2}}} = \sqrt{\frac{M_{\mathrm{O_2}}}{M_{\mathrm{H_2}}}} = \sqrt{\frac{32}{2}} = 4\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The pressure of an ideal gas is doubled and its volume is reduced to one-fourth of its initial value. What happens to the internal energy of a fixed mass of this ideal gas?

  1. (A) It doubles.
  2. (B) It becomes half of its initial value.
  3. (C) It remains unchanged.
  4. (D) It becomes one-fourth of its initial value.
Answer: (B)

Explanation: For an ideal gas, internal energy \(U \propto T\), and by the ideal gas law \(PV = \mu R T\), we have \(T \propto PV\). Here, \((PV)_{\text{final}} = (2P)\left(\frac{V}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{2}PV\). Thus \(T\) becomes half, and internal energy \(U\) becomes half of its initial value.

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

If the absolute temperature of an ideal gas is increased from \(27\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(927\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), the root-mean-square speed of its molecules will:

  1. (A) Increase by a factor of \(2\)
  2. (B) Increase by a factor of \(4\)
  3. (C) Increase by a factor of \(\sqrt{34.3}\)
  4. (D) Remain unchanged
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Always convert to kelvin: \(T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(T_2 = 927 + 273 = 1200\,\mathrm{K}\). Since \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} \propto \sqrt{T}\), we get \(\frac{v_2}{v_1} = \sqrt{\frac{1200}{300}} = \sqrt{4} = 2\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A diatomic ideal gas molecule has \(3\) translational degrees of freedom, \(2\) rotational degrees of freedom, and \(1\) active vibrational mode. What is the ratio \(\gamma = C_p / C_v\) for this gas?

  1. (A) \(\frac{7}{5}\)
  2. (B) \(\frac{5}{3}\)
  3. (C) \(\frac{9}{7}\)
  4. (D) \(\frac{11}{9}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: One vibrational mode contributes \(2\) quadratic degrees of freedom (kinetic + potential). Total effective degrees of freedom \(f_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3 + 2 + 2 = 7\). Therefore, \(C_v = \frac{7}{2}R\), \(C_p = \frac{9}{2}R\), and \(\gamma = \frac{9}{7}\).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A gas mixture consists of \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of Argon (monatomic) and \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of Nitrogen (rigid diatomic) at room temperature. What is the total internal energy of the mixture?

  1. (A) \(\frac{15}{2}RT\)
  2. (B) \(\frac{19}{2}RT\)
  3. (C) \(\frac{21}{2}RT\)
  4. (D) \(11\,RT\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Total internal energy \(U = U_{\mathrm{Ar}} + U_{\mathrm{N_2}} = \mu_1 \left(\frac{3}{2}RT\right) + \mu_2 \left(\frac{5}{2}RT\right) = 3\left(\frac{3}{2}RT\right) + 2\left(\frac{5}{2}RT\right) = \frac{9}{2}RT + \frac{10}{2}RT = \frac{19}{2}RT\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The mean free path of molecules in an ideal gas is \(\lambda\) at number density \(n\) and molecular diameter \(d\). If the number density is halved and the molecular diameter is doubled, the new mean free path becomes:

  1. (A) \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\)
  2. (B) \(\frac{\lambda}{4}\)
  3. (C) \(2\lambda\)
  4. (D) \(\frac{\lambda}{8}\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: From \(\lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}n\pi d^2}\), we have \(\lambda \propto \frac{1}{n d^2}\). Replacing \(n \to n/2\) and \(d \to 2d\) gives \(\lambda_{\text{new}} = \frac{\lambda}{(1/2)(2)^2} = \frac{\lambda}{2}\).

Practice Question 7 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): A crystalline solid at room temperature typically has a molar specific heat close to \(3R\).

Reason (R): Each atom in a 3D crystal lattice has three vibrational modes, and each vibrational mode contributes \(k_{\mathrm{B}}T\) to the average energy per atom.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Each atom oscillates along \(x, y, z\) directions (\(3\) vibrational modes), each having kinetic and potential energy (\(\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T + \frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T = k_{\mathrm{B}}T\)). For \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\), \(U = 3 N_{\mathrm{A}} k_{\mathrm{B}} T = 3RT\), so \(C = \frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}T} = 3R\). Both statements are true and (R) directly explains (A).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

At a given temperature, the pressure of an ideal gas is \(P\) and its mass density is \(\rho\). If the gas is heated at constant volume until its pressure becomes \(4P\), the ratio of the new rms speed to the initial rms speed is:

  1. (A) \(1 : 1\)
  2. (B) \(2 : 1\)
  3. (C) \(4 : 1\)
  4. (D) \(1 : 2\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Since volume and mass are constant, density \(\rho\) remains constant. From \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3P}{\rho}}\), we get \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} \propto \sqrt{P}\) at constant \(\rho\). When \(P \to 4P\), \(v_{\mathrm{rms}}\) becomes \(\sqrt{4} = 2\) times its initial value, giving a ratio of \(2 : 1\).

7. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

Common Pitfalls to Avoid in NEET UG

  • Forgetting Kelvin Conversion: Never plug Celsius temperatures into \(PV = \mu RT\), \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} \propto \sqrt{T}\), or \(\lambda \propto T\). Always add \(273\) (or \(273.15\)).
  • Gauge Pressure vs. Absolute Pressure: Pressure gauges measure \(P_{\text{gauge}} = P_{\text{abs}} - P_{\text{atm}}\). Always use absolute pressure in gas law calculations.
  • Counting Vibrational Modes Wrongly: A single vibrational mode adds \(2\) quadratic degrees of freedom (kinetic + potential), contributing \(k_{\mathrm{B}}T\) per molecule, not \(\frac{1}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\).
  • Confusing \(\overline{v^2}\) with \((\bar{v})^2\): The mean of squared speeds is always greater than the square of the mean speed when molecules have a distribution of speeds.
