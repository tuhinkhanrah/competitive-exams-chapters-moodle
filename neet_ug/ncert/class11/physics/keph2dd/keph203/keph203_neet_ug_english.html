Thermal Properties of Matter

Thermometry, Thermal Expansion, Calorimetry, Phase Transitions, Heat Transfer, and Newton's Law of Cooling

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Roadmap

Thermal physics tests both conceptual clarity and rapid quantitative execution in NEET UG. After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Convert temperatures across Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin, and arbitrary linear thermometric scales, and relate gas thermometry to absolute zero.
  • Calculate linear, area, and volume thermal expansion in solids, evaluate thermal stress in constrained rods, and explain the anomalous expansion of water.
  • Apply the principle of calorimetry to mixtures involving phase changes (melting, freezing, boiling, condensation) without falling into equilibrium-temperature traps.
  • Analyze steady-state conduction in series and parallel composite slabs, distinguish convection and radiation, and apply Wien's displacement law, Stefan-Boltzmann law, and Newton's law of cooling.
High-Yield NEET Focus: Pay special attention to series/parallel thermal resistance, mixture calorimetry with latent heat, Wien's and Stefan-Boltzmann scaling ratios, and the average-temperature approximation in Newton's law of cooling.

2. Heat, Temperature, and Thermometry

Core Intuition: Temperature vs. Heat

Temperature is a macroscopic measure of the degree of hotness or coldness of a body; it determines the direction of spontaneous energy flow. Heat is thermal energy in transit between two systems (or a system and its surroundings) solely due to a temperature difference. Once thermal equilibrium is reached (equal temperatures), net heat transfer stops. The SI unit of heat is the joule (\(\mathrm{J}\)), and the SI unit of temperature is the kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\)).

Temperature Scales & Linear Calibration

Any standard thermometer relies on a measurable physical property (such as liquid volume, gas pressure, or electrical resistance) that varies monotonically with temperature. Using two reproducible fixed points—the normal ice point and steam point of pure water at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\)—any linear thermometric property \(X\) obeys:

General Linear Scale & Standard Conversions
\[ \frac{X_T - X_0}{X_{100} - X_0} = \frac{t_C - 0}{100} = \frac{t_F - 32}{180} = \frac{T - 273.15}{100} \]

Specifically, \(T(\mathrm{K}) = t_C(^\circ\mathrm{C}) + 273.15\), and \(t_F = \frac{9}{5}t_C + 32\). Note that a temperature interval of \(1\,\mathrm{K}\) equals \(1\,^\circ\mathrm{C} = 1.8\,^\circ\mathrm{F}\).

Ideal-Gas Equation & Absolute Zero

Unlike liquid-in-glass thermometers, which depend slightly on the liquid used, low-density gas thermometers give identical readings independent of the gas. Combining Boyle's law (\(PV = \text{constant}\) at constant \(T\)) and Charles' law (\(V/T = \text{constant}\) at constant \(P\)) yields the ideal-gas equation:

\[ PV = \mu R T \]

where \(\mu\) is the number of moles and \(R = 8.31\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) is the universal gas constant. At constant volume, \(P \propto T\). Extrapolating the straight-line \(P\)–\(t_C\) graph for any low-density gas to zero pressure converges at \(-273.15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), defining absolute zero (\(0\,\mathrm{K}\)).

Worked Example 1 (Thermometer Calibration)

Problem: A faulty Celsius thermometer (assumed to have uniform bore) reads \(2\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) when immersed in melting ice and \(97\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in steam at standard atmospheric pressure. What is the true Celsius temperature when this thermometer reads \(59\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)?

Solution: Using the linear proportion between the faulty reading \(R\) and the true Celsius temperature \(t_C\):

\[ \frac{R - R_{\text{ice}}}{R_{\text{steam}} - R_{\text{ice}}} = \frac{t_C - 0}{100 - 0} \] \[ \frac{59 - 2}{97 - 2} = \frac{t_C}{100} \implies \frac{57}{95} = \frac{t_C}{100} \implies \frac{3}{5} = \frac{t_C}{100} \]

Therefore, \(t_C = 0.6 \times 100 = 60\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

3. Thermal Expansion of Matter

Linear, Area, and Volume Expansion

Most materials expand on heating because the average separation between oscillating atoms increases with temperature. For small temperature changes \(\Delta T\), fractional dimensional changes are proportional to \(\Delta T\):

Expansion Relations (Isotropic Solids)
\[ \frac{\Delta l}{l} = \alpha_l \Delta T, \qquad \frac{\Delta A}{A} = \alpha_A \Delta T, \qquad \frac{\Delta V}{V} = \alpha_v \Delta T \]

For an isotropic solid, \(\alpha_A = 2\alpha_l\) and \(\alpha_v = 3\alpha_l\), so \(\alpha_l : \alpha_A : \alpha_v = 1 : 2 : 3\). Note that the unit of all expansion coefficients is \(\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) (or \(^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1}\)).

Cavity & Hole Rule: When a solid containing a hole or hollow cavity is heated uniformly, the hole expands by the exact same factor as a solid piece of the same material. Think of uniform thermal expansion as a photographic enlargement—every linear distance, including internal diameters, increases.

Thermal Stress in Constrained Rods

If a rod of length \(l\), cross-sectional area \(A\), and Young's modulus \(Y\) is rigidly clamped at both ends so it cannot expand or contract when its temperature changes by \(\Delta T\), a compressive or tensile thermal strain \(\frac{\Delta l}{l} = \alpha_l \Delta T\) develops. The resulting thermal stress and force are:

\[ \text{Thermal Stress} = Y \alpha_l \Delta T, \qquad F = Y A \alpha_l \Delta T \]

Notice that the thermal stress and force are independent of the rod's length \(l\).

Density Variation, Ideal Gases, and Anomalous Expansion of Water

Since mass \(m = \rho V\) remains constant during heating, density decreases as volume increases: \(\rho' = \frac{\rho}{1 + \alpha_v \Delta T} \approx \rho (1 - \alpha_v \Delta T)\), giving \(\frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho} \approx -\alpha_v \Delta T\).

For an ideal gas at constant pressure, \(P \Delta V = \mu R \Delta T\), so \(\frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{\Delta T}{T}\), which gives \(\alpha_v = \frac{1}{T}\) (temperature-dependent, unlike solids and liquids where \(\alpha_v\) is nearly constant over ordinary ranges).

Anomalous Expansion of Water: Between \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), water contracts on heating and expands on cooling. Consequently, water reaches its maximum density (\(1.000\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\)) at \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). In winter, surface water cools to \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and sinks; once surface water cools below \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), it becomes less dense, stays at the top, and freezes into ice at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). This insulates the \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) water below, allowing aquatic life to survive.

Worked Example 2 (Thermal Stress)

Problem: A steel wire of cross-sectional area \(2.0\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) is stretched taut between two rigid supports at \(30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) with negligible initial tension. If the temperature drops to \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), find the tension developed in the wire. (Assume \(\alpha_{\text{steel}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\) and \(Y_{\text{steel}} = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\).)

Solution: The temperature drop is \(\Delta T = 30 - 10 = 20\,\mathrm{K}\). Thermal strain prevented by the supports is:

\[ \frac{\Delta l}{l} = \alpha_{\text{steel}} \Delta T = (1.2 \times 10^{-5})(20) = 2.4 \times 10^{-4} \]

Converting area to SI units: \(A = 2.0\,\mathrm{mm^2} = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\). The tension is:

\[ F = Y_{\text{steel}} A \left(\frac{\Delta l}{l}\right) = (2.0 \times 10^{11})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})(2.4 \times 10^{-4}) = 96\,\mathrm{N} \]

4. Specific Heat Capacity, Calorimetry, and Phase Change

Heat Capacity & Specific Heat

  • Heat capacity (\(S\)): Heat required to raise the temperature of the entire body by \(1\,\mathrm{K}\): \(S = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T}\) (\(\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\)).
  • Specific heat capacity (\(s\)): Heat required per unit mass per unit temperature rise: \(s = \frac{1}{m}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T}\) (\(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)).
  • Molar specific heat capacity (\(C\)): Heat required per mole per unit temperature rise: \(C = \frac{1}{\mu}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta T}\) (\(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\)). For gases, we specify \(C_p\) (constant pressure) or \(C_v\) (constant volume).

Water has an exceptionally high specific heat capacity (\(4186\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \approx 1\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}\,^\circ C^{-1}}\)), which makes it an effective coolant in engines and moderates coastal climates.

Principle of Calorimetry

In an isolated system (such as a well-insulated calorimeter), total energy is conserved:

\[ \text{Heat Lost by Hotter Bodies} = \text{Heat Gained by Colder Bodies} \]

If a calorimeter has mass \(m_c\) and specific heat \(s_c\), its water equivalent is \(W = \frac{m_c s_c}{s_w}\), representing the mass of water that absorbs the same heat per degree rise as the calorimeter.

Phase Transitions, Latent Heat, and Triple Point

During a change of state (melting, freezing, vaporisation, condensation, or sublimation), temperature remains constant while heat is absorbed or released to alter intermolecular potential energy. This energy is latent heat:

\[ Q = m L \]

For water at \(1\,\mathrm{atm}\): latent heat of fusion \(L_f \approx 3.33 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) (\(80\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}\)) at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and latent heat of vaporisation \(L_v \approx 22.6 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) (\(540\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}\)) at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

  • Effect of Pressure on Melting Point (Regelation): Because ice has a larger volume than an equal mass of water, increasing pressure lowers the melting point of ice. Pressing a weighted wire onto ice melts it beneath the wire; once the wire passes, pressure drops and the water refreezes—a phenomenon called regelation.
  • Effect of Pressure on Boiling Point: Increasing pressure raises the boiling point (e.g., faster cooking in a pressure cooker). Lower atmospheric pressure at high altitudes lowers the boiling point of water, making open-pot cooking slower.
  • Triple Point: On a \(P\)–\(T\) phase diagram, the sublimation, fusion, and vaporisation curves meet at a unique point where solid, liquid, and vapour coexist in equilibrium. For water, the triple point is at \(273.16\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(0.01\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)) and \(0.006\,\mathrm{atm}\).
Worked Example 3 (Mixture Calorimetry Trap)

Problem: \(0.10\,\mathrm{kg}\) of ice at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is mixed with \(0.20\,\mathrm{kg}\) of water at \(30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in an insulated vessel of negligible heat capacity. Find the final temperature and composition of the mixture. (Assume \(s_w = 4200\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), \(L_f = 3.36 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\).)

Solution: First check whether the warm water can melt all the ice:

\[ Q_{\text{available}} = m_w s_w \Delta T = (0.20)(4200)(30 - 0) = 25{,}200\,\mathrm{J} \] \[ Q_{\text{to melt all ice}} = m_i L_f = (0.10)(3.36 \times 10^5) = 33{,}600\,\mathrm{J} \]

Since \(Q_{\text{available}} < Q_{\text{to melt all ice}}\), only part of the ice melts, and the final equilibrium temperature is \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The mass of ice melted is:

\[ m_{\text{melted}} = \frac{25{,}200}{3.36 \times 10^5} = 0.075\,\mathrm{kg} = 75\,\mathrm{g} \]

Final mixture at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\): \(25\,\mathrm{g}\) of ice and \(275\,\mathrm{g}\) of liquid water.

5. Modes of Heat Transfer & Newton's Law of Cooling

Conduction & Thermal Resistance

In conduction, thermal energy transfers between adjacent regions of a material via molecular collisions and free-electron motion without bulk flow of matter. In steady state across a uniform rod of length \(L\), cross-sectional area \(A\), and thermal conductivity \(K\), the heat current \(H = \frac{dQ}{dt}\) is:

Fourier's Law & Electrical Analogy
\[ H = \frac{dQ}{dt} = K A \frac{T_1 - T_2}{L} = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{R_{\text{th}}}, \qquad \text{where } R_{\text{th}} = \frac{L}{K A} \]

Just like electrical resistors: in series, \(R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2\) (same heat current \(H\) through both slabs); in parallel, \(\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\) (same temperature difference \(\Delta T\) across both slabs).

Convection

Convection transfers heat through actual bulk movement of a fluid. In natural convection, buoyancy drives warmer, less-dense fluid upward and cooler fluid downward (causing daytime sea breezes, nighttime land breezes, and trade winds). In forced convection, a pump or fan drives the flow (such as blood circulation or automobile cooling systems).

Thermal Radiation, Wien's Law, and Stefan-Boltzmann Law

Radiation transfers energy via electromagnetic waves at \(3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and requires no material medium. Every body above \(0\,\mathrm{K}\) emits a continuous spectrum of thermal radiation:

Radiation Laws (Use Absolute Temperature T in Kelvin!)
  • Wien's Displacement Law: \(\lambda_m T = b \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K}\), where \(\lambda_m\) is the wavelength of peak emission intensity. As \(T\) increases, \(\lambda_m\) shifts to shorter wavelengths (red-hot → yellow → white-hot).
  • Stefan-Boltzmann Law: Total radiant power emitted by a surface of area \(A\) and emissivity \(e\) (\(0 \le e \le 1\)) is \(H = e \sigma A T^4\), where \(\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}\).
  • Net Rate of Heat Loss: In surroundings at \(T_s\), \(H_{\text{net}} = e \sigma A (T^4 - T_s^4)\).

Newton's Law of Cooling

For small temperature differences between a body at \(T\) and its surroundings at \(T_s\), the rate of heat loss is proportional to the excess temperature \((T - T_s)\):

\[ -\frac{dQ}{dt} = k (T - T_s) \implies -\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{k}{ms}(T - T_s) = K(T - T_s) \]

Integrating gives exponential cooling: \(T(t) - T_s = (T_0 - T_s)e^{-Kt}\), so \(\ln(T - T_s)\) vs. \(t\) is a straight line with slope \(-K\). For NEET numericals over a finite interval from \(T_i\) to \(T_f\) in time \(t\), use the average-temperature approximation:

\[ \frac{T_i - T_f}{t} = K \left(\frac{T_i + T_f}{2} - T_s\right) \]
Worked Example 4 (Series Conduction)

Problem: Two slabs of equal cross-sectional area \(A\) and equal thickness \(d\) have thermal conductivities \(K_1 = 60\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) and \(K_2 = 120\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\). They are joined in series with the outer face of slab 1 at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and the outer face of slab 2 at \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Find the steady-state interface temperature \(T_0\) and the equivalent thermal conductivity \(K_{\text{eq}}\).

Solution: In steady state, \(H_1 = H_2\):

\[ \frac{K_1 A (100 - T_0)}{d} = \frac{K_2 A (T_0 - 10)}{d} \implies 60(100 - T_0) = 120(T_0 - 10) \] \[ 100 - T_0 = 2T_0 - 20 \implies 3T_0 = 120 \implies T_0 = 40\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \]

For two equal-thickness slabs in series, the equivalent thermal conductivity is the harmonic mean:

\[ K_{\text{eq}} = \frac{2 K_1 K_2}{K_1 + K_2} = \frac{2 \times 60 \times 120}{60 + 120} = 80\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \]

7. NEET UG Practice Questions

Attempt all ten original practice questions before checking the worked solutions below each item.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

At what temperature does the Fahrenheit scale read numerically twice the Celsius scale reading?

  • (A) \(160\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (B) \(320\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (C) \(80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) \(-40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)

Answer: (A)

Solution: Let \(t_C = x\), so \(t_F = 2x\). Using \(\frac{t_F - 32}{9} = \frac{t_C}{5}\):

\[ \frac{2x - 32}{9} = \frac{x}{5} \implies 10x - 160 = 9x \implies x = 160\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \]

(At \(160\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), the Fahrenheit thermometer reads \(320\,^\circ\mathrm{F}\)).

Practice Question 2 (Conceptual MCQ)

A uniform metallic circular disc has a concentric circular hole of diameter \(d\). When the disc is heated uniformly, what happens to the diameter \(d\) of the hole and the density \(\rho\) of the metal?

  • (A) \(d\) increases and \(\rho\) decreases
  • (B) \(d\) decreases and \(\rho\) decreases
  • (C) \(d\) increases and \(\rho\) increases
  • (D) \(d\) decreases and \(\rho\) increases

Answer: (A)

Solution: On uniform heating, every linear dimension of a solid—including internal holes or cavities—expands at the same fractional rate \(\alpha_l \Delta T\), so \(d\) increases. Because total mass is constant while volume increases, the density \(\rho = m/V\) decreases.

Practice Question 3 (Numerical MCQ)

A solid metal block has a coefficient of volume expansion \(\alpha_v = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\). If its temperature is raised by \(40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), what is the approximate percentage decrease in its density?

  • (A) \(1\%\)
  • (B) \(2\%\)
  • (C) \(4\%\)
  • (D) \(0.5\%\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: For small expansion, \(\frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho} \approx -\alpha_v \Delta T\):

\[ \left|\frac{\Delta \rho}{\rho}\right| \times 100\% = (5.0 \times 10^{-4})(40) \times 100\% = 0.02 \times 100\% = 2\% \]
Practice Question 4 (Conceptual MCQ)

Which of the following statements regarding the thermal behaviour of pure water at atmospheric pressure is correct?

  • (A) Water contracts on heating from \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
  • (B) The density of water is maximum at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
  • (C) As water cools from \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), its volume increases and density decreases.
  • (D) Lakes freeze from the bottom upward because ice is denser than water at \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Answer: (C)

Solution: Water has maximum density at \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Between \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(0\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\), cooling causes water to expand, so its volume increases and density decreases, keeping colder water and ice at the surface.

Practice Question 5 (Calorimetry & Phase Change)

\(10\,\mathrm{g}\) of steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is passed into \(90\,\mathrm{g}\) of water at \(20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in an insulated calorimeter of negligible heat capacity. What is the final equilibrium temperature of the mixture? (Take \(s_w = 1\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}\,^\circ C^{-1}}\), \(L_v = 540\,\mathrm{cal\,g^{-1}}\).)

  • (A) \(64\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (B) \(82\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (C) \(90\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: Heat needed to warm \(90\,\mathrm{g}\) of water from \(20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is \(90 \times 1 \times 80 = 7200\,\mathrm{cal}\). Heat released by condensing \(10\,\mathrm{g}\) of steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is \(10 \times 540 = 5400\,\mathrm{cal}\). Since \(5400 < 7200\), all steam condenses and then cools to final temperature \(T\):

\[ 5400 + 10(100 - T) = 90(T - 20) \implies 6400 - 10T = 90T - 1800 \implies 100T = 8200 \implies T = 82\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \]
Practice Question 6 (Graph Interpretation)

A solid sample of mass \(m\) is heated at constant power \(P\). In its temperature-versus-time graph, the solid-phase line has a steeper slope than the liquid-phase line, and the horizontal melting plateau is shorter than the horizontal boiling plateau. Which conclusion is correct?

  • (A) \(s_{\text{solid}} < s_{\text{liquid}}\) and \(L_f < L_v\)
  • (B) \(s_{\text{solid}} > s_{\text{liquid}}\) and \(L_f < L_v\)
  • (C) \(s_{\text{solid}} < s_{\text{liquid}}\) and \(L_f > L_v\)
  • (D) \(s_{\text{solid}} > s_{\text{liquid}}\) and \(L_f > L_v\)

Answer: (A)

Solution: Since \(\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{P}{ms}\), a steeper slope implies a smaller specific heat capacity (\(s_{\text{solid}} < s_{\text{liquid}}\)). Since plateau duration \(\Delta t = \frac{mL}{P}\), a shorter plateau implies a smaller latent heat (\(L_f < L_v\)).

Practice Question 7 (Thermal Conduction)

Two rods of identical dimensions have thermal conductivities \(K\) and \(3K\). What is the ratio of their equivalent thermal conductivity when connected in parallel to that when connected in series?

  • (A) \(4 : 3\)
  • (B) \(3 : 4\)
  • (C) \(2 : 3\)
  • (D) \(16 : 9\)

Answer: (A)

Solution: For identical dimensions, in parallel \(K_p = \frac{K + 3K}{2} = 2K\), and in series \(K_s = \frac{2(K)(3K)}{K + 3K} = \frac{3}{2}K\). Thus:

\[ \frac{K_p}{K_s} = \frac{2K}{(3/2)K} = \frac{4}{3} \]
Practice Question 8 (Blackbody Radiation)

A blackbody at \(1500\,\mathrm{K}\) emits maximum intensity at wavelength \(\lambda_1\). If its temperature is raised to \(3000\,\mathrm{K}\), the peak wavelength becomes \(\lambda_2\) and the total radiated power increases by a factor of \(n\). Find \(\lambda_2 / \lambda_1\) and \(n\).

  • (A) \(1/2\) and \(8\)
  • (B) \(1/2\) and \(16\)
  • (C) \(2\) and \(16\)
  • (D) \(1/4\) and \(16\)

Answer: (B)

Solution: By Wien's law, \(\lambda_m T = \text{constant}\), so \(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} = \frac{1500}{3000} = \frac{1}{2}\). By the Stefan-Boltzmann law, \(H \propto T^4\), so \(n = \left(\frac{3000}{1500}\right)^4 = 2^4 = 16\).

Practice Question 9 (Newton's Law of Cooling)

A cup of hot coffee cools from \(75\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(65\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in \(4\,\mathrm{minutes}\) in a room maintained at \(25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). How long will it take to cool from \(55\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(45\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in the same room?

  • (A) \(4.0\,\mathrm{min}\)
  • (B) \(5.6\,\mathrm{min}\)
  • (C) \(7.2\,\mathrm{min}\)
  • (D) \(8.5\,\mathrm{min}\)

Answer: (C)

Solution: Using the average-temperature form of Newton's law of cooling:

\[ \frac{75 - 65}{4} = K\left(\frac{75 + 65}{2} - 25\right) \implies \frac{10}{4} = 45K \] \[ \frac{55 - 45}{t} = K\left(\frac{55 + 45}{2} - 25\right) \implies \frac{10}{t} = 25K \]

Dividing the two equations gives \(\frac{t}{4} = \frac{45}{25} = \frac{9}{5} \implies t = 7.2\,\mathrm{minutes}\).

Practice Question 10 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Given below are two statements:
Statement I: Steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) causes more severe burns than boiling water at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
Statement II: Steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) has a higher specific heat capacity than liquid water at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (C)

Solution: Statement I is correct because \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) of steam at \(100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) releases an extra \(22.6 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{J}\) of latent heat of vaporisation upon condensing on the skin. Statement II is incorrect because this extra heat comes from latent heat of vaporisation, not from specific heat capacity (in fact, liquid water has a higher specific heat capacity than steam).

8. Common Pitfalls & NEET Revision Checklist

Avoid These Four Classic Exam Mistakes:
  • Celsius vs. Kelvin in Radiation & Gas Laws: Always convert \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(\mathrm{K}\) before using \(PV = \mu RT\), \(\alpha_v = 1/T\), \(\lambda_m T = b\), or \(H = e\sigma A T^4\). Only temperature differences \(\Delta T\) are numerically identical in \(\mathrm{K}\) and \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
  • Blindly Assuming Final Phase in Calorimetry: Before writing a single equation for ice-water or steam-water mixtures, check whether the available sensible heat is enough to complete the phase change.
  • Hole Contraction Myth: Uniform heating expands cavities and holes outward; it never shrinks them.
  • Thermal Resistance Confusion: Remember \(R_{\text{th}} = L/(KA)\) is proportional to \(L\) and inversely proportional to \(K\) and \(A\)—never average conductivities arithmetically for series slabs!

9. Concise Final Recap

Temperature governs thermal equilibrium, while heat is energy transferred across a temperature difference. Low-density gases define the absolute Kelvin scale with zero at \(-273.15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Solids expand linearly (\(\alpha_v = 3\alpha_l\)) and develop length-independent thermal stress \(Y\alpha_l\Delta T\) when constrained, whereas water exhibits a density maximum at \(4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Calorimetry tracks both sensible heating (\(ms\Delta T\)) and isothermal latent heat (\(mL\)). Finally, heat flows via conduction (\(H = \Delta T / R_{\text{th}}\)), fluid convection, and electromagnetic radiation (\(H = e\sigma A T^4\), \(\lambda_m T = b\)), with small temperature excesses obeying Newton's exponential cooling law.
