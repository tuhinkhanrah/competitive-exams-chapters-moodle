Oscillations and Simple Harmonic Motion

A complete conceptual and problem-solving guide aligned to the NEET UG examination standard.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Overview

Oscillatory motion is one of the highest-yield topics in NEET UG Physics because its mathematical framework underpins mechanical vibrations, sound waves, alternating current circuits, and electromagnetic waves. Questions in the exam frequently test graph interpretation, phase relationships, spring combinations, energy partitioning, and effective gravity in pendulums.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between periodic motion, oscillatory motion, and simple harmonic motion (SHM).
  • Represent SHM kinematically using sinusoidal functions and the uniform circular motion (reference circle) model.
  • Relate displacement, velocity, and acceleration in terms of magnitude, direction, and phase difference.
  • Apply the linear restoring force law \(F = -kx\) to determine time periods of spring-block systems.
  • Analyze kinetic, potential, and total mechanical energy as functions of both time and position.
  • Derive and apply the time period of a simple pendulum under standard and accelerating conditions.

2. Periodic Motion, Oscillations, and SHM

In mechanics, motions are classified by whether they repeat over time and whether they possess a stable equilibrium point:

  • Periodic Motion: Any motion that repeats itself identically after a fixed, smallest interval of time called the time period \(T\). Examples include planetary orbits and uniform circular motion.
  • Oscillatory (Vibratory) Motion: A special type of periodic motion in which a body moves to and fro repeatedly about a fixed equilibrium (mean) position. At the mean position, the net external force on the body is zero; displacing the body slightly activates a restoring force pulling it back toward equilibrium. Low-frequency back-and-forth motion is commonly called oscillation, while high-frequency motion is called vibration.
  • Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM): The simplest mathematical form of oscillatory motion, occurring when the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement from the mean position and always points toward it.
Key Hierarchy Rule

Every simple harmonic motion is oscillatory, and every oscillatory motion is periodic. However, the reverse is not true: uniform circular motion is periodic but not oscillatory, and a ball bouncing elastically on the floor is oscillatory/periodic but not simple harmonic.

Period, Frequency, and Angular Frequency

The frequency \(\nu\) (or \(f\)) is the number of complete cycles executed per unit time, measured in hertz (\(1\,\mathrm{Hz} = 1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\)). The angular frequency \(\omega\) measures the rate of change of phase in radians per second:

\[ \nu = \frac{1}{T}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi\nu = \frac{2\pi}{T} \]

Mathematical Representation & Fourier Superposition

In general, "displacement" in oscillations can mean linear position \(x\), angular deflection \(\theta\), pressure variation in a sound wave, or alternating voltage across a capacitor. Any periodic function can be expressed as a superposition of sine and cosine functions of harmonic frequencies (Fourier's theorem).

A linear combination of a sine and a cosine of the same angular frequency \(\omega\) always reduces to a single SHM:

\[ x(t) = A\sin\omega t + B\cos\omega t = R\sin(\omega t + \phi) \] \[ \text{where } R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2} \quad \text{and} \quad \tan\phi = \frac{B}{A} \]

In contrast, a sum such as \(\sin\omega t + \cos 2\omega t + \sin 4\omega t\) is periodic (with period equal to the least common multiple of the individual periods, here \(2\pi/\omega\)), but it is not simple harmonic because it contains multiple frequencies. Functions that grow or decay monotonically, like \(e^{-\omega t}\) or \(\log(\omega t)\), are non-periodic.

3. Kinematics of SHM & The Reference Circle

A particle oscillating along the \(x\)-axis between \(-A\) and \(+A\) executes SHM if its displacement \(x(t)\) from the origin varies sinusoidally with time:

\[ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) \]
  • Amplitude (\(A\)): The maximum magnitude of displacement from the mean position (taken as positive).
  • Phase (\(\omega t + \phi\)): The time-varying argument of the cosine function that uniquely specifies both the position and direction of motion at instant \(t\).
  • Phase Constant (\(\phi\)): The initial phase at \(t = 0\), determined by the initial position \(x(0)\) and initial velocity \(v(0)\).

Connection to Uniform Circular Motion

Imagine a reference particle \(P\) moving anticlockwise at constant angular speed \(\omega\) along a circle of radius \(A\) (the reference circle). If the radius vector \(OP\) starts at angle \(\phi\) with the positive \(x\)-axis at \(t = 0\), then at time \(t\) it makes an angle \((\omega t + \phi)\). The perpendicular projection \(P'\) of \(P\) onto the \(x\)-diameter has coordinate \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\), executing exact SHM. Similarly, its projection on the \(y\)-diameter executes SHM given by \(y(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)\), which differs in phase by \(\pi/2\).

Velocity and Acceleration in SHM

Differentiating \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with respect to time (or projecting the tangential velocity \(\omega A\) and centripetal acceleration \(\omega^2 A\) of the reference particle onto the \(x\)-axis) gives:

\[ v(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\omega A\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \pm\,\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2} \] \[ a(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\omega^2 A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 x(t) \]

Notice the critical phase relationships and extreme values:

  • Mean Position (\(x = 0\)): Speed is maximum (\(v_{\max} = \omega A\)) and acceleration is zero.
  • Extreme Positions (\(x = \pm A\)): Velocity is instantaneously zero and acceleration magnitude is maximum (\(a_{\max} = \omega^2 A\)), directed back toward the origin.
  • Phase Differences: Velocity leads displacement by \(\pi/2\) radians (\(90^\circ\)), and acceleration leads displacement by \(\pi\) radians (\(180^\circ\)), meaning acceleration is always opposite in sign to displacement.

4. Force Law and Spring-Mass Oscillators

Applying Newton's second law \(F = ma\) to the acceleration relation \(a = -\omega^2 x\) yields the defining force law for a linear harmonic oscillator:

\[ F(x) = -kx, \qquad \text{where } k = m\omega^2 \] \[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \]

Here \(k\) is the force constant (or spring constant, in \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\)). Notice that the time period \(T\) depends only on inertia (\(m\)) and stiffness (\(k\)); it is completely independent of amplitude \(A\), total energy, or phase constant \(\phi\).

Common Spring Configurations for NEET UG

  • Parallel Combination (or block between two walls): When a mass \(m\) is connected to two springs \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) such that a displacement \(x\) elongates one by \(x\) and compresses (or elongates) the other by \(x\), both forces act in the same restoring direction: \(k_{\text{eff}} = k_1 + k_2\), giving \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k_1 + k_2}}\).
  • Series Combination: For two springs connected end-to-end, \(\frac{1}{k_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2}\), giving \(k_{\text{eff}} = \frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\).
  • Two Masses on a Single Spring: If two equal masses \(m\) are attached to either end of a spring of constant \(k\) on a smooth horizontal surface, the center of mass stays fixed at the midpoint. Each half-spring has stiffness \(2k\), so the oscillation period is \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{2k}}\) (equivalently using reduced mass \(\mu = \frac{m}{2}\) in \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{k}}\)).

5. Energy Conservation in SHM

Since the linear restoring force \(F = -kx\) is conservative, the total mechanical energy \(E\) of an undamped harmonic oscillator remains constant at all times, continuously transforming between kinetic energy \(K\) and elastic potential energy \(U\):

\[ K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}k(A^2 - x^2) = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi) \] \[ U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\cos^2(\omega t + \phi) \] \[ E = K + U = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 A^2 \]

Notice that because \(\sin^2(\omega t + \phi) = \frac{1}{2}[1 - \cos 2(\omega t + \phi)]\), both \(K\) and \(U\) oscillate with angular frequency \(2\omega\), meaning their time period is \(T/2\) (they peak twice during every full oscillation of the particle). At \(x = 0\), energy is 100% kinetic; at \(x = \pm A\), energy is 100% potential; and at \(x = \pm A/\sqrt{2}\), \(K = U = E/2\).

6. The Simple Pendulum & Physical Oscillators

An ideal simple pendulum consists of a point mass (bob) \(m\) suspended from a rigid support by a massless, inextensible string of length \(L\). When displaced by an angle \(\theta\) from the vertical, the gravitational force \(mg\) resolves into two components:

  • Radial component (\(mg\cos\theta\)): Balances with tension \(T\) to provide the centripetal acceleration along the circular arc; it produces zero torque about the pivot.
  • Tangential component (\(mg\sin\theta\)): Acts toward the mean position, producing a restoring torque \(\tau = -mgL\sin\theta\).

Using \(\tau = I\alpha\) with moment of inertia \(I = mL^2\), we get \(\alpha = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta\). For small angular displacements (up to about \(20^\circ\), where \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) in radians to within 2%), this becomes \(\alpha = -\left(\frac{g}{L}\right)\theta\), which is angular SHM with:

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \]
  • Seconds Pendulum: A pendulum with time period \(T = 2\,\mathrm{s}\) (1 second per half-swing). On Earth (\(g \approx 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)), its length is \(L = \frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} \approx 1\,\mathrm{m}\).
  • Accelerating Frames: If the pivot accelerates with acceleration \(\vec{a}_0\), replace \(g\) by the magnitude of effective gravity \(g_{\text{eff}} = |\vec{g} - \vec{a}_0|\). For example, in a car turning on a horizontal circle of radius \(R\) at speed \(v\), \(g_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{g^2 + (v^2/R)^2}\).

8. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Identifying SHM & Period from a Function

Problem: Suppose a particle's position (in assumed SI units) is given by \(x(t) = 3\sin(4\pi t) + 4\cos(4\pi t)\). Determine whether the motion is simple harmonic, and find its amplitude and time period.

Solution: Both terms share the same angular frequency \(\omega = 4\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). Writing the linear combination in single-sine form \(R\sin(\omega t + \phi)\):

\[ R = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\,\mathrm{m}, \qquad \tan\phi = \frac{4}{3} \]

Since \(x(t) = 5\sin(4\pi t + \phi)\), the motion is simple harmonic with amplitude \(A = 5\,\mathrm{m}\) and time period:

\[ T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{4\pi} = 0.5\,\mathrm{s} \]

Worked Example 2: Finding Amplitude and Phase from Initial Conditions

Problem: (Assumed values) A particle executes SHM along the \(x\)-axis according to \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with angular frequency \(\omega = 2\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). At \(t = 0\), its position is \(x(0) = 2\,\mathrm{cm}\) and its velocity is \(v(0) = -4\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\). Calculate the amplitude \(A\) and initial phase \(\phi\).

Solution: At \(t = 0\):

\[ x(0) = A\cos\phi = 2\,\mathrm{cm} \]

\[ v(0) = -\omega A\sin\phi = -2A\sin\phi = -4\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}} \implies A\sin\phi = 2\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Squaring and adding both equations gives:

\[ A^2(\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi) = 2^2 + (2\sqrt{3})^2 = 4 + 12 = 16 \implies A = 4\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Dividing the two relations gives \(\tan\phi = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{2} = \sqrt{3}\). Since both \(\cos\phi > 0\) and \(\sin\phi > 0\), \(\phi\) lies in the first quadrant, so \(\phi = \frac{\pi}{3}\,\mathrm{rad}\) (\(60^\circ\)).

Worked Example 3: Energy Partition in a Spring Oscillator

Problem: (Assumed values) A \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) block attached to a horizontal spring of stiffness \(k = 200\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) oscillates with amplitude \(A = 12\,\mathrm{cm}\) on a frictionless table. Find the kinetic energy and potential energy when the block is \(6\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the equilibrium position.

Solution: Convert distances to metres: \(A = 0.12\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(x = 0.06\,\mathrm{m} = A/2\).

Total mechanical energy:

\[ E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.12)^2 = 1.44\,\mathrm{J} \]

Potential energy at \(x = A/2\):

\[ U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}k\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{E}{4} = \frac{1.44}{4} = 0.36\,\mathrm{J} \]

Kinetic energy by conservation of energy:

\[ K = E - U = 1.44 - 0.36 = 1.08\,\mathrm{J} \]

Worked Example 4: Pendulum in an Accelerating Elevator

Problem: (Assumed values) A simple pendulum has a time period of \(2.0\,\mathrm{s}\) when an elevator is stationary (\(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)). What is its time period when the elevator accelerates upward at \(2.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)?

Solution: In an upward-accelerating frame, \(g_{\text{eff}} = g + a_0 = 10 + 2.5 = 12.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Since \(T \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{g_{\text{eff}}}}\):

\[ \frac{T'}{T} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{g_{\text{eff}}}} = \sqrt{\frac{10}{12.5}} = \sqrt{0.8} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}} \approx 0.894 \]

\[ T' = 2.0 \times 0.894 \approx 1.79\,\mathrm{s} \]

9. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All items below are original practice questions designed for NEET UG preparation.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following functions of time represents a periodic motion that is not simple harmonic?

  • (A) \(\sin\omega t - \cos\omega t\)
  • (B) \(\sin^2\omega t\)
  • (C) \(\cos\omega t + \cos 3\omega t\)
  • (D) \(e^{-\omega^2 t^2}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Option (A) equals \(\sqrt{2}\sin(\omega t - \pi/4)\), which is a single-frequency SHM. Option (B) equals \(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\cos 2\omega t\), which is SHM about \(x = 1/2\) with angular frequency \(2\omega\). Option (D) decays monotonically to zero and is non-periodic. Option (C) is a sum of two different frequencies (\(\omega\) and \(3\omega\)), so it repeats every \(2\pi/\omega\) (periodic) but is not a single-frequency SHM.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle executes SHM with amplitude \(A\) and angular frequency \(\omega\). At what displacement from the mean position is its kinetic energy equal to three times its potential energy?

  • (A) \(\pm\frac{A}{4}\)
  • (B) \(\pm\frac{A}{2}\)
  • (C) \(\pm\frac{A}{\sqrt{2}}\)
  • (D) \(\pm\frac{\sqrt{3}A}{2}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Given \(K = 3U\), and \(K + U = E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\). Substituting \(K = 3U\) gives \(4U = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\). Since \(U = \frac{1}{2}kx^2\), we have \(4\left(\frac{1}{2}kx^2\right) = \frac{1}{2}kA^2 \implies x^2 = \frac{A^2}{4} \implies x = \pm\frac{A}{2}\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle moves uniformly in a circle of radius \(4\,\mathrm{cm}\) in the clockwise direction with a time period of \(8\,\mathrm{s}\). At \(t = 0\), its radius vector points along the positive \(y\)-axis. The \(x\)-projection of its motion is given (in \(\mathrm{cm}\)) by:

  • (A) \(x(t) = 4\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right)\)
  • (B) \(x(t) = -4\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right)\)
  • (C) \(x(t) = 4\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right)\)
  • (D) \(x(t) = 4\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{4} + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Angular speed \(\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{8} = \frac{\pi}{4}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). At \(t = 0\), the particle is at angle \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) from the \(+x\)-axis. Moving clockwise, its angle at time \(t\) is \(\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \omega t\right)\). Thus, \(x(t) = A\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi t}{4}\right) = 4\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{4}\right)\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In simple harmonic motion, what is the phase difference between the velocity of the particle and its acceleration?

  • (A) \(0\,\mathrm{rad}\)
  • (B) \(\frac{\pi}{4}\,\mathrm{rad}\)
  • (C) \(\frac{\pi}{2}\,\mathrm{rad}\)
  • (D) \(\pi\,\mathrm{rad}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: For \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\), velocity is \(v = -\omega A\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \omega A\cos(\omega t + \phi + \pi/2)\) and acceleration is \(a = -\omega^2 A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \omega^2 A\cos(\omega t + \phi + \pi)\). Hence acceleration leads velocity by \(\pi - \pi/2 = \pi/2\,\mathrm{rad}\).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A block of mass \(m\) is held between two fixed rigid walls by two identical springs, each of spring constant \(k\), placed on opposite sides of the block on a smooth horizontal surface. What is the angular frequency of small horizontal oscillations of the block?

  • (A) \(\sqrt{\frac{k}{2m}}\)
  • (B) \(\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\)
  • (C) \(\sqrt{\frac{2k}{m}}\)
  • (D) \(2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Displacing the block by \(x\) to the right stretches the left spring by \(x\) (pulling left with force \(kx\)) and compresses the right spring by \(x\) (pushing left with force \(kx\)). Net restoring force is \(F = -2kx\), so \(k_{\text{eff}} = 2k\) and \(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{2k}{m}}\).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle executes SHM with time period \(T = 12\,\mathrm{s}\). What is the minimum time taken by the particle to move from the mean position \(x = 0\) to half its amplitude \(x = +A/2\)?

  • (A) \(1\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (B) \(1.5\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (C) \(2\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (D) \(3\,\mathrm{s}\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Starting from \(x = 0\) toward \(+A\), we can use \(x(t) = A\sin\omega t\). Setting \(A/2 = A\sin\omega t\) gives \(\sin\omega t = 1/2 \implies \omega t = \pi/6\). Since \(\omega = 2\pi/T\), we get \(\frac{2\pi}{T}t = \frac{\pi}{6} \implies t = \frac{T}{12} = \frac{12}{12} = 1\,\mathrm{s}\).

Practice Question 7 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): The time period of a simple pendulum oscillating with a small angular amplitude is independent of the mass of the bob.

Reason (R): The restoring torque on the bob is proportional to the mass of the bob, and the moment of inertia about the support is also proportional to the mass of the bob.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: From \(\tau = I\alpha\), we have \(-mgL\theta = (mL^2)\alpha\). The mass \(m\) cancels from both sides, yielding \(\alpha = -(g/L)\theta\), so \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\) is independent of \(m\).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A simple pendulum has a time period of \(4.0\,\mathrm{s}\) on Earth (\(g_E = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)). If taken to a planet where the acceleration due to gravity is \(g_P = 2.45\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), what will be its new time period?

  • (A) \(2.0\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (B) \(4.0\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (C) \(8.0\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (D) \(16.0\,\mathrm{s}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Since \(T \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\) for fixed length \(L\), we have \(\frac{T_P}{T_E} = \sqrt{\frac{g_E}{g_P}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.8}{2.45}} = \sqrt{4} = 2\). Therefore, \(T_P = 2 \times 4.0\,\mathrm{s} = 8.0\,\mathrm{s}\).

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A body oscillates with SHM according to \(x = 0.25\cos(8\pi t + \pi/6)\), where \(x\) is in metres and \(t\) is in seconds. What is the maximum speed of the body?

  • (A) \(0.25\pi\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(2\pi\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(4\pi\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(8\pi\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Comparing with \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\), we identify \(A = 0.25\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(\omega = 8\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\). Maximum speed is \(v_{\max} = \omega A = 8\pi \times 0.25 = 2\pi\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A solid cylindrical block of height \(h = 20\,\mathrm{cm}\) and density \(\rho = 0.6\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) floats vertically in a liquid of density \(\rho_l = 1.2\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\). If depressed slightly and released, it oscillates vertically. Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), what is the time period of oscillation? (Neglect damping.)

  • (A) \(\frac{\pi}{5}\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (B) \(\frac{2\pi}{5}\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (C) \(\frac{2\pi}{10}\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (D) \(\frac{\pi}{10}\,\mathrm{s}\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: When depressed by \(x\), the extra buoyant force is \(F = -A x \rho_l g\), so \(k_{\text{eff}} = A\rho_l g\). The mass of the cylinder is \(m = A h \rho\). Hence \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k_{\text{eff}}}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{h\rho}{\rho_l g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.20 \times 0.6}{1.2 \times 10}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.12}{12}} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.01} = 2\pi(0.1) = \frac{\pi}{5}\,\mathrm{s}\).

10. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent Exam Traps:
  • Degrees vs. Radians: In \(\alpha = -(g/L)\theta\), \(\theta\) must be in radians. If no unit is written inside a trigonometric argument like \(\cos(2\pi t + 1.5)\), it is always in radians.
  • Frequency of Energy: If a particle oscillates with frequency \(\nu\), its kinetic energy \(K\) and potential energy \(U\) oscillate with frequency \(2\nu\) (time period \(T/2\)).
  • Unequal Half-Quarter Times: A particle takes \(T/12\) to go from \(0\) to \(A/2\), but \(T/6\) (twice as long!) to go from \(A/2\) to \(A\) because it slows down near the extreme.
  • Sign of Restoring Force: Always verify the negative sign in \(a = -\omega^2 x\) and \(F = -kx\). An equation like \(a = +10x\) represents exponential runaway, not SHM.

11. Concise Final Recap

Simple harmonic motion is governed by the linear restoring law \(F = -kx\), producing sinusoidal displacement \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with angular frequency \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\) and period \(T = 2\pi/\omega\) independent of amplitude. Velocity leads displacement by \(\pi/2\), acceleration leads by \(\pi\), total mechanical energy \(E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2\) stays constant, and a simple pendulum approximates SHM for small angles with \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g_{\text{eff}}}\).
