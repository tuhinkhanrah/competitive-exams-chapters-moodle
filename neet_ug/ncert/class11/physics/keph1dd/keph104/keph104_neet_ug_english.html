Laws of Motion

Complete Conceptual Tutorial, Free-Body Analysis, Friction & Circular Dynamics, and Exam-Oriented Practice

1. Learning Objectives & Syllabus Context

Kinematics describes how objects move using position, velocity, and acceleration. Dynamics explains why motion changes. In NEET UG Physics, Laws of Motion is a high-yield foundational chapter: its principles govern pulley systems, inclined planes, friction, apparent weight in elevators, impulse-momentum collisions, and circular turns.

After mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between Aristotle's misconception and Galileo's law of inertia.
  • Apply Newton's three laws of motion to single particles and multi-body systems.
  • Use impulse and conservation of linear momentum in collision and recoil problems.
  • Construct accurate Free-Body Diagrams (FBDs) for equilibrium and accelerated motion.
  • Analyze static, kinetic, and rolling friction, including the angle of repose.
  • Solve circular motion problems on level and banked curved roads.

2. Inertia and Newton's First Law of Motion

2.1 Overcoming Everyday Misconceptions

Everyday experience can be misleading: a ball rolling on a floor eventually stops unless pushed. Aristotle reasoned from such observations that a continuous external force is necessary to sustain uniform motion. However, Galileo recognized that a rolling ball slows down only because an opposing force—friction—acts on it. If all resistive forces (friction, air drag) were eliminated, a body moving on a horizontal surface would continue indefinitely at constant velocity.

Galileo's thought experiments with inclined planes showed that objects accelerate down a slope, decelerate up a slope, and neither accelerate nor decelerate on a frictionless horizontal plane. Thus, the state of rest and the state of uniform straight-line motion are dynamically equivalent: both correspond to zero acceleration.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Galileo's idealized incline argument demonstrating the law of inertia.

2.2 Inertia and Newton's First Law

Inertia is the intrinsic property of a body by virtue of which it resists any change in its state of rest or of uniform motion along a straight line. Mass is the quantitative measure of translational inertia: a heavier object requires a larger force to produce the same change in velocity.

Newton's First Law: Every body continues in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled by a net external force to change that state. \[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{0} \iff \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} \]

Key Logical Distinction: If a book rests stationary on a table, we do not say "weight equals normal reaction, so it is at rest." Rather, because we observe the book to be unaccelerated (\(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\)), the First Law tells us that the net external force is zero, which implies that the self-adjusting normal force \(N\) balances the weight \(mg\).

3. Linear Momentum, Newton's Second Law & Impulse

3.1 Linear Momentum

The linear momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) of a particle of mass \(m\) moving with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) is defined as:

\[ \mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v} \] SI Unit: \(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{N\,s}\) | Dimensions: \([\mathrm{M L T^{-1}}]\) | Vector quantity

3.2 Newton's Second Law of Motion

The rate of change of linear momentum of a body is directly proportional to the net external force applied to it, and the change takes place in the direction in which the force acts. In SI units, the proportionality constant is chosen as \(1\):

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}}{\mathrm{d}t} \] For a body of constant mass \(m\): \[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t} = m\mathbf{a} \]

The SI unit of force is the newton (\(\mathrm{N}\)): \(1\,\mathrm{N} = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\). One newton is the force that gives a \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) mass an acceleration of \(1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

  • Vector Component Form: \(F_x = ma_x\), \(F_y = ma_y\), and \(F_z = ma_z\). A force perpendicular to velocity changes only the direction of velocity (as in uniform circular motion), not its speed.
  • Local and Instantaneous Relation: Acceleration at a given instant depends strictly on the net force acting at that exact instant, not on the past motion of the object. For example, a stone dropped from an accelerating train immediately loses its horizontal acceleration once it leaves the hand (neglecting air drag).
  • System Applicability: For a system of particles or a rigid body, \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}}\) represents the sum of external forces only; internal forces cancel in pairs.

3.3 Impulse and the Impulse–Momentum Theorem

When a large force acts for a very short duration (such as a bat hitting a ball or a hammer striking a nail), neither the instantaneous force nor the exact contact time is easy to measure, but their product—impulse (\(\mathbf{J}\))—equals the total change in momentum:

\[ \mathbf{J} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf{F}\,\mathrm{d}t = \mathbf{F}_{\text{avg}}\,\Delta t = \Delta \mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}_f - m\mathbf{v}_i \]

Graphically, impulse is the area under the Force–Time (\(F\)–\(t\)) curve. For a fixed change in momentum \(\Delta \mathbf{p}\), increasing the collision time \(\Delta t\) reduces the average impact force \(F_{\text{avg}}\)—which is why a fielder pulls their hands backward while catching a fast ball.

4. Newton's Third Law & Conservation of Momentum

4.1 Action–Reaction Pairs

Forces in nature always arise from mutual interactions between two bodies; a single isolated force never exists. Newton's Third Law states that if body A exerts a force \(\mathbf{F}_{BA}\) on body B, then body B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force \(\mathbf{F}_{AB}\) on body A:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{AB} = -\mathbf{F}_{BA} \]
  • Simultaneity: Action and reaction act at the exact same instant; neither is the cause of the other.
  • Different Bodies: Because \(\mathbf{F}_{AB}\) acts on A and \(\mathbf{F}_{BA}\) acts on B, they never cancel each other when analyzing the motion of a single body.
  • Same Nature: Gravitational action has a gravitational reaction; normal contact force has a normal contact reaction.

4.2 Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum

Consider an isolated system of interacting particles with no net external force (\(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}\)). Since internal forces occur in equal and opposite pairs (\(\mathbf{F}_{ij} + \mathbf{F}_{ji} = \mathbf{0}\)), their impulses cancel identically. By the Second Law, \(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}_{\text{total}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathbf{0}\), which yields:

\[ \mathbf{P}_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{p}_1 + \mathbf{p}_2 + \dots + \mathbf{p}_n = \text{constant} \]

For a two-body collision or explosion without external forces, \(\mathbf{p}_{A,i} + \mathbf{p}_{B,i} = \mathbf{p}_{A,f} + \mathbf{p}_{B,f}\). This holds for both elastic and inelastic collisions. In gun recoil, a stationary gun–bullet system has zero initial momentum, so \(m_b \mathbf{v}_b + m_g \mathbf{v}_g = \mathbf{0}\), giving recoil speed \(v_g = \frac{m_b v_b}{m_g}\) opposite to the bullet.

5. Equilibrium, Common Forces & Free-Body Diagrams

5.1 Translational Equilibrium

A particle is in translational equilibrium when the net external force on it is zero (\(\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\)), meaning \(\sum F_x = 0\), \(\sum F_y = 0\), and \(\sum F_z = 0\). For three concurrent coplanar forces \(\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \mathbf{F}_3\) in equilibrium, their vector arrows form a closed triangle, and Lami's Theorem applies:

\[ \frac{F_1}{\sin\alpha} = \frac{F_2}{\sin\beta} = \frac{F_3}{\sin\gamma} \] where \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) are the angles opposite to \(\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \mathbf{F}_3\) respectively.

5.2 Common Forces in Mechanics

  • Weight (\(mg\)): Non-contact gravitational pull of the Earth directed vertically downward.
  • Normal Reaction (\(N\)): Component of contact force perpendicular to the surfaces in contact, pushing away from the surface.
  • Tension (\(T\)): Pulling force transmitted along a taut string or rope. For a massless, inextensible string over a frictionless pulley, \(T\) is uniform throughout the string.
  • Spring Force (\(F_s = -kx\)): Restoring force proportional to deformation \(x\) from natural length, where \(k\) is the spring constant.

5.3 Step-by-Step Free-Body Diagram (FBD) Protocol

  1. Isolate the chosen body (or sub-system) from its surroundings in your sketch.
  2. Draw only the external forces acting on the chosen body (gravity, normal, tension, friction, applied forces). Never include forces exerted by the body on other objects, and never draw \(ma\) as a separate force.
  3. Choose convenient perpendicular axes (for an incline, align \(x\) along the slope and \(y\) perpendicular to it).
  4. Resolve angled forces into components and apply \(\sum F_x = ma_x\) and \(\sum F_y = ma_y\).

5.4 Apparent Weight in an Accelerating Elevator

For a person of mass \(m\) standing on a weighing scale in a lift, the scale reads the normal reaction \(N\) acting upward on the person:

  • At rest or constant velocity (\(a = 0\)): \(N = mg\).
  • Accelerating upward at \(a\): \(N - mg = ma \implies N = m(g + a)\) (feels heavier).
  • Accelerating downward at \(a\): \(mg - N = ma \implies N = m(g - a)\) (feels lighter).
  • Free fall (\(a = g\) downward): \(N = 0\) (apparent weightlessness).

6. Friction: Static, Kinetic, and Rolling

Friction is the component of contact force parallel to the surfaces in contact that opposes impending or actual relative motion between the two surfaces. At the microscopic level, contact forces arise from electromagnetic interactions between atoms at the interface.

  • Static Friction (\(f_s\)): Acts when there is no relative sliding. It is a self-adjusting force that matches the applied tangential force up to a maximum limiting value: \[ f_s \le f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N \] where \(\mu_s\) is the coefficient of static friction.
  • Kinetic (Sliding) Friction (\(f_k\)): Acts once relative sliding begins. It has a nearly constant magnitude independent of contact area and speed: \[ f_k = \mu_k N \] Experimentally, \(\mu_k < \mu_s\), so \(f_k < f_{s,\max}\).
  • Angle of Repose (\(\theta_{\text{repose}}\)): The maximum angle of an inclined plane at which a block placed on it just begins to slide: \[ mg\sin\theta = \mu_s mg\cos\theta \implies \tan\theta_{\text{repose}} = \mu_s \]
  • Rolling Friction: Occurs when a wheel or sphere rolls over a surface due to slight momentary deformation at the contact zone. For the same normal load, rolling friction is 2 to 3 orders of magnitude smaller than sliding friction (\(f_r \ll f_k < f_{s,\max}\)), which is why ball bearings and lubricants are used in machinery.

7. Dynamics of Circular Motion

A particle moving in a circle of radius \(R\) with speed \(v\) has a centripetal acceleration \(a_c = \frac{v^2}{R}\) directed toward the centre. By Newton's Second Law, a net inward radial force—the centripetal force—must act on it:

\[ F_c = \frac{mv^2}{R} = m\omega^2 R \]

Centripetal force is not a separate type of force; it is the role played by tension, gravity, normal reaction, friction, or their combination.

7.1 Vehicle on a Level Circular Road

On a flat road, \(N = mg\). The inward static friction \(f_s\) provides the centripetal force: \(\frac{mv^2}{R} = f_s \le \mu_s N = \mu_s mg\). Therefore, the maximum safe speed without skidding is:

\[ v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s R g} \]

7.2 Vehicle on a Banked Circular Road

Raising the outer edge of a curved road by an angle \(\theta\) allows the horizontal component of the normal reaction \(N\sin\theta\) to assist friction in providing centripetal force.

Tutorial Diagram 5: Forces acting on a vehicle negotiating a banked curve at high speed (friction acts down the slope).

Balancing vertical forces gives \(N\cos\theta = mg + f\sin\theta\), and horizontal centripetal force gives \(N\sin\theta + f\cos\theta = \frac{mv^2}{R}\). Setting \(f = \mu_s N\) yields the maximum permissible speed, and setting \(\mu_s = 0\) yields the optimum speed (zero tyre wear):

\[ v_0 = \sqrt{Rg\tan\theta} \qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{\max} = \sqrt{Rg\left(\frac{\mu_s + \tan\theta}{1 - \mu_s\tan\theta}\right)} \]

9. Worked Examples (NEET UG Standard)

Worked Example 1: Impulse in an Oblique Wall Impact

A rubber ball of mass \(0.20\,\mathrm{kg}\) (assumed) strikes a rigid vertical wall with a speed of \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at an angle of \(37^\circ\) to the normal and rebounds symmetrically with the same speed. If the contact lasts for \(0.02\,\mathrm{s}\), calculate (a) the magnitude of the impulse imparted to the ball and (b) the average force exerted by the ball on the wall. (Assume \(\cos 37^\circ = 0.8\), \(\sin 37^\circ = 0.6\).)

Solution: Let the \(x\)-axis be perpendicular to the wall (into the wall) and the \(y\)-axis be parallel to the wall.

Initial momentum components: \(p_{x,i} = mu\cos 37^\circ = 0.20 \times 10 \times 0.8 = 1.6\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\); \(\;p_{y,i} = -mu\sin 37^\circ\).

Final momentum components: \(p_{x,f} = -mu\cos 37^\circ = -1.6\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\); \(\;p_{y,f} = -mu\sin 37^\circ\).

(a) Since \(\Delta p_y = 0\), the impulse on the ball is purely along the normal: \[ J = |\Delta p_x| = |p_{x,f} - p_{x,i}| = |-1.6 - 1.6| = 3.2\,\mathrm{N\,s} \]

(b) By Newton's Third Law, the average force on the wall equals the magnitude of the average force on the ball: \[ F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{3.2}{0.02} = 160\,\mathrm{N} \text{ (directed into the wall)} \]

Worked Example 2: Connected Blocks with Kinetic Friction

A \(6\,\mathrm{kg}\) block rests on a rough horizontal table (\(\mu_k = 0.25\), assumed) and is attached by a light inextensible string over a smooth massless pulley to a hanging \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) block. Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string. (Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).)

Solution: Normal reaction on the \(6\,\mathrm{kg}\) block is \(N = m_1 g = 6 \times 10 = 60\,\mathrm{N}\).

Kinetic friction opposing motion is \(f_k = \mu_k N = 0.25 \times 60 = 15\,\mathrm{N}\).

Since the hanging weight \(m_2 g = 40\,\mathrm{N} > 15\,\mathrm{N}\), the system accelerates. Applying Newton's Second Law to each block:

For the \(6\,\mathrm{kg}\) block: \(T - 15 = 6a\).

For the \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) block: \(40 - T = 4a\).

Adding the two equations gives \(25 = 10a \implies a = 2.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

Substituting \(a\) back: \(T = 40 - 4(2.5) = 30\,\mathrm{N}\).

Worked Example 3: Self-Adjusting Static Friction on a Moving Flatbed

A \(15\,\mathrm{kg}\) crate (assumed) rests on the flat horizontal floor of a delivery van. The coefficient of static friction between the crate and the floor is \(\mu_s = 0.35\). (a) What is the maximum forward acceleration the van can have without the crate slipping? (b) What is the frictional force on the crate when the van accelerates at \(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)? (Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).)

Solution: Static friction provides the forward acceleration to the crate so it moves with the van.

(a) Maximum acceleration occurs when \(f_s = f_{s,\max} = \mu_s mg\): \[ a_{\max} = \frac{\mu_s mg}{m} = \mu_s g = 0.35 \times 10 = 3.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \]

(b) Since \(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} < 3.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), the crate does not slip. Static friction adjusts to provide only the required acceleration: \[ f_s = ma = 15 \times 2.0 = 30\,\mathrm{N} \text{ (forward)} \]

Worked Example 4: Banked Highway Curve

A circular highway curve of radius \(100\,\mathrm{m}\) (assumed) is banked at an angle \(\theta\) such that \(\tan\theta = 0.40\). If \(\mu_s = 0.25\) between tyres and road, find (a) the optimum speed for zero tyre wear and (b) the maximum safe speed. (Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).)

Solution:

(a) Optimum speed (where \(f = 0\)): \[ v_0 = \sqrt{Rg\tan\theta} = \sqrt{100 \times 10 \times 0.40} = \sqrt{400} = 20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

(b) Maximum safe speed: \[ v_{\max} = \sqrt{Rg\frac{\mu_s + \tan\theta}{1 - \mu_s\tan\theta}} = \sqrt{1000 \times \frac{0.25 + 0.40}{1 - (0.25)(0.40)}} = \sqrt{1000 \times \frac{0.65}{0.90}} \approx 26.9\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

10. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: These are original exam-style practice questions aligned with the NEET UG format. Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) unless stated otherwise.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A passenger sitting inside a train moving horizontally with a uniform acceleration of \(2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) drops a small coin of mass \(20\,\mathrm{g}\) from a window. Neglecting air resistance, what is the magnitude of the net force acting on the coin immediately after it is released?

  • (A) \(0.04\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(0.20\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(0.24\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(0\,\mathrm{N}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Newton's Second Law is a local, instantaneous relation. Once the coin leaves the passenger's hand, the train no longer exerts any horizontal force on it. Neglecting air resistance, only gravity acts on the coin: \(F_{\text{net}} = mg = 0.020\,\mathrm{kg} \times 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 0.20\,\mathrm{N}\) vertically downward.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle of mass \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) moves along the \(x\)-axis such that its position \(x\) (in \(\mathrm{m}\)) varies with time \(t\) (in \(\mathrm{s}\)) as \(x = 0\) for \(t < 0\), \(x = 3t\) for \(0 \le t \le 4\,\mathrm{s}\), and \(x = 12\,\mathrm{m}\) for \(t > 4\,\mathrm{s}\). What are the magnitudes of the impulse imparted to the particle at \(t = 0\) and at \(t = 4\,\mathrm{s}\), respectively?

  • (A) \(3\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) and \(3\,\mathrm{N\,s}\)
  • (B) \(12\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) and \(0\,\mathrm{N\,s}\)
  • (C) \(6\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) and \(6\,\mathrm{N\,s}\)
  • (D) \(0\,\mathrm{N\,s}\) and \(6\,\mathrm{N\,s}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Velocity \(v = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\) is \(0\) for \(t < 0\), \(3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for \(0 < t < 4\,\mathrm{s}\), and \(0\) for \(t > 4\,\mathrm{s}\). At \(t = 0\), \(|J_1| = m|v_f - v_i| = 2|3 - 0| = 6\,\mathrm{N\,s}\). At \(t = 4\,\mathrm{s}\), \(|J_2| = 2|0 - 3| = 6\,\mathrm{N\,s}\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A student of mass \(50\,\mathrm{kg}\) stands on a weighing scale inside an elevator. What is the ratio of the scale reading when the elevator accelerates upward at \(2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) to the reading when it accelerates downward at \(2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)?

  • (A) \(3 : 2\)
  • (B) \(2 : 3\)
  • (C) \(5 : 4\)
  • (D) \(1 : 1\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: Upward acceleration: \(N_1 = m(g + a) = 50(10 + 2) = 600\,\mathrm{N}\). Downward acceleration: \(N_2 = m(g - a) = 50(10 - 2) = 400\,\mathrm{N}\). Thus, \(\frac{N_1}{N_2} = \frac{600}{400} = \frac{3}{2}\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Three blocks of masses \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\), and \(5\,\mathrm{kg}\) are placed in contact on a smooth horizontal surface. A horizontal force of \(30\,\mathrm{N}\) is applied to the \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) block, pushing all three blocks together. What is the contact force between the \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(5\,\mathrm{kg}\) blocks?

  • (A) \(9\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(24\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(15\,\mathrm{N}\)
Correct Answer: (D)

Solution: Common acceleration of the three-block system is \(a = \frac{F}{m_1 + m_2 + m_3} = \frac{30}{2 + 3 + 5} = 3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The contact force \(N_{35}\) is the sole horizontal force accelerating the \(5\,\mathrm{kg}\) block: \(N_{35} = 5 \times 3 = 15\,\mathrm{N}\).

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A stationary shell of mass \(5\,\mathrm{kg}\) explodes into two fragments of masses \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\). If the \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) fragment flies off with a speed of \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), what is the speed of the \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\) fragment?

  • (A) \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(22.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Since no external horizontal force acts during the explosion, total linear momentum is conserved: \(0 = m_1 v_1 - m_2 v_2 \implies 3 v_2 = 2 \times 15 \implies v_2 = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) in the opposite direction.

Practice Question 6 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Static friction is a self-adjusting force.
Reason (R): For a body at rest on a rough horizontal surface, the magnitude of static friction always equals \(\mu_s N\), where \(\mu_s\) is the coefficient of static friction and \(N\) is the normal reaction.

  • (A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true but R is false.
  • (D) A is false but R is true.
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Assertion (A) is true because static friction adjusts its magnitude to match the applied tangential force up to a limit. Reason (R) is false because \(f_s \le \mu_s N\); it reaches \(\mu_s N\) only at the verge of sliding.

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A block of mass \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) is placed on a rough inclined plane making an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the horizontal. If the coefficient of static friction between the block and the plane is \(\mu_s = 0.6\), what is the frictional force acting on the block? (Take \(\sqrt{3} \approx 1.73\).)

  • (A) \(20\,\mathrm{N}\) up the plane
  • (B) \(20.8\,\mathrm{N}\) up the plane
  • (C) \(20\,\mathrm{N}\) down the plane
  • (D) \(12\,\mathrm{N}\) up the plane
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: The component of weight down the plane is \(mg\sin 30^\circ = 4 \times 10 \times 0.5 = 20\,\mathrm{N}\). The maximum static friction is \(f_{s,\max} = \mu_s mg\cos 30^\circ = 0.6 \times 40 \times \frac{1.73}{2} \approx 20.8\,\mathrm{N}\). Since \(mg\sin 30^\circ < f_{s,\max}\), the block remains at rest and static friction balances the downward pull: \(f_s = 20\,\mathrm{N}\) up the plane.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A motorcyclist rides on a flat circular track of radius \(45\,\mathrm{m}\). If the coefficient of static friction between the tyres and the track is \(0.5\), what is the maximum speed with which the motorcyclist can take the turn without skidding?

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(22.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (D)

Solution: On a level circular road, \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s R g} = \sqrt{0.5 \times 45 \times 10} = \sqrt{225} = 15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 9 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Action and reaction forces in Newton's third law act simultaneously on two different bodies and therefore do not cancel each other when analyzing the motion of a single body.
Statement II: For a book resting on a horizontal table, the downward weight of the book and the upward normal reaction exerted by the table on the book form an action–reaction pair.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because both weight (Earth pulling the book) and normal reaction (table pushing the book) act on the same body (the book) and are of different physical origins (gravitational vs. electromagnetic contact).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A stone of mass \(0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) tied to a light string of length \(2\,\mathrm{m}\) is whirled in a horizontal circle on a smooth frictionless table. If the maximum tension the string can withstand is \(100\,\mathrm{N}\), what is the maximum speed the stone can have before the string snaps?

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(40\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(14.1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Tension provides the centripetal force: \(T = \frac{mv^2}{L}\). Setting \(T = T_{\max} = 100\,\mathrm{N}\), we get \(100 = \frac{0.5\,v_{\max}^2}{2} \implies v_{\max}^2 = 400 \implies v_{\max} = 20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

11. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Blindly setting \(f_s = \mu_s N\): Always check whether the driving force actually exceeds \(f_{s,\max}\). If the body is at rest below the threshold, \(f_s\) equals the applied force, not \(\mu_s N\).
  • Equating \(N = mg\) everywhere: \(N = mg\) holds only on a horizontal surface with zero vertical acceleration and no other vertical forces. On an incline, \(N = mg\cos\theta\); in a lift, \(N = m(g \pm a)\).
  • Treating \(ma\) or centripetal force as an extra force on an FBD: \(ma\) is the result of the net force, not an additional physical agency.
  • Confusing equilibrium with action–reaction pairs: Equal and opposite forces on the same body are never an action–reaction pair.
  • [ ] Can I write component equations \(\sum F_x = ma_x\) and \(\sum F_y = ma_y\) from any FBD?
  • [ ] Do I remember that impulse equals area under the \(F\)–\(t\) curve and change in momentum?
  • [ ] Can I distinguish \(\mu_s\) (limiting static) from \(\mu_k\) (kinetic) and recall \(\mu_k < \mu_s\)?
  • [ ] Can I recall both \(v_0 = \sqrt{Rg\tan\theta}\) and \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{Rg\frac{\mu_s + \tan\theta}{1 - \mu_s\tan\theta}}\)?

12. Concise Final Recap

Newton's three laws form a unified framework: the First Law defines inertial frames and zero-acceleration equilibrium (\(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{0}\)); the Second Law quantifies how net external force alters momentum (\(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}}{\mathrm{d}t} = m\mathbf{a}\)); and the Third Law establishes that forces occur in simultaneous, equal, and opposite pairs on different bodies, leading directly to conservation of linear momentum in isolated systems. Mastering Free-Body Diagrams, self-adjusting static friction, and radial centripetal dynamics unlocks the vast majority of mechanics problems in NEET UG.
