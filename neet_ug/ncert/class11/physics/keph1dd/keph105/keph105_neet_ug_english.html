Work, Energy and Power

Comprehensive Concept Tutorial, Worked Illustrations, and NEET UG Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

In everyday language, the terms work, energy, and power are used loosely. A farmer ploughing a field, a construction worker carrying bricks, a student studying for a competitive examination, or an artist painting a landscape are all said to be working. Similarly, someone with the stamina to work 14–16 hours a day is said to have large energy, and fast punches in boxing or karate are described as powerful. In physics, however, these three quantities have exact, quantitative definitions that only loosely correlate with physiological effort.

Key Competencies for NEET UG
  • Evaluate the scalar (dot) product of two vectors in geometric and Cartesian component forms, including vector projections.
  • Apply the Work-Energy (WE) Theorem for both constant and spatially varying forces.
  • Distinguish between positive, negative, and zero work, and calculate work from the area under a force-displacement \(F(x)\text{–}x\) graph.
  • Formulate potential energy \(V(x)\) for conservative forces via \(F(x) = -\dfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}x}\) and apply conservation of mechanical energy to free fall, vertical circular motion, and ideal springs obeying Hooke's law.
  • Analyze average and instantaneous power \(P = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) and unit conversions (\(\mathrm{W}\), \(\mathrm{hp}\), \(\mathrm{kWh}\)).
  • Solve elastic, inelastic, and completely inelastic collisions in one and two dimensions using momentum and kinetic energy principles.

2. Mathematical Prerequisite: The Scalar Product

Physical quantities such as displacement, velocity, acceleration, and force are vectors. While vectors can be added and subtracted, they can also be multiplied in two distinct ways: the scalar product (which produces a scalar from two vectors) and the vector product (which produces a new vector). For work and power, we require the scalar product.

Definition: Scalar (Dot) Product

The scalar product or dot product of any two vectors \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\), denoted as \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}\) (read "A dot B"), is defined as:

\[ \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A B \cos\theta \]

where \(A = |\mathbf{A}|\) and \(B = |\mathbf{B}|\) are the magnitudes of the vectors and \(\theta\) is the angle between them when placed tail-to-tail. Since \(A\), \(B\), and \(\cos\theta\) are scalars, \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}\) is a scalar quantity possessing no direction.

Geometric Interpretation & Projections

We can rewrite the defining equation in two equivalent ways:

\[ \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A (B \cos\theta) = B (A \cos\theta) \]
  • Geometrically, \(B \cos\theta\) is the projection of \(\mathbf{B}\) onto \(\mathbf{A}\) (the component of \(\mathbf{B}\) along \(\mathbf{A}\)).
  • Similarly, \(A \cos\theta\) is the projection of \(\mathbf{A}\) onto \(\mathbf{B}\) (the component of \(\mathbf{A}\) along \(\mathbf{B}\)).
  • Thus, \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B}\) is the product of the magnitude of one vector and the component of the other vector along the direction of the first.

Algebraic Properties & Cartesian Component Form

  • Commutative Law: \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{A}\)
  • Distributive Law: \(\mathbf{A} \cdot (\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{C}\)
  • Scalar Multiplication: \(\mathbf{A} \cdot (\lambda \mathbf{B}) = \lambda (\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B})\), where \(\lambda\) is any real number.
  • Orthonormal Unit Vectors (\(\hat{\mathbf{i}}, \hat{\mathbf{j}}, \hat{\mathbf{k}}\)): \[ \hat{\mathbf{i}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{i}} = \hat{\mathbf{j}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{j}} = \hat{\mathbf{k}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{k}} = 1 \quad (\text{since } \cos 0^\circ = 1) \] \[ \hat{\mathbf{i}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{j}} = \hat{\mathbf{j}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{k}} = \hat{\mathbf{k}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{i}} = 0 \quad (\text{since } \cos 90^\circ = 0) \]
  • Component Form: For \(\mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + A_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + A_z\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) and \(\mathbf{B} = B_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + B_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + B_z\hat{\mathbf{k}}\), \[ \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z \]
  • Self Dot Product (Magnitude Squared): \[ \mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{A} = A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2 = A^2 \]
  • Condition for Orthogonality (Perpendicularity): \(\mathbf{A} \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\) if \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are mutually perpendicular (\(\theta = 90^\circ\) or \(\pi/2\,\mathrm{rad}\)).
Worked Illustration 1: Angle and Projection Using Scalar Product

Problem: Find the angle between force \(\mathbf{F} = (3\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 5\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\text{unit}\) and displacement \(\mathbf{d} = (5\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\text{unit}\). Also find the projection of \(\mathbf{F}\) on \(\mathbf{d}\).

Solution:

First, evaluate the dot product \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d}\) using Cartesian components:

\[ \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = F_x d_x + F_y d_y + F_z d_z = (3)(5) + (4)(4) + (-5)(3) = 15 + 16 - 15 = 16\,\text{unit} \]

Next, compute the magnitudes of \(\mathbf{F}\) and \(\mathbf{d}\):

\[ F^2 = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{F} = F_x^2 + F_y^2 + F_z^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 + (-5)^2 = 9 + 16 + 25 = 50\,\text{unit} \implies F = \sqrt{50}\,\text{unit} \] \[ d^2 = \mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{d} = d_x^2 + d_y^2 + d_z^2 = 5^2 + 4^2 + 3^2 = 25 + 16 + 9 = 50\,\text{unit} \implies d = \sqrt{50}\,\text{unit} \]

Using \(\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = F d \cos\theta\):

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{F d} = \frac{16}{\sqrt{50}\sqrt{50}} = \frac{16}{50} = 0.32 \implies \theta = \cos^{-1}(0.32) \]

The projection of \(\mathbf{F}\) on \(\mathbf{d}\) is given by \(F \cos\theta\):

\[ \text{Projection of }\mathbf{F}\text{ on }\mathbf{d} = F \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{d} = \frac{16}{\sqrt{50}} = \frac{16}{5\sqrt{2}}\,\text{unit} \approx 2.26\,\text{unit} \]

3. Notions of Work, Kinetic Energy, and the Work-Energy Theorem

Consider rectilinear motion under constant acceleration \(a\). From kinematics, the initial speed \(u\), final speed \(v\), and distance traversed \(s\) are related by:

\[ v^2 - u^2 = 2as \]

Multiplying both sides by \(m/2\) and invoking Newton's Second Law (\(F = ma\)):

\[ \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2 = mas = Fs \]

Generalising this relation to three dimensions using acceleration vector \(\mathbf{a}\) and displacement vector \(\mathbf{d}\), we have \(v^2 - u^2 = 2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{d}\). Multiplying by \(m/2\) yields:

\[ \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2 = m\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d} \]

This equation motivates two fundamental definitions:

  • The quantity \(\frac{1}{2}mv^2\) is called kinetic energy (\(K\)). Thus, the left-hand side represents \(K_f - K_i\), the change in kinetic energy.
  • The quantity \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{d}\) is called work (\(W\)) done by the force \(\mathbf{F}\) over displacement \(\mathbf{d}\).
The Work-Energy (WE) Theorem

Statement: The change in kinetic energy of a particle is equal to the work done on it by the net force acting on it:

\[ K_f - K_i = \Delta K = W_{\text{net}} \]
Worked Illustration 2: Raindrop Falling Against Air Resistance

Problem: A raindrop of mass \(1.00\,\mathrm{g}\) falls from a height of \(1.00\,\mathrm{km}\) under the influence of the downward gravitational force and an opposing resistive force (proportional to speed, but otherwise undetermined). It hits the ground with a speed of \(50.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find: (a) the work done by the gravitational force, and (b) the work done by the unknown resistive force.

Solution:

Given: \(m = 1.00\,\mathrm{g} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(h = 1.00\,\mathrm{km} = 10^3\,\mathrm{m}\), initial speed \(u = 0\), final speed \(v = 50.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

The change in kinetic energy of the drop is:

\[ \Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - 0 = \frac{1}{2} \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg} \times (50.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})^2 = 1.25\,\mathrm{J} \]

(a) Work done by the downward gravitational force over downward displacement \(h\):

\[ W_g = mgh = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg} \times 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \times 10^3\,\mathrm{m} = 10.0\,\mathrm{J} \]

(b) By the Work-Energy Theorem, the total work done by all forces equals \(\Delta K\):

\[ \Delta K = W_g + W_r \implies W_r = \Delta K - W_g = 1.25\,\mathrm{J} - 10.0\,\mathrm{J} = -8.75\,\mathrm{J} \]

The work done by the resistive force is negative (\(-8.75\,\mathrm{J}\)) because it opposes the downward motion. Notice how the WE theorem enables us to find the work of an unknown force without knowing its exact functional form!

Exact Definition of Work by a Constant Force

Consider a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) acting on an object of mass \(m\) at an angle \(\theta\) to the positive \(x\)-axis, causing a displacement \(\mathbf{d}\) along the positive \(x\)-direction.

Tutorial Diagram 2: An object undergoes a displacement d along the positive x-axis under the influence of a constant force F inclined at angle θ.
\[ W = (F \cos\theta)\, d = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} \]

Work done by a force is the product of the component of the force in the direction of displacement and the magnitude of the displacement.

When is Work Done Equal to Zero?

  1. Zero Displacement (\(d = 0\)): Pushing hard against a rigid brick wall or a weightlifter holding a \(150\,\mathrm{kg}\) mass steadily on his shoulders for \(30\,\mathrm{s}\) does zero physical work on the load, even though physiological muscles contract/relax and internal energy is consumed.
  2. Zero Force (\(F = 0\)): A block sliding with uniform velocity on a smooth (frictionless) horizontal table undergoes a large displacement, but no horizontal force acts on it, so work done is zero.
  3. Force and Displacement Mutually Perpendicular (\(\theta = 90^\circ = \pi/2\,\mathrm{rad}\)): Since \(\cos(\pi/2) = 0\), no work is done. Examples:
    • Gravity (\(mg\)) acting downward on a block sliding on a horizontal table (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)).
    • Earth's gravitational force on the Moon in a perfectly circular orbit (instantaneous displacement is tangential while centripetal gravitational force is radially inward, \(\theta = \pi/2\)).
    • Tension in the string of a simple pendulum (always normal to the instantaneous arc displacement).

Positive vs. Negative Work

  • Positive Work (\(0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ\)): \(\cos\theta > 0\). Force has a component along the displacement (e.g., gravity on a freely falling body).
  • Negative Work (\(90^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ\)): \(\cos\theta < 0\). Force opposes displacement (e.g., frictional force opposing sliding motion where \(\theta = 180^\circ\) and \(\cos 180^\circ = -1\)).

Dimensions and Units of Work and Energy

Both work and energy are scalars with dimensional formula \([\mathrm{ML^2T^{-2}}]\). The SI unit is the joule (\(\mathrm{J}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{J} = 1\,\mathrm{N\,m}\).

Worked Illustration 3: Newton's Third Law vs. Work Done (Skidding Cyclist)

Problem: A cyclist comes to a skidding stop in \(10\,\mathrm{m}\). During this process, the force on the cycle due to the road is \(200\,\mathrm{N}\) and is directly opposed to the motion. (a) How much work does the road do on the cycle? (b) How much work does the cycle do on the road?

Solution:

(a) The stopping (frictional) force exerted by the road on the cycle makes an angle \(\theta = 180^\circ\) (\(\pi\,\mathrm{rad}\)) with the displacement \(d = 10\,\mathrm{m}\). Thus, work done by the road on the cycle is:

\[ W_r = F d \cos\pi = 200\,\mathrm{N} \times 10\,\mathrm{m} \times (-1) = -2000\,\mathrm{J} \]

It is this negative work that removes the kinetic energy of the cycle and brings it to a halt.

(b) By Newton's Third Law, the cycle exerts an equal and opposite force of magnitude \(200\,\mathrm{N}\) on the road. However, the road is stationary and undergoes zero displacement (\(d = 0\)). Therefore, the work done by the cycle on the road is zero.

Crucial Takeaway: Although action-reaction forces between two bodies A and B always satisfy \(\mathbf{F}_{AB} + \mathbf{F}_{BA} = \mathbf{0}\), the work done on A by B is not necessarily equal and opposite to the work done on B by A (\(W_{AB} + W_{BA}\) need not be zero).

4. Kinetic Energy and Work Done by a Variable Force

If an object of mass \(m\) moves with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\), its kinetic energy \(K\) is defined as:

\[ K = \frac{1}{2} m (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}) = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m} \]

where \(p = mv\) is the magnitude of linear momentum. Kinetic energy is a scalar and is always non-negative (\(K \ge 0\)). It measures the work an object can do by virtue of its motion.

Bullet \(5 \times 10^{-2}\) \(200\) \(10^3\)
Stone dropped from \(10\,\mathrm{m}\) \(1\) \(14\) \(10^2\)

Worked Illustration 4: Bullet Penetrating Plywood

Problem: In a ballistics demonstration, a police officer fires a bullet of mass \(50.0\,\mathrm{g}\) with speed \(200\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) on soft plywood of thickness \(2.00\,\mathrm{cm}\). The bullet emerges with only \(10\%\) of its initial kinetic energy. What is the emergent speed of the bullet?

Solution:

Initial kinetic energy of the bullet (\(m = 50.0\,\mathrm{g} = 0.05\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(v_i = 200\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)):

\[ K_i = \frac{1}{2} m v_i^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.05\,\mathrm{kg} \times (200\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})^2 = 1000\,\mathrm{J} \]

Final kinetic energy is \(10\%\) of \(K_i\):

\[ K_f = 0.10 \times 1000\,\mathrm{J} = 100\,\mathrm{J} \]

Let \(v_f\) be the emergent speed:

\[ \frac{1}{2} m v_f^2 = 100\,\mathrm{J} \implies v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 100\,\mathrm{J}}{0.05\,\mathrm{kg}}} = \sqrt{4000}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 63.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Observation: Even though kinetic energy is reduced by \(90\%\), the speed is reduced by approximately \(68\%\) (because \(v \propto \sqrt{K}\)).

Work Done by a Variable Force

In nature, constant forces are rare; variable forces \(F(x)\) are far more common. For an infinitesimally small displacement \(\Delta x\), the force \(F(x)\) can be treated as approximately constant, so the elemental work done is \(\Delta W = F(x)\,\Delta x\) (the area of a narrow strip of width \(\Delta x\) and height \(F(x)\)). Summing over all strips from initial position \(x_i\) to final position \(x_f\) and taking the limit as \(\Delta x \to 0\):

\[ W = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \sum_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,\Delta x = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \text{Area under the } F(x)\text{ vs. }x\text{ curve} \]

Proof of the Work-Energy Theorem for a Variable Force

In one dimension, the time rate of change of kinetic energy is:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}K}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{2}mv^2\right) = m \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} v = F v = F \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

Thus, \(\mathrm{d}K = F\,\mathrm{d}x\). Integrating from initial position \(x_i\) (kinetic energy \(K_i\)) to final position \(x_f\) (kinetic energy \(K_f\)):

\[ \int_{K_i}^{K_f} \mathrm{d}K = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \implies K_f - K_i = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = W \]
Important Comparison: Work-Energy Theorem vs. Newton's Second Law

The Work-Energy Theorem is an integral scalar form of Newton's Second Law. While extremely convenient for relating speeds and positions, it does not contain complete dynamical information:

  • Temporal (time) information is integrated over and is not explicitly available.
  • Directional (vector) information in 2D/3D is absent because work and kinetic energy are scalars.

Worked Illustration 6: Variable Retarding Force (\(F_r = -k/x\))

Problem: A block of mass \(m = 1\,\mathrm{kg}\), moving on a horizontal surface with initial speed \(v_i = 2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), enters a rough patch ranging from \(x = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(x = 2.01\,\mathrm{m}\). The retarding force \(F_r\) in this range is given by:

\[ F_r = -\frac{k}{x} \quad \text{for } 0.1 < x < 2.01\,\mathrm{m} \quad (\text{and } F_r = 0 \text{ outside this range}) \]

where \(k = 0.5\,\mathrm{J}\). Find the final kinetic energy \(K_f\) and final speed \(v_f\) of the block as it crosses the patch. (Take \(\ln(20.1) \approx 3.0\)).

Solution:

Using the Work-Energy Theorem for a variable force:

\[ K_f = K_i + \int_{0.10}^{2.01} \left(-\frac{k}{x}\right)\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 - k \Big[\ln x\Big]_{0.10}^{2.01} = \frac{1}{2}(1)(2)^2 - 0.5\,\ln\left(\frac{2.01}{0.10}\right) \] \[ K_f = 2 - 0.5\,\ln(20.1) = 2 - 0.5(3.0) = 2 - 1.5 = 0.5\,\mathrm{J} \]

Final speed of the block:

\[ v_f = \sqrt{\frac{2K_f}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 0.5}{1}} = 1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]

Note: Here \(\ln X = \log_e X = 2.303 \log_{10} X\) denotes the natural logarithm to base \(e\).

5. Potential Energy and Conservation of Mechanical Energy

Potential energy is the "stored energy" possessed by a system by virtue of the position or configuration of its parts (such as a stretched bowstring, compressed fault lines in the Earth's crust, or a raised object). When constraints are removed, this stored energy manifests as kinetic energy.

Mathematical Relation Between Conservative Force and Potential Energy

A force \(F(x)\) is called conservative if the work done by it depends only on the initial and final positions and is independent of the path taken. For such a force, we define a potential energy function \(V(x)\) such that:

\[ F(x) = -\frac{\mathrm{d}V(x)}{\mathrm{d}x} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \Delta V = V_f - V_i = -\int_{x_i}^{x_f} F(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = -W_c \]

The change in potential energy \(\Delta V\) is equal to the negative of the work done by the conservative force.

Three Equivalent Characterisations of a Conservative Force

  1. Derivability from a Scalar Potential: \(F(x) = -\dfrac{\mathrm{d}V(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\).
  2. Path Independence: Work done by the force depends only on the endpoints \(x_i\) and \(x_f\): \(W = V(x_i) - V(x_f)\).
  3. Zero Work over any Closed Loop: Work done by a conservative force in a closed path (\(x_i = x_f\)) is identically zero: \(\oint \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r} = 0\).

Examples of Conservative Forces: Gravitational force, ideal spring force (Hooke's law), electrostatic force.
Examples of Non-Conservative Forces: Frictional force, viscous drag / air resistance (work done depends on path length and is non-zero in a closed loop; no potential energy function can be associated with them).

Principle of Conservation of Mechanical Energy

By the Work-Energy Theorem, \(\Delta K = W\). If only conservative forces do work, then \(W = -\Delta V\). Combining these:

\[ \Delta K + \Delta V = 0 \implies \Delta(K + V) = 0 \implies K_i + V(x_i) = K_f + V(x_f) = E = \text{constant} \]

The sum \(E = K + V(x)\) is called the total mechanical energy of the system. If both conservative forces (\(F_c\)) and non-conservative forces (\(F_{nc}\)) perform work, the modified work-energy relation is:

\[ \Delta E = E_f - E_i = (K_f + V_f) - (K_i + V_i) = W_{nc} \]

Case Study 1: Free Fall Under Gravity

Near the Earth's surface (height \(h \ll R_E\)), gravitational acceleration \(g\) is constant. Taking the ground as the reference level \(V(0) = 0\) and upward direction as positive, \(V(h) = mgh\) and \(F = -\dfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}h} = -mg\). For a ball of mass \(m\) dropped from rest at cliff height \(H\):

  • At top (\(x = H\)): \(E_H = mgH + 0 = mgH\) (purely potential).
  • At intermediate height \(h\): \(E_h = mgh + \frac{1}{2}mv_h^2\). Equating \(E_h = E_H \implies v_h^2 = 2g(H - h)\).
  • At ground (\(x = 0\)): \(E_0 = 0 + \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2\). Equating \(E_0 = E_H \implies v_f = \sqrt{2gH}\) (purely kinetic).

Case Study 2: Motion in a Vertical Circle

Worked Illustration 7: Critical Velocities and Tension in a Vertical Circle

Problem: A bob of mass \(m\) is suspended by a light string of length \(L\). It is imparted a horizontal velocity \(v_0\) at the lowest point A such that it completes a semi-circular trajectory in the vertical plane with the string becoming slack only on reaching the topmost point C. Obtain expressions for: (i) \(v_0\), (ii) the speeds at points B (horizontal string) and C (topmost point), and (iii) the ratio of kinetic energies \((K_B / K_C)\). Comment on the trajectory after C.

Solution:

(i) Tension \(T\) is always perpendicular to the displacement of the bob, so it does zero work. Mechanical energy is conserved. Taking potential energy \(V = 0\) at the lowest point A:

  • At lowest point A: \(E_A = \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2\), and radial equation of motion is \(T_A - mg = \dfrac{mv_0^2}{L}\).
  • At topmost point C (height \(2L\) above A): The string just slackens, so \(T_C = 0\). Gravity alone provides the centripetal force: \[ mg = \frac{mv_C^2}{L} \implies v_C^2 = gL \]
  • Total mechanical energy at C is: \[ E_C = \frac{1}{2}mv_C^2 + mg(2L) = \frac{1}{2}m(gL) + 2mgL = \frac{5}{2}mgL \]
  • Equating \(E_A = E_C\): \[ \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = \frac{5}{2}mgL \implies v_0 = \sqrt{5gL} \]

(ii) Speed at C is \(v_C = \sqrt{gL}\). At point B (height \(L\) above A), conservation of energy gives:

\[ E_B = \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2 + mgL = \frac{5}{2}mgL \implies \frac{1}{2}mv_B^2 = \frac{3}{2}mgL \implies v_B = \sqrt{3gL} \]

(Bonus for NEET: Tension at A is \(T_A = mg + \frac{m(5gL)}{L} = 6mg\), and tension at B is \(T_B = \frac{mv_B^2}{L} = 3mg\).)

(iii) Ratio of kinetic energies at B and C:

\[ \frac{K_B}{K_C} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}mv_B^2}{\frac{1}{2}mv_C^2} = \frac{3gL}{gL} = 3 : 1 \]

Trajectory after C: Because the bob still has horizontal velocity \(v_C = \sqrt{gL}\) at C, if the string is not cut, the bob continues along its circular path to complete the revolution. If the string were cut at C, it would execute horizontal projectile motion.

6. Potential Energy of an Ideal Spring

Consider a block of mass \(m\) attached to a light (massless) spring resting on a smooth horizontal surface. For an ideal spring obeying Hooke's law, the restoring spring force \(F_s\) is proportional and opposite to the displacement \(x\) from the equilibrium position (\(x = 0\)):

\[ F_s = -k x \]

where \(k\) is the spring constant (SI unit: \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\); dimensions: \([\mathrm{MT^{-2}}]\)). A large \(k\) indicates a stiff spring; a small \(k\) indicates a soft spring.

  • Work done by the spring force from \(x_i\) to \(x_f\): \[ W_s = -\int_{x_i}^{x_f} kx\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{2}kx_i^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx_f^2 \]
  • Work done by the spring force from \(x = 0\) to \(x = x_m\): \(W_s = -\frac{1}{2}kx_m^2\), whereas the work done by the slow external pulling/compressing agency is \(W_{\text{ext}} = +\frac{1}{2}kx_m^2\).
  • Elastic Potential Energy of a Spring (choosing \(V(0) = 0\) at the natural length):
    \[ V(x) = \frac{1}{2} k x^2 \]
  • Conservation of Mechanical Energy in a Spring-Mass System: When a block of mass \(m\) is pulled to amplitude \(x_m\) and released from rest on a frictionless surface, at any position \(x \in [-x_m, +x_m]\): \[ E = \frac{1}{2}kx_m^2 = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 + \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \] At \(x = 0\), potential energy is zero and kinetic energy is maximum, yielding maximum speed: \[ v_m = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}\, x_m \]

Worked Illustration 8: Car Colliding with a Bumper Spring (Smooth vs. Rough Road)

Problem: A car of mass \(1000\,\mathrm{kg}\) moving with a speed of \(18.0\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) collides with a horizontally mounted spring of spring constant \(k = 5.25 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) (\(6.25 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) in some standard approximations; here we use the source data directly).
(a) Find the maximum compression \(x_m\) of the spring on a smooth (frictionless) road.
(b) Find the maximum compression \(x_m\) if the road is rough with coefficient of kinetic friction \(\mu = 0.5\) (take \(g = 10.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

Solution:

First convert the initial speed to SI units: \(v = 18.0\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}} = 18.0 \times \frac{5}{18}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
Initial kinetic energy of the car:

\[ K_i = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg} \times (5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})^2 = 1.25 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{J} \]

(a) Smooth Road (\(\mu = 0\)): At maximum compression \(x_m\), the car comes momentarily to rest (\(K_f = 0\)) and its entire initial kinetic energy is stored as spring potential energy:

\[ \frac{1}{2} k x_m^2 = 1.25 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{J} \implies x_m = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.25 \times 10^4}{6.25 \times 10^3}} = 2.00\,\mathrm{m} \quad (\text{or } 2.18\,\mathrm{m}\text{ if }k=5.25\times 10^3\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}; \text{ standard textbook value is } 2.00\,\mathrm{m}) \]

(b) Rough Road (\(\mu = 0.5\)): Both the restoring spring force and kinetic friction \(\mu mg\) oppose the motion. Applying the Work-Energy Theorem (\(\Delta K = W_{\text{net}}\)):

\[ 0 - \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = -\frac{1}{2}kx_m^2 - \mu mg x_m \implies k x_m^2 + 2\mu mg x_m - mv^2 = 0 \]

Here \(\mu mg = 0.5 \times 10^3 \times 10 = 5 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{N}\). Taking the positive root of the quadratic equation:

\[ x_m = \frac{-\mu mg + \sqrt{(\mu mg)^2 + m k v^2}}{k} = 1.35\,\mathrm{m} \]

As expected, friction dissipates part of the mechanical energy, resulting in a smaller compression (\(1.35\,\mathrm{m} < 2.00\,\mathrm{m}\)).

7. Power

Power is defined as the time rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

  • Average Power: \(P_{\text{av}} = \dfrac{W}{t}\)
  • Instantaneous Power: Since elemental work \(\mathrm{d}W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\), the instantaneous power is:
    \[ P = \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = F v \cos\theta \]
  • Nature, Dimensions & Units: Power is a scalar quantity with dimensions \([\mathrm{ML^2T^{-3}}]\). Its SI unit is the watt (\(\mathrm{W}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{W} = 1\,\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}\).
  • Horsepower (\(\mathrm{hp}\)): Used in automobiles and motors: \[ 1\,\mathrm{hp} = 746\,\mathrm{W} \]
  • Note on \(\mathrm{kWh}\): \(1\,\text{kilowatt hour (kWh)} = 1000\,\mathrm{W} \times 3600\,\mathrm{s} = 3.6 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J}\) is a unit of energy, not power!
Worked Illustration 9: Power Delivered by an Elevator Motor

Problem: An elevator carrying a maximum load of \(1800\,\mathrm{kg}\) (elevator + passengers) moves up with a constant speed of \(2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The frictional force opposing the motion is \(4000\,\mathrm{N}\). Determine the minimum power delivered by the motor to the elevator in watts and in horsepower (take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

Solution:

Total downward force opposing the upward motion of the elevator:

\[ F_{\text{down}} = mg + F_f = (1800 \times 10) + 4000 = 18000 + 4000 = 22000\,\mathrm{N} \]

To move upward at constant speed \(v = 2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the motor must supply an upward pulling force \(F = 22000\,\mathrm{N}\). Therefore, the minimum power delivered is:

\[ P = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = F v = 22000\,\mathrm{N} \times 2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 44000\,\mathrm{W} = 44\,\mathrm{kW} \]

In horsepower:

\[ P = \frac{44000}{746}\,\mathrm{hp} \approx 59\,\mathrm{hp} \]

8. Collisions in One and Two Dimensions

During any collision between two particles of masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\), mutual impulsive forces \(\mathbf{F}_{12}\) and \(\mathbf{F}_{21}\) act over a brief collision time \(\Delta t\). By Newton's Third Law, \(\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21}\) at every instant, which implies:

\[ \Delta \mathbf{p}_1 + \Delta \mathbf{p}_2 = \mathbf{F}_{12}\Delta t + \mathbf{F}_{21}\Delta t = \mathbf{0} \]

Hence, total linear momentum is conserved in all collisions (elastic or inelastic) at every instant of the collision.

Classification of Collisions

Type of Collision Linear Momentum Total Kinetic Energy (Before vs. After) Deformation & Post-Collision Motion
Elastic Collision Conserved Conserved (\(K_i = K_f\))
(Note: During \(\Delta t\), part of \(K\) is momentarily stored as deformation potential energy)		 Deformation is completely relieved; bodies separate without loss of mechanical energy.
Inelastic Collision Conserved Not conserved (\(K_f < K_i\)); part of \(K_i\) is lost as heat/sound. Deformation is only partly relieved.
Completely Inelastic Collision Conserved Maximum possible loss of kinetic energy consistent with momentum conservation. Deformation is not relieved; the two bodies stick and move together with common velocity \(v_f\).

One-Dimensional (Head-On) Collisions

Let mass \(m_1\) moving with initial velocity \(v_{1i}\) collide head-on with stationary mass \(m_2\) (\(v_{2i} = 0\)).

1. Completely Inelastic 1D Collision (Bodies Stick Together)

  • Common Final Velocity: By momentum conservation, \(m_1 v_{1i} = (m_1 + m_2) v_f \implies\) \[ v_f = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2} v_{1i} \]
  • Loss in Kinetic Energy (\(\Delta K_{\text{loss}}\)):
    \[ \Delta K = K_i - K_f = \frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1i}^2 - \frac{1}{2}(m_1 + m_2)v_f^2 = \frac{1}{2} \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} v_{1i}^2 \]

2. Elastic 1D Collision (\(m_2\) Initially at Rest)

Momentum conservation: \(m_1 v_{1i} = m_1 v_{1f} + m_2 v_{2f} \implies m_1(v_{1i} - v_{1f}) = m_2 v_{2f}\).
Kinetic energy conservation: \(m_1 v_{1i}^2 = m_1 v_{1f}^2 + m_2 v_{2f}^2 \implies m_1(v_{1i} - v_{1f})(v_{1i} + v_{1f}) = m_2 v_{2f}^2\).
Dividing the second equation by the first gives the relative velocity relation:

\[ v_{2f} = v_{1i} + v_{1f} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad v_{2f} - v_{1f} = v_{1i} \quad (\text{Velocity of separation} = \text{Velocity of approach}) \]

Solving for the final velocities \(v_{1f}\) and \(v_{2f}\):

\[ v_{1f} = \left(\frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right) v_{1i} \qquad \text{and} \qquad v_{2f} = \left(\frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\right) v_{1i} \]
  • Special Case I (\(m_1 = m_2\), Equal Masses): \(v_{1f} = 0\) and \(v_{2f} = v_{1i}\). The two identical bodies exchange their velocities; the incident mass stops dead and the target mass moves off with the initial speed \(v_{1i}\).
  • Special Case II (\(m_2 \gg m_1\), Light Body Hitting a Massive Target): \(v_{1f} \approx -v_{1i}\) and \(v_{2f} \approx 0\). The heavy target remains nearly undisturbed while the lighter mass rebounds with its velocity reversed.
  • Special Case III (\(m_1 \gg m_2\), Heavy Body Hitting a Very Light Target): \(v_{1f} \approx v_{1i}\) and \(v_{2f} \approx 2v_{1i}\).
Worked Illustration 10: Slowing Down of Fast Neutrons in a Nuclear Reactor (Moderator)

Problem: In a nuclear reactor, a high-speed neutron (typically \(10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)) must be slowed down to \(10^3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) so that it can have a high probability of interacting with \(^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U}\) and causing fission. Find the fraction of kinetic energy retained by the neutron (\(f_1\)) and the fraction transferred to the stationary target nucleus (\(f_2\)) in a head-on elastic collision. Evaluate \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) for: (a) deuterium (\(m_2 = 2m_1\)) in heavy water (\(\mathrm{D_2O}\)), and (b) carbon (\(m_2 = 12m_1\)) in graphite.

Solution:

Initial kinetic energy of the neutron is \(K_{1i} = \frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1i}^2\). After an elastic head-on collision with a stationary nucleus of mass \(m_2\), its final kinetic energy is:

\[ K_{1f} = \frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1f}^2 = \frac{1}{2}m_1 \left(\frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right)^2 v_{1i}^2 \]

1. Fraction of kinetic energy retained by the neutron:

\[ f_1 = \frac{K_{1f}}{K_{1i}} = \left(\frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right)^2 \]

2. Fraction of kinetic energy transferred to (gained by) the moderating nucleus:

\[ f_2 = \frac{K_{2f}}{K_{1i}} = 1 - f_1 = \frac{4 m_1 m_2}{(m_1 + m_2)^2} \]
  • (a) For Deuterium (\(m_2 = 2m_1\)): \[ f_1 = \left(\frac{1 - 2}{1 + 2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{9} \approx 11.1\%, \qquad f_2 = 1 - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{8}{9} \approx 88.9\% \] Almost \(90\%\) of the neutron's kinetic energy is transferred to the deuterium nucleus!
  • (b) For Carbon-12 (\(m_2 = 12m_1\)): \[ f_1 = \left(\frac{1 - 12}{1 + 12}\right)^2 = \frac{121}{169} \approx 71.6\%, \qquad f_2 = 1 - \frac{121}{169} = \frac{48}{169} \approx 28.4\% \]

Worked Illustration 11: Oblique Elastic Collision of Two Identical Billiard Balls

Problem: A cue ball of mass \(m_1\) undergoes a glancing (non-head-on) elastic collision with an identical stationary target billiard ball (\(m_2 = m_1\)). The player wants to sink the target ball in a corner pocket at an angle \(\theta_2 = 37^\circ\) with respect to the initial direction of the cue ball. Neglecting friction and rotational motion, find the scattering angle \(\theta_1\) of the cue ball.

Solution:

Since \(m_1 = m_2 = m\), conservation of linear momentum gives:

\[ \mathbf{v}_{1i} = \mathbf{v}_{1f} + \mathbf{v}_{2f} \]

Taking the scalar product of each side with itself:

\[ v_{1i}^2 = (\mathbf{v}_{1f} + \mathbf{v}_{2f}) \cdot (\mathbf{v}_{1f} + \mathbf{v}_{2f}) = v_{1f}^2 + v_{2f}^2 + 2\,\mathbf{v}_{1f}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{2f} = v_{1f}^2 + v_{2f}^2 + 2 v_{1f} v_{2f}\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \]

Since the collision is elastic and the masses are equal, conservation of kinetic energy requires:

\[ v_{1i}^2 = v_{1f}^2 + v_{2f}^2 \]

Comparing the two equations immediately yields:

\[ 2 v_{1f} v_{2f}\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = 0 \implies \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = 0 \implies \theta_1 + \theta_2 = 90^\circ \]

Given \(\theta_2 = 37^\circ\), we obtain:

\[ \theta_1 = 90^\circ - 37^\circ = 53^\circ \]

Golden Rule for NEET: Whenever two equal masses undergo a glancing (2D) elastic collision with one of them initially at rest, they always move at right angles (\(90^\circ\)) to each other after the collision.

Application 1: Interpreting 1D Potential Energy Graphs & Classical Turning Points

Problem: A particle moves in one dimension with a given total mechanical energy \(E\) (indicated by a horizontal line on each plot below). Since kinetic energy \(K = E - V(x)\) can never be negative (\(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \ge 0\)), the particle cannot exist in any region where \(V(x) > E\). For each of the four potential energy profiles shown below, specify the forbidden regions where the particle cannot be found and the minimum total energy \(E_{\min}\) the particle must possess.

  • Profile (a): For \(x > a\), \(V(x) = V_0 > E\), so the particle cannot be found in the region \(x > a\). The minimum total energy is \(E_{\min} = 0\).
  • Profile (b): Everywhere on the graph, the potential energy steps exceed \(E\). Thus, the particle cannot be found in any region (\(-\infty < x < \infty\)) for the indicated energy \(E\). Minimum total energy required is the lowest potential step on the graph.
  • Profile (c): For \(x < a\) and \(x > b\), \(V(x) = V_0 > E\). Thus, the particle is trapped inside the well \(a < x < b\) and cannot be found in \(x < a\) or \(x > b\). Minimum total energy is \(E_{\min} = -V_1\).
  • Profile (d): \(V(x) > E\) in the barrier intervals, so the particle cannot be found in \(-\frac{b}{2} < x < -\frac{a}{2}\) and \(\frac{a}{2} < x < \frac{b}{2}\). Minimum total energy is \(E_{\min} = -V_1\).

Harmonic Oscillator Turning Points: Similarly, if a particle executing simple harmonic motion under \(V(x) = \frac{1}{2}kx^2\) with \(k = 0.5\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) has total energy \(E = 1\,\mathrm{J}\), it must turn back where \(K = 0 \implies V(x) = E\):

\[ \frac{1}{2}(0.5)x^2 = 1\,\mathrm{J} \implies x^2 = 4 \implies x = \pm 2\,\mathrm{m} \]
Application 2: Elastic Collision of Identical Ball Bearings in Contact (Newton's Cradle Principle)

Problem: Two identical ball bearings (2 and 3) are in contact with each other and resting on a frictionless table. They are hit head-on by another identical ball bearing (1) of the same mass \(m\) moving initially with speed \(v\). If the collision is elastic, which of the following outcomes is physically possible?
Outcome (i): Ball 1 comes to rest (\(v_1 = 0\)), while balls 2 and 3 move together with speed \(v/2\).
Outcome (ii): Balls 1 and 2 come to rest (\(v = 0\)), while ball 3 moves off with speed \(v\).
Outcome (iii): All three balls move together with speed \(v/3\).

Solution:

Before collision, total linear momentum is \(p_i = mv\) and total kinetic energy is \(K_i = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\). Although all three outcomes conserve momentum (\(p_f = mv\)), let us check kinetic energy \(K_f\):

  • Outcome (i): \(K_f = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(2m)\left(\frac{v}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}mv^2 \neq K_i\) (Violates elastic energy conservation).
  • Outcome (ii): \(K_f = 0 + \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = K_i\) (Conserves both momentum and kinetic energy! Physically, ball 1 transfers its velocity to ball 2 in an instantaneous elastic collision, and ball 2 immediately transfers it to ball 3).
  • Outcome (iii): \(K_f = \frac{1}{2}(3m)\left(\frac{v}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{6}mv^2 \neq K_i\) (Violates elastic energy conservation).

Therefore, only Outcome (ii) is possible.

    • When a rocket casing burns up due to atmospheric friction, the heat energy comes at the expense of the rocket's mechanical energy (not the atmosphere).

    • An artificial satellite losing total mechanical energy \(E = -\frac{GMm}{2r}\) due to thin atmospheric drag moves to a lower orbit (smaller \(r\)), where loss of potential energy \(\Delta V\) is twice the magnitude of \(\Delta E\), causing its kinetic energy and speed to increase progressively.

Practice Question 1 [Single-Correct MCQ — Scalar Product & Work]

A particle is constrained to move along the \(z\)-axis of a Cartesian coordinate system and is subjected to a constant force \(\mathbf{F} = (-\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 2\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N}\). What is the work done by this force in moving the particle through a distance of \(4\,\mathrm{m}\) along the positive \(z\)-axis?

  • (A) \(4\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(8\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(12\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(16\,\mathrm{J}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: The displacement vector of the particle moving \(4\,\mathrm{m}\) along the positive \(z\)-axis is \(\mathbf{d} = 4\hat{\mathbf{k}}\,\mathrm{m}\). Work done by the constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) is:

\[ W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{d} = (-\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 2\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{k}}) \cdot (0\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{k}}) = (-1)(0) + (2)(0) + (3)(4) = 12\,\mathrm{J} \]
Practice Question 2 [Single-Correct MCQ — Work-Energy Theorem with Position-Dependent Velocity]

A body of mass \(0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) travels in a straight line with velocity \(v = a x^{3/2}\), where \(a = 5\,\mathrm{m^{-1/2}\,s^{-1}}\). What is the work done by the net force during its displacement from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 2\,\mathrm{m}\)?

  • (A) \(25\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(50\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(100\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(12.5\,\mathrm{J}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Given \(m = 0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(v(x) = 5 x^{3/2}\).
At \(x_i = 0\): initial speed \(v_i = 5(0)^{3/2} = 0\).
At \(x_f = 2\,\mathrm{m}\): final speed \(v_f = 5(2)^{3/2} = 5\sqrt{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \implies v_f^2 = 25 \times 8 = 200\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\).
By the Work-Energy Theorem, the work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy:

\[ W_{\text{net}} = K_f - K_i = \frac{1}{2} m v_f^2 - 0 = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.5\,\mathrm{kg} \times 200\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}} = 50\,\mathrm{J} \]
Practice Question 3 [Single-Correct MCQ — Power Time-Dependence under Constant Acceleration]

A body is initially at rest and undergoes one-dimensional motion with constant acceleration. The instantaneous power delivered to it at time \(t\) is proportional to:

  • (A) \(t^{1/2}\)
  • (B) \(t\)
  • (C) \(t^{3/2}\)
  • (D) \(t^2\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Since acceleration \(a\) is constant and initial velocity \(u = 0\), the velocity at time \(t\) is \(v = at\), and the force is constant (\(F = ma\)). Therefore, instantaneous power is:

Practice Question 4 [Single-Correct MCQ — Motion Under Constant Power]

A body initially at rest is moving unidirectionally under the influence of a source delivering constant power \(P_0\). Its displacement \(x\) in time \(t\) is proportional to:

  • (A) \(t^{1/2}\)
  • (B) \(t\)
  • (C) \(t^{3/2}\)
  • (D) \(t^2\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: For constant power \(P_0\), the work done in time \(t\) is \(W = P_0 t\). By the Work-Energy Theorem (starting from rest):

\[ \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = P_0 t \implies v = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \sqrt{\frac{2P_0}{m}}\, t^{1/2} \]

Integrating with respect to \(t\) from \(0\) to \(t\):

\[ x(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2P_0}{m}} \int_0^t t^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2P_0}{m}}\, t^{3/2} \implies x \propto t^{3/2} \]

An electric pump on the ground floor of a building pumps up water to fill an overhead tank of volume \(30\,\mathrm{m^3}\) in \(15\,\mathrm{min}\). Time taken: \(t = 15\,\mathrm{min} = 15 \times 60\,\mathrm{s} = 900\,\mathrm{s}\).
Output mechanical power required to lift the water:

\[ P_{\text{out}} = \frac{mgh}{t} = \frac{3 \times 10^4 \times 9.8 \times 40}{900} = \frac{1.176 \times 10^7}{900} \approx 13066.7\,\mathrm{W} \approx 13.07\,\mathrm{kW} \]

Since efficiency \(\eta = \dfrac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} = 0.30\), the electric power consumed (\(P_{\text{in}}\)) is:

\[ P_{\text{in}} = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{0.30} = \frac{13.067\,\mathrm{kW}}{0.30} \approx 43.56\,\mathrm{kW} \approx 43.6\,\mathrm{kW} \]
Practice Question 6 [Single-Correct MCQ — Energy Dissipation in a Simple Pendulum]

The bob of a simple pendulum of length \(L = 1.5\,\mathrm{m}\) is released from rest from a horizontal position. If the bob dissipates \(5\%\) of its initial mechanical energy against air resistance before reaching the lowermost point, with what speed does it arrive at the lowermost point? (Take \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

  • (A) \(5.29\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(5.42\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(4.85\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(6.12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Taking the lowermost point as the reference level (\(V = 0\)), the initial potential energy at the horizontal position is \(E_i = mgL\). Since \(5\%\) of this energy is dissipated against air resistance, \(95\%\) of \(E_i\) is converted into kinetic energy at the bottom:

\[ \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = 0.95\, m g L \implies v = \sqrt{2 \times 0.95 \times g \times L} = \sqrt{1.90 \times 9.8 \times 1.5} = \sqrt{27.93}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 5.285\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 5.29\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]
Practice Question 7 [Single-Correct MCQ — Pendulum Elastic Collision]

The bob A of a simple pendulum is released from rest at an angle of \(30^\circ\) to the vertical. At the lowest point of its swing, it hits head-on another bob B of the exact same mass resting on a horizontal frictionless table. Assuming the collision to be perfectly elastic and neglecting the sizes of the bobs, how high does bob A rise after the collision?

  • (A) It rises to an angle of \(15^\circ\) to the vertical.
  • (B) It rises back to an angle of \(30^\circ\) to the vertical.
  • (C) It does not rise at all (height \(= 0\)).
  • (D) It rises to half its initial vertical height.
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: At the lowest point, bob A moves horizontally and undergoes a one-dimensional (head-on) elastic collision with stationary bob B of equal mass (\(m_A = m_B = m\)). In a 1D elastic collision between two equal masses, the bodies exchange their velocities: bob A comes completely to rest (\(v_{Af} = 0\)) while bob B moves off with the velocity of A. Since bob A has zero velocity immediately after the collision, it does not rise at all.

Practice Question 8 [Single-Correct MCQ — Cosmic Ray Electron vs. Proton Speed Ratio]

In a cosmic ray experiment, an electron is detected with kinetic energy \(10\,\mathrm{keV}\) and a proton is detected with kinetic energy \(100\,\mathrm{keV}\). Given electron mass \(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\) and proton mass \(m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\), which particle is faster and what is the approximate ratio of their speeds \((v_e / v_p)\)?

  • (A) Proton is faster; \(v_e / v_p \approx 0.074\)
  • (B) Electron is faster; \(v_e / v_p \approx 13.5\)
  • (C) Electron is faster; \(v_e / v_p \approx 183\)
  • (D) Both have the same speed; \(v_e / v_p = 1\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: From \(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\), we have \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2K}{m}}\). Therefore, the ratio of the electron speed to the proton speed is:

\[ \frac{v_e}{v_p} = \sqrt{\frac{K_e}{K_p} \times \frac{m_p}{m_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{10\,\mathrm{keV}}{100\,\mathrm{keV}} \times \frac{1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}}} = \sqrt{0.1 \times 1833.15} = \sqrt{183.3} \approx 13.54 \]

Thus, the electron is faster than the proton by a factor of about \(13.5\).

Practice Question 9 [Single-Correct MCQ — Gas Molecule Rebounding from a Wall]

A gas molecule hits a rigid stationary horizontal wall with a speed of \(200\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the normal, and rebounds with the exact same speed of \(200\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at \(30^\circ\) to the normal. Which of the following statements is correct regarding the collision of the molecule-wall system?

  • (A) Total linear momentum is conserved and the collision is elastic.
  • (B) Total linear momentum is not conserved, but the collision is elastic.
  • (C) Total linear momentum is conserved, but the collision is inelastic.
  • (D) Neither momentum nor kinetic energy is conserved.
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Total linear momentum of the isolated (molecule + wall) system is always conserved. The wall receives a normal impulse equal and opposite to the change in momentum of the molecule (\(\Delta p = 2mv\cos 30^\circ\)), though the wall's recoil velocity is negligibly small because its mass is enormous (\(M_{\text{wall}} \gg m\)). Since the molecule rebounds with the same speed (\(200\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)) and the massive wall acquires negligible kinetic energy (\(\Delta K_{\text{wall}} = \frac{\Delta p^2}{2M_{\text{wall}}} \approx 0\)), the total kinetic energy is also conserved; hence the collision is elastic.

Practice Question 10 [Assertion–Reason — Conservative Forces & Potential Energy]

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as Assertion (A) and the other is labelled as Reason (R).
Assertion (A): When a conservative force does positive work on a body, the potential energy of the body decreases.
Reason (R): For a conservative force, the change in potential energy is defined as the negative of the work done by the conservative force (\(\Delta V = -W_c\)).
In the light of the above statements, choose the correct option:

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: By definition, \(\Delta V = V_f - V_i = -\int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_c\,\mathrm{d}x = -W_c\). Therefore, whenever the conservative force does positive work (\(W_c > 0\), such as gravity acting on a falling stone), \(\Delta V < 0\), meaning potential energy decreases. Both statements are true and (R) directly explains (A).

Practice Question 11 [Multi-Statement Evaluation — Collisions & Conservation Laws]

Consider the following four statements regarding collisions between two bodies:

  1. Statement I: In an elastic collision of two bodies, the linear momentum and kinetic energy of each individual body are conserved separately.
  2. Statement II: During the short contact time \(\Delta t\) of an elastic collision between two billiard balls, the total linear momentum of the two-ball system is conserved at every instant, but the total kinetic energy is not constant during contact.
  3. Statement III: Work done in the motion of a body over a closed loop is zero for every force in nature.
  4. Statement IV: If the interaction force between two colliding billiard balls depends only on the separation distance between their centres, the collision is necessarily elastic.

Which of the above statements are correct?

  • (A) Statements I and III only
  • (B) Statements II and IV only
  • (C) Statements I, II, and IV only
  • (D) Statements II, III, and IV only
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution:

  • Statement I is False: It is the total momentum and total kinetic energy of the two-body system that are conserved, not those of each individual body (energy and momentum are exchanged between the bodies).
  • Statement II is True: Momentum is conserved at every instant of \(\Delta t\), whereas kinetic energy is partially converted into elastic deformation potential energy during contact and is only restored after the balls separate.
  • Statement III is False: Closed-loop work is zero only for conservative forces; for non-conservative forces like friction, work over a closed path is negative (non-zero).
  • Statement IV is True: A force that depends only on separation distance is conservative, so no mechanical energy is dissipated during the collision and recovery, making the collision elastic.
Practice Question 12 [Single-Correct MCQ — Raindrop Terminal Velocity & Resistive Work]

A spherical raindrop of radius \(r = 2\,\mathrm{mm}\) falls from a height of \(500\,\mathrm{m}\) above the ground. It falls with decreasing acceleration due to viscous air resistance until at half its original height (\(250\,\mathrm{m}\)) it attains its terminal speed of \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and moves with uniform speed thereafter. What is the work done by the gravitational force in each half of its journey, and what is the total work done by the resistive force over the entire \(500\,\mathrm{m}\) journey? (Take density of water \(\rho = 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

  • (A) \(W_{g,\text{half}} = 0.082\,\mathrm{J}\); \(W_r = -0.163\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(W_{g,\text{half}} = 0.164\,\mathrm{J}\); \(W_r = -0.082\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(W_{g,\text{half}} = 0.082\,\mathrm{J}\); \(W_r = -0.082\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(W_{g,\text{half}} = 0.041\,\mathrm{J}\); \(W_r = -0.163\,\mathrm{J}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Mass of the spherical raindrop (\(r = 2 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}\)):

\[ m = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho = \frac{4}{3} \times 3.1416 \times (2 \times 10^{-3})^3 \times 10^3 \approx 3.35 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{kg} \]

Since gravitational work \(W_g = mgh\) depends only on vertical displacement, the work done by gravity in the first half (\(h_1 = 250\,\mathrm{m}\)) and second half (\(h_2 = 250\,\mathrm{m}\)) are equal:

\[ W_{g,\text{half}} = (3.35 \times 10^{-5}) \times 9.8 \times 250 \approx 0.082\,\mathrm{J} \]

Total gravitational work over \(500\,\mathrm{m}\) is \(W_{g,\text{total}} = 2 \times 0.082 = 0.164\,\mathrm{J}\). Final kinetic energy of the drop is:

\[ K_f = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times (3.35 \times 10^{-5}) \times 10^2 \approx 0.00168\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.68 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J} \]

By the Work-Energy Theorem (\(\Delta K = W_{g,\text{total}} + W_r\)):

\[ W_r = \Delta K - W_{g,\text{total}} = 0.00168\,\mathrm{J} - 0.164\,\mathrm{J} \approx -0.1625\,\mathrm{J} \approx -0.163\,\mathrm{J} \]

11. Common Pitfalls & Rapid Revision Summary

Frequent NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid
  1. Ambiguous "Work Done" Statements: Never calculate work without specifying which force and which body are under consideration. For instance, when lifting a bucket slowly at constant speed, the work done by the lifting force is positive (\(+mgh\)), the work done by gravity is negative (\(-mgh\)), and the net work done is zero.
  2. Confusing \(\mathrm{kWh}\) with Power: Kilowatt-hour (\(\mathrm{kWh}\)) is a commercial unit of energy (\(3.6 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{J}\)), whereas kilowatt (\(\mathrm{kW}\)) is a unit of power.
  3. Applying \(\Delta K = 0\) During Collision Contact: Even in a perfectly elastic collision, kinetic energy is conserved only after the collision is complete, not during the brief contact interval \(\Delta t\) when the bodies are deformed.
  4. Changing the Zero of Potential Energy Mid-Problem: The reference point where \(V = 0\) is arbitrary, but once chosen in a problem, you must adhere to it consistently throughout the solution.
