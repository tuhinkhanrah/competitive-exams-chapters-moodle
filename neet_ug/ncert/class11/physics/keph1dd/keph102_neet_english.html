1. Learning Objectives & Overview

Motion is common to everything in the universe—from blood flowing in arteries and leaves falling from trees to the Earth revolving around the Sun and galaxies moving within their local groups. In physics, motion is defined as the change in position of an object with time.

Key Learning Objectives for NEET UG
  • Understand the point object approximation and when a physical body can be treated as a particle.
  • Distinguish clearly between path length (distance) and displacement, and between speed and velocity over finite intervals versus at a single instant.
  • Determine instantaneous velocity and acceleration both analytically (using differential calculus) and graphically (using slopes of tangent lines).
  • Calculate displacement from the area under a velocity-time (\(v\)-\(t\)) curve.
  • Derive and apply the kinematic equations of motion for constant acceleration, including vertical motion under gravity (free fall), Galileo's law of odd numbers, stopping distance, and reaction time.
  • Analyze one-dimensional relative motion between two moving bodies.

2. Core Concepts & Explanations

2.1 Kinematics, Rectilinear Motion, and the Point Object Approximation

Kinematics is the branch of mechanics that studies ways to describe motion without going into the causes of motion. When we confine our study to the motion of objects along a single straight line, it is called rectilinear motion (or one-dimensional motion).

To specify the position of an object along a straight line, we choose a convenient reference point as the origin (\(x = 0\)). By convention, positions to the right of the origin (or upward) are often taken as positive, and positions to the left (or downward) as negative, though the choice of origin and positive direction of the axis is entirely a matter of convenience.

Point Object Approximation

In kinematics, we treat moving objects as point objects (particles). This approximation is valid so far as the size of the object is much smaller than the distance it moves in a reasonable duration of time. For example, a railway carriage moving without jerks between two stations or a monkey sitting on top of a man cycling smoothly on a circular track can be treated as point objects. Conversely, a spinning cricket ball that turns sharply on hitting the ground or a tumbling beaker slipping off the edge of a table cannot be treated as point objects because their size and rotational motion are significant over the short distances considered.

2.2 Path Length vs. Displacement and Average Speed vs. Average Velocity

Consider a particle moving along the \(x\)-axis from an initial position \(x_1\) at time \(t_1\) to a final position \(x_2\) at time \(t_2\):

  • Displacement (\(\Delta x\)): The change in position of the object, given by \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1\). It is an algebraic (vector in 1D) quantity whose sign indicates the direction of net motion.
  • Path Length (Total Distance): The actual total length of the path traversed by the particle during the time interval \(\Delta t = t_2 - t_1\). Path length is always positive and never decreases with time.
  • Average Velocity (\(\bar{v}\)): The net displacement divided by the total time interval: \[\bar{v} = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\]
  • Average Speed: The total path length divided by the total time interval: \[\text{Average Speed} = \frac{\text{Total Path Length}}{\text{Total Time Interval}}\]

Because the total path length is always greater than or equal to the magnitude of displacement, the average speed of an object is always greater than or equal to the magnitude of its average velocity over a given time interval. The equality holds true if and only if the object moves along a straight line in the same constant direction without turning back.

2.3 Instantaneous Velocity and Instantaneous Speed

Average velocity tells us how fast an object has been moving over a finite time interval, but it does not tell us how fast it moves at different instants during that interval. To describe motion at a specific instant \(t\), we define instantaneous velocity (or simply velocity \(v\)) as the limit of the average velocity as the time interval \(\Delta t\) becomes infinitesimally small:

\[v = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\]

In the language of calculus, \(\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\) is the differential coefficient of position \(x\) with respect to time \(t\), representing the instantaneous rate of change of position with respect to time.

Why Instantaneous Speed Always Equals the Magnitude of Instantaneous Velocity

Instantaneous speed (or simply speed) is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity: \(\text{Speed} = |v| = \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|\). For example, velocities of \(+24.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(-24.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) both have an associated speed of \(24.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Although average speed over a finite time interval can be strictly greater than the magnitude of average velocity (if the particle reverses direction), at an instant (\(\Delta t \to 0\)), an infinitesimally small time interval allows no reversal of direction. Within \(\mathrm{d}t\), the particle moves strictly in one direction, so the infinitesimal path length \(\mathrm{d}s\) is identical to the magnitude of infinitesimal displacement \(|\mathrm{d}x|\). Consequently, instantaneous speed is always equal to the magnitude of instantaneous velocity.

2.4 Acceleration

When the velocity of an object changes during its course of motion, how should we describe this change—as the rate of change of velocity with distance or with time? Even in Galileo's time this was a central question. Through his studies of freely falling bodies and motion on inclined planes, Galileo concluded that the rate of change of velocity with time is a constant of motion for all objects in free fall, whereas the change in velocity with distance decreases as the distance of fall increases. This led to the definition of acceleration as the rate of change of velocity with time.

Average Acceleration (\(\bar{a}\)) over a time interval \(\Delta t = t_2 - t_1\):

\[\bar{a} = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\]

Instantaneous Acceleration (\(a\)) at an instant \(t\):

\[a = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2x}{\mathrm{d}t^2} = v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}\]

The SI unit of acceleration is \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and its dimensional formula is \([\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}]\). On a velocity-time (\(v\)-\(t\)) plot, the average acceleration is the slope of the straight line connecting \((t_1, v_1)\) and \((t_2, v_2)\), while the instantaneous acceleration is the slope of the tangent to the \(v\)-\(t\) curve at that instant.

Curvature of Position-Time (\(x\)-\(t\)) Graphs and Sign of Acceleration

Like velocity, acceleration in one dimension can be positive, negative, or zero:

  • Positive Acceleration (\(a > 0\)): The slope of the \(x\)-\(t\) graph (\(\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\)) increases with time, so the \(x\)-\(t\) graph curves upward (concave upward).
  • Negative Acceleration (\(a < 0\)): The slope of the \(x\)-\(t\) graph decreases with time, so the \(x\)-\(t\) graph curves downward (concave downward).
  • Zero Acceleration (\(a = 0\)): Velocity is constant, so the \(x\)-\(t\) graph is a straight line.

Velocity-Time (\(v\)-\(t\)) Graphs for Constant Acceleration

When acceleration \(a\) is constant, the average acceleration equals the instantaneous acceleration over any interval, and \(v = v_0 + at\). Thus, the \(v\)-\(t\) graph is a straight line inclined to the time axis. The four fundamental cases of constant-acceleration \(v\)-\(t\) graphs are illustrated below:

(a) Moving in +x direction with positive acceleration (v > 0, a > 0; speeding up).
(b) Moving in +x direction with negative acceleration (v > 0, a < 0; slowing down).
(c) Moving in −x direction with negative acceleration (v < 0, a < 0; speeding up in −x direction).
(d) Moving in +x direction until t₁ (where v = 0), then turning back in −x direction under the same negative acceleration.
Note on Realistic Motion vs. Sharp Kinks on Graphs

Idealized textbook graphs sometimes show sharp corners or kinks at certain points, which mathematically imply that the functions are not differentiable at those points. In any realistic physical situation, velocity and acceleration cannot change values abruptly at an instant—changes are always continuous, meaning realistic \(x\)-\(t\), \(v\)-\(t\), and \(a\)-\(t\) graphs are smooth everywhere.

2.5 Area Under the Velocity-Time Graph & Kinematic Equations

A fundamental property of any velocity-time (\(v\)-\(t\)) graph is that the area under the \(v\)-\(t\) curve between two instants represents the displacement over that time interval (with areas above the time axis taken as positive and areas below taken as negative; the sum of absolute areas gives the total path length). How can an area on a graph equal a distance? Because the vertical axis has dimensions of velocity (\([\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}]\)) and the horizontal axis has dimensions of time (\([\mathrm{T}]\)), so their product has dimensions \([\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}][\mathrm{T}] = [\mathrm{L}]\), which is displacement.

Graphical Derivation of Kinematic Equations for Constant Acceleration

Consider an object moving with constant acceleration \(a\), initial velocity \(v_0\) at \(t = 0\), and final velocity \(v\) at time \(t\):

  1. Velocity-Time Relation: From the definition of constant acceleration \(\bar{a} = a = \frac{v - v_0}{t - 0}\), we obtain: \[v = v_0 + at\]
  2. Position-Time Relation: From Tutorial Diagram 2b, the displacement \(x\) is the area under the \(v\)-\(t\) graph between \(0\) and \(t\): \[x = \text{Area of }\triangle\mathrm{ABC} + \text{Area of rectangle }\mathrm{OACD} = \frac{1}{2}(v - v_0)t + v_0 t\] Since \(v - v_0 = at\), substituting this into the above relation gives: \[x = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\] Notice also that \(x = \frac{1}{2}(v - v_0)t + v_0 t = \left(\frac{v + v_0}{2}\right)t = \bar{v}t\), where \[\bar{v} = \frac{v + v_0}{2} \quad \text{(valid for constant acceleration only)}\] This shows that for constant acceleration, the average velocity over any interval is simply the arithmetic mean of the initial and final velocities.
  3. Velocity-Displacement Relation: Substituting \(t = \frac{v - v_0}{a}\) into \(x = \bar{v}t = \left(\frac{v + v_0}{2}\right)t\): \[x = \left(\frac{v + v_0}{2}\right)\left(\frac{v - v_0}{a}\right) = \frac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2a} \implies v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax\]

If the initial position of the particle at \(t = 0\) is \(x_0\) instead of \(0\), we replace displacement \(x\) with \((x - x_0)\), giving the general kinematic equations:

\[v = v_0 + at\] \[x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\] \[v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\]

2.6 Relative Velocity in One Dimension

Motion is always relative to an observer (frame of reference). Consider two objects \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\) moving along the same straight line (\(x\)-axis) with uniform velocities \(v_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{B}}\) measured relative to the ground:

  • The velocity of object A relative to object B (\(v_{\mathrm{AB}}\)) is: \[v_{\mathrm{AB}} = v_{\mathrm{A}} - v_{\mathrm{B}}\]
  • The velocity of object B relative to object A (\(v_{\mathrm{BA}}\)) is: \[v_{\mathrm{BA}} = v_{\mathrm{B}} - v_{\mathrm{A}} = -v_{\mathrm{AB}}\]

When applying these formulas, always substitute \(v_{\mathrm{A}}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{B}}\) with their proper algebraic signs according to the chosen positive axis. Specifically, if \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\) move in the same direction, the magnitude of their relative velocity is the difference of their speeds (\(|v_{\mathrm{A}} - v_{\mathrm{B}}|\)); if they move in opposite directions, the magnitude of their relative velocity is the sum of their speeds (\(|v_{\mathrm{A}}| + |v_{\mathrm{B}}|\)).

Worked Example 1: Instantaneous vs. Average Velocity using Calculus

Problem: The position of an object moving along the \(x\)-axis is given by \(x = a + bt^2\), where \(a = 8.5\,\mathrm{m}\), \(b = 2.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and \(t\) is measured in seconds. What is its velocity at \(t = 0\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(t = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}\)? What is the average velocity between \(t = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(t = 4.0\,\mathrm{s}\)?

Solution:

In the notation of differential calculus, the instantaneous velocity is:

\[v = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(a + bt^2) = 2bt = 2(2.5)t = 5.0\,t\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]
  • At \(t = 0\,\mathrm{s}\): \(v = 5.0(0) = 0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
  • At \(t = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}\): \(v = 5.0(2.0) = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

The average velocity between \(t_1 = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(t_2 = 4.0\,\mathrm{s}\) is:

\[\bar{v} = \frac{x(4.0) - x(2.0)}{4.0 - 2.0} = \frac{(a + 16b) - (a + 4b)}{2.0} = \frac{12b}{2.0} = 6.0\,b = 6.0 \times 2.5 = 15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

Check: Since \(x\) is quadratic in \(t\), acceleration is constant (\(a_{\mathrm{acc}} = 2b = 5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)). Thus \(\bar{v} = \frac{v(2.0) + v(4.0)}{2} = \frac{10 + 20}{2} = 15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Worked Example 2: Derivation of Kinematic Equations via Calculus

Problem: Obtain the three equations of motion for constant acceleration using the method of integral calculus.

Solution:

1. First Equation (\(v = v_0 + at\)): By definition, \(a = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} \implies \mathrm{d}v = a\,\mathrm{d}t\). Integrating both sides from initial state \((t = 0, v = v_0)\) to final state \((t, v)\) for constant \(a\):

\[\int_{v_0}^{v} \mathrm{d}v = \int_{0}^{t} a\,\mathrm{d}t = a\int_{0}^{t} \mathrm{d}t \implies v - v_0 = at \implies v = v_0 + at\]

2. Second Equation (\(x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)): By definition, \(v = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} \implies \mathrm{d}x = v\,\mathrm{d}t\). Integrating from \((t = 0, x = x_0)\) to \((t, x)\):

\[\int_{x_0}^{x} \mathrm{d}x = \int_{0}^{t} v\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_{0}^{t} (v_0 + at)\,\mathrm{d}t \implies x - x_0 = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2 \implies x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\]

3. Third Equation (\(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\)): Using the chain rule, \(a = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} \implies v\,\mathrm{d}v = a\,\mathrm{d}x\). Integrating from \((x_0, v_0)\) to \((x, v)\):

\[\int_{v_0}^{v} v\,\mathrm{d}v = \int_{x_0}^{x} a\,\mathrm{d}x \implies \frac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2} = a(x - x_0) \implies v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\]

Key Advantage: Unlike the graphical method, the integral forms \(\int \mathrm{d}v = \int a(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\) and \(\int v\,\mathrm{d}v = \int a(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) can be used for non-uniform acceleration as well.

Worked Example 3: Vertical Projection from the Top of a Building

Problem: A ball is thrown vertically upwards with a velocity of \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) from the top of a multistorey building. The height of the point from where the ball is thrown is \(25.0\,\mathrm{m}\) from the ground. (a) How high will the ball rise? (b) How long will it be before the ball hits the ground? Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

Tutorial Diagram 3: Vertical projection of a ball from a 25 m building (A) to highest point (B) and ground (C).

Solution:

Take the \(y\)-axis in the vertically upward direction with the origin (\(y = 0\)) at the ground.

(a) Maximum height risen above the launch point:
Given \(v_0 = +20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(a = -g = -10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and at the highest point \(\mathrm{B}\), \(v = 0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Using \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(y - y_0)\):

\[0 = (20)^2 + 2(-10)(y - y_0) \implies y - y_0 = \frac{400}{20} = 20\,\mathrm{m}\]

Thus, the ball rises \(20\,\mathrm{m}\) above the top of the building (reaching a height \(y = 25 + 20 = 45\,\mathrm{m}\) above the ground).

(b) Total time before hitting the ground:

  • First Method (Splitting the path into upward A→B and downward B→C):
    For upward motion \(\mathrm{A \to B}\), \(v = v_0 + at_1 \implies 0 = 20 - 10t_1 \implies t_1 = 2\,\mathrm{s}\).
    From maximum height \(\mathrm{B}\) (\(y_0 = 45\,\mathrm{m}\), \(v_0 = 0\)) to ground \(\mathrm{C}\) (\(y = 0\)): \[y = y_0 + v_0 t_2 + \frac{1}{2}at_2^2 \implies 0 = 45 + 0 + \frac{1}{2}(-10)t_2^2 \implies 5t_2^2 = 45 \implies t_2 = 3\,\mathrm{s}\] Total time \(t = t_1 + t_2 = 2\,\mathrm{s} + 3\,\mathrm{s} = 5\,\mathrm{s}\).
  • Second Method (Direct algebraic displacement from A to C — Recommended for NEET):
    Since acceleration is constant throughout the entire flight, apply \(y = y_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\) directly between initial position \(y_0 = 25\,\mathrm{m}\) and final ground position \(y = 0\,\mathrm{m}\): \[0 = 25 + 20t + \frac{1}{2}(-10)t^2 \implies 5t^2 - 20t - 25 = 0 \implies t^2 - 4t - 5 = 0\] \[(t - 5)(t + 1) = 0 \implies t = 5\,\mathrm{s} \quad (\text{since } t > 0)\] The second method is much faster because we do not need to split the path when acceleration is constant.

Worked Example 4: Motion of an Object Under Free Fall & Graphical Representation

Problem: Discuss the motion of an object released from rest under free fall near the Earth's surface (neglecting air resistance) and plot the variations of acceleration, velocity, and position with time.

Solution:

An object released near the Earth's surface is accelerated downward under gravity with magnitude \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Worked Example 6: Stopping Distance of Vehicles & Highway Retardation

Problem: (a) Derive an expression for the stopping distance \(d_s\) of a vehicle moving with initial velocity \(v_0\) when brakes apply a uniform deceleration \(a\) (where \(a < 0\)). (b) A car moving along a straight highway with a speed of \(126\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) is brought to a stop within a distance of \(200\,\mathrm{m}\). What is the uniform retardation of the car, and how long does it take for the car to stop?

Solution:

(a) Stopping Distance Formula: When the vehicle comes to a complete stop, its final velocity is \(v = 0\). Using \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax\) with \(x = d_s\):

\[0 = v_0^2 + 2a d_s \implies d_s = \frac{-v_0^2}{2a}\]

Since \(d_s \propto v_0^2\) for a given deceleration, doubling the initial velocity increases the stopping distance by a factor of \(4\), and tripling the initial velocity increases it by a factor of \(9\). (For instance, experimental braking distances of \(10\,\mathrm{m}\), \(20\,\mathrm{m}\), \(34\,\mathrm{m}\), and \(50\,\mathrm{m}\) for speeds of \(11\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) closely follow \(d_s \propto v_0^2\)).

(b) Highway Car Calculation:
Convert initial speed to SI units: \(v_0 = 126\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}} = 126 \times \frac{5}{18}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 35\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
Given stopping distance \(d_s = 200\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(v = 0\):

\[a = \frac{-v_0^2}{2d_s} = \frac{-(35)^2}{2 \times 200} = \frac{-1225}{400} = -3.0625\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \approx -3.06\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\]

Thus, the uniform retardation is \(3.06\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The time taken to stop is found from \(v = v_0 + at\):

\[t = \frac{0 - v_0}{a} = \frac{-35}{-3.0625} = \frac{80}{7}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 11.43\,\mathrm{s}\]

Worked Example 7: Reaction Time Measurement

Problem: Reaction time is the time a person takes to observe, think, and act. In a simple experiment, a friend holds a ruler vertically above the gap between your thumb and forefinger and drops it without warning. You catch it after it has fallen a vertical distance \(d = 21.0\,\mathrm{cm}\). Estimate your reaction time \(t_r\) (take \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

Solution:

The ruler drops from rest (\(v_0 = 0\)) under free fall (\(a = -g = -9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)). Taking the downward distance fallen as \(d = 21.0\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.21\,\mathrm{m}\), the magnitude of displacement is:

\[d = \frac{1}{2}gt_r^2 \implies t_r = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 0.21}{9.8}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.42}{9.8}} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{70}}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 0.207\,\mathrm{s} \approx 0.2\,\mathrm{s}\]

Worked Example 8: Average Velocity vs. Average Speed in Round-Trip Motion

Problem: A man walks on a straight road from his home to a market \(2.5\,\mathrm{km}\) away with a speed of \(5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\). Finding the market closed, he instantly turns and walks back home with a speed of \(7.5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\). What is the (a) magnitude of average velocity, and (b) average speed of the man over the time interval: (i) \(0\) to \(30\,\mathrm{min}\), (ii) \(0\) to \(50\,\mathrm{min}\), and (iii) \(0\) to \(40\,\mathrm{min}\)?

Solution:

Time taken to reach the market (\(2.5\,\mathrm{km}\) at \(5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\)): \(t_1 = \frac{2.5\,\mathrm{km}}{5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}} = 0.5\,\mathrm{h} = 30\,\mathrm{min}\).
Time taken to walk back home (\(2.5\,\mathrm{km}\) at \(7.5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\)): \(t_2 = \frac{2.5\,\mathrm{km}}{7.5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}} = \frac{1}{3}\,\mathrm{h} = 20\,\mathrm{min}\).
Total time for the round trip \(= 30 + 20 = 50\,\mathrm{min} = \frac{5}{6}\,\mathrm{h}\).

  • (i) Interval \(0\) to \(30\,\mathrm{min}\) (\(0.5\,\mathrm{h}\)): The man walks straight from home to the market without turning.
    Displacement \(= 2.5\,\mathrm{km}\), Path length \(= 2.5\,\mathrm{km}\).
    Magnitude of average velocity \(= \frac{2.5\,\mathrm{km}}{0.5\,\mathrm{h}} = 5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\).
    Average speed \(= \frac{2.5\,\mathrm{km}}{0.5\,\mathrm{h}} = 5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\).
  • (ii) Interval \(0\) to \(50\,\mathrm{min}\) (\(\frac{5}{6}\,\mathrm{h}\)): The man returns to his starting point (home).
    Net displacement \(= 0\,\mathrm{km}\), Total path length \(= 2.5 + 2.5 = 5.0\,\mathrm{km}\).
    Magnitude of average velocity \(= \frac{0}{5/6\,\mathrm{h}} = 0\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\).
    Average speed \(= \frac{5.0\,\mathrm{km}}{5/6\,\mathrm{h}} = 6.0\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\).
  • (iii) Interval \(0\) to \(40\,\mathrm{min}\) (\(\frac{2}{3}\,\mathrm{h}\)): In the first \(30\,\mathrm{min}\), he covers \(2.5\,\mathrm{km}\) forward. In the next \(10\,\mathrm{min}\) (\(\frac{1}{6}\,\mathrm{h}\)), he walks back toward home a distance of \(7.5\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}} \times \frac{1}{6}\,\mathrm{h} = 1.25\,\mathrm{km}\).
    Net displacement \(= 2.5 - 1.25 = 1.25\,\mathrm{km}\); Total path length \(= 2.5 + 1.25 = 3.75\,\mathrm{km}\).
    Magnitude of average velocity \(= \frac{1.25\,\mathrm{km}}{2/3\,\mathrm{h}} = 1.875\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\).
    Average speed \(= \frac{3.75\,\mathrm{km}}{2/3\,\mathrm{h}} = 5.625\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\).

Worked Example 9: Forward-and-Backward Step Motion (The Drunkard Problem)

Problem: A drunkard walking in a narrow lane takes \(5\) steps forward and \(3\) steps backward, followed again by \(5\) steps forward and \(3\) steps backward, and so on. Each step is \(1\,\mathrm{m}\) long and requires \(1\,\mathrm{s}\). Determine how long the drunkard takes to fall into a pit \(13\,\mathrm{m}\) away from the start.

Solution:

In one complete cycle of \(5\) steps forward and \(3\) steps backward:

  • Time taken per cycle \(= 5\,\mathrm{s} + 3\,\mathrm{s} = 8\,\mathrm{s}\).
  • Net forward displacement per cycle \(= 5\,\mathrm{m} - 3\,\mathrm{m} = 2\,\mathrm{m}\).

Crucial Trap to Avoid: Once the drunkard reaches \(x = 13\,\mathrm{m}\), he falls into the pit and does not take backward steps! Since his final forward run covers \(5\,\mathrm{m}\), we must first find the time taken to reach \(13\,\mathrm{m} - 5\,\mathrm{m} = 8\,\mathrm{m}\).

  • Number of cycles to reach \(x = 8\,\mathrm{m}\) is \(\frac{8\,\mathrm{m}}{2\,\mathrm{m/cycle}} = 4\text{ cycles}\).
  • Time for \(4\) cycles \(= 4 \times 8\,\mathrm{s} = 32\,\mathrm{s}\) (at \(t = 32\,\mathrm{s}\), he is at \(x = 8\,\mathrm{m}\)).
  • Next, he takes \(5\) steps forward (\(5\,\mathrm{m}\) in \(5\,\mathrm{s}\)), reaching \(x = 8\,\mathrm{m} + 5\,\mathrm{m} = 13\,\mathrm{m}\) at time: \[t = 32\,\mathrm{s} + 5\,\mathrm{s} = 37\,\mathrm{s}\]

Thus, the drunkard falls into the pit at \(t = 37\,\mathrm{s}\).

Worked Example 10: Relative Velocity of a Bullet Hitting a Moving Car

Problem: A police van moving on a highway with a speed of \(30\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) fires a bullet at a thief's car speeding away in the same direction with a speed of \(192\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\). If the muzzle speed of the bullet (relative to the police van) is \(150\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), with what speed does the bullet hit the thief's car?

Solution:

Convert all vehicle speeds to \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (multiplying \(\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) by \(\frac{5}{18}\)):

  • Speed of police van relative to ground: \(v_{\mathrm{P}} = 30 \times \frac{5}{18} = \frac{25}{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
  • Speed of thief's car relative to ground: \(v_{\mathrm{T}} = 192 \times \frac{5}{18} = \frac{160}{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
  • Muzzle speed of bullet relative to police van: \(v_{\mathrm{BP}} = 150\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Since the bullet is fired in the forward direction from the moving police van, the speed of the bullet relative to the ground is:

\[v_{\mathrm{B}} = v_{\mathrm{BP}} + v_{\mathrm{P}} = 150 + \frac{25}{3} = \frac{475}{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

The speed relevant for damaging the thief's car is the relative speed of the bullet with respect to the thief's car (\(v_{\mathrm{BT}}\)):

\[v_{\mathrm{BT}} = v_{\mathrm{B}} - v_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{475}{3} - \frac{160}{3} = \frac{315}{3} = 105\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 2 [Differential Calculus — Instantaneous Velocity & Acceleration]

The position \(x\) (in metres) of a particle moving along a straight line varies with time \(t\) (in seconds) as \(x(t) = 2t^3 - 12t^2 + 18t + 5\). What is the acceleration of the particle at the instants when its instantaneous velocity becomes zero?

  • (A) \(0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(+12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (B) \(-12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(+12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (C) \(-6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(+6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (D) \(-24\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(+24\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: Differentiating \(x(t)\) with respect to \(t\) gives instantaneous velocity: \[v(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = 6t^2 - 24t + 18 = 6(t^2 - 4t + 3) = 6(t - 1)(t - 3)\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\] Setting \(v(t) = 0\) gives \(t = 1\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\). At \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\), \(a(3) = 12(3) - 24 = +12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Notice that even though velocity is momentarily zero at \(t = 1\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(t = 3\,\mathrm{s}\), acceleration is non-zero!
Practice Question 3 [Chain Rule \(a = v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}\) — Velocity as a Function of Position]

A particle moves along the positive \(x\)-axis such that its time coordinate \(t\) varies with position \(x\) according to the relation \(t = \alpha x^2 + \beta x\), where \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are positive constants. If \(v\) is the instantaneous velocity of the particle, its acceleration \(a\) is given by:

  • (A) \(-2\alpha v^3\)
  • (B) \(-2\alpha v^2\)
  • (C) \(-2\beta v^3\)
  • (D) \(+\alpha v^3\)
Correct Answer: (A)
Worked Solution: Differentiate \(t = \alpha x^2 + \beta x\) with respect to \(x\): \[\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}x} = 2\alpha x + \beta \implies \frac{1}{v} = 2\alpha x + \beta \implies v = (2\alpha x + \beta)^{-1}\] Now use \(a = v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}\): \[\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} = -(2\alpha x + \beta)^{-2}(2\alpha) = -2\alpha v^2\] Therefore, \(a = v(-2\alpha v^2) = -2\alpha v^3\).

For a particle executing one-dimensional simple harmonic motion along the \(x\)-axis with time period \(T = 2.0\,\mathrm{s}\), its position-time (\(x\)-\(t\)) graph is sinusoidal: starting at \(x = 0\) at \(t = 0\), moving to negative \(x\) for \(0 < t < 1.0\,\mathrm{s}\) (reaching minimum at \(t = 0.5\,\mathrm{s}\)), and moving to positive \(x\) for \(1.0\,\mathrm{s} < t < 2.0\,\mathrm{s}\) (reaching maximum at \(t = 1.5\,\mathrm{s}\)).

Assertion (A): At \(t = 0.3\,\mathrm{s}\), the position \(x\) is negative, the velocity \(v\) is negative, and the acceleration \(a\) is positive; whereas at \(t = 1.2\,\mathrm{s}\), \(x\) is positive, \(v\) is positive, and \(a\) is negative.

Reason (R): On an \(x\)-\(t\) graph, the sign of velocity is given by the sign of the slope of the tangent (\(\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\)), and the sign of acceleration is given by the concavity of the curve (\(\frac{\mathrm{d}^2x}{\mathrm{d}t^2} > 0\) if curved upward, \(\frac{\mathrm{d}^2x}{\mathrm{d}t^2} < 0\) if curved downward).

  • (A) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, and Reason (R) is the correct explanation of Assertion (A).
  • (B) Both Assertion (A) and Reason (R) are true, but Reason (R) is not the correct explanation of Assertion (A).
  • (C) Assertion (A) is true, but Reason (R) is false.
  • (D) Assertion (A) is false, but Reason (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)
Worked Solution:
At \(t = 0.3\,\mathrm{s}\) (between \(0\) and \(0.5\,\mathrm{s}\)): the curve lies below the \(t\)-axis (\(x < 0\)), slopes downward (\(v = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} < 0\)), and is concave upward near the trough (\(a = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2x}{\mathrm{d}t^2} > 0\); also in SHM, \(a = -\omega^2 x > 0\) when \(x < 0\)).
At \(t = 1.2\,\mathrm{s}\) (between \(1.0\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(1.5\,\mathrm{s}\)): the curve lies above the \(t\)-axis (\(x > 0\)), slopes upward (\(v > 0\)), and is concave downward near the crest (\(a < 0\)). Thus both A and R are true, and R directly explains A.

Practice Question 13 [Repeated Inelastic Bouncing Under Gravity]

A ball is dropped from rest from a height of \(90\,\mathrm{m}\) onto a horizontal floor (take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)). At each collision with the floor, the ball loses one-tenth (\(\frac{1}{10}\)) of its speed. What is the speed of the ball just after its first collision with the floor, and at what time \(t\) after being dropped does it reach the highest point of its first rebound?

  • (A) \(27\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(5.7\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (B) \(30\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(6.0\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (C) \(27\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(3.0\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (D) \(24\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(5.4\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{s}\)
Correct Answer: (A)
Worked Solution:
1. Time to fall \(h = 90\,\mathrm{m}\) from rest: \[t_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 90}{10}} = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{s}\] 2. Speed just before the first collision: \[v_1 = gt_1 = 10 \times 3\sqrt{2} = 30\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\] 3. Since the ball loses \(\frac{1}{10}\) of its speed on impact, its upward rebound speed is \(\frac{9}{10}v_1\): \[v_{\text{rebound}} = 0.9 \times 30\sqrt{2} = 27\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\] 4. Time to rise from the floor to the peak of the first rebound: \[t_2 = \frac{v_{\text{rebound}}}{g} = \frac{27\sqrt{2}}{10} = 2.7\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{s}\] Total time from release to the first rebound peak \(= t_1 + t_2 = 3\sqrt{2} + 2.7\sqrt{2} = 5.7\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{s}\) (or using \(g = 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(t_1 = \frac{30}{7}\,\mathrm{s} \approx 4.29\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(t_1 + t_2 = 1.9 \times 4.29 \approx 8.14\,\mathrm{s}\)).
Practice Question 14 [Relative Motion — Overtaking of Two Trains]

Two trains \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\), each of length \(200\,\mathrm{m}\), are moving on parallel tracks in the same direction with uniform speeds of \(30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), respectively. Initially, the front of train \(\mathrm{A}\) is just behind the rear of train \(\mathrm{B}\). How much time does train \(\mathrm{A}\) take to completely overtake train \(\mathrm{B}\)?

  • (A) \(20\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (B) \(40\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (C) \(8\,\mathrm{s}\)
  • (D) \(10\,\mathrm{s}\)
Correct Answer: (B)
Worked Solution:
Relative velocity of train \(\mathrm{A}\) with respect to train \(\mathrm{B}\): \[v_{\mathrm{AB}} = v_{\mathrm{A}} - v_{\mathrm{B}} = 30 - 20 = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\] To completely overtake train \(\mathrm{B}\) from rear-to-front alignment, train \(\mathrm{A}\) must cover a relative distance equal to the sum of the lengths of both trains: \[d_{\mathrm{rel}} = L_{\mathrm{A}} + L_{\mathrm{B}} = 200\,\mathrm{m} + 200\,\mathrm{m} = 400\,\mathrm{m}\] Time required \(= \frac{d_{\mathrm{rel}}}{v_{\mathrm{AB}}} = \frac{400\,\mathrm{m}}{10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}} = 40\,\mathrm{s}\).

6. Points to Ponder, Common Pitfalls & Final Revision Recap

Essential Points to Ponder (High-Yield NEET Traps)
  1. Choice of Origin and Axis Direction: The origin and the positive direction of an axis are a matter of choice. Always specify this choice first before assigning algebraic signs (\(+\) or \(-\)) to position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration.
  2. Speeding Up vs. Slowing Down: If a particle is speeding up, its acceleration is in the direction of its velocity (\(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign, \(v \cdot a > 0\)). If its speed is decreasing, its acceleration is in the direction opposite to its velocity (\(v\) and \(a\) have opposite signs, \(v \cdot a < 0\)). This physical truth is completely independent of the choice of origin and axis!
  3. Sign of Acceleration Alone Does Not Indicate Speeding Up: A negative acceleration (\(a < 0\)) does not necessarily mean retardation (slowing down). For example, if upward is chosen as \(+y\), gravity gives \(a = -g < 0\). For a ball thrown upward (\(v > 0\)), this negative acceleration decreases speed; for a ball falling downward (\(v < 0\)), the exact same negative acceleration increases speed!
  4. Zero Velocity Does Not Imply Zero Acceleration: A particle may be momentarily at rest (\(v = 0\)) and yet have a non-zero acceleration (\(a \neq 0\)), such as a vertically projected ball at the highest point of its path (\(v = 0\), \(a = -g\)).
  5. Exact Definitions vs. Constant-Acceleration Equations: The calculus definitions \(v = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\) and \(a = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}\) are exact and universally valid for all motion. In contrast, the kinematic equations (\(v = v_0 + at\), \(x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\), \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)\), and \(\bar{v} = \frac{v_0 + v}{2}\)) are valid only when acceleration is constant in both magnitude and direction.

Concise Formula Checklist for Quick Revision

  • Average Velocity & Speed: \(\bar{v} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\), \(\quad v_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Path Length}}{\Delta t} \ge |\bar{v}|\).
  • Instantaneous Velocity & Speed: \(v = \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \text{slope of } x\text{-}t\text{ graph}\), \(\quad \text{Instantaneous speed} = |v|\).
  • Instantaneous Acceleration: \(a = \frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2x}{\mathrm{d}t^2} = v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x} = \text{slope of } v\text{-}t\text{ graph}\).
  • Area under \(v\)-\(t\) Graph: \(\Delta x = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v\,\mathrm{d}t\) (algebraic area = displacement; sum of absolute areas = distance).
  • Stopping Distance & Reaction Time: \(d_s = \frac{-v_0^2}{2a} \propto v_0^2\); \(\quad t_r = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}}\).
  • Galileo's Law of Odd Numbers (from rest): \(s_1 : s_2 : s_3 : s_4 : \dots = 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 : \dots\)
  • 1D Relative Velocity: \(v_{\mathrm{AB}} = v_{\mathrm{A}} - v_{\mathrm{B}} = -v_{\mathrm{BA}}\).
