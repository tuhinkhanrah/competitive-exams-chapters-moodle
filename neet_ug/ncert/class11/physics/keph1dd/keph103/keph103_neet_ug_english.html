Motion in a Plane: Vectors, Projectile Motion, and Uniform Circular Motion

A complete conceptual, mathematical, and exam-oriented tutorial for two-dimensional kinematics.

1. Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

Along a straight line, only two directions exist, so simple positive and negative signs suffice to describe position, velocity, and acceleration. When an object moves on a flat surface (a two-dimensional plane) or in three-dimensional space, direction becomes continuous. To model such motion accurately, we must use vectors and resolve two-dimensional motion into independent perpendicular components.

Learning Objectives
  • Distinguish cleanly between scalar and vector quantities, including common exam traps such as electric current and pressure.
  • Add, subtract, and resolve vectors both graphically (triangle and parallelogram laws) and analytically (orthogonal components and unit vectors).
  • Apply calculus and vector kinematics to motion in a plane with constant acceleration.
  • Analyze oblique and horizontal projectile motion using the independence of horizontal and vertical motion.
  • Describe uniform circular motion in terms of angular speed, linear speed, and centripetal acceleration.

2. Scalars and Vectors

2.1 Core Distinction

Physical quantities are broadly grouped into scalars and vectors based on whether directional geometry governs how they combine:

  • Scalar quantity: Completely specified by a magnitude (a real number with an appropriate unit) and follows ordinary algebra. Examples include mass, distance, speed, temperature, density, work, energy, power, charge, and electric current.
  • Vector quantity: Possesses both magnitude and direction, and obeys the triangle law (equivalently, the parallelogram law) of vector addition. Examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, linear momentum, impulse, and angular velocity.
NEET UG Concept Check: Direction Alone Is Not Enough

Having a direction does not automatically make a quantity a vector. Electric current has both magnitude and a direction of flow along a wire, yet it is a scalar because currents at a junction add algebraically rather than by the parallelogram law. Similarly, pressure is a scalar.

2.2 Position, Displacement, Equality, and Scaling

To locate a particle \(P\) in a plane relative to an origin \(O\), we draw the position vector \(\mathbf{r} = \overrightarrow{OP}\). If the particle moves from \(P\) (at time \(t_1\), position \(\mathbf{r}_1\)) to \(P'\) (at time \(t_2\), position \(\mathbf{r}_2\)), the displacement vector is the straight arrow from \(P\) to \(P'\):

\[\Delta \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1\]

Displacement depends only on the initial and final endpoints, never on the actual curved path followed. Consequently, the path length (distance) is always greater than or equal to the magnitude of displacement: \(\text{Path length} \ge |\Delta\mathbf{r}|\), with equality holding only when the particle moves in a straight line without reversing direction.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Position vectors \(\mathbf{r}_1\), \(\mathbf{r}_2\) and the displacement vector \(\Delta\mathbf{r}\), which is independent of the curved path taken between \(P\) and \(P'\).
  • Equality of vectors: Two free vectors \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are equal (\(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{B}\)) if and only if they have the same magnitude and the same direction. Shifting a free vector parallel to itself leaves it unchanged.
  • Multiplication by a real number \(\lambda\): Multiplying \(\mathbf{A}\) by \(\lambda > 0\) scales its magnitude by \(\lambda\) while preserving direction. Multiplying by \(-\lambda\) (\(\lambda > 0\)) scales its magnitude by \(\lambda\) and reverses its direction by \(180^\circ\).
  • Null (zero) vector \(\mathbf{0}\): A vector of zero magnitude (\(|\mathbf{0}| = 0\)) with an arbitrary/unspecified direction, satisfying \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{0}\).

3. Vector Addition, Subtraction, and Resolution

3.1 Graphical and Analytical Addition

Two vectors \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) can be added graphically using the triangle law (placing the tail of \(\mathbf{B}\) at the head of \(\mathbf{A}\) and joining the tail of \(\mathbf{A}\) to the head of \(\mathbf{B}\)) or equivalently the parallelogram law (placing both tails at a common origin and taking the diagonal from that origin). Vector addition is both commutative and associative:

\[\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{A} \qquad \text{and} \qquad (\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}) + \mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A} + (\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{C})\]

If \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) have magnitudes \(A\) and \(B\) with an angle \(\theta\) between them (tail-to-tail), the magnitude \(R\) of the resultant \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\) and the angle \(\alpha\) that \(\mathbf{R}\) makes with \(\mathbf{A}\) are given by:

\[R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + 2AB\cos\theta} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \tan\alpha = \frac{B\sin\theta}{A + B\cos\theta}\]

In addition, the Law of Sines relates the sides and angles of the vector triangle: \(\dfrac{R}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{A}{\sin\beta} = \dfrac{B}{\sin\alpha}\), where \(\beta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\).

Tutorial Diagram 2: Parallelogram law of vector addition showing resultant \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\), angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\), and angle \(\alpha\) made by \(\mathbf{R}\) with \(\mathbf{A}\).

3.2 Subtraction and Triangle Inequalities

Vector subtraction is defined as adding the negative vector: \(\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A} + (-\mathbf{B})\). Replacing \(\theta\) with \(180^\circ - \theta\) in the cosine formula gives:

\[|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}| = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2 - 2AB\cos\theta}\]

From the geometry of vector triangles, the resultant of two vectors always satisfies the triangle inequalities:

\[|A - B| \le |\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}| \le A + B \qquad \text{and} \qquad |A - B| \le |\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}| \le A + B\]

Notice that for \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\), the maximum magnitude \(A + B\) occurs at \(\theta = 0^\circ\) and the minimum magnitude \(|A - B|\) occurs at \(\theta = 180^\circ\).

3.3 Unit Vectors and Rectangular Components

A unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) has magnitude \(1\), no dimensions, and no units; it specifies direction only. Any non-zero vector \(\mathbf{A}\) can be written as \(\mathbf{A} = A\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{A}/|\mathbf{A}|\). Along the mutually perpendicular \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-axes, the standard unit vectors are \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), and \(\hat{\mathbf{k}}\).

In the \(x\)-\(y\) plane, if \(\mathbf{A}\) makes an angle \(\theta\) with the positive \(x\)-axis:

\[\mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + A_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}, \quad \text{where } A_x = A\cos\theta, \quad A_y = A\sin\theta\] \[A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x}\]

In three dimensions, \(\mathbf{A} = A_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + A_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} + A_z\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) with \(A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}\). Using components makes adding or subtracting any number of vectors effortless: simply add or subtract corresponding \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-components independently.

4. Kinematics in a Plane

4.1 Velocity and Acceleration in Two Dimensions

For a particle at \(\mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y(t)\hat{\mathbf{j}}\):

  • Average velocity over interval \(\Delta t\): \(\overline{\mathbf{v}} = \dfrac{\Delta\mathbf{r}}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), directed along \(\Delta\mathbf{r}\).
  • Instantaneous velocity: \(\mathbf{v} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \dfrac{\Delta\mathbf{r}}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t} = v_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + v_y\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), where \(v_x = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\) and \(v_y = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t}\). Crucially, \(\mathbf{v}\) is always tangent to the path at every point.
  • Average and instantaneous acceleration: \[\overline{\mathbf{a}} = \frac{\Delta\mathbf{v}}{\Delta t}, \qquad \mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t} = a_x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + a_y\hat{\mathbf{j}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 x}{\mathrm{d}t^2}\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 y}{\mathrm{d}t^2}\hat{\mathbf{j}}\]

Unlike one-dimensional motion where \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\) are restricted to the same line (\(0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\)), in two or three dimensions the angle between \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\) can take any value between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\).

4.2 Motion with Constant Acceleration

When \(\mathbf{a}\) is constant in both magnitude and direction, the vector kinematic equations hold:

\[\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2\]

Resolving these into \(x\)- and \(y\)-components reveals a powerful principle: motion in a plane with constant acceleration is equivalent to two independent, simultaneous one-dimensional motions along perpendicular axes:

\[x = x_0 + v_{0x}t + \frac{1}{2}a_x t^2, \qquad v_x = v_{0x} + a_x t\]

\[y = y_0 + v_{0y}t + \frac{1}{2}a_y t^2, \qquad v_y = v_{0y} + a_y t\]

5. Projectile Motion

5.1 Independence of Horizontal and Vertical Motion

A projectile is an object launched into flight and moving solely under gravity (neglecting air resistance). Taking the launch point as the origin \((0,0)\), the positive \(x\)-axis horizontally forward, and the positive \(y\)-axis vertically upward, the acceleration components are:

\[a_x = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad a_y = -g\]

If the projectile is launched with speed \(v_0\) at angle \(\theta_0\) above the horizontal, its initial velocity components are \(v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta_0\) and \(v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta_0\). At any time \(t\):

\[x(t) = (v_0\cos\theta_0)t, \qquad v_x(t) = v_0\cos\theta_0 \quad (\text{constant})\]

\[y(t) = (v_0\sin\theta_0)t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2, \qquad v_y(t) = v_0\sin\theta_0 - gt\]

Tutorial Diagram 3: Parabolic path of a projectile launched at angle \(\theta_0\). At the highest point, \(v_y = 0\) while \(v_x = v_0\cos\theta_0\) and downward acceleration \(g\) persist.

5.2 Equation of Trajectory and Key Formulas

Eliminating \(t = \dfrac{x}{v_0\cos\theta_0}\) yields the equation of the trajectory, which is a parabola opening downward:

\[y = (\tan\theta_0)x - \frac{g}{2(v_0\cos\theta_0)^2}x^2\]
Projectile Parameter Condition Used Formula (Level Ground)
Time to maximum height (\(t_m\)) \(v_y = 0\) \(t_m = \dfrac{v_0\sin\theta_0}{g}\)
Total time of flight (\(T_f\)) \(y = 0\) (for \(t > 0\)) \(T_f = 2t_m = \dfrac{2v_0\sin\theta_0}{g}\)
Maximum height (\(H_m\)) \(t = t_m\) \(H_m = \dfrac{(v_0\sin\theta_0)^2}{2g}\)
Horizontal range (\(R\)) \(x = v_{0x}T_f\) \(R = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta_0}{g}\)
Maximum range (\(R_{\max}\)) \(\theta_0 = 45^\circ\) \(R_{\max} = \dfrac{v_0^2}{g}\)
High-Yield NEET UG Projectile Identities
  • Complementary angles: For a given launch speed \(v_0\), angles \(\theta_0\) and \((90^\circ - \theta_0)\) — or equivalently \(45^\circ + \alpha\) and \(45^\circ - \alpha\) — yield the exact same horizontal range \(R\), because \(\sin 2(90^\circ - \theta_0) = \sin 2\theta_0\).
  • Range-to-height ratio: \(\dfrac{R}{H_m} = \dfrac{4}{\tan\theta_0}\), or \(R\tan\theta_0 = 4H_m\).
  • Horizontal projection from height \(h\): If an object is thrown horizontally (\(v_{0y} = 0\)) from height \(h\), the time to reach the ground is \(t = \sqrt{2h/g}\) (independent of \(v_{0x}\)) and the horizontal distance covered is \(x = v_{0x}\sqrt{2h/g}\).

6. Uniform Circular Motion (UCM)

When an object moves along a circular path of radius \(r\) at a constant speed \(v\), its motion is called uniform circular motion. Even though the speed \(v\) is constant, the velocity vector \(\mathbf{v}\) changes direction continuously along the tangent, so the object undergoes a continuous acceleration.

Tutorial Diagram 4: In uniform circular motion, velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) is tangential and centripetal acceleration \(\mathbf{a}_c\) points radially inward toward center \(C\).

By similar triangles for a small angular displacement \(\Delta\theta\), the magnitude of this centripetal acceleration is \(a_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r}\), and its direction is always radially inward toward the center of the circle. Using angular speed \(\omega = \dfrac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}\) (related to linear speed by \(v = \omega r\)), time period \(T\), and frequency \(\nu = \dfrac{1}{T}\):

\[\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi\nu, \qquad v = \omega r = 2\pi\nu r\] \[a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r = 4\pi^2\nu^2 r\]
Crucial Restriction on Kinematic Equations

In UCM, the magnitude of \(\mathbf{a}_c\) is constant, but its direction changes continuously as the particle moves around the circle. Therefore, \(\mathbf{a}_c\) is not a constant vector, and the constant-acceleration equations \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \mathbf{a}t\) and \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2\) do not apply to UCM.

7. Worked Examples (Original Scenarios)

All numerical values in the examples below are chosen as hypothetical illustrative assumptions to demonstrate problem-solving techniques.

Worked Example 1: Vector Addition in a Crosswind

Problem: A delivery drone flies due North at a steady airspeed of \(12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). A horizontal wind blows from West to East at \(5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Find the magnitude and direction of the drone's resultant velocity relative to the ground.

Solution: Let East be the \(+x\)-axis (\(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\)) and North be the \(+y\)-axis (\(\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)). The velocity vectors are \(\mathbf{v}_d = 12\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_w = 5.0\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

The resultant velocity is \(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_d + \mathbf{v}_w = 5.0\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 12\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Its magnitude is:

\[v = \sqrt{5.0^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

The angle \(\theta\) made with the North direction (towards East) satisfies:

\[\tan\theta = \frac{v_w}{v_d} = \frac{5.0}{12} \implies \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{5}{12}\right) \approx 22.6^\circ \text{ East of North}\]

Worked Example 2: 2D Motion with Constant Acceleration

Problem: A small puck starts from the origin at \(t = 0\) with initial velocity \(\mathbf{v}_0 = 4.0\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and moves in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane under a constant acceleration \(\mathbf{a} = (2.0\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3.0\hat{\mathbf{j}})\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). (a) Find its \(y\)-coordinate when its \(x\)-coordinate is \(21\,\mathrm{m}\). (b) Find its speed at that instant.

Solution:

(a) Using \(x(t) = v_{0x}t + \frac{1}{2}a_x t^2\):

\[21 = 4.0t + \frac{1}{2}(2.0)t^2 \implies t^2 + 4t - 21 = 0 \implies (t + 7)(t - 3) = 0\]

Taking the physically meaningful positive time, \(t = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}\). Now find \(y\) at \(t = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}\) (with \(v_{0y} = 0\)):

\[y(3.0) = 0 + \frac{1}{2}(3.0)(3.0)^2 = 13.5\,\mathrm{m}\]

(b) The velocity components at \(t = 3.0\,\mathrm{s}\) are \(v_x = 4.0 + 2.0(3.0) = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(v_y = 0 + 3.0(3.0) = 9.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Thus, the speed is:

\[v = \sqrt{10^2 + 9.0^2} = \sqrt{181} \approx 13.45\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 3: Oblique Projectile Motion

Problem: A ball is launched from level ground at \(25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at an angle of \(53^\circ\) above the horizontal. Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(\sin 53^\circ \approx 0.8\), and \(\cos 53^\circ \approx 0.6\), calculate (a) the time of flight, (b) the maximum height, and (c) the horizontal range.

Solution: Initial velocity components are \(v_{0x} = 25 \times 0.6 = 15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(v_{0y} = 25 \times 0.8 = 20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

  • (a) Time of flight: \(T_f = \dfrac{2v_{0y}}{g} = \dfrac{2 \times 20}{10} = 4.0\,\mathrm{s}\).
  • (b) Maximum height: \(H_m = \dfrac{v_{0y}^2}{2g} = \dfrac{20^2}{2 \times 10} = 20\,\mathrm{m}\).
  • (c) Horizontal range: \(R = v_{0x} \times T_f = 15 \times 4.0 = 60\,\mathrm{m}\).
Worked Example 4: Horizontal Projection from a Height

Problem: A supply parcel is released horizontally at \(30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) from a drone hovering \(125\,\mathrm{m}\) above flat ground. Neglecting air resistance and taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), find the time to hit the ground and the impact speed.

Solution: For vertical free fall from rest (\(v_{0y} = 0\)) over a drop of \(h = 125\,\mathrm{m}\):

\[h = \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \implies 125 = \frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 \implies t^2 = 25 \implies t = 5.0\,\mathrm{s}\]

At impact, \(v_x = 30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (unchanged) and \(v_y = -gt = -10 \times 5.0 = -50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The impact speed is:

\[v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2} = \sqrt{30^2 + (-50)^2} = \sqrt{3400} \approx 58.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 5: Uniform Circular Motion

Problem: A small bead moves in a horizontal circular groove of radius \(r = 0.50\,\mathrm{m}\) at a constant rate of \(120\,\mathrm{rpm}\). Determine its angular speed, linear speed, and centripetal acceleration.

Solution: The frequency is \(\nu = \dfrac{120}{60} = 2.0\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

  • Angular speed: \(\omega = 2\pi\nu = 2\pi(2.0) = 4\pi \approx 12.57\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\).
  • Linear speed: \(v = \omega r = 4\pi \times 0.50 = 2\pi \approx 6.28\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
  • Centripetal acceleration: \(a_c = \omega^2 r = (4\pi)^2 \times 0.50 = 8\pi^2 \approx 78.96\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) directed toward the center.

8. NEET UG Practice Questions with Worked Solutions

These original practice questions are designed in standard NEET UG formats (single-correct MCQs, assertion-reason, and statement-based questions) to test conceptual clarity and numerical accuracy.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following groups contains only vector quantities?

  • (A) Impulse, angular velocity, displacement
  • (B) Electric current, pressure, work
  • (C) Speed, density, impulse
  • (D) Angular frequency, mass, force
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Impulse, angular velocity, and displacement all possess magnitude and direction and obey the vector laws of addition. Electric current, pressure, work, speed, density, angular frequency, and mass are scalar quantities.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two forces, each of magnitude \(F\), act simultaneously on a particle. If the magnitude of their resultant is also \(F\), what is the angle between the two forces?

  • (A) \(60^\circ\)
  • (B) \(90^\circ\)
  • (C) \(120^\circ\)
  • (D) \(150^\circ\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Using \(R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + 2AB\cos\theta}\) with \(A = B = R = F\):

\[F^2 = F^2 + F^2 + 2F^2\cos\theta \implies 1 = 2 + 2\cos\theta \implies \cos\theta = -\frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = 120^\circ\]

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Given two vectors \(\mathbf{A} = 2\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 5\hat{\mathbf{j}}\) and \(\mathbf{B} = 4\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{j}}\), what is the unit vector along \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\)?

  • (A) \(0.8\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0.6\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)
  • (B) \(0.6\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0.8\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)
  • (C) \(0.5\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0.5\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)
  • (D) \(6\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{j}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = (2+4)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (5+3)\hat{\mathbf{j}} = 6\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{j}}\). Its magnitude is \(|\mathbf{R}| = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10\). Therefore, the unit vector is:

\[\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \frac{\mathbf{R}}{|\mathbf{R}|} = \frac{6\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{j}}}{10} = 0.6\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 0.8\hat{\mathbf{j}}\]

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The position vector of a particle moving in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane is given by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (6t\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 2t^2\hat{\mathbf{j}})\,\mathrm{m}\), where \(t\) is in seconds. What is the speed of the particle at \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\)?

  • (A) \(8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(14\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Differentiating \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) with respect to \(t\) gives \(\mathbf{v}(t) = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t} = 6\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4t\hat{\mathbf{j}}\). At \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\), \(\mathbf{v}(2) = 6\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 8\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), so the speed is:

\[v = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A projectile is fired from level ground at an angle \(\theta_0\) such that its horizontal range is \(4\sqrt{3}\) times its maximum height. The angle of projection \(\theta_0\) is:

  • (A) \(30^\circ\)
  • (B) \(45^\circ\)
  • (C) \(60^\circ\)
  • (D) \(75^\circ\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Using the identity \(\dfrac{R}{H_m} = \dfrac{4}{\tan\theta_0}\):

\[4\sqrt{3} = \frac{4}{\tan\theta_0} \implies \tan\theta_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \implies \theta_0 = 30^\circ\]

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A ball is projected from the ground with speed \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at an angle of \(60^\circ\) above the horizontal. What is the magnitude of the change in velocity of the ball between the point of projection and the highest point of its path?

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(10\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(20\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Initial velocity is \(\mathbf{v}_0 = (20\cos 60^\circ)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (20\sin 60^\circ)\hat{\mathbf{j}} = 10\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 10\sqrt{3}\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). At the highest point, \(v_y = 0\), so \(\mathbf{v}_H = 10\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Thus:

\[\Delta\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_H - \mathbf{v}_0 = -10\sqrt{3}\hat{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \implies |\Delta\mathbf{v}| = 10\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A stone is thrown horizontally with a speed of \(10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) from the top of an \(80\,\mathrm{m}\) high tower. Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and neglecting air resistance, how far from the base of the tower does the stone strike the ground?

  • (A) \(20\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(40\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(60\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(80\,\mathrm{m}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Time to fall \(h = 80\,\mathrm{m}\) vertically from rest is \(t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2h}{g}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 80}{10}} = 4.0\,\mathrm{s}\). The horizontal distance traveled is \(x = v_{0x}t = 10 \times 4.0 = 40\,\mathrm{m}\).

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle moves in a circle of radius \(2.0\,\mathrm{m}\) at a constant speed of \(4.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). What is the magnitude of its average acceleration during a quarter revolution?

  • (A) \(\dfrac{8\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (B) \(\dfrac{16\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (C) \(8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
  • (D) \(0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: In a quarter revolution, the velocity turns by \(90^\circ\), so \(|\Delta\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{v^2 + v^2} = v\sqrt{2} = 4\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The time taken for a quarter circle is \(\Delta t = \dfrac{\pi r}{2v} = \dfrac{\pi(2.0)}{2(4.0)} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}\,\mathrm{s}\). Hence:

\[|\overline{\mathbf{a}}| = \frac{|\Delta\mathbf{v}|}{\Delta t} = \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\pi/4} = \frac{16\sqrt{2}}{\pi}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\]

Practice Question 9 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): In uniform circular motion, the speed of a particle remains constant, yet its acceleration is non-zero.

Reason (R): Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, and in uniform circular motion the direction of velocity changes continuously.

  • (A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true but R is false.
  • (D) A is false but R is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Velocity is a vector quantity. Even when speed (magnitude) is constant, a continuous change in direction produces a non-zero velocity change \(\Delta\mathbf{v}\), resulting in centripetal acceleration \(\mathbf{a}_c\) toward the center.

Practice Question 10 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Statement I: For a projectile launched from level ground (neglecting air resistance), the horizontal component of velocity remains constant throughout the flight.

Statement II: The net acceleration of a projectile at the highest point of its trajectory is zero.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Because gravity acts purely vertically (\(a_x = 0\)), \(v_x\) stays constant. However, at the highest point, only \(v_y = 0\); the downward acceleration \(g\) remains non-zero throughout the entire flight.

9. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Zero velocity vs. zero acceleration at projectile apex: At maximum height, \(v_y = 0\), but the speed is \(v_0\cos\theta_0 \neq 0\) (unless thrown straight up) and acceleration is still \(g\) downward.
  • Average speed vs. average velocity: Average speed \(\ge |\text{average velocity}|\) over any time interval. In a complete circular revolution, displacement is zero so \(\overline{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{0}\), whereas average speed is \(v\).
  • Centripetal acceleration over one full cycle: In UCM, the instantaneous acceleration magnitude is \(v^2/r\), but the acceleration vector averaged over one complete revolution is a null vector because \(\Delta\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}\).

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Resultant magnitude: \(R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + 2AB\cos\theta}\), bounded by \(|A - B| \le R \le A + B\).
  • Unit vector along \(\mathbf{A}\): \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{A}/|\mathbf{A}|\).
  • 2D kinematics: \(x\) and \(y\) motions are independent and linked only by time \(t\).
  • Projectile on level ground: \(T_f = \dfrac{2v_0\sin\theta_0}{g}\), \(H_m = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta_0}{2g}\), \(R = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta_0}{g}\).
  • Uniform circular motion: \(v = \omega r\), \(a_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r = 4\pi^2\nu^2 r\) toward the center.

10. Concise Final Recap

Motion in a plane is governed by vector algebra and the principle of superposition of perpendicular motions. Resolving vectors into \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{j}}\) components converts 2D problems into straightforward 1D equations. Projectile motion combines constant horizontal velocity with uniform vertical free fall, producing a symmetric parabola, while uniform circular motion demonstrates how a constant-magnitude force directed perpendicular to velocity continuously curves the trajectory with centripetal acceleration \(v^2/r\).
