Units and Measurement

SI Base Quantities, Significant Figures, Error Propagation, and Dimensional Analysis

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Context

Every quantitative observation in physics rests on comparing an unknown physical quantity against an internationally accepted standard. In NEET UG Physics, questions from this topic test numerical accuracy, unit conversions, significant-figure rules, uncertainty estimation, and dimensional reasoning—skills that also serve as rapid sanity checks across mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, and optics.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between the seven SI base quantities, derived quantities, and the two dimensionless supplementary units (radian and steradian).
  • Convert physical quantities across systems of units (SI, CGS, FPS, and custom hypothetical unit systems) using algebraic and dimensional methods.
  • Determine significant figures, express values in scientific notation with order of magnitude, and apply rounding-off rules accurately.
  • Combine uncertainties and significant figures correctly in addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.
  • Apply the Principle of Homogeneity of dimensions to verify equations, identify unknown constants, and deduce physical relationships while recognizing the limits of dimensional analysis.

2. Physical Quantities and the International System of Units (SI)

A physical quantity is expressed as the product of a numerical value \(n\) and a chosen unit \(u\):

\[ Q = n \cdot u \]

Because the numerical measure \(n\) scales inversely with the size of the chosen unit \(u\), changing units satisfies \(n_1 u_1 = n_2 u_2\). A smaller unit yields a larger numerical value, and vice versa.

2.1 Base Units, Derived Units, and Earlier Systems

A minimal set of independent quantities—called fundamental or base quantities—suffices to express all other derived quantities through algebraic combinations. Historically, three mechanical systems were widely used:

  • CGS system: centimetre (cm) for length, gram (g) for mass, and second (s) for time.
  • FPS (British) system: foot (ft) for length, pound (lb) for mass, and second (s) for time.
  • MKS system: metre (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, and second (s) for time.

Modern science and engineering use the Système International d'Unités (SI), which is decimal-based and built upon seven base units. Since the 2018 revision by the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), each SI base unit is anchored to an exact fixed numerical value of a fundamental physical constant.

Base Quantity SI Unit (Symbol) Defining Constant (Exact Value)
Length metre (m) Speed of light in vacuum, \(c = 299\,792\,458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Mass kilogram (kg) Planck constant, \(h = 6.62607015 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\)
Time second (s) Caesium-133 hyperfine frequency, \(\Delta\nu_{\mathrm{Cs}} = 9\,192\,631\,770\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
Electric current ampere (A) Elementary charge, \(e = 1.602176634 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)
Thermodynamic temperature kelvin (K) Boltzmann constant, \(k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\)
Amount of substance mole (mol) Avogadro constant, \(N_{\mathrm{A}} = 6.02214076 \times 10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}\)
Luminous intensity candela (cd) Luminous efficacy at \(540 \times 10^{12}\,\mathrm{Hz}\), \(K_{\mathrm{cd}} = 683\,\mathrm{lm\,W^{-1}}\)

Note: When expressing amount of substance in moles, the elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, or specified groups) must be stated clearly.

2.2 Plane Angle and Solid Angle (Dimensionless Units)

Alongside the seven base units, SI defines two dimensionless derived units for angular geometry:

  • Plane angle (\(\theta\)): Defined as the ratio of arc length \(s\) to radius \(r\) in a circle: \(\mathrm{d}\theta = \mathrm{d}s / r\). Its SI unit is the radian (rad). A full circle subtends \(2\pi\,\mathrm{rad}\).
  • Solid angle (\(\Omega\)): Defined as the ratio of the intercepted spherical surface area \(\mathrm{d}A\) (centered at apex \(O\)) to the square of the radius \(r\): \(\mathrm{d}\Omega = \mathrm{d}A / r^2\). Its SI unit is the steradian (sr). A complete sphere subtends \(4\pi\,\mathrm{sr}\).

3. Significant Figures, Scientific Notation, and Rounding Off

No physical measurement is exact; every instrument has a finite least count. The recorded value of a measurement contains all digits known with certainty plus the first uncertain digit. Together, these digits are called significant figures.

3.1 Rules for Counting Significant Figures

  1. All non-zero digits are significant (e.g., \(42.85\) has 4 significant figures).
  2. Captive zeros between two non-zero digits are always significant, regardless of the decimal point (e.g., \(5.004\) has 4 significant figures).
  3. Leading zeros to the left of the first non-zero digit in a number less than 1 are not significant; they only locate the decimal point (e.g., \(0.00308\) has 3 significant figures: 3, 0, 8).
  4. Trailing zeros without a decimal point are not significant (e.g., \(2400\,\mathrm{m}\) has 2 significant figures).
  5. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant because they reflect instrument precision (e.g., \(6.400\) and \(0.0520\) have 4 and 3 significant figures, respectively).
  6. Exact numbers in formulas (such as \(2\) in \(2\pi r\) or \(n\) in \(T = t/n\)) have infinite significant figures and never limit the precision of a calculation.
Key Principle: Unit Conversion Cannot Change Precision

Changing the unit of a measurement does not alter the number of significant figures. For example, \(4.700\,\mathrm{m} = 4.700 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{cm} = 4.700 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{km}\) all have 4 significant figures. Writing \(4700\,\mathrm{mm}\) without scientific notation creates ambiguity; hence, scientific notation (\(a \times 10^{b}\), with \(1 \le a < 10\)) is the preferred way to report measurements—every digit in \(a\) is significant, and \(10^{b}\) does not affect significant figures.

3.2 Order of Magnitude

In scientific notation \(a \times 10^{b}\), if we round \(a\) to \(1\) (when \(a \le 5\)) or to \(10\) (when \(5 < a \le 10\)), the resulting exponent of \(10\) is called the order of magnitude of the quantity. For example, \(1.28 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}\) has order of magnitude \(7\), whereas \(6.4 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 10 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m} = 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}\) also has order of magnitude \(7\).

3.3 Arithmetic Operations and Rounding-Off Rules

Operation Governing Rule Illustration (Assumed Values)
Addition / Subtraction Retain as many decimal places as the measurement with the fewest decimal places. \(14.32\,\mathrm{g} + 2.1\,\mathrm{g} = 16.42\,\mathrm{g} \rightarrow 16.4\,\mathrm{g}\) (1 decimal place)
Multiplication / Division Retain as many significant figures as the measurement with the fewest significant figures. \(6.12\,\mathrm{m} \times 1.3\,\mathrm{m} = 7.956\,\mathrm{m}^2 \rightarrow 8.0\,\mathrm{m}^2\) (2 significant figures)

Rounding-off convention when dropping uncertain digits:

  • If the first dropped digit is greater than 5, increase the preceding digit by 1 (e.g., \(3.268 \rightarrow 3.27\)).
  • If the first dropped digit is less than 5, leave the preceding digit unchanged (e.g., \(3.264 \rightarrow 3.26\)).
  • If the first dropped digit is exactly 5 (followed by zeros or nothing): leave the preceding digit unchanged if it is even (\(2.745 \rightarrow 2.74\)), and increase it by 1 if it is odd (\(2.735 \rightarrow 2.74\)).
  • In multi-step calculations, retain at least one extra guard digit in intermediate steps and round off only the final answer to prevent cumulative rounding error.

3.4 Propagating Uncertainty with Significant Figures

If a length \(l = 12.4 \pm 0.1\,\mathrm{cm}\) (relative error \(\pm 0.8\%\)) and width \(b = 5.2 \pm 0.1\,\mathrm{cm}\) (relative error \(\pm 1.9\%\)) are multiplied to find area \(A = l \cdot b\), the percentage errors add:

\[ \frac{\Delta A}{A} = \frac{\Delta l}{l} + \frac{\Delta b}{b} \]

Here, \(A = 64.48\,\mathrm{cm}^2\) with a combined relative error of \(2.7\%\), giving an absolute uncertainty \(\Delta A \approx 1.74\,\mathrm{cm}^2\). Rounding to match precision yields \(A = 64 \pm 2\,\mathrm{cm}^2\).

4. Dimensions and Dimensional Analysis

The dimensions of a physical quantity are the powers (exponents) to which the seven base quantities must be raised to represent that quantity. Square brackets \([\,\cdot\,]\) denote "the dimensions of". The seven base dimensions are \([\mathrm{L}]\), \([\mathrm{M}]\), \([\mathrm{T}]\), \([\mathrm{A}]\), \([\mathrm{K}]\), \([\mathrm{mol}]\), and \([\mathrm{cd}]\).

A dimensional formula expresses a quantity in terms of base dimensions (for example, force has dimensional formula \([\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]\)), and equating the quantity symbol to its dimensional formula gives its dimensional equation: \([F] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]\).

Physical Quantity Relation Dimensional Formula
Velocity / Speed \(\text{Displacement} / \text{Time}\) \([\mathrm{M^{0}\,L\,T^{-1}}]\)
Acceleration \(\text{Velocity} / \text{Time}\) \([\mathrm{M^{0}\,L\,T^{-2}}]\)
Force \(\text{Mass} \times \text{Acceleration}\) \([\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]\)
Work / Energy / Torque \(\text{Force} \times \text{Displacement}\) \([\mathrm{M\,L^{2}\,T^{-2}}]\)
Power \(\text{Work} / \text{Time}\) \([\mathrm{M\,L^{2}\,T^{-3}}]\)
Pressure / Stress / Young's modulus \(\text{Force} / \text{Area}\) \([\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-2}}]\)
Coefficient of viscosity (\(\eta\)) \(F / (A \cdot \mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}x)\) \([\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-1}}]\)
Universal gravitational constant (\(G\)) \(F r^2 / (m_1 m_2)\) \([\mathrm{M^{-1}\,L^{3}\,T^{-2}}]\)
Planck constant (\(h\)) \(\text{Energy} / \text{Frequency}\) \([\mathrm{M\,L^{2}\,T^{-1}}]\)

4.1 Principle of Homogeneity and Applications

Principle of Homogeneity: Only physical quantities with identical dimensions can be added, subtracted, or equated. Consequently, every additive term on both sides of a valid physical equation must possess the exact same dimensional formula. Furthermore, the arguments of transcendental functions—such as \(\sin(\theta)\), \(\cos(\theta)\), \(\ln(x)\), and \(e^{x}\)—must be dimensionless (\([\mathrm{M^{0}L^{0}T^{0}}]\)).

Tutorial Diagram 2: The three primary applications of dimensional analysis in physics.
Limitations of Dimensional Analysis (High-Yield for NEET UG)
  • It cannot determine dimensionless constants (such as \(1/2\), \(2\pi\), or \(6\pi\)) in a formula.
  • A dimensionally consistent equation is not guaranteed to be physically correct (e.g., \(K = mv^2\) and \(K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\) have the same dimensions), though a dimensionally inconsistent equation is definitely wrong.
  • It cannot derive relations involving trigonometric, logarithmic, or exponential functions, nor equations formed by adding or subtracting multiple terms (such as \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)).
  • In mechanics, it cannot uniquely solve for exponents if a quantity depends on more than three independent dimensional variables.

5. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Converting Units into a Hypothetical System

Problem: Assume a new system of units in which the unit of mass is \(\alpha\,\mathrm{kg}\), the unit of length is \(\beta\,\mathrm{m}\), and the unit of time is \(\gamma\,\mathrm{s}\). Express a power of \(12\,\mathrm{W}\) (where \(1\,\mathrm{W} = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-3}}\)) in this new system.

Solution: The dimensional formula of power is \([\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-3}}]\), so \(a = 1\), \(b = 2\), and \(c = -3\). Using \(n_2 = n_1 \left(\frac{M_1}{M_2}\right)^a \left(\frac{L_1}{L_2}\right)^b \left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)^c\):

\[ n_2 = 12 \left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{kg}}{\alpha\,\mathrm{kg}}\right)^1 \left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{m}}{\beta\,\mathrm{m}}\right)^2 \left(\frac{1\,\mathrm{s}}{\gamma\,\mathrm{s}}\right)^{-3} = 12\,\alpha^{-1}\,\beta^{-2}\,\gamma^{3} \]

Thus, \(12\,\mathrm{W}\) equals \(12\,\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-2}\gamma^{3}\) new units of power.

Worked Example 2: Significant Figures in Density Calculation

Problem: Suppose a metal cylinder has a measured mass of \(48.62\,\mathrm{g}\) and a measured volume of \(5.4\,\mathrm{cm^3}\). Calculate its density to the appropriate number of significant figures.

Solution: The mass \(48.62\,\mathrm{g}\) has 4 significant figures, while the volume \(5.4\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) has 2 significant figures. For division, the final result must retain the smaller count (2 significant figures):

\[ \rho = \frac{48.62\,\mathrm{g}}{5.4\,\mathrm{cm^3}} = 9.0037\ldots\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} \approx 9.0\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} \]

Note that the trailing zero in \(9.0\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) is significant and must be written to convey 2 significant figures.

Worked Example 3: Combining Uncertainties in Area

Problem: Assume the length and breadth of a rectangular glass slide are measured as \(l = 8.0 \pm 0.1\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(b = 4.0 \pm 0.1\,\mathrm{cm}\). Report the area of the slide with its absolute uncertainty.

Solution: Nominal area \(A = l \times b = 8.0 \times 4.0 = 32.0\,\mathrm{cm^2}\). The fractional uncertainty in area is:

\[ \frac{\Delta A}{A} = \frac{\Delta l}{l} + \frac{\Delta b}{b} = \frac{0.1}{8.0} + \frac{0.1}{4.0} = 0.0125 + 0.025 = 0.0375 \]

Absolute uncertainty: \(\Delta A = 0.0375 \times 32.0 = 1.2\,\mathrm{cm^2}\). Rounding \(\Delta A\) to one significant figure (consistent with the error terms) gives \(1\,\mathrm{cm^2}\), so the area is \(32 \pm 1\,\mathrm{cm^2}\).

Worked Example 4: Dimensional Consistency with Transcendental Functions

Problem: A damped mechanical oscillator has displacement \(y(t) = A e^{-\lambda t} \sin(\omega t + \phi)\), where \(t\) is time. Find the dimensions of \(\lambda\) and \(\omega\).

Solution: The arguments of both the exponential function \(e^{-\lambda t}\) and the sine function \(\sin(\omega t + \phi)\) must be dimensionless:

\[ [\lambda t] = [\mathrm{M^0 L^0 T^0}] \implies [\lambda] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}] \] \[ [\omega t] = [\mathrm{M^0 L^0 T^0}] \implies [\omega] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}] \]

Both \(\lambda\) (damping constant) and \(\omega\) (angular frequency) have dimensions \([\mathrm{T^{-1}}]\).

Worked Example 5: Deducing a Physical Relation

Problem: Assume the frequency \(f\) of a vibrating stretched string depends on its length \(l\), tension \(F\), and mass per unit length \(\mu\). Use dimensional analysis to deduce the dependence of \(f\) on \(l\), \(F\), and \(\mu\).

Solution: Let \(f = k\,l^{a} F^{b} \mu^{c}\), where \(k\) is a dimensionless constant. Writing dimensions on both sides (\([f] = [\mathrm{T^{-1}}]\), \([l] = [\mathrm{L}]\), \([F] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]\), \([\mu] = [\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}}]\)):

\[ [\mathrm{M^0 L^0 T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{L}]^{a} [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]^{b} [\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}}]^{c} = [\mathrm{M}^{b+c}\,\mathrm{L}^{a+b-c}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2b}] \]

Equating exponents: \(-2b = -1 \implies b = \frac{1}{2}\); \(b + c = 0 \implies c = -\frac{1}{2}\); \(a + b - c = 0 \implies a + \frac{1}{2} - \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = 0 \implies a = -1\).

Hence, \(f = \frac{k}{l}\sqrt{\frac{F}{\mu}}\). (Dimensional analysis leaves \(k\) undetermined; for the fundamental mode, \(k = \frac{1}{2}\).)

6. NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are modeled on the NEET UG examination pattern (single-correct 4-option MCQs, including Statement-based and Assertion-Reason formats). Attempt each question before reviewing the worked solution.

Practice Question 1

Which of the following pairs of physical quantities has identical SI units, yet both quantities are dimensionless?

  • (A) Work and torque
  • (B) Plane angle and solid angle (wait: do they have identical units?) — let us check carefully: Strain and refractive index
  • (C) Plane angle and angular displacement
  • (D) Impulse and linear momentum
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Both plane angle and angular displacement are ratios of arc length to radius (\(\theta = s/r\)), so both are dimensionless (\([\mathrm{M^0 L^0 T^0}]\)) and share the SI unit radian (rad). Strain and refractive index are dimensionless but have no unit. Work/torque and impulse/momentum are dimensional pairs.

Practice Question 2

In a hypothetical system of units, the unit of force is \(100\,\mathrm{N}\), the unit of length is \(10\,\mathrm{m}\), and the unit of time is \(100\,\mathrm{s}\). What is the unit of mass in this system?

  • (A) \(10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg}\)
  • (B) \(10^{4}\,\mathrm{kg}\)
  • (C) \(10^{5}\,\mathrm{kg}\)
  • (D) \(10^{6}\,\mathrm{kg}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Since \([F] = [\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}]\), we have \([\mathrm{M}] = [F\,L^{-1}\,T^{2}]\). Substituting the new base units:

\[ M_{\text{new}} = \frac{F \cdot T^{2}}{L} = \frac{(100\,\mathrm{N}) \times (100\,\mathrm{s})^{2}}{10\,\mathrm{m}} = \frac{10^{2} \times 10^{4}}{10}\,\mathrm{kg} = 10^{5}\,\mathrm{kg} \]
Practice Question 3

The number of significant figures in \(0.04050\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(4.050 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{cm}\) are, respectively:

  • (A) 3 and 3
  • (B) 4 and 4
  • (C) 5 and 4
  • (D) 4 and 3
Answer: (B)

Explanation: In \(0.04050\,\mathrm{m}\), the two leading zeros are not significant, while the captive zero and the trailing zero after the decimal point are significant (digits 4, 0, 5, 0 → 4 significant figures). In \(4.050 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{cm}\), all four digits in the base number \(4.050\) are significant (4 significant figures).

Practice Question 4

Three masses are measured as \(12.42\,\mathrm{g}\), \(3.1\,\mathrm{g}\), and \(0.256\,\mathrm{g}\). What is their sum reported to the correct number of significant figures?

  • (A) \(15.776\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (B) \(15.78\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (C) \(15.8\,\mathrm{g}\)
  • (D) \(16\,\mathrm{g}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Arithmetic sum: \(12.42 + 3.1 + 0.256 = 15.776\,\mathrm{g}\). Under the rule for addition, the result must retain as many decimal places as the least precise measurement (\(3.1\,\mathrm{g}\), which has 1 decimal place). Rounding \(15.776\) to 1 decimal place gives \(15.8\,\mathrm{g}\).

Practice Question 5

A student measures the side of a cube as \(a = 2.00\,\mathrm{cm}\) with an uncertainty of \(\pm 0.02\,\mathrm{cm}\). What is the percentage error in the calculated volume of the cube?

  • (A) \(1\%\)
  • (B) \(2\%\)
  • (C) \(3\%\)
  • (D) \(6\%\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Volume \(V = a^{3}\). The fractional error is \(\frac{\Delta V}{V} = 3\frac{\Delta a}{a}\). Therefore, the percentage error is:

\[ 3 \times \left(\frac{0.02}{2.00} \times 100\%\right) = 3 \times 1\% = 3\% \]
Practice Question 6

If energy \(E\), velocity \(v\), and time \(T\) are chosen as the fundamental quantities, what is the dimensional formula of surface tension?

  • (A) \([E\,v^{-2}\,T^{-2}]\)
  • (B) \([E\,v^{-1}\,T^{-2}]\)
  • (C) \([E\,v^{-2}\,T^{-1}]\)
  • (D) \([E^{2}\,v^{-1}\,T^{-2}]\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Surface tension \(S = \frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Length}} = \frac{\text{Energy}}{\text{Area}}\). Since length \(L = v \cdot T\), area \(A = L^{2} = v^{2} T^{2}\). Thus:

\[ [S] = \frac{[E]}{[v^{2} T^{2}]} = [E\,v^{-2}\,T^{-2}] \]
Practice Question 7

In the equation \(P = \frac{a - t^{2}}{b\,x}\), where \(P\) is pressure, \(x\) is distance, and \(t\) is time, what are the dimensions of the ratio \(a/b\)?

  • (A) \([\mathrm{M\,L^{0}\,T^{-2}}]\)
  • (B) \([\mathrm{M\,L^{0}\,T^{2}}]\)
  • (C) \([\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-2}}]\)
  • (D) \([\mathrm{M^{0}\,L\,T^{-2}}]\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: By the Principle of Homogeneity, \(a\) can be subtracted from \(t^{2}\) only if \([a] = [\mathrm{T^{2}}]\). Then \(P = \frac{[a]}{[b][x]}\) gives:

\[ \frac{[a]}{[b]} = [P][x] = [\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-2}}][\mathrm{L}] = [\mathrm{M\,L^{0}\,T^{-2}}] \]
Practice Question 8

The critical velocity \(v_c\) of a liquid flowing through a tube of radius \(r\) depends on the coefficient of viscosity \(\eta\), density \(\rho\), and radius \(r\). Using dimensional analysis, \(v_c\) is proportional to:

  • (A) \(\eta\,\rho^{-1}\,r^{-1}\)
  • (B) \(\eta^{-1}\,\rho\,r\)
  • (C) \(\eta\,\rho\,r^{-1}\)
  • (D) \(\eta\,\rho^{-1}\,r\)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Let \(v_c \propto \eta^{a} \rho^{b} r^{c}\). Substituting dimensions (\([\eta] = [\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-1}}]\), \([\rho] = [\mathrm{M\,L^{-3}}]\), \([r] = [\mathrm{L}]\)):

\[ [\mathrm{M^0 L^1 T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{M}^{a+b}\,\mathrm{L}^{-a-3b+c}\,\mathrm{T}^{-a}] \]

Equating powers: \(-a = -1 \implies a = 1\); \(a + b = 0 \implies b = -1\); \(-1 - 3(-1) + c = 1 \implies 2 + c = 1 \implies c = -1\). Thus \(v_c \propto \eta\,\rho^{-1}\,r^{-1}\).

Practice Question 9 (Statement-Based)

Statement I: Every physically correct equation in physics must be dimensionally homogeneous.
Statement II: Every dimensionally homogeneous equation is guaranteed to be physically exact.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  • (C) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
  • (D) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
Answer: (B)

Explanation: A physically valid equation must have identical dimensions for all additive terms (Statement I is true). However, dimensional analysis cannot detect incorrect dimensionless factors (for example, \(K = \frac{1}{4}mv^{2}\) is dimensionally homogeneous with energy, yet physically wrong). Hence, Statement II is false.

Practice Question 10 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Reporting \(2.50\,\mathrm{m}\) as \(250\,\mathrm{cm}\) without scientific notation can mislead a reader about the number of significant figures.
Reason (R): Trailing zeros in a number without a decimal point are not treated as significant by standard convention, whereas changing units does not change measurement precision.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: \(2.50\,\mathrm{m}\) has 3 significant figures, so \(250\,\mathrm{cm}\) must also have 3 significant figures. However, writing \(250\,\mathrm{cm}\) without a decimal point makes it appear to have only 2 significant figures. Writing \(2.50 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{cm}\) avoids this ambiguity. Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) correctly explains (A).

7. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Mistakes to Avoid
  • Mixing addition and multiplication rules: Addition/subtraction depends on the least number of decimal places, whereas multiplication/division depends on the least number of significant figures.
  • Confusing "dimensionless" with "unitless": Plane angle (rad) and solid angle (sr) are dimensionless quantities that still carry specific SI units.
  • Forgetting the even/odd rule for 5: When rounding off a trailing 5, round to the nearest even digit (e.g., \(4.65 \rightarrow 4.6\), \(4.75 \rightarrow 4.8\)).
  • Overlooking transcendental arguments: Always set the argument inside \(\sin(\cdots)\), \(\cos(\cdots)\), \(\ln(\cdots)\), or \(e^{(\cdots)}\) to \([\mathrm{M^0 L^0 T^0}]\) before solving for unknown constants.
Quick Revision Checklist
  • 7 SI base units: m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd; 2 supplementary units: rad, sr.
  • \(1\,\mathrm{Å} = 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\); \(1\,\mathrm{b} = 10^{-28}\,\mathrm{m^2}\); \(1\,\mathrm{L} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\); \(1\,\mathrm{atm} = 1.01325 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{Pa}\).
  • Scientific notation \(a \times 10^{b}\) preserves significant figures unambiguously across unit conversions.
  • Dimensional failure proves an equation wrong; dimensional success is necessary but not sufficient for physical correctness.

8. Concise Final Recap

Accurate physics rests on three pillars covered in this tutorial: a standardized SI unit framework built on seven base constants, significant-figure and error rules that ensure computed results never overstate experimental precision, and dimensional analysis, which verifies equation homogeneity and reveals scaling laws across physical systems. Mastering these tools equips you to solve unit-conversion, error-propagation, and dimensional-reasoning problems rapidly and reliably in NEET UG.
