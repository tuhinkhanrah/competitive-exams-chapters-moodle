Systems of Particles and Rotational Motion

A complete, exam-oriented guide to rigid-body kinematics, centre of mass, torque, angular momentum, mechanical equilibrium, and fixed-axis rotational dynamics.

1. Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

Until now, mechanics focused on single point-like particles whose spatial extent was ignored. Real objects—such as wheels, bones, beams, and planets—have finite dimensions and internal structure. To analyze how extended objects translate, rotate, and balance, we model them either as a system of particles or as an idealized rigid body.

What You Will Master for NEET UG
  • Distinguishing pure translation, fixed-axis rotation, and combined rolling motion.
  • Locating the centre of mass (CM) of discrete systems, symmetric bodies, and composite/cut-out plates.
  • Applying linear momentum conservation to systems with internal explosions or interactions.
  • Computing torque (\(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}\)) and angular momentum (\(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}\)) using vector products.
  • Solving static equilibrium problems using force and moment balance, and distinguishing centre of gravity from centre of mass.
  • Using moment of inertia (\(I\)), radius of gyration (\(k\)), rotational kinetic energy, \(\tau = I\alpha\), and conservation of angular momentum (\(I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2\)).

2. Rigid Bodies and Types of Motion

An ideal rigid body has a fixed, unchanging shape: the distance between every pair of constituent particles remains constant regardless of applied forces. Real solids deform slightly under stress, but when deformations are negligible, the rigid-body model is highly accurate.

Three Fundamental Modes of Rigid-Body Motion

  • Pure Translational Motion: Every particle of the body has the exact same instantaneous velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) and acceleration \(\mathbf{a}\). Any line drawn inside the body keeps a constant orientation in space.
  • Pure Rotational Motion (Fixed Axis): When a rigid body is constrained along a fixed straight line (the axis of rotation), every particle outside the axis moves in a circle centred on the axis, in a plane perpendicular to the axis. Particles lying on the axis remain stationary.
  • Combined Translation and Rotation: An unconstrained body—such as a cylinder rolling down an incline or a spinning baton thrown through the air—undergoes translation of its centre of mass together with rotation about an axis passing through the centre of mass. In rolling without slipping, the instantaneous velocity of the point of contact with the stationary surface is zero.

Angular Variables and Relation to Linear Velocity

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, every particle sweeps out the same angular displacement \(\Delta\theta\) in time \(\Delta t\). The instantaneous angular velocity is \(\omega = \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t}\) (SI unit: \(\mathrm{rad/s}\)), and the angular acceleration is \(\alpha = \frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}t}\) (SI unit: \(\mathrm{rad/s}^2\)).

As a vector, \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) points along the axis of rotation according to the right-hand screw rule: curl the fingers of your right hand in the sense of rotation, and your thumb points along \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\). The linear velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) of a particle at position \(\mathbf{r}\) from an origin on the axis is:

\[ \mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r} \qquad \text{with magnitude} \qquad v = \omega r_\perp \]

where \(r_\perp\) is the perpendicular distance of the particle from the rotation axis. Note that \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) is identical for all particles, whereas \(v\) scales linearly with \(r_\perp\).

Kinematic Equations for Uniform Angular Acceleration

When a rigid body rotates about a fixed axis with constant angular acceleration \(\alpha\), starting from initial angle \(\theta_0\) and initial angular velocity \(\omega_0\):

\[ \omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t \qquad | \qquad \theta - \theta_0 = \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2 \qquad | \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha(\theta - \theta_0) \]

3. Centre of Mass and Linear Momentum of a System

The centre of mass (CM) of a system of particles is the unique point that moves as though the entire mass of the system were concentrated there and all external forces acted at that point.

Position of the Centre of Mass

For \(n\) discrete particles of masses \(m_1, m_2, \dots, m_n\) at position vectors \(\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \dots, \mathbf{r}_n\) with total mass \(M = \sum m_i\):

\[ \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \mathbf{r}_i}{M}, \qquad X = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{M}, \quad Y = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{M}, \quad Z = \frac{\sum m_i z_i}{M} \]

For two particles \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) separated by distance \(d\), the CM lies on the line joining them at distances \(r_1 = \frac{m_2 d}{m_1 + m_2}\) from \(m_1\) and \(r_2 = \frac{m_1 d}{m_1 + m_2}\) from \(m_2\). Thus, the CM is always closer to the heavier mass.

For a continuous rigid body, summation becomes integration over mass elements \(\mathrm{d}m\):

\[ \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\int \mathbf{r}\,\mathrm{d}m \]

If the origin is placed at the CM, then \(\int \mathbf{r}\,\mathrm{d}m = \mathbf{0}\). By reflection symmetry, the CM of any uniform ring, disc, rod, cylinder, cube, or sphere lies at its geometric centre. Note that the CM need not lie inside the material of the body (for example, a hollow ring or an L-shaped plate).

NEET UG Shortcut: Cavity / Cut-Out Method

When a portion of mass \(m_{\mathrm{cut}}\) (or area \(A_{\mathrm{cut}}\) for a uniform lamina) is removed from a uniform body of original mass \(M_0\) (area \(A_0\)), treat the cavity as a negative mass \(-m_{\mathrm{cut}}\) superposed on the intact body:

\[ \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{M_0 \mathbf{R}_0 - m_{\mathrm{cut}} \mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{cut}}}{M_0 - m_{\mathrm{cut}}} \]

Motion of the Centre of Mass & Conservation of Linear Momentum

Differentiating \(M\mathbf{R} = \sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i\) with respect to time gives the total linear momentum \(\mathbf{P}\) of the system:

\[ \mathbf{P} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \mathbf{v}_i = M\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{CM}} \]

Differentiating once more and using Newton's laws, internal action-reaction forces between particles cancel in pairs (\(\sum \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{int}} = \mathbf{0}\)), leaving only the net external force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}\):

\[ M\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} \]

Law of Conservation of Linear Momentum: If \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} = \mathbf{0}\), then \(\mathbf{P} = \text{constant}\) and \(\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{CM}} = \text{constant}\). Even if internal forces cause violent changes—such as a mid-air shell explosion or radioactive decay—the trajectory of the CM remains completely unaffected by internal forces.

4. Vector Product, Torque, and Angular Momentum

Key Properties of the Vector (Cross) Product

The cross product of two vectors \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) separated by angle \(\theta\) (\(0^\circ \le \theta \le 180^\circ\)) is a vector \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}\) of magnitude \(ab\sin\theta\), directed perpendicular to the plane of \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) via the right-hand rule:

  • Anti-commutative: \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = -(\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a})\), and \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\).
  • Distributive: \(\mathbf{a} \times (\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{c}\).
  • Unit vectors: \(\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{j}} \times \hat{\mathbf{k}} = \hat{\mathbf{i}}\), \(\hat{\mathbf{k}} \times \hat{\mathbf{i}} = \hat{\mathbf{j}}\) (cyclic order is positive; reverse order is negative).
  • Determinant form: \[ \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ a_x & a_y & a_z \\ b_x & b_y & b_z \end{vmatrix} = (a_y b_z - a_z b_y)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (a_z b_x - a_x b_z)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (a_x b_y - a_y b_x)\hat{\mathbf{k}} \]

Moment of Force (Torque)

The rotational analogue of force is torque (or moment of force). If a force \(\mathbf{F}\) acts at position \(\mathbf{r}\) relative to a reference point \(O\), the torque about \(O\) is:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}, \qquad \tau = r F \sin\theta = r_\perp F = r F_\perp \]

Here \(r_\perp = r\sin\theta\) is the lever arm (perpendicular distance from \(O\) to the line of action of \(\mathbf{F}\)). Torque has SI units of \(\mathrm{N\,m}\) and dimensions \([\mathrm{M L^2 T^{-2}}]\). Although dimensionally identical to work, torque is a vector whereas work is a scalar. Torque vanishes if \(r = 0\), \(F = 0\), or the line of action passes through \(O\) (\(\theta = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\)).

Angular Momentum and Newton's Second Law for Rotation

The angular momentum \(\mathbf{l}\) of a particle of linear momentum \(\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}\) at position \(\mathbf{r}\) about origin \(O\) is:

\[ \mathbf{l} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}, \qquad l = r p \sin\theta = r_\perp p = r p_\perp \]

Its SI unit is \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\) (or \(\mathrm{J\,s}\)) and dimensions are \([\mathrm{M L^2 T^{-1}}]\). For a particle moving at constant velocity along a straight line, its angular momentum about any fixed point remains constant because \(r_\perp\) is constant.

For a system of \(n\) particles, \(\mathbf{L} = \sum \mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{p}_i\). Assuming internal forces between any two particles are equal, opposite, and act along the line joining them, the net internal torque vanishes (\(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{int}} = \mathbf{0}\)), giving:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{L}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{ext}} \]

Conservation of Angular Momentum: When \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{ext}} = \mathbf{0}\), the total angular momentum \(\mathbf{L}\) of the system remains constant in both magnitude and direction.

5. Mechanical Equilibrium, Couples, and Centre of Gravity

A rigid body is in complete mechanical equilibrium when both its linear momentum and angular momentum are constant in time:

  1. Translational Equilibrium: \(\sum \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{0}\) (net external force is zero; linear acceleration of CM is zero).
  2. Rotational Equilibrium: \(\sum \boldsymbol{\tau}_i = \mathbf{0}\) (net external torque about any point is zero; angular acceleration is zero).

If \(\sum \mathbf{F}_i = \mathbf{0}\) holds, then the rotational equilibrium condition \(\sum \boldsymbol{\tau}_i = \mathbf{0}\) is independent of the choice of reference origin.

Couple and Partial Equilibrium

A body can satisfy one condition without the other. A couple consists of two equal and opposite forces (\(\mathbf{F}\) and \(-\mathbf{F}\)) acting along different parallel lines separated by perpendicular distance \(d\). For a couple, \(\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) (translational equilibrium), yet \(\tau = F d \neq 0\) (no rotational equilibrium), producing pure rotation. Moreover, the moment of a couple is independent of the point about which moments are calculated.

Principle of Moments for a Lever

For a light rod pivoted at a fulcrum with load \(F_1\) at load arm \(d_1\) and effort \(F_2\) at effort arm \(d_2\), rotational equilibrium about the fulcrum requires:

\[ F_1 d_1 = F_2 d_2 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \text{Mechanical Advantage (M.A.)} = \frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{d_2}{d_1} \]

Centre of Gravity (CG) vs. Centre of Mass (CM)

The centre of gravity (CG) is the point where the total gravitational torque on an extended body vanishes: \(\sum \mathbf{r}_i \times m_i \mathbf{g}_i = \mathbf{0}\). In a uniform gravitational field (true for everyday laboratory-sized objects), \(\mathbf{g}\) factors out, so \(\sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{0}\) and the CG coincides with the CM. For astronomically large bodies where \(\mathbf{g}\) varies across the body, the CG and CM do not coincide.

6. Moment of Inertia and Fixed-Axis Rotational Dynamics

In linear motion, mass \(m\) measures inertia against linear acceleration. In rotation about a fixed axis, the corresponding quantity is the moment of inertia \(I\):

\[ I = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i r_i^2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad I = M k^2 \]

where \(r_i\) is the perpendicular distance of particle \(m_i\) from the axis of rotation, and \(k = \sqrt{I/M}\) is the radius of gyration—the effective distance from the axis at which the entire mass \(M\) could be concentrated to yield the same \(I\). Unlike mass, \(I\) depends on the body's shape, mass distribution, and the position and orientation of the rotation axis. Its SI unit is \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\).

Moments of Inertia of Standard Uniform Bodies

Uniform Body (Mass \(M\)) Axis of Rotation Moment of Inertia (\(I\)) Radius of Gyration (\(k\))
Thin circular ring (radius \(R\)) Perpendicular to plane, through centre \(MR^2\) \(R\)
Thin circular ring (radius \(R\)) Along any diameter \(\frac{1}{2}MR^2\) \(\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\)
Thin rod (length \(L\)) Perpendicular to rod, through midpoint \(\frac{1}{12}ML^2\) \(\frac{L}{\sqrt{12}}\)
Circular disc (radius \(R\)) Perpendicular to disc, through centre \(\frac{1}{2}MR^2\) \(\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\)
Circular disc (radius \(R\)) Along any diameter \(\frac{1}{4}MR^2\) \(\frac{R}{2}\)
Hollow cylinder (radius \(R\)) Symmetry axis of cylinder \(MR^2\) \(R\)
Solid cylinder (radius \(R\)) Symmetry axis of cylinder \(\frac{1}{2}MR^2\) \(\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\)
Solid sphere (radius \(R\)) Along any diameter \(\frac{2}{5}MR^2\) \(\sqrt{\frac{2}{5}}\,R\)

Core Equations of Fixed-Axis Rotational Dynamics

When a rigid body rotates about a fixed \(z\)-axis, only the torque component along the \(z\)-axis causes rotation (constraint forces at the bearings cancel perpendicular torque components):

Translational Quantity / Law Rotational Analogue (Fixed Axis)
Displacement \(x\), velocity \(v\), acceleration \(a\) Angle \(\theta\), angular velocity \(\omega\), angular acceleration \(\alpha\)
Mass \(M\) Moment of inertia \(I = \sum m_i r_i^2\)
Newton's 2nd Law: \(F = Ma\) Rotational 2nd Law: \(\tau = I\alpha\)
Work: \(\mathrm{d}W = F\,\mathrm{d}s\) Work: \(\mathrm{d}W = \tau\,\mathrm{d}\theta\)
Kinetic Energy: \(K = \frac{1}{2}Mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2M}\) Kinetic Energy: \(K = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{L^2}{2I}\)
Power: \(P = Fv\) Power: \(P = \tau\omega\)
Linear Momentum: \(p = Mv\) Angular Momentum (symmetric body): \(L_z = I\omega\)

Angular Momentum in Fixed-Axis Rotation & Its Conservation

For a rigid body rotating about the \(z\)-axis, \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{L}_z + \mathbf{L}_\perp\), where \(L_z = I\omega\). If the rotation axis is an axis of symmetry of the body, diametrically opposite particles cancel \(\mathbf{L}_\perp\), so \(\mathbf{L} = I\omega\hat{\mathbf{k}}\) is parallel to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\). If the axis is not a symmetry axis, \(\mathbf{L}_\perp \neq \mathbf{0}\) and \(\mathbf{L}\) does not point along the rotation axis.

When no external torque acts about the rotation axis (\(\tau_{\mathrm{ext}} = 0\)), angular momentum is conserved even if the body changes its shape (so \(I\) changes):

\[ I_1 \omega_1 = I_2 \omega_2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad K = \frac{L^2}{2I} \propto \frac{1}{I} \]

7. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Centre of Mass of a Disc with a Circular Cavity

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A uniform circular metal plate of radius \(R = 12\,\mathrm{cm}\) has a circular hole of radius \(r = 4\,\mathrm{cm}\) punched out of it. The centre of the hole lies at a distance \(d = 6\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the centre of the plate. Find the distance of the centre of mass of the remaining plate from the original centre.

Solution:

Place the origin \(O(0, 0)\) at the centre of the intact disc, and let the centre of the hole lie along the \(+x\)-axis at \(x_2 = +6\,\mathrm{cm}\). Since the plate has uniform areal mass density, mass is proportional to area:

\[ A_1 = \pi R^2 = 144\pi\,\mathrm{cm^2}, \qquad A_2 = \pi r^2 = 16\pi\,\mathrm{cm^2} \]

Treating the cavity as a negative area \(-A_2\) at \(x_2 = 6\,\mathrm{cm}\):

\[ X_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{A_1(0) - A_2 x_2}{A_1 - A_2} = \frac{-16\pi(6)}{144\pi - 16\pi} = \frac{-96}{128} = -0.75\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Result: The centre of mass shifts \(0.75\,\mathrm{cm}\) away from the original centre, on the side opposite to the hole.

Worked Example 2: Static Equilibrium of a Loaded Plank

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A uniform plank of length \(4.0\,\mathrm{m}\) and mass \(12\,\mathrm{kg}\) rests horizontally on two knife-edge supports \(A\) and \(B\) located \(0.5\,\mathrm{m}\) from the left end and \(1.0\,\mathrm{m}\) from the right end, respectively. A \(8.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) block is placed \(1.5\,\mathrm{m}\) from the left end. Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\), calculate the normal reactions \(N_A\) and \(N_B\).

Solution:

Weights acting downward: \(W_{\mathrm{plank}} = 12 \times 10 = 120\,\mathrm{N}\) at its midpoint (\(2.0\,\mathrm{m}\) from left end), and \(W_{\mathrm{block}} = 8 \times 10 = 80\,\mathrm{N}\) at \(1.5\,\mathrm{m}\) from left end.

For translational equilibrium in the vertical direction:

\[ N_A + N_B = 120 + 80 = 200\,\mathrm{N} \]

For rotational equilibrium, take moments about support \(A\) (located at \(0.5\,\mathrm{m}\) from the left end). The distances from \(A\) are: block at \(1.0\,\mathrm{m}\), plank CM at \(1.5\,\mathrm{m}\), and support \(B\) at \(3.0 - 0.5 = 2.5\,\mathrm{m}\):

\[ N_B(2.5) - 80(1.0) - 120(1.5) = 0 \implies 2.5 N_B = 80 + 180 = 260 \implies N_B = 104\,\mathrm{N} \]

Substituting back: \(N_A = 200 - 104 = 96\,\mathrm{N}\).

Result: \(N_A = 96\,\mathrm{N}\) and \(N_B = 104\,\mathrm{N}\).

Worked Example 3: Unwinding a Solid Cylinder

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A uniform solid cylinder of mass \(10\,\mathrm{kg}\) and radius \(0.4\,\mathrm{m}\) is mounted on a frictionless horizontal axle. A light cord wound around its rim is pulled tangentially with a steady force of \(30\,\mathrm{N}\). Starting from rest, find (a) its angular acceleration, (b) its angular speed after \(4\,\mathrm{m}\) of cord is unwound, and (c) its rotational kinetic energy.

Solution:

(a) Moment of inertia: \(I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2 = \frac{1}{2}(10)(0.4)^2 = 0.8\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Torque: \(\tau = FR = 30 \times 0.4 = 12\,\mathrm{N\,m}\).

\[ \alpha = \frac{\tau}{I} = \frac{12}{0.8} = 15\,\mathrm{rad/s^2} \]

(b) Angular displacement: \(\theta = \frac{s}{R} = \frac{4}{0.4} = 10\,\mathrm{rad}\). Using \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\theta\):

\[ \omega^2 = 0 + 2(15)(10) = 300\,\mathrm{rad^2/s^2} \implies \omega = 10\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{rad/s} \approx 17.3\,\mathrm{rad/s} \]

(c) Rotational kinetic energy: \(K = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.8)(300) = 120\,\mathrm{J}\). (Notice this equals the work done by the pull: \(W = Fs = 30 \times 4 = 120\,\mathrm{J}\)).

Worked Example 4: Angular Momentum Conservation and Kinetic Energy Change

Problem (Assumed Scenario): A student stands on a frictionless turntable rotating at \(30\,\mathrm{rev/min}\) with arms outstretched. When the student pulls their arms inward, the moment of inertia drops to \(60\%\) of its initial value. Find the new angular speed and the ratio of final to initial rotational kinetic energy.

Solution:

Since no external torque acts about the vertical axis, \(I_1 \omega_1 = I_2 \omega_2\). Given \(I_2 = 0.6 I_1 = \frac{3}{5}I_1\):

\[ \omega_2 = \frac{I_1}{I_2}\omega_1 = \frac{5}{3} \times 30 = 50\,\mathrm{rev/min} \]

Since \(K = \frac{L^2}{2I}\) and \(L\) is constant:

\[ \frac{K_2}{K_1} = \frac{I_1}{I_2} = \frac{5}{3} \]

Result: The new speed is \(50\,\mathrm{rev/min}\), and the kinetic energy increases by a factor of \(5/3\) due to internal muscular work done by the student.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Three point masses of \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\), and \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\) are located at \((0, 0)\), \((4\,\mathrm{m}, 0)\), and \((0, 4\,\mathrm{m})\) respectively. What are the coordinates of the centre of mass of the system?

  • (A) \(\left(\frac{4}{3}\,\mathrm{m},\, 2\,\mathrm{m}\right)\)
  • (B) \(\left(2\,\mathrm{m},\, \frac{4}{3}\,\mathrm{m}\right)\)
  • (C) \(\left(\frac{2}{3}\,\mathrm{m},\, 2\,\mathrm{m}\right)\)
  • (D) \(\left(\frac{4}{3}\,\mathrm{m},\, \frac{4}{3}\,\mathrm{m}\right)\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Total mass \(M = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6\,\mathrm{kg}\).

\[ X_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{1(0) + 2(4) + 3(0)}{6} = \frac{8}{6} = \frac{4}{3}\,\mathrm{m}, \qquad Y_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{1(0) + 2(0) + 3(4)}{6} = \frac{12}{6} = 2\,\mathrm{m} \]
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A shell of mass \(4\,\mathrm{kg}\) is launched from flat ground at \(45^\circ\) to the horizontal with speed \(20\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{m/s}\). At the highest point of its flight, it explodes into two equal \(2\,\mathrm{kg}\) fragments. One fragment drops vertically downward from rest immediately after the explosion. How far from the launch point does the other fragment land? (Take \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\))

  • (A) \(80\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (B) \(100\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (C) \(120\,\mathrm{m}\)
  • (D) \(160\,\mathrm{m}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: The horizontal range without explosion is \(R = \frac{u^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g} = \frac{(20\sqrt{2})^2 \sin 90^\circ}{10} = \frac{800}{10} = 80\,\mathrm{m}\). Because explosion forces are internal, the CM still lands at \(X_{\mathrm{CM}} = 80\,\mathrm{m}\). The first fragment drops straight down from the peak at \(x_1 = R/2 = 40\,\mathrm{m}\). Using \(X_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2}\):

\[ 80 = \frac{2(40) + 2x_2}{4} \implies 320 = 80 + 2x_2 \implies x_2 = 120\,\mathrm{m} \]
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A force \(\mathbf{F} = (2\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 3\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N}\) acts on a particle whose position vector relative to the origin is \(\mathbf{r} = (\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 2\hat{\mathbf{j}} - \hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{m}\). The torque acting on the particle about the origin is:

  • (A) \((\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{j}} + 7\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)
  • (B) \((-\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 3\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 7\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)
  • (C) \((-\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 7\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)
  • (D) \((5\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 3\hat{\mathbf{j}} - \hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N\,m}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Using \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{\mathbf{i}} & \hat{\mathbf{j}} & \hat{\mathbf{k}} \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & -3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (2 - 3)\hat{\mathbf{i}} - (1 - (-2))\hat{\mathbf{j}} + (-3 - 4)\hat{\mathbf{k}} = (-\hat{\mathbf{i}} - 3\hat{\mathbf{j}} - 7\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{N\,m} \]
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A particle of mass \(0.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) moves along the line \(y = 4\,\mathrm{m}\) in the \(x\)-\(y\) plane with a constant speed of \(6\,\mathrm{m/s}\) in the \(+x\) direction. What is its angular momentum about the origin?

  • (A) \(12\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\) along \(+z\) axis
  • (B) \(12\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\) along \(-z\) axis
  • (C) \(24\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\) along \(-z\) axis
  • (D) Zero, because the motion is in a straight line
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: At any instant, \(\mathbf{r} = x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{j}}\) and \(\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v} = (0.5 \times 6)\hat{\mathbf{i}} = 3\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{kg\,m/s}\). Thus:

\[ \mathbf{l} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p} = (x\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4\hat{\mathbf{j}}) \times 3\hat{\mathbf{i}} = 12(\hat{\mathbf{j}} \times \hat{\mathbf{i}}) = -12\hat{\mathbf{k}}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s} \]
Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A ceiling fan rotating at \(120\,\mathrm{rpm}\) is switched off and decelerates uniformly to rest in \(10\,\mathrm{s}\). How many revolutions does it complete during this \(10\,\mathrm{s}\) interval?

  • (A) \(5\)
  • (B) \(10\)
  • (C) \(20\)
  • (D) \(40\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Initial angular speed \(\omega_0 = \frac{120}{60} = 2\,\mathrm{rev/s}\), final speed \(\omega = 0\). With uniform angular deceleration, the number of revolutions is:

\[ N = \left(\frac{\omega_0 + \omega}{2}\right)t = \left(\frac{2 + 0}{2}\right) \times 10 = 10\,\mathrm{revolutions} \]

  • (A) \(\sqrt{5} : 2\)
  • (B) \(2 : \sqrt{5}\)
  • (C) \(5 : 4\)
  • (D) \(\sqrt{5} : \sqrt{2}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: For a ring about a diameter, \(I_{\mathrm{ring}} = \frac{1}{2}MR^2 \implies k_{\mathrm{ring}} = \frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}\). For a solid sphere about a diameter, \(I_{\mathrm{sphere}} = \frac{2}{5}MR^2 \implies k_{\mathrm{sphere}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{5}}R\). Therefore:

\[ \frac{k_{\mathrm{ring}}}{k_{\mathrm{sphere}}} = \frac{1/\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2/5}} = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2} \]
Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

To keep a rotor spinning at a constant angular speed of \(120\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) against frictional resistance, a motor must apply a steady torque of \(150\,\mathrm{N\,m}\). What power does the motor deliver?

  • (A) \(1.25\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (B) \(9\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (C) \(18\,\mathrm{kW}\)
  • (D) \(36\,\mathrm{kW}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Instantaneous rotational power is \(P = \tau\omega = 150 \times 120 = 18000\,\mathrm{W} = 18\,\mathrm{kW}\).

Practice Question 8 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): A couple acting on a free rigid body produces pure rotational motion without any linear acceleration of its centre of mass.
Reason (R): A couple consists of two equal and opposite forces whose lines of action do not coincide, making the net external force zero while the net torque is non-zero.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Since \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \mathbf{F} + (-\mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{0}\), the acceleration of the centre of mass is zero (\(\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{CM}} = \mathbf{0}\)). However, because the lines of action are separated by a non-zero perpendicular distance \(d\), the net torque \(\tau = Fd \neq 0\), producing pure rotation.

Practice Question 9 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: The centre of gravity of an extended body always coincides with its centre of mass, regardless of the size of the body.
Statement II: For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis that is not an axis of symmetry, the total angular momentum vector \(\mathbf{L}\) need not be parallel to the angular velocity vector \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: Statement I is incorrect because CG and CM coincide only when the gravitational field \(\mathbf{g}\) is uniform across the body; for very large bodies where \(\mathbf{g}\) varies, they differ. Statement II is correct because \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{L}_z + \mathbf{L}_\perp\), and \(\mathbf{L}_\perp\) vanishes in general only when the fixed axis is an axis of symmetry.

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A horizontal circular disc of moment of inertia \(I_1 = 4\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) rotates freely about its vertical symmetry axis at \(10\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). A second non-rotating coaxial disc of moment of inertia \(I_2 = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) is gently dropped onto it. What is the final common angular speed and the loss in kinetic energy due to friction between the discs?

  • (A) \(8\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(40\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(8\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(20\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(12.5\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(40\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(5\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(100\,\mathrm{J}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Friction between the discs is an internal torque for the two-disc system, so total angular momentum is conserved:

\[ I_1 \omega_1 = (I_1 + I_2)\omega_f \implies 4(10) = (4 + 1)\omega_f \implies \omega_f = 8\,\mathrm{rad/s} \]

Initial kinetic energy \(K_i = \frac{1}{2}(4)(10)^2 = 200\,\mathrm{J}\). Final kinetic energy \(K_f = \frac{1}{2}(5)(8)^2 = 160\,\mathrm{J}\). Loss in kinetic energy \(= 200 - 160 = 40\,\mathrm{J}\).

9. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist, and Final Recap

High-Yield NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Assuming \(\mathbf{L}\) is always parallel to \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\): \(\mathbf{L} = I\boldsymbol{\omega}\) holds only when the rotation axis is an axis of symmetry. In general, \(\mathbf{L}\) has a perpendicular component \(\mathbf{L}_\perp\).
  • Confusing Torque and Work: Both have dimensions \([\mathrm{M L^2 T^{-2}}]\), but torque is a vector (\(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}\), unit \(\mathrm{N\,m}\)) while work is a scalar (\(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\), unit \(\mathrm{J}\)).
  • Assuming Kinetic Energy is Conserved with Angular Momentum: When \(I\) changes due to internal forces (e.g., folding arms or dropping a second disc), \(L\) is conserved, but \(K = \frac{L^2}{2I}\) changes!
  • Forgetting Internal Forces Do Not Move the CM: Explosions or mutual attractions never alter \(\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{CM}}\) or \(\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{CM}}\); only \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}\) does.
Quick Revision Checklist & Recap
  • CM Formula: \(\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{\sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i}{M}\); \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} = M\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{CM}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}}{\mathrm{d}t}\).
  • Cross-Product Laws: \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}\), \(\mathbf{l} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}\), \(\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}\).
  • Equilibrium: \(\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) AND \(\sum \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{0}\) (torque origin is arbitrary once \(\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\)).
  • Fixed-Axis Dynamics: \(\tau = I\alpha\), \(K = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{L^2}{2I}\), \(P = \tau\omega\), and \(I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2\) when \(\tau_{\mathrm{ext}} = 0\).
