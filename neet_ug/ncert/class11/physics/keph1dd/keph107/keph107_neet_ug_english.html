NEET UG Physics Complete Standalone Tutorial

Gravitation: Core Principles, Orbital Mechanics & Problem-Solving

A structured, exam-focused guide covering Newton's law of gravitation, variations of gravitational acceleration, potential energy, Kepler's laws, escape speed, and satellite dynamics.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Scope

Gravitation is a high-yield mechanics topic in NEET UG because its mathematical structure closely parallels electrostatics while testing ratio-based reasoning, graph interpretation, and conservation laws. By mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Apply Newton's universal law of gravitation and the superposition principle to point masses and spherically symmetric bodies.
  • Calculate how the acceleration due to gravity \(g\) changes with altitude, depth, and planetary radius or density.
  • Compute gravitational potential energy and gravitational potential for multi-particle systems using the infinity reference convention.
  • Connect Kepler's three laws of planetary motion to angular momentum conservation and inverse-square central forces.
  • Determine escape speed, orbital speed, orbital period, and total mechanical energy of bound satellites.

2. Universal Law of Gravitation & Shell Theorem

2.1 Inverse-Square Law in Scalar and Vector Form

Every particle of matter attracts every other particle with a force that acts along the straight line joining them. For two point masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) separated by a distance \(r\), the magnitude of this mutually attractive force is:

\[ F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \]

Here, \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,kg^{-2}}\) is the universal gravitational constant, first measured accurately by Henry Cavendish using a torsion balance. Unlike electrostatic forces, gravity is always attractive and is independent of the intervening medium.

In vector notation, if \(\mathbf{r}_{12} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1\) is the position vector pointing from \(m_1\) to \(m_2\), and \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12} = \mathbf{r}_{12}/r\) is the corresponding unit vector, then the force \(\mathbf{F}_{21}\) exerted on \(m_2\) by \(m_1\) is directed opposite to \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12}\):

\[ \mathbf{F}_{21} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^3}\,\mathbf{r}_{12} \]

By Newton's third law, \(\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21}\): the two masses pull each other with equal magnitude and opposite direction regardless of how different their masses are.

2.2 Principle of Superposition

When a test mass \(m_0\) interacts with multiple point masses \(m_1, m_2, \dots, m_n\), each pair interacts independently as if the others were absent. The net gravitational force on \(m_0\) is the vector sum of the individual forces:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 + \dots + \mathbf{F}_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbf{F}_i \]

2.3 The Shell Theorem for Extended Spherical Bodies

Because planets and stars are extended spheres rather than point particles, we use the Shell Theorem (derived via integral calculus) for any uniform spherical shell of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\):

  • External point (\(r > R\)): The shell attracts an external point mass \(m\) as if the entire mass \(M\) of the shell were concentrated at its geometric centre, giving \(F = GMm/r^2\).
  • Internal point (\(r < R\)): The net gravitational force exerted by the shell on a point mass anywhere inside the hollow cavity is strictly zero (\(F = 0\)), because pulls from opposite portions of the shell cancel out completely.
Tutorial Diagram 1: By the Shell Theorem, a uniform spherical shell of mass \(M\) pulls an external mass \(m\) as if all mass were at its centre \(O\), whereas an internal mass experiences zero net force from the shell.
Crucial Misconception: No Gravitational Shielding

Although a hollow spherical shell exerts zero force on a mass placed inside it, it does not block the gravitational pull of external bodies (such as the Earth or Sun). Unlike a Faraday cage in electrostatics, gravitational shielding is impossible.

3. Acceleration Due to Gravity (\(g\)) & Its Variations

3.1 Surface Value and Planetary Density

Treating Earth as a spherically symmetric body of mass \(M_E\), radius \(R_E\), and mean density \(\rho\), a mass \(m\) on the surface lies outside all concentric shells. Equating weight \(mg\) to the gravitational pull gives:

\[ g = \frac{G M_E}{R_E^2} = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R_E \]

Notice that \(g\) is independent of the falling object's mass \(m\). Near Earth's surface, \(g \approx 9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Knowing \(g\), \(R_E\), and \(G\) allows us to determine Earth's mass \(M_E = g R_E^2 / G \approx 6.0 \times 10^{24}\,\mathrm{kg}\).

3.2 Variation with Altitude (\(h\))

At a height \(h\) above Earth's surface, the distance from the centre is \(r = R_E + h\). The exact acceleration due to gravity is:

\[ g(h) = \frac{G M_E}{(R_E + h)^2} = \frac{g}{\left(1 + \dfrac{h}{R_E}\right)^2} \]

When the altitude is very small compared to Earth's radius (\(h \ll R_E\)), binomial expansion \((1 + x)^{-2} \approx 1 - 2x\) yields the linear approximation:

\[ g(h) \approx g \left(1 - \frac{2h}{R_E}\right) \quad \text{for } h \ll R_E \]

3.3 Variation with Depth (\(d\))

At a depth \(d\) below the surface of a uniform solid sphere of radius \(R_E\), the particle is at distance \(r = R_E - d\) from the centre. The outer spherical shell of thickness \(d\) exerts zero net force on it. Only the inner sphere of radius \(r = R_E - d\) and mass \(M_{\text{in}} = M_E (r/R_E)^3\) contributes:

\[ g(d) = \frac{G M_{\text{in}}}{(R_E - d)^2} = \frac{G M_E}{R_E^3}(R_E - d) = g \left(1 - \frac{d}{R_E}\right) \]

Thus, \(g\) is maximum at Earth's surface and decreases whether you ascend above it or descend below it, becoming zero at the centre (\(d = R_E\)). For small changes (\(h, d \ll R_E\)), the fractional decrease at height \(h\) is twice that at depth \(d = h\).

Tutorial Diagram 2: Variation of gravitational acceleration \(g(r)\) with radial distance \(r\) from the centre of a uniform solid Earth of radius \(R_E\). Inside (\(r < R_E\)), \(g\) increases linearly; outside (\(r > R_E\)), \(g\) decays as \(1/r^2\).

4. Gravitational Potential & Potential Energy

4.1 Conservative Force & Gravitational Potential Energy

Gravitational force is a central, conservative force: the work done in moving a particle between two points depends only on the initial and final radial distances, not on the path taken. Taking the potential energy at infinite separation (\(r \to \infty\)) to be zero by convention, the gravitational potential energy \(U(r)\) of a two-particle system of masses \(M\) and \(m\) separated by \(r\) is always negative:

\[ U(r) = -\frac{G M m}{r} \]

The negative sign signifies a bound system: an external agent must do positive work to separate the masses to infinity. For a system of \(n\) point masses, the total potential energy is the algebraic sum over all distinct pairs \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\):

\[ U_{\text{total}} = -\sum_{i < j} \frac{G m_i m_j}{r_{ij}} \]

4.2 Near-Surface Approximation vs. Exact Difference

When lifting a mass \(m\) from Earth's surface (\(r_1 = R_E\)) to a height \(h\) (\(r_2 = R_E + h\)), the exact increase in potential energy is:

\[ \Delta U = U(R_E + h) - U(R_E) = G M_E m \left(\frac{1}{R_E} - \frac{1}{R_E + h}\right) = \frac{m g h}{1 + \dfrac{h}{R_E}} \]

Only when \(h \ll R_E\) does this simplify to the familiar classroom formula \(\Delta U \approx mgh\). For large altitudes (comparable to \(R_E\)), using \(mgh\) significantly overestimates the energy required.

4.3 Gravitational Potential (\(V\))

Gravitational potential \(V(r)\) at a point is defined as the gravitational potential energy per unit test mass placed at that point (a scalar quantity, measured in \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\)):

\[ V(r) = \frac{U(r)}{m} = -\frac{G M}{r} \quad (\text{for } r \ge R \text{ outside a uniform sphere}) \]

Inside a uniform spherical shell of radius \(R\), the field is zero, so the potential remains constant everywhere inside and equals its surface value: \(V_{\text{inside}} = -GM/R\).

5. Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

From decades of naked-eye planetary data collected by Tycho Brahe, Johannes Kepler formulated three empirical laws that Newton later explained using his inverse-square law of gravitation:

  1. Law of Orbits: Every planet revolves around the Sun in an elliptical orbit with the Sun situated at one of the two foci. The closest point to the Sun is the perihelion (\(r_{\min} = a(1-e)\)) and the farthest point is the aphelion (\(r_{\max} = a(1+e)\)), where \(a\) is the semi-major axis and \(e\) is the eccentricity.
  2. Law of Areas: The radius vector joining the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time. Mathematically, the areal velocity is constant:
    \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{constant} \]
    Because gravity is a central force (zero torque about the Sun), angular momentum \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}\) is conserved. Consequently, \(r_P v_P = r_A v_A\): a planet moves fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion.
  3. Law of Periods: The square of the orbital time period \(T\) of any planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis \(a\) of its elliptical orbit:
    \[ T^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi^2}{G M_S}\right) a^3 \]
    where \(M_S\) is the mass of the central body (the Sun). This relation holds equally for satellites orbiting a planet when \(M_S\) is replaced by the planet's mass.

6. Escape Speed & Satellite Mechanics

6.1 Escape Speed (\(v_e\))

The escape speed from the surface of a planet of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\) is the minimum launch speed needed for a projectile to reach infinity with zero residual speed (total mechanical energy \(E = 0\)). Applying energy conservation from surface to infinity:

\[ \frac{1}{2} m v_e^2 - \frac{G M m}{R} = 0 \implies v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{R}} = \sqrt{2 g R} \]

For Earth, \(v_e \approx 11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). For the Moon, lower mass and smaller radius give \(v_e \approx 2.3\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\), which is comparable to thermal speeds of gas molecules—explaining why the Moon cannot retain an atmosphere.

Key NEET fact: Escape speed is completely independent of the projectile's mass \(m\) and the angle of projection (neglecting air resistance). If launched with speed \(v > v_e\), the speed far away at infinity is \(v_\infty = \sqrt{v^2 - v_e^2}\).

6.2 Circular Orbit Speed & Time Period

For a satellite of mass \(m\) in a circular orbit of radius \(r = R_E + h\), gravity provides the required centripetal force:

\[ \frac{m v_o^2}{r} = \frac{G M_E m}{r^2} \implies v_o = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{g R_E^2}{R_E + h}} \]

For a satellite skimming very close to Earth's surface (\(h \ll R_E\)), \(v_o = \sqrt{g R_E} \approx 7.9\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\), so \(v_e = \sqrt{2}\,v_o\). Its orbital period is \(T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{R_E/g} \approx 85\,\mathrm{minutes}\).

6.3 Energy of an Orbiting Satellite

For a satellite in a circular orbit of radius \(r = R_E + h\), the kinetic, potential, and total mechanical energies are tightly linked:

\[ K = +\frac{G M_E m}{2r}, \qquad U = -\frac{G M_E m}{r}, \qquad E = K + U = -\frac{G M_E m}{2r} \]

Notice the signature ratio for circular orbits: \(E = -K = \frac{1}{2}U\). The binding energy required to liberate the satellite to infinity is \(|E| = +\frac{G M_E m}{2r}\). Because both the astronaut and the spacecraft are in continuous free fall toward Earth with the same acceleration \(g(h)\), the normal contact force between them is zero, producing the sensation of weightlessness.

8. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Comparing \(g\) at Altitude and Depth

Problem (assumed hypothetical values): On a newly discovered rocky planet of radius \(R_p = 4000\,\mathrm{km}\), the surface gravitational acceleration is \(g_0\). At what depth \(d\) below the surface is the gravitational acceleration equal to its value at an altitude \(h = 100\,\mathrm{km}\) above the surface? (Assume uniform planetary density.)

Solution: Since \(h = 100\,\mathrm{km} \ll R_p = 4000\,\mathrm{km}\) (ratio \(h/R_p = 0.025\)), we can use the linear altitude approximation:

\[ g(h) \approx g_0 \left(1 - \frac{2h}{R_p}\right) \]

At depth \(d\), the exact formula for a uniform sphere is:

\[ g(d) = g_0 \left(1 - \frac{d}{R_p}\right) \]

Equating \(g(d) = g(h)\) gives:

\[ 1 - \frac{d}{R_p} = 1 - \frac{2h}{R_p} \implies d = 2h = 2 \times 100\,\mathrm{km} = 200\,\mathrm{km} \]

Result: The gravitational acceleration at a depth of \(200\,\mathrm{km}\) matches that at an altitude of \(100\,\mathrm{km}\).

Worked Example 2: Gravitational Potential Energy of a Triangular System

Problem (assumed hypothetical values): Three small asteroids of masses \(m\), \(2m\), and \(3m\) are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side length \(L\). Find the total gravitational potential energy of this configuration.

Solution: There are \(\frac{3(3-1)}{2} = 3\) distinct pairs of masses, each separated by distance \(L\). Summing the pairwise potential energies:

\[ U_{\text{total}} = -\frac{G(m)(2m)}{L} - \frac{G(2m)(3m)}{L} - \frac{G(3m)(m)}{L} \] \[ U_{\text{total}} = -\frac{G m^2}{L}(2 + 6 + 3) = -\frac{11 G m^2}{L} \]

Result: The total gravitational potential energy is \(-\dfrac{11 G m^2}{L}\).

Worked Example 3: Satellite Orbit Transfer Energy

Problem (assumed hypothetical values): A communication satellite of mass \(m = 500\,\mathrm{kg}\) orbits Earth in a circular path of radius \(2R_E\). Taking \(g = 10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(R_E = 6.4 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m}\) as standard assumed values, calculate the minimum energy required to shift it to a circular orbit of radius \(3R_E\).

Solution: The total mechanical energy in a circular orbit of radius \(r\) is \(E(r) = -\dfrac{G M_E m}{2r}\). Thus:

\[ \Delta E = E(3R_E) - E(2R_E) = -\frac{G M_E m}{6R_E} - \left(-\frac{G M_E m}{4R_E}\right) = \frac{G M_E m}{12 R_E} \]

Using \(G M_E = g R_E^2\):

\[ \Delta E = \frac{m g R_E}{12} = \frac{500 \times 10 \times 6.4 \times 10^6}{12}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 2.67 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{J} \]

Result: The minimum energy required is approximately \(2.67 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{J}\).

Worked Example 4: Speed Beyond Escape Velocity

Problem (assumed hypothetical values): A probe is launched vertically from a planet whose escape speed is \(v_e = 10\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) with an initial speed of \(v_i = 26\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). Ignoring atmospheric drag, find its speed very far from the planet.

Solution: By conservation of mechanical energy from the surface (\(r = R\)) to infinity (\(r \to \infty\)):

\[ \frac{1}{2} m v_i^2 - \frac{G M m}{R} = \frac{1}{2} m v_\infty^2 + 0 \]

Since \(\dfrac{G M}{R} = \dfrac{1}{2} v_e^2\), we obtain:

\[ v_\infty^2 = v_i^2 - v_e^2 = 26^2 - 10^2 = 676 - 100 = 576\,(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}})^2 \] \[ v_\infty = \sqrt{576} = 24\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}} \]

Result: The probe's residual speed far from the planet is \(24\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\).

9. NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are designed to match the NEET UG Physics syllabus, difficulty, and format. Attempt each question before reviewing the worked solution.

Practice Question 1 · Single-Correct MCQ
If the radius of Earth were to shrink by \(2\%\) while its total mass remained unchanged, how would the acceleration due to gravity \(g\) on the surface change?
  • (A) Decrease by \(2\%\)
  • (B) Increase by \(2\%\)
  • (C) Decrease by \(4\%\)
  • (D) Increase by \(4\%\)
Correct Answer: (D)

Solution: Since \(g = GM_E / R_E^2\) and \(M_E\) is constant, \(g \propto R_E^{-2}\). For small fractional changes, \(\dfrac{\Delta g}{g} = -2\dfrac{\Delta R_E}{R_E}\). Given \(\dfrac{\Delta R_E}{R_E} = -2\%\), we get \(\dfrac{\Delta g}{g} = -2(-2\%) = +4\%\). Hence, \(g\) increases by \(4\%\).

Practice Question 2 · Single-Correct MCQ
At what height above Earth's surface does the acceleration due to gravity drop to \(25\%\) of its surface value? (Let \(R_E\) be Earth's radius.)
  • (A) \(R_E / 2\)
  • (B) \(R_E\)
  • (C) \(2 R_E\)
  • (D) \(3 R_E\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Using the exact altitude formula \(g(h) = g \left(\dfrac{R_E}{R_E + h}\right)^2\): setting \(g(h) = \dfrac{g}{4}\) gives \(\left(\dfrac{R_E}{R_E + h}\right)^2 = \dfrac{1}{4} \implies \dfrac{R_E}{R_E + h} = \dfrac{1}{2}\), so \(R_E + h = 2R_E \implies h = R_E\).

Practice Question 3 · Single-Correct MCQ
Two planets \(X\) and \(Y\) have uniform densities in the ratio \(2:3\) and radii in the ratio \(3:4\). What is the ratio of the acceleration due to gravity on their surfaces, \(g_X : g_Y\)?
  • (A) \(1:2\)
  • (B) \(2:1\)
  • (C) \(8:9\)
  • (D) \(9:8\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Solution: In terms of density \(\rho\) and radius \(R\), \(g = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi G \rho R\), so \(g \propto \rho R\). Therefore, \(\dfrac{g_X}{g_Y} = \left(\dfrac{\rho_X}{\rho_Y}\right)\left(\dfrac{R_X}{R_Y}\right) = \left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)\left(\dfrac{3}{4}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2}\).

Practice Question 4 · Single-Correct MCQ
A body is weighed at Earth's surface as \(180\,\mathrm{N}\). Assuming Earth is a uniform sphere of radius \(R_E\), what will be its weight at a depth \(d = R_E / 3\) below the surface?
  • (A) \(60\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (B) \(90\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (C) \(120\,\mathrm{N}\)
  • (D) \(150\,\mathrm{N}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Weight \(W(d) = m g(d) = m g_0 \left(1 - \dfrac{d}{R_E}\right) = W_0 \left(1 - \dfrac{1}{3}\right) = 180 \times \dfrac{2}{3} = 120\,\mathrm{N}\).

Practice Question 5 · Single-Correct MCQ
A particle of mass \(m\) is placed at the centre of a uniform hemispherical shell of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\). What is the magnitude of the gravitational potential at the centre?
  • (A) \(0\)
  • (B) \(-\dfrac{G M}{2R}\)
  • (C) \(-\dfrac{G M}{R}\)
  • (D) \(-\dfrac{2 G M}{R}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Every mass element \(\mathrm{d}m\) of the hemispherical shell lies at the exact same distance \(R\) from the centre. Because gravitational potential is a scalar, \(V = \int -\dfrac{G\,\mathrm{d}m}{R} = -\dfrac{G}{R}\int \mathrm{d}m = -\dfrac{GM}{R}\).

Practice Question 6 · Single-Correct MCQ
A satellite revolves around a planet in an elliptical orbit. Its speeds at the closest distance \(r_1\) and farthest distance \(r_2\) from the planet are \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) respectively. Which relation is correct?
  • (A) \(v_1 / v_2 = r_1 / r_2\)
  • (B) \(v_1 / v_2 = r_2 / r_1\)
  • (C) \(v_1 / v_2 = (r_2 / r_1)^2\)
  • (D) \(v_1 / v_2 = \sqrt{r_2 / r_1}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: At both periapsis and apoapsis, the velocity vector is perpendicular to the radius vector. By conservation of angular momentum, \(m v_1 r_1 = m v_2 r_2 \implies \dfrac{v_1}{v_2} = \dfrac{r_2}{r_1}\).

Practice Question 7 · Single-Correct MCQ
An exoplanet orbits a star in a circular path of radius \(4R\) with a time period \(T\). Another exoplanet orbits the same star in a circular path of radius \(R\). What is the orbital period of the second exoplanet?
  • (A) \(T / 2\)
  • (B) \(T / 4\)
  • (C) \(T / 8\)
  • (D) \(T / 16\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: By Kepler's third law, \(T^2 \propto r^3\). Thus \(\left(\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^2 = \left(\dfrac{R}{4R}\right)^3 = \dfrac{1}{64}\), giving \(\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = \dfrac{1}{8} \implies T_2 = \dfrac{T}{8}\).

Practice Question 8 · Single-Correct MCQ
Planet \(A\) has twice the mass and twice the radius of Earth. If the escape speed from Earth is \(11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\), what is the escape speed from planet \(A\)?
  • (A) \(5.6\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(15.8\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(22.4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Escape speed is \(v_e = \sqrt{\dfrac{2GM}{R}}\). Since both \(M\) and \(R\) are doubled (\(M_A = 2M_E\), \(R_A = 2R_E\)), the ratio \(M_A / R_A = M_E / R_E\) remains unchanged. Thus \(v_{e,A} = 11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 9 · Single-Correct MCQ
A satellite of mass \(m\) is in a circular orbit around Earth with kinetic energy \(K\). What is the minimum additional energy required to free the satellite completely from Earth's gravitational pull?
  • (A) \(K / 2\)
  • (B) \(K\)
  • (C) \(2K\)
  • (D) \(3K / 2\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: For a circular orbit, the total energy is \(E = -K\). To escape to infinity where total energy is \(0\), the energy to be supplied is \(0 - E = +K\).

Practice Question 10 · Single-Correct MCQ
A body of mass \(m\) is projected from Earth's surface to a maximum height equal to \(2R_E\) above the surface. What is the work done against gravity?
  • (A) \(\dfrac{1}{3} m g R_E\)
  • (B) \(\dfrac{2}{3} m g R_E\)
  • (C) \(2 m g R_E\)
  • (D) \(3 m g R_E\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Solution: Using \(\Delta U = \dfrac{m g h}{1 + h/R_E}\) with \(h = 2R_E\), we get \(\Delta U = \dfrac{m g (2R_E)}{1 + 2} = \dfrac{2}{3} m g R_E\).

Practice Question 11 · Assertion–Reason MCQ
Assertion (A): An astronaut inside an orbiting space station feels weightless.
Reason (R): At the altitude of the space station, Earth's gravitational force on the astronaut is zero.
  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Assertion (A) is true because the astronaut and the station are in continuous free fall with the same centripetal acceleration, so the normal reaction is zero. Reason (R) is false because Earth's gravitational pull is still strong at typical orbital altitudes (it is what keeps the station in orbit!).

Practice Question 12 · Statement-Based MCQ
Read the two statements below and choose the correct option:
Statement I: Kepler's second law (law of areas) holds for any central force, not exclusively for the inverse-square gravitational force.
Statement II: For a planet moving in an elliptical orbit around the Sun, its linear momentum remains constant throughout the orbit.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (C)

Solution: Statement I is correct: any central force produces zero torque about the force centre, conserving angular momentum \(\mathbf{L}\) and keeping areal velocity \(L/(2m)\) constant. Statement II is incorrect: the planet experiences a non-zero net external force from the Sun, so its linear momentum \(\mathbf{p}\) continuously changes in both magnitude and direction.

10. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Avoid These Frequent Exam Errors
  • Using \(\Delta U = mgh\) at large heights: Only valid when \(h \ll R_E\). For \(h\) comparable to \(R_E\), always use \(\Delta U = \dfrac{mgh}{1 + h/R_E}\) or \(-\dfrac{GMm}{r_2} + \dfrac{GMm}{r_1}\).
  • Using \(g(h) \approx g(1 - 2h/R_E)\) for large altitudes: When \(h\) is a significant fraction of \(R_E\) (e.g., \(h = R_E/2\) or \(h = R_E\)), you must use the exact formula \(g(h) = \dfrac{g}{(1 + h/R_E)^2}\).
  • Forgetting the negative sign in \(U\) and \(E\): Bound orbits always have \(E < 0\). When a satellite moves to a higher orbit, its total energy \(E\) increases (becomes less negative), even though its orbital speed and kinetic energy \(K\) decrease.
  • Confusing weightlessness with zero gravity: Gravity provides the centripetal force in orbit; weightlessness occurs because the normal reaction force is zero during free fall.

Quick Pre-Exam Checklist

  • Can you write \(g\) in terms of both \((M, R)\) and \((\rho, R)\)?
  • Do you remember that \(g\) is maximum at the surface and zero at the centre?
  • Can you instantly relate \(K\), \(U\), and \(E\) for a circular orbit (\(E = -K = U/2\))?
  • Do you know that \(v_e = \sqrt{2}\,v_o\) near a planet's surface and is independent of projectile mass and launch angle?

11. Concise Final Recap

Core Takeaway: Gravitation is governed by an inverse-square central force \(F = GMm/r^2\), which ensures conservation of both angular momentum (yielding Kepler's law of areas) and total mechanical energy. Outside any spherically symmetric mass, gravity behaves as if all mass were at the centre; inside a uniform hollow shell, the shell exerts zero net force. Gravitational acceleration peaks at Earth's surface and decreases both upward and downward. Finally, bound circular orbits are characterized by negative total energy \(E = -GMm/(2r) = -K = U/2\), whereas escape requires \(E \ge 0\), achieved at speed \(v_e = \sqrt{2gR_E} \approx 11.2\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\).
