After studying this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Explain why the cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of life and trace the formulation of the modern Cell Theory.
  • Compare cell sizes, shapes, and the fundamental organisation of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  • Describe the prokaryotic cell envelope, mesosomes, surface appendages (flagella, pili, fimbriae), \(70\text{S}\) ribosomes, and inclusion bodies.

Unit Perspective: Cellular Organisation and Reductionist Biology

Biology is the study of living organisms. While describing the form and appearance of organisms highlights their vast diversity, the cell theory emphasises the unity underlying this diversity—namely, the cellular organisation of all life forms. A critical mystery of living phenomena (physiological and behavioural processes) is the requirement of the integrity of cellular organisation for living phenomena to be demonstrated or observed.

To study and understand physiological and behavioural processes, scientists take a physico-chemical approach and use cell-free systems to investigate. By analysing living tissues for elements and compounds, we identify what types of organic compounds are present and ask how they carry out gross physiological processes such as digestion, excretion, memory, defense, and recognition. Answering what forms the molecular basis of all physiological processes—and explaining abnormal processes during diseased conditions—by applying the concepts and techniques of physics and chemistry to understand biology is called Reductionist Biology.

Pioneers in Structural Biology: G.N. Ramachandran (1922 – 2001)

  • Born: October 8, 1922, in a small town near Cochin on the southwestern coast of India. His father, a professor of mathematics at a local college, shaped his strong interest in mathematics.
  • Education: Graduated in 1942 as the top-ranking student in the B.Sc. (Honors) Physics course of the University of Madras. Received his Ph.D. from Cambridge University in 1949.
  • Scientific Influence: While at Cambridge, he met Linus Pauling and was deeply influenced by Pauling's publications on models of the α-helix and β-sheet structures, which directed Ramachandran's attention to solving the structure of collagen.
  • Major Contributions: Founder of the 'Madras school' of conformational analysis of biopolymers. His discovery of the triple helical structure of collagen published in Nature in 1954 and his analysis of the allowed conformations of proteins through the use of the 'Ramachandran plot' rank among the most outstanding contributions in structural biology. He passed away at the age of 78 on April 7, 2001.

2. What is a Cell? & The Cell Theory

What makes an organism living, or what does a living thing possess that an inanimate object lacks? The answer is the presence of the basic unit of life—the cell—in all living organisms. Organisms composed of a single cell are called unicellular organisms, while those composed of many cells are called multicellular organisms.

Unicellular organisms are capable of:

  1. Independent existence, and
  2. Performing the essential functions of life.

Anything less than a complete structure of a cell does not ensure independent living. Hence, the cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms.

Historical Milestones & Cell Theory

  • Antonie Von Leeuwenhoek: First saw and described a live cell.
  • Robert Brown (1831): Later discovered and described the nucleus as a cell organelle.
  • Microscopy: The invention of the microscope and its improvement leading to the electron microscope (in the 1950s) revealed all the structural details of the cell.
  • Matthias Schleiden (1838): A German botanist who examined a large number of plants and observed that all plants are composed of different kinds of cells which form the tissues of the plant.
  • Theodore Schwann (1839): A German zoologist who studied different types of animal cells and reported that cells had a thin outer layer which is today known as the plasma membrane. Based on his studies on plant tissues, he also concluded that the presence of a cell wall is a unique character of plant cells. On this basis, Schwann proposed the hypothesis that the bodies of animals and plants are composed of cells and products of cells.
  • Formulation & Limitation: Schleiden and Schwann together formulated the cell theory. However, this theory did not explain how new cells were formed.
  • Rudolf Virchow (1855): First explained that cells divided and new cells are formed from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula-e cellula). He modified the hypothesis of Schleiden and Schwann to give the cell theory a final shape.

Modern Cell Theory

Cell theory as understood today states:

  1. All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
  2. All cells arise from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula-e cellula).

An Overview of the Cell

  • Outer Boundaries: A typical plant cell (such as an onion peel cell) has a distinct cell wall as its outer boundary and just within it is the cell membrane. Animal cells (such as human cheek cells) have an outer membrane as the delimiting structure of the cell and lack a cell wall.
  • Nucleus & Genetic Material: Inside each eukaryotic cell is a dense membrane-bound structure called the nucleus, which contains the chromosomes that in turn contain the genetic material, DNA.
    • Eukaryotic cells: Cells that have membrane-bound nuclei.
    • Prokaryotic cells: Cells that lack a membrane-bound nucleus.
  • Cytoplasm: In both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, a semi-fluid matrix called cytoplasm occupies the volume of the cell. The cytoplasm is the main arena of cellular activities in both plant and animal cells; various chemical reactions occur in it to keep the cell in the 'living state'.
  • Membrane-Bound Organelles: Besides the nucleus, eukaryotic cells have other membrane-bound distinct structures called organelles like the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), the Golgi complex, lysosomes, mitochondria, microbodies, and vacuoles. Prokaryotic cells lack such membrane-bound organelles.
  • Non-Membrane Bound Organelles:
    • Ribosomes: Found in all cells (both eukaryotic and prokaryotic). Within the cell, ribosomes are found not only in the cytoplasm, but also within two organelles—chloroplasts (in plants) and mitochondria—and on rough ER.
    • Centrosome: Present in animal cells; helps in cell division.

3. Prokaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic cells are represented by bacteria, blue-green algae (cyanobacteria), mycoplasma, and PPLO (Pleuro Pneumonia Like Organisms). They are generally smaller and multiply more rapidly than eukaryotic cells.

Basic Shapes of Bacteria

Although prokaryotes exhibit a wide variety of shapes and functions, their cellular organisation is fundamentally similar. The four basic shapes of bacteria are:

  1. Bacillus: Rod-like
  2. Coccus: Spherical
  3. Vibrio: Comma-shaped
  4. Spirillum: Spiral

General Organisation of Prokaryotes

  • Cell Wall & Exception: All prokaryotes have a cell wall surrounding the cell membrane except in Mycoplasma (which is cell-wall-less).
  • Cytoplasm & Genetic Material: The semi-fluid matrix filling the cell is the cytoplasm. There is no well-defined nucleus. The genetic material is basically naked, not enveloped by a nuclear membrane.
  • Genomic DNA vs. Plasmid DNA: In addition to the genomic DNA (the single chromosome / circular DNA), many bacteria have small circular DNA outside the genomic DNA called plasmids.
    • Functions of Plasmid DNA: Confers certain unique phenotypic characters to such bacteria (e.g., resistance to antibiotics) and is used to monitor bacterial transformation with foreign DNA.
  • Organelles & Unique Structures: No membrane-bound organelles (like those in eukaryotes) are found in prokaryotic cells except for ribosomes (\(70\text{S}\)). Prokaryotes possess unique reserve deposits called inclusion bodies and a specialised differentiated form of cell membrane called the mesosome (infoldings of the cell membrane).

Cell Envelope and its Modifications

Most prokaryotic cells, particularly bacterial cells, have a chemically complex cell envelope consisting of a tightly bound three-layered structure that acts together as a single protective unit (though each layer performs a distinct function):

  1. Glycocalyx (Outermost layer): Differs in composition and thickness among different bacteria.
    • Slime layer: A loose sheath in some bacteria.
    • Capsule: A thick and tough layer in other bacteria.
  2. Cell Wall (Middle layer): Determines the shape of the cell and provides strong structural support to prevent the bacterium from bursting or collapsing.
  3. Plasma Membrane (Innermost layer): Selectively permeable in nature and interacts with the outside world. Structurally, this membrane is similar to that of eukaryotes.

Gram Staining Classification: On the basis of differences in the cell envelopes and the manner in which they respond to the staining procedure developed by Gram, bacteria are classified into two groups:

  • Gram positive bacteria: Those that take up the Gram stain.
  • Gram negative bacteria: Those that do not take up the Gram stain.

Membranous Extensions in Prokaryotes

  • Mesosome: A special membranous structure formed by the extensions of the plasma membrane into the cell in the form of vesicles, tubules, and lamellae.
    • Functions of Mesosomes: Help in (1) cell wall formation, (2) DNA replication and distribution to daughter cells, (3) respiration, (4) secretion processes, and (5) increasing the surface area of the plasma membrane and enzymatic content.
  • Chromatophores: In some prokaryotes like cyanobacteria, there are other membranous extensions into the cytoplasm called chromatophores which contain pigments.

Surface Appendages: Flagella, Pili, and Fimbriae

Appendage Structure & Origin Role / Function
Flagella Thin filamentous extensions from the cell wall in motile bacteria. Composed of three parts: filament (longest portion extending from cell surface to outside), hook, and basal body. Motility (locomotion). Bacteria show a range in number and arrangement of flagella.
Pili Elongated tubular structures made of a special protein. Do not play a role in motility.
Fimbriae Small bristle-like fibres sprouting out of the cell. Do not play a role in motility. Help attach the bacteria to rocks in streams and also to host tissues.

Ribosomes and Inclusion Bodies in Prokaryotes

  • Prokaryotic Ribosomes: Associated with the plasma membrane of the cell. They are about \(15\,\mathrm{nm} \times 20\,\mathrm{nm}\) in size and are made of two subunits—\(50\text{S}\) and \(30\text{S}\) units—which when present together form \(70\text{S}\) prokaryotic ribosomes. Ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis.
  • Polyribosomes (Polysome): Several ribosomes may attach to a single mRNA and form a chain called polyribosomes or a polysome. The ribosomes of a polysome translate the mRNA into proteins.
  • Inclusion Bodies: Reserve material in prokaryotic cells is stored in the cytoplasm in the form of inclusion bodies. They are not bound by any membrane system and lie free in the cytoplasm.
    • Examples: Phosphate granules, cyanophycean granules, and glycogen granules.
    • Gas vacuoles: Found in blue-green and purple and green photosynthetic bacteria.

4. Eukaryotic Cells: Membrane, Wall, and Organelles

Eukaryotes include all the protists, plants, animals, and fungi. Eukaryotic cells feature:

  • Extensive compartmentalisation of cytoplasm through the presence of membrane-bound organelles.
  • An organised nucleus with a double-layered nuclear envelope.
  • A variety of complex locomotory and cytoskeletal structures.
  • Genetic material organised into chromosomes.

Key Differences Between Plant and Animal Cells

  • Plant cells possess: Cell wall, plastids, and a large central vacuole (all of which are absent in animal cells).
  • Animal cells possess: Centrioles (centrosome), which are absent in almost all plant cells.

4.1 Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane)

The detailed structure of the cell membrane was studied only after the advent of the electron microscope in the 1950s. Chemical studies on the cell membrane, especially in human red blood cells (RBCs), enabled scientists to deduce the possible structure of the plasma membrane.

Chemical Composition

  • Composed of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates.
  • Lipids: Major lipids are phospholipids arranged in a bilayer.
    • Polar heads (hydrophilic): Arranged towards the outer sides.
    • Hydrophobic tails (nonpolar): Composed of saturated hydrocarbons arranged towards the inner part. This ensures that the nonpolar tail of saturated hydrocarbons is protected from the aqueous environment.
    • In addition to phospholipids, the membrane also contains cholesterol.
  • Protein-to-Lipid Ratio: Varies considerably in different cell types. In human beings, the membrane of the erythrocyte (RBC) has approximately \(52\%\) protein and \(40\%\) lipids.
  • Classification of Membrane Proteins (based on ease of extraction):
    • Peripheral proteins: Lie on the surface of the membrane (easily extracted).
    • Integral proteins: Partially or totally buried in the membrane.

Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972)

Proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1972, the widely accepted Fluid Mosaic Model states that the quasi-fluid nature of lipid enables lateral movement of proteins within the overall bilayer. This ability to move within the membrane is measured as its fluidity.

Functional Importance of Membrane Fluidity: Essential for cell growth, formation of intercellular junctions, secretion, endocytosis, and cell division.

Transport Across the Plasma Membrane

One of the most important functions of the plasma membrane is the transport of molecules across it. The membrane is selectively permeable to some molecules present on either side of it.

Transport Type Direction Relative to Gradient ATP Energy Requirement Mechanism & Examples
Passive Transport: Simple Diffusion Along the concentration gradient (higher to lower concentration) No energy required Neutral solutes move across the membrane by simple diffusion.
Passive Transport: Osmosis From higher to lower water concentration No energy required Movement of water by diffusion across the selectively permeable membrane.
Facilitated Diffusion (Carrier-mediated) Along the concentration gradient No energy required Polar molecules cannot pass through the nonpolar lipid bilayer; they require a carrier protein of the membrane to facilitate their transport.
Active Transport Against the concentration gradient (lower to higher concentration) Energy-dependent process (ATP is utilised) Pumps ions or molecules against their gradient. Example: \(\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}\) Pump.

4.2 Cell Wall

A non-living rigid structure called the cell wall forms an outer covering for the plasma membrane of fungi and plants.

  • Functions of the Cell Wall:
    1. Gives shape to the cell.
    2. Protects the cell from mechanical damage and infection.
    3. Helps in cell-to-cell interaction.
    4. Provides a barrier to undesirable macromolecules.
  • Chemical Composition by Organism Group:
    • Algae: Cell wall is made of cellulose, galactans, mannans, and minerals like calcium carbonate (\(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\)).
    • Other Plants: Consists of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectins, and proteins.
  • Layers of the Plant Cell Wall:
    • Primary Wall: The cell wall of a young plant cell; it is capable of growth, which gradually diminishes as the cell matures.
    • Secondary Wall: Formed as the cell matures on the inner (towards plasma membrane) side of the cell.
    • Middle Lamella: A layer mainly of calcium pectate which holds or glues the different neighbouring cells together.
  • Plasmodesmata: The cell wall and middle lamellae may be traversed by plasmodesmata, which connect the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells.

4.3 The Endomembrane System

While each membranous organelle is distinct in terms of its structure and function, many of these are considered together as an endomembrane system because their functions are coordinated.

  • Included in the Endomembrane System (4 organelles):
    1. Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)
    2. Golgi complex (Golgi apparatus)
    3. Lysosomes
    4. Vacuoles
  • Excluded from the Endomembrane System: Mitochondria, chloroplasts, and peroxisomes are not considered part of the endomembrane system because their functions are not coordinated with the above components.

4.3.1 The Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

Electron microscopic studies of eukaryotic cells reveal a network or reticulum of tiny tubular structures scattered in the cytoplasm called the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). ER divides the intracellular space into two distinct compartments:

  • Luminal compartment: Space inside the ER.
  • Extra-luminal compartment: The cytoplasm outside the ER.
Feature Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER) Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (SER)
Surface Ribosomes Bears ribosomes (\(80\text{S}\)) attached to its outer surface. Devoid of ribosomes; appears smooth.
Structural Continuity Extensive and continuous with the outer membrane of the nucleus. Tubular network continuous with RER.
Primary Function Actively involved in protein synthesis and secretion. Major site for synthesis of lipid. In animal cells, lipid-like steroidal hormones are synthesised in SER.

4.3.2 Golgi Apparatus

  • Discovery: Camillo Golgi (1898) first observed densely stained reticular structures near the nucleus, later named Golgi bodies after him.
  • Structure: Consists of many flat, disc-shaped sacs or cisternae of \(0.5\,\mathrm{µm}\text{ to }1.0\,\mathrm{µm}\) diameter, stacked parallel to each other. A varied number of cisternae are present in a Golgi complex.
  • Polarity of Cisternae: Concentrically arranged near the nucleus with two distinct but interconnected faces:
    • Convex cis face: The forming face.
    • Concave trans face: The maturing face.
  • Coordinated Function with ER: Principally performs the function of packaging materials to be delivered either to intracellular targets or secreted outside the cell.
    • Materials to be packaged in the form of vesicles from the ER fuse with the cis face of the Golgi apparatus and move towards the maturing face. This explains why the Golgi apparatus remains in close association with the endoplasmic reticulum.
    • A number of proteins synthesised by ribosomes on the endoplasmic reticulum are modified in the cisternae of the Golgi apparatus before they are released from its trans face.
    • Key Biosynthetic Site: Golgi apparatus is the important site of formation of glycoproteins and glycolipids.

4.3.3 Lysosomes

  • Origin: Single membrane-bound vesicular structures formed by the process of packaging in the Golgi apparatus.
  • Enzymatic Content: Isolated lysosomal vesicles are very rich in almost all types of hydrolytic enzymes (hydrolases – lipases, proteases, carbohydrases) that are optimally active at the acidic pH.
  • Function: Capable of digesting carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids.

4.3.4 Vacuoles

  • Structure & Contents: Membrane-bound space found in the cytoplasm containing water, sap, excretory product, and other materials not useful for the cell.
  • Tonoplast: The vacuole is bound by a single membrane called the tonoplast. In plant cells, vacuoles can occupy up to \(90\%\) of the volume of the cell.
  • Active Transport Across Tonoplast: In plants, the tonoplast facilitates the transport of a number of ions and other materials against concentration gradients into the vacuole; hence, their concentration is significantly higher in the vacuole than in the cytoplasm.
  • Specialised Vacuoles:
    • Contractile vacuole (in Amoeba): Important for osmoregulation and excretion.
    • Food vacuoles (in many cells, as in protists): Formed by engulfing food particles.

4.4 Mitochondria and Plastids (Semi-Autonomous Double-Membrane Organelles)

4.4.1 Mitochondria

  • Visibility & Number: Unless specifically stained, mitochondria are not easily visible under the microscope. The number of mitochondria per cell is variable depending on the physiological activity of the cells.
  • Shape & Dimensions: Considerably variable in shape and size. Typically sausage-shaped or cylindrical having:
    • Diameter: \(0.2\text{–}1.0\,\mathrm{µm}\) (average \(0.5\,\mathrm{µm}\))
    • Length: \(1.0\text{–}4.1\,\mathrm{µm}\)
  • Double Membrane Organisation: Outer membrane and inner membrane divide its lumen distinctly into two aqueous compartments: the outer compartment and the inner compartment.
    • Outer membrane: Forms the continuous limiting boundary of the organelle.
    • Inner compartment (Matrix): Filled with a dense homogeneous substance called the matrix.
    • Cristae: The inner membrane forms a number of infoldings called cristae (singular: crista) towards the matrix. Cristae increase the surface area.
    • Both membranes have their own specific enzymes associated with mitochondrial function.
  • Function: Sites of aerobic respiration (oxidative phosphorylation). They produce cellular energy in the form of ATP, hence they are called the 'power houses' of the cell.
  • Matrix Components & Division: The matrix possesses a single circular DNA molecule, a few RNA molecules, ribosomes (\(70\text{S}\)), and the components required for the synthesis of proteins. Mitochondria divide by fission.

4.4.2 Plastids

Plastids are found in all plant cells and in euglenoides. They are easily observed under the microscope as they are large. Based on the type of pigments they bear, plastids are classified into three types:

  1. Chloroplasts (Green plastids): Contain chlorophyll and carotenoid pigments responsible for trapping light energy essential for photosynthesis.
  2. Chromoplasts (Coloured plastids): Contain fat-soluble carotenoid pigments like carotene, xanthophylls, and others. This gives the part of the plant a yellow, orange, or red colour.
  3. Leucoplasts (Colourless plastids): Colourless plastids of varied shapes and sizes with stored nutrients:
    • Amyloplasts: Store carbohydrates (starch), e.g., potato.
    • Elaioplasts: Store oils and fats.
    • Aleuroplasts: Store proteins.

Detailed Structure of Chloroplasts

  • Location, Shape & Size: Majority of the chloroplasts of green plants are found in the mesophyll cells of the leaves. They are lens-shaped, oval, spherical, discoid, or even ribbon-like organelles having:
    • Length: \(5\text{–}10\,\mathrm{µm}\)
    • Width: \(2\text{–}4\,\mathrm{µm}\)
  • Number per Cell: Varies from \(1\) per cell in Chlamydomonas (a green alga) to \(20\text{–}40\) per cell in the mesophyll.
  • Membranes & Stroma: Double membrane-bound. Of the two, the inner chloroplast membrane is relatively less permeable. The space limited by the inner membrane of the chloroplast is called the stroma.
  • Thylakoids, Grana & Stroma Lamellae:
    • Thylakoids: Organised flattened membranous sacs present in the stroma. The membrane of the thylakoids encloses a space called a lumen. Chlorophyll pigments are present in the thylakoids.
    • Grana (singular: granum): Thylakoids arranged in stacks like the piles of coins (also called intergranal thylakoids). Grana are the site of light reactions.
    • Stroma lamellae: Flat membranous tubules connecting the thylakoids of the different grana.
  • Stroma Contents: Contains enzymes required for the synthesis of carbohydrates and proteins (site of dark reactions). It also contains small, double-stranded circular DNA molecules and \(70\text{S}\) ribosomes (smaller than the \(80\text{S}\) cytoplasmic ribosomes).

5. Ribosomes, Cytoskeleton, Locomotory Structures & Nucleus

5.1 Ribosomes

  • Discovery: Granular structures first observed under the electron microscope as dense particles by George Palade (1953).
  • Composition: Composed of ribonucleic acid (RNA) and proteins and are not surrounded by any membrane.
  • Types & Subunits: Each ribosome has two subunits (larger and smaller):
    • Eukaryotic ribosomes (\(80\text{S}\)): Composed of \(60\text{S}\) (larger) and \(40\text{S}\) (smaller) subunits.
    • Prokaryotic ribosomes (\(70\text{S}\)): Composed of \(50\text{S}\) (larger) and \(30\text{S}\) (smaller) subunits. (Also found inside eukaryotic mitochondria and chloroplasts).
  • Meaning of 'S': Stands for Svedberg's Unit (sedimentation coefficient); it is indirectly a measure of density and size.

5.2 Cytoskeleton

An elaborate network of filamentous proteinaceous structures consisting of microtubules, microfilaments, and intermediate filaments present in the cytoplasm is collectively referred to as the cytoskeleton.

Functions: Mechanical support, motility, and maintenance of the shape of the cell.

5.3 Cilia and Flagella vs. Centrosome and Centrioles

5.4 Nucleus and Chromosomes

  • Discovery & Naming:
    • Robert Brown (1831): First described the nucleus as a cell organelle.
    • Flemming: Gave the name chromatin to the material of the nucleus stained by basic dyes.
  • Variation in Number of Nuclei per Cell: Normally, there is only one nucleus per cell, though multinucleated variations occur. Some mature cells even lack a nucleus (enucleated):
    • Erythrocytes (RBCs) of many mammals
    • Sieve tube cells of vascular plants

    Conceptual Note: Mature mammalian erythrocytes and sieve tube cells are considered living while functional because they maintain cytoplasmic metabolic activities (and sieve tube elements are closely regulated by adjacent nucleated companion cells).

  • Interphase Nucleus Components: The nucleus of a cell when it is not dividing (interphase nucleus) consists of:
    1. Nuclear Envelope: Consists of two parallel membranes with a space between them of \(10\text{ to }50\,\mathrm{nm}\) called the perinuclear space. It forms a barrier between materials inside the nucleus and the cytoplasm. The outer membrane usually remains continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum and bears ribosomes on it.
    2. Nuclear Pores: At a number of places the nuclear envelope is interrupted by minute pores formed by the fusion of its two membranes. Nuclear pores are the passages through which movement of RNA and protein molecules takes place in both directions between the nucleus and the cytoplasm.
    3. Nucleoplasm (Nuclear Matrix): Contains the nucleolus and chromatin.
    4. Nucleolus (plural: nucleoli): One or more spherical structures present in the nucleoplasm. It is not a membrane-bound structure; its content is continuous with the rest of the nucleoplasm.
      • Function: Site for active ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis. Larger and more numerous nucleoli are present in cells actively carrying out protein synthesis.
    5. Chromatin: Loose and indistinct network of nucleoprotein fibres in the interphase nucleus. Chromatin contains DNA, some basic proteins called histones, some non-histone proteins, and also RNA.

Chromosomes and Classification Based on Centromere Position

During different stages of cell division, cells show structured chromosomes in place of the nucleus. A single human cell has approximately a \(2\,\mathrm{m}\) (two metre) long thread of DNA distributed among its \(46\) (\(23\) pairs) chromosomes.

  • Centromere (Primary Constriction): Every chromosome (visible only in dividing cells) essentially has a primary constriction or centromere, which holds two chromatids of a chromosome together.
  • Kinetochores: Disc-shaped structures present on the sides of the centromere (where spindle fibres attach during cell division).
  • Secondary Constriction & Satellite: Sometimes a few chromosomes have non-staining secondary constrictions at a constant location. This gives the appearance of a small fragment called the satellite.
Chromosome Type Position of Centromere Relative Arm Lengths
1. Metacentric Middle centromere Forms two equal arms of the chromosome.
2. Sub-metacentric Slightly away from the middle of the chromosome Results in one shorter arm and one longer arm.
3. Acrocentric Situated close to its end Forms one extremely short and one very long arm.
4. Telocentric Terminal centromere (at the tip) Centromere is at the terminal end.

7. Worked Conceptual Examples & Guided Discussions

The following worked examples address core conceptual inquiries and analytical comparisons essential for complete mastery of the chapter.

Worked Example 1: Why is the cell considered the basic unit of life, and how do multicellular organisms exhibit division of labour?

Discussion: In unicellular organisms, a single cell is capable of (i) independent existence and (ii) performing all essential functions of life. Anything less than a complete structure of a cell does not ensure independent living. Therefore, the cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms. In multicellular organisms, the body is composed of millions of cells differentiated into various shapes (such as biconcave red blood cells for gas transport, long branched nerve cells for impulse conduction, or oval mesophyll cells rich in chloroplasts for photosynthesis) and tissues, allowing different groups of cells to specialise in distinct physiological functions—a phenomenon known as division of labour.

Worked Example 2: What is a mesosome in a prokaryotic cell, and what functions does it perform?

Discussion: A mesosome is a specialised differentiated form of the cell membrane characteristic of prokaryotes. It is formed by the infoldings/extensions of the plasma membrane into the cell in the form of vesicles, tubules, and lamellae. Its functions are:

  1. Cell wall formation.
  2. DNA replication and distribution to daughter cells.
  3. Respiration and secretion processes.
  4. Increasing the surface area of the plasma membrane and its enzymatic content.

Worked Example 3: How do neutral solutes and polar molecules differ in their transport across the plasma membrane?

Discussion:

  • Neutral solutes: Move across the plasma membrane by simple diffusion along the concentration gradient (from higher concentration to lower concentration) without any requirement of energy (passive transport).
  • Polar molecules: Cannot pass directly through the nonpolar (hydrophobic) lipid bilayer in the same way. Instead, they require a carrier protein of the membrane to facilitate their transport across the membrane (facilitated diffusion). When ions or molecules are transported against their concentration gradient (from lower to higher concentration), energy in the form of ATP is utilised via active transport (e.g., the \(\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}\) pump).

Worked Example 4: Both lysosomes and vacuoles are endomembrane structures, yet they differ in terms of their functions. Explain.

Discussion: Both lysosomes and vacuoles are single membrane-bound organelles whose functions are coordinated with the ER and Golgi apparatus as part of the endomembrane system, yet their physiological roles are distinct:

  • Lysosomes: Formed by packaging in the Golgi apparatus, they are rich in hydrolytic enzymes (hydrolases—lipases, proteases, carbohydrases) optimally active at acidic pH. Their primary function is the intracellular digestion of carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids.
  • Vacuoles: Bound by a single membrane called the tonoplast, they store water, sap, excretory products, and other materials not useful for the cell. In plants, the tonoplast actively transports ions and materials against concentration gradients into the vacuole. In Amoeba, contractile vacuoles perform osmoregulation and excretion, while in protists, food vacuoles are formed by engulfing food particles.

Worked Example 5: Compare the two double membrane-bound semi-autonomous cell organelles (mitochondria and chloroplasts).

Discussion:

  • Shared Characteristics: Both mitochondria and chloroplasts are double membrane-bound organelles excluded from the endomembrane system. Both possess their own circular DNA (single circular DNA in mitochondrial matrix; small double-stranded circular DNA in chloroplast stroma), RNA, \(70\text{S}\) ribosomes, and protein synthesis machinery.
  • Distinct Characteristics & Functions:
    • Mitochondria: Present in both plant and animal cells; inner membrane folds inward into cristae enclosing the matrix; site of aerobic respiration and oxidative phosphorylation generating ATP ('power houses' of the cell).
    • Chloroplasts: Present in plant cells and euglenoides; inner membrane is relatively less permeable and encloses the stroma containing flattened membranous sacs called thylakoids stacked into grana connected by stroma lamellae; contain chlorophyll and carotenoid pigments to trap light energy for photosynthesis (light reactions in grana, dark reactions/carbohydrate synthesis in stroma).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Choice MCQ)

Which of the following statements regarding the discovery of the cell and the formulation of the Cell Theory is incorrect?

  • (A) Antonie Von Leeuwenhoek first saw and described a live cell, whereas Robert Brown discovered the nucleus.
  • (B) Matthias Schleiden observed that all plants are composed of different kinds of cells which form the tissues of the plant.
  • (C) Theodore Schwann concluded that the presence of a cell wall is a unique character of plant cells and also explained how new cells are formed from pre-existing cells.
  • (D) Rudolf Virchow modified the hypothesis of Schleiden and Schwann by stating Omnis cellula-e cellula in \(1855\).
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Theodore Schwann (\(1839\)) reported the thin outer layer (plasma membrane), concluded that the cell wall is a unique character of plant cells, and proposed that the bodies of animals and plants are composed of cells and products of cells. However, the cell theory formulated by Schleiden and Schwann did not explain how new cells were formed. It was Rudolf Virchow (\(1855\)) who first explained that cells divided and new cells are formed from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula-e cellula).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Choice MCQ)

Regarding the distribution of ribosomes within cells, where are ribosomes found in a typical photosynthetic plant cell?

  • (A) Only free in the cytoplasm and attached to the Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum.
  • (B) In the cytoplasm, on the Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum, on the outer nuclear membrane, inside mitochondria, and inside chloroplasts.
  • (C) Inside the nucleolus, Golgi cisternae, lysosomes, and cytoplasm.
  • (D) Exclusively associated with the plasma membrane as \(70\text{S}\) subunits.
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Ribosomes are non-membrane bound organelles found in all cells. In a eukaryotic plant cell, \(80\text{S}\) ribosomes occur freely in the cytoplasm, attached to the surface of the Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER), and on the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope (which is continuous with RER). Additionally, \(70\text{S}\) ribosomes are present within two double-membrane organelles: mitochondria (in the matrix) and chloroplasts (in the stroma).

Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason Type)

Assertion (A): In human erythrocytes, the plasma membrane contains approximately \(52\%\) proteins and \(40\%\) lipids, and polar molecules require carrier proteins to cross this membrane.

Reason (R): The inner part of the phospholipid bilayer is composed of nonpolar hydrophobic tails of saturated hydrocarbons, which prevents polar molecules from passing through by simple diffusion.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Biochemical studies on the human erythrocyte (RBC) membrane showed it has approximately \(52\%\) protein and \(40\%\) lipids. Because the core of the phospholipid bilayer consists of nonpolar, hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails, polar molecules cannot pass through the nonpolar lipid bilayer on their own and therefore require membrane carrier proteins to facilitate their transport across the membrane. Thus, both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) directly explains why polar molecules need carrier proteins.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Choice MCQ)

Practice Question 10 (Single-Choice Analytical MCQ)

What is the total number of microtubules present in the cross-section of a single eukaryotic cilium/flagellum (axoneme) compared to a single centriole?

  • (A) Axoneme has \(11\) microtubules; Centriole has \(9\) microtubules.
  • (B) Axoneme has \(20\) microtubules (\(9\) doublets \(+ 2\) central); Centriole has \(27\) microtubules (\(9\) triplets \(+ 0\) central).
  • (C) Axoneme has \(27\) microtubules; Centriole has \(20\) microtubules.
  • (D) Both have \(18\) microtubules arranged in a \(9+0\) array.
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution:

  • In the axoneme of a eukaryotic cilium or flagellum (\(9+2\) array), there are \(9\) peripheral doublets (\(9 \times 2 = 18\) microtubules) and \(1\) pair of centrally located microtubules (\(2\) microtubules), giving a total of \(18 + 2 = 20\) microtubules (along with \(9\) radial spokes).
  • In a centriole (\(9+0\) cartwheel organisation), there are \(9\) evenly spaced peripheral triplets of tubulin (\(9 \times 3 = 27\) microtubules) and no central microtubules (instead, there is a proteinaceous central hub connected by \(9\) radial spokes).

9. Common Exam Pitfalls & Final Revision Checklist

Common NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Pitfall 1 — Robert Brown vs. Leeuwenhoek: Do not mark Robert Brown as the discoverer of the cell. Antonie Von Leeuwenhoek first saw and described a live cell; Robert Brown (\(1831\)) discovered the nucleus.
  • Pitfall 2 — Cell Wall Exception in Prokaryotes: All prokaryotes have a cell wall surrounding the cell membrane except Mycoplasma.
  • Pitfall 3 — Pili and Fimbriae vs. Flagella: Pili and fimbriae are surface structures in bacteria, but they do NOT play any role in motility. Fimbriae help in attachment to rocks in streams and host tissues.
  • Pitfall 4 — Inclusion Bodies & Nucleolus Membrane Status: Neither prokaryotic inclusion bodies nor the eukaryotic nucleolus nor ribosomes nor centrosomes are bound by a membrane.
  • Pitfall 5 — Tonoplast Ion Concentration: In plants, the tonoplast transports ions against concentration gradients into the vacuole; therefore, ion concentration is significantly higher in the vacuole than in the cytoplasm.
  • Pitfall 6 — Svedberg's Unit ('S') is Not Additive: \(50\text{S} + 30\text{S} = 70\text{S}\) (prokaryotic/organellar) and \(60\text{S} + 40\text{S} = 80\text{S}\) (eukaryotic cytoplasmic) because 'S' measures the sedimentation coefficient (an indirect measure of density and size), not simple mass addition.
  • Pitfall 7 — Centromere vs. Kinetochore: The centromere is the primary constriction holding two chromatids together, whereas kinetochores are the disc-shaped structures on the sides of the centromere.

Concise Final Recap

  • Cell Theory: Formulated by Schleiden (\(1838\)) and Schwann (\(1839\)); completed by Rudolf Virchow (\(1855\), Omnis cellula-e cellula).
  • Prokaryotes: Naked circular genomic DNA + plasmids; \(70\text{S}\) ribosomes at plasma membrane; three-layered cell envelope (glycocalyx \(\rightarrow\) cell wall \(\rightarrow\) plasma membrane); mesosomes (vesicles, tubules, lamellae); chromatophores in cyanobacteria; inclusion bodies free in cytoplasm.
  • Plasma Membrane: Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicolson, \(1972\)); phospholipid bilayer + cholesterol + integral/peripheral proteins + carbohydrates (\(52\%\) protein, \(40\%\) lipid in human RBC).
  • Endomembrane System: ER (RER for protein synthesis/secretion; SER for lipids/steroidal hormones) \(\rightarrow\) Golgi apparatus (cis forming to trans maturing face; glycoproteins & glycolipids) \(\rightarrow\) Lysosomes (acidic hydrolases) & Vacuoles (tonoplast). Excludes mitochondria, chloroplasts, and peroxisomes.
  • Mitochondria & Plastids: Double membrane-bound, contain circular DNA and \(70\text{S}\) ribosomes. Mitochondria have cristae and perform aerobic respiration (ATP generation). Chloroplasts have grana (light reactions) and stroma (dark reactions, carbohydrate/protein synthesis).
  • Cytoskeleton & Motility: Microtubules, microfilaments, intermediate filaments. Cilia/flagella have a membrane-covered \(9+2\) axoneme emerging from basal bodies; centrioles have a non-membranous \(9+0\) cartwheel of tubulin triplets.
  • Nucleus & Chromosomes: Double membrane with perinuclear space (\(10\text{–}50\,\mathrm{nm}\)) and bidirectional nuclear pores; nucleoplasm contains chromatin (DNA + histones + non-histones + RNA) and nucleolus (rRNA synthesis). Chromosomes are classified by centromere position into metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, and telocentric.
