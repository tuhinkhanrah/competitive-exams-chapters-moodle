Locomotion and Movement

After mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Classify cellular movements in the human body into amoeboid, ciliary, flagellar, and muscular categories with specific physiological examples.
  • Compare skeletal, visceral (smooth), and cardiac muscles based on location, microscopic appearance, and neural regulation.
  • Analyze the hierarchical organization of a skeletal muscle down to the sarcomere and the molecular structure of actin, tropomyosin, troponin, and myosin (meromyosin).

2. Introduction to Movement and Locomotion

Movement is one of the defining features of living organisms. Both plants and animals display a wide spectrum of movements:

  • Protoplasmic streaming: A simple form of movement observed in unicellular organisms such as Amoeba.
  • Appendage movements: Many organisms move cilia, flagella, or tentacles.
  • Organ/part movements: Human beings can move limbs, jaws, eyelids, tongue, etc., without necessarily changing their spatial location.
Fundamental Principle: When voluntary movements result in a change of place or location (such as walking, running, climbing, flying, or swimming), they are termed locomotion. Locomotory structures do not have to be distinct from structures performing other physiological movements:
  • In Paramoecium, cilia move food through the cytopharynx and also propel the organism during locomotion.
  • In Hydra, tentacles capture prey and are also used for locomotion.
  • In humans, limbs are used both to adjust body posture and to perform locomotion.
Therefore, movements and locomotion cannot be studied in isolation: all locomotions are movements, but all movements are not locomotions.

Animals perform locomotion according to their habitat and situational demands—primarily to search for food, shelter, mates, suitable breeding grounds, favourable climatic conditions, or to escape from enemies and predators.

2.1 Types of Movement in Human Cells

Cells of the human body exhibit three primary types of movement, alongside specialized flagellar movement:

Type of Movement Mechanism / Structures Involved Biological Examples & Functions
Amoeboid Movement Effected by pseudopodia formed by streaming of protoplasm (as in Amoeba). Cytoskeletal elements such as microfilaments are also directly involved. Specialized blood cells in humans such as macrophages and leucocytes.
Ciliary Movement Coordinated beating of cilia (hair-like outgrowths of the cell membrane) lining internal tubular organs with ciliated epithelium. 1. Trachea: Removes dust particles and foreign substances inhaled with atmospheric air.
2. Female reproductive tract (Fallopian tubes): Facilitates passage of ova.
Flagellar Movement Propulsion driven by flagella (longer outgrowths of the cell membrane). 1. Swimming of human spermatozoa.
2. Maintenance of water current in the canal system of sponges.
3. Locomotion of Protists such as Euglena.
Muscular Movement Relies on the contractile property of muscles coordinated with the skeletal and neural systems. Movement of human limbs, jaws, tongue, eyelids, body posture changes, and whole-body locomotion.

3. Muscle: Classification and Ultrastructure

Muscle is a specialized tissue of mesodermal origin. In an adult human, muscles contribute approximately 40–50% of total body weight. Muscle tissue possesses four cardinal physiological properties:

  1. Excitability: Ability to respond to a stimulus by generating an electrical action potential.
  2. Contractility: Ability to shorten forcefully when adequately stimulated.
  3. Extensibility: Ability to be stretched without sustaining damage.
  4. Elasticity: Ability to recoil and return to its original resting length after being stretched or contracted.

3.1 Classification of Muscles

Muscles are classified according to three criteria: location, appearance under a microscope, and nature of regulation of their activities.

Feature Skeletal Muscle Visceral Muscle Cardiac Muscle
Location Closely associated with skeletal components (bones). Inner walls of hollow visceral organs (alimentary canal, reproductive tract, etc.). Exclusively in the wall of the heart (myocardium).
Microscopic Appearance Striated (striped appearance). Fibres are unbranched and multinucleated (syncytium). Non-striated / Smooth (lacks striations). Striated; many cardiac muscle cells assemble in a branching pattern.
Nature of Regulation Voluntary (under direct conscious control of the nervous system). Involuntary (not under voluntary control of the nervous system). Involuntary (nervous system does not control their activities directly).
Major Functions Locomotory actions and changes of body posture. Transport of food through the digestive tract (peristalsis) and gametes through the genital tract. Rhythmic contraction and relaxation to pump blood throughout the body.

3.2 Anatomical Organization of a Skeletal Muscle

Each organized skeletal muscle in the human body is composed of numerous muscle bundles, also called fascicles, held together by a common collagenous connective tissue layer known as fascia.

  • Each fascicle (muscle bundle) contains numerous elongated cylindrical cells called muscle fibres (the anatomical unit of muscle).
  • Sarcolemma: The plasma membrane lining each muscle fibre.
  • Sarcoplasm: The cytoplasm enclosed by the sarcolemma. Because a skeletal muscle fibre contains many nuclei within a continuous sarcoplasm, it is a syncytium.
  • Sarcoplasmic Reticulum: The specialized endoplasmic reticulum of muscle fibres; it serves as the intracellular storehouse of calcium ions (\(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)).
  • Myofibrils (Myofilaments): A large number of parallelly arranged rod-like filaments running longitudinally through the sarcoplasm.

3.3 Structure of the Myofibril and Sarcomere

Each myofibril displays alternating dark and light bands along its length due to the specific spatial arrangement of two contractile proteins—Actin and Myosin—arranged as rod-like structures parallel to each other and to the longitudinal axis of the myofibril:

  • I-band (Isotropic band / Light band): Contains only actin (thin) filaments. An elastic fibre called the 'Z' line bisects the centre of each I-band. The thin filaments are firmly anchored to the Z-line.
  • A-band (Anisotropic band / Dark band): Contains myosin (thick) filaments (and overlapping ends of thin filaments in the resting state). The thick filaments in the middle of the A-band are held together by a thin fibrous membrane called the 'M' line.
  • Sarcomere: The portion of the myofibril between two successive 'Z' lines. It represents the functional unit of contraction. Each sarcomere consists of one central A-band flanked by two half I-bands (one half I-band on each side).
  • 'H' zone (Hensen's zone): In the resting state, the free ends of the thick filaments are partially overlapped by the edges of thin filaments on either side, leaving a central portion of the thick filaments not overlapped by thin filaments. This central un-overlapped region of the A-band is the 'H' zone.
1 Sarcomere = \(\frac{1}{2}\) I-band + 1 A-band + \(\frac{1}{2}\) I-band (between two successive Z-lines)

3.4 Structure of Contractile Proteins

A. Actin (Thin) Filament

Each thin filament is a complex of three distinct proteins (one main contractile protein and two regulatory proteins):

  1. Actin: Composed of two 'F' (filamentous) actins helically wound around each other. Each 'F' actin is a polymer of monomeric 'G' (Globular) actins.
  2. Tropomyosin: Two filaments of this fibrous protein run closely along the grooves of the 'F' actins throughout their entire length.
  3. Troponin: A complex three-subunit globular protein distributed at regular intervals along the tropomyosin. In the resting state, a subunit of troponin masks the active binding sites for myosin on the actin filaments.

B. Myosin (Thick) Filament

Each thick filament is a polymerized protein formed by many monomeric units called Meromyosins. Each meromyosin consists of two key regions:

  1. Heavy Meromyosin (HMM): Consists of a globular head and a short arm. The HMM component projects outwards at a regular distance and angle from the surface of a polymerized myosin filament and is known as the cross arm. The globular head functions as an active ATPase enzyme and possesses both ATP binding sites and active binding sites for actin.
  2. Light Meromyosin (LMM): Forms the elongated helical tail that aggregates with other tails to form the central shaft of the thick filament.

4. Mechanism of Muscle Contraction (Sliding Filament Theory)

The mechanism of muscle contraction is explained by the Sliding Filament Theory, which states that contraction of a muscle fibre takes place by the sliding of the thin (actin) filaments over the thick (myosin) filaments.

4.1 Step-by-Step Physiological Sequence

  1. Neural Initiation & Motor Unit: Muscle contraction is initiated by a signal transmitted from the Central Nervous System (CNS) via a motor neuron. A motor neuron together with the muscle fibres connected to it constitutes a motor unit.
  2. Neuromuscular Junction (Motor-End Plate): The anatomical junction between a motor neuron and the sarcolemma of the muscle fibre is called the neuromuscular junction or motor-end plate.
  3. Neurotransmitter Release & Action Potential: Arrival of a neural signal at the neuromuscular junction triggers the release of the neurotransmitter Acetylcholine, which generates an action potential in the sarcolemma.
  4. Calcium Ion (\(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)) Release: The action potential spreads throughout the muscle fibre and stimulates the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release stored \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) ions into the sarcoplasm.
  5. Unmasking of Active Sites: Elevated sarcoplasmic \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) levels cause \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) to bind to a specific subunit of troponin on the actin filaments, inducing a conformational shift that removes the masking of active sites for myosin.
  6. ATP Hydrolysis & Cross-Bridge Formation: Utilizing energy released from ATP hydrolysis (\(\mathrm{ATP \rightarrow ADP + P_i}\)) catalyzed by the myosin head ATPase, the energized myosin head binds to the exposed active sites on actin to form a cross-bridge.
  7. Power Stroke (Sliding/Rotation): The attached myosin head rotates/tilts towards the centre of the 'A' band, pulling the attached actin filaments inwards. Consequently, the 'Z' lines attached to these actins are pulled inwards, causing a shortening of the sarcomere (contraction). During this step, \(\mathrm{ADP}\) and \(\mathrm{P_i}\) are released from the myosin head.
  8. Cross-Bridge Detachment: A new \(\mathrm{ATP}\) molecule binds to the myosin head, causing the cross-bridge to break. The myosin head then hydrolyzes the newly bound \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and repeats the cycle of cross-bridge formation and breakage, producing further sliding.
  9. Muscle Relaxation: Cycling continues until \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) ions are actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic cisternae. Removal of \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) allows troponin-tropomyosin to re-mask the active sites on actin filaments, returning the 'Z' lines to their original resting position (relaxation).

4.2 Dimensional Changes in Sarcomere Bands During Contraction

High-Yield NEET Concept — Changes in Sarcomere Regions During Contraction:
  • Length of 'A' band: REMAINS CONSTANT (retains its original length) because thick filaments neither shorten nor contract.
  • Length of 'I' band: DECREASES (gets reduced) as thin filaments slide deeper into the A-band.
  • Length of 'H' zone: DECREASES progressively and disappears in a maximally contracted sarcomere when opposing thin filaments meet/overlap at the centre.
  • Length of Sarcomere (distance between two successive Z-lines): DECREASES (shortens) as Z-lines are pulled inwards.
  • Length of individual myofilaments (actin and myosin): REMAINS UNCHANGED.

4.3 Muscle Fatigue, Red Muscle Fibres, and White Muscle Fibres

The reaction time of fibres varies among different muscles. Repeated activation of muscles leads to the accumulation of lactic acid due to anaerobic breakdown of glycogen in them, causing muscle fatigue.

Muscle tissue contains a red-coloured oxygen-storing pigment called myoglobin. Based on myoglobin content and metabolic specialization, skeletal muscle fibres are categorized into Red fibres and White fibres:

Characteristic Red Muscle Fibres (Aerobic Muscles) White Muscle Fibres (Anaerobic Muscles)
Myoglobin Content High (gives a reddish appearance). Very low / less (appears pale or whitish).
Mitochondria Number Plenty (numerous); utilize stored oxygen for sustained aerobic ATP production. Few in number.
Sarcoplasmic Reticulum Moderate/lower amount. High amount (enables rapid \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) release for fast contractions).
Primary Energy Pathway Aerobic respiration (hence called aerobic muscles). Anaerobic glycolysis (rapidly accumulates lactic acid and fatigues faster).

5. Human Skeletal System

The skeletal system consists of a framework of bones and a few cartilages. Both bone and cartilage are specialized connective tissues:

  • Bone: Has a very hard matrix due to calcium salts in it.
  • Cartilage: Has a slightly pliable matrix due to chondroitin salts.

In an adult human being, the skeletal system comprises 206 bones (plus a few cartilages) grouped into two principal divisions:

  1. Axial Skeleton: 80 bones distributed along the main longitudinal axis of the body (Skull, Vertebral column, Sternum, and Ribs).
  2. Appendicular Skeleton: 126 bones comprising the bones of the limbs (120 bones) along with their girdles (6 bones).

5.1 Axial Skeleton (80 Bones)

A. The Skull Region (29 Bones Total: 22 Skull Bones + 1 Hyoid + 6 Ear Ossicles)

  • Skull Bones (22 bones): Composed of two sets—Cranial and Facial:
    • Cranial bones (8): Form the hard protective outer covering, the cranium, for the brain. These include: Frontal (1), Parietal (2), Temporal (2), Occipital (1), Sphenoid (1), and Ethmoid (1).
    • Facial bones (14): Form the front part of the skull (including Nasal, Lacrimal, Zygomatic, Maxilla, Mandible, etc.).
  • Hyoid Bone (1): A single U-shaped bone located at the base of the buccal cavity.
  • Ear Ossicles (6 bones = 3 in each middle ear): Each middle ear contains three tiny bones—Malleus, Incus, and Stapes.
  • Dicondylic Skull: The human skull articulates with the superior region of the vertebral column (the first cervical vertebra, atlas) with the help of two occipital condyles; hence humans possess a dicondylic skull.
Tutorial Diagram 6: Lateral view of the human skull illustrating cranial bones, facial bones, occipital condyle, and the U-shaped hyoid bone.

B. Vertebral Column (26 Bones in Adult)

  • Formed by 26 serially arranged units called vertebrae and is placed dorsally, extending from the base of the skull to constitute the main framework of the trunk.
  • Neural Canal: Each vertebra has a central hollow portion called the neural canal through which the spinal cord passes.
  • Atlas & Axis: The first cervical vertebra is the atlas, which articulates with the two occipital condyles of the skull. The second cervical vertebra is the axis.
  • Regional Differentiation (from skull downwards):
    1. Cervical: 7 vertebrae (the number of cervical vertebrae is 7 in almost all mammals, including human beings).
    2. Thoracic: 12 vertebrae.
    3. Lumbar: 5 vertebrae.
    4. Sacral (Sacrum): 1-fused (formed by fusion of 5 sacral vertebrae in adults).
    5. Coccygeal (Coccyx): 1-fused (formed by fusion of 4 coccygeal vertebrae in adults).
  • Functions: Protects the spinal cord, supports the head, and serves as the point of attachment for the ribs and musculature of the back.
Adult Human Vertebral Formula: \(\mathrm{C_7\,T_{12}\,L_5\,S_{(1\text{-fused})}\,Co_{(1\text{-fused})} = 26\text{ Vertebrae}}\)

C. Sternum (1 Bone) & Ribs (12 Pairs = 24 Bones)

  • Sternum: A flat bone located on the ventral midline of the thorax.
  • Ribs (12 pairs): Each rib is a thin flat bone connected dorsally to the vertebral column (thoracic vertebrae) and ventrally to the sternum. Each rib has two articulation surfaces on its dorsal end and is therefore called bicephalic.
  • Classification of the 12 Pairs of Ribs:
    • True Ribs (Vertebrosternal ribs — Pairs 1 to 7): First 7 pairs. Attached dorsally to the thoracic vertebrae and connected ventrally directly to the sternum with the help of hyaline cartilage.
    • False Ribs (Vertebrochondral ribs — Pairs 8, 9, and 10): Do not articulate directly with the sternum; instead, they join the 7th rib with the help of hyaline cartilage.
    • Floating Ribs (Vertebral ribs — Pairs 11 and 12): Last 2 pairs. Attached dorsally to thoracic vertebrae but are not connected ventrally, hence called floating ribs.
  • Rib Cage: Formed collectively by the Thoracic vertebrae + Ribs + Sternum.
Tutorial Diagram 7: (a) Regions of the adult human vertebral column (26 bones); (b) Organization of the rib cage showing sternum, true ribs (1–7), false/vertebrochondral ribs (8–10), and floating ribs (11–12).

5.2 Appendicular Skeleton (126 Bones)

The bones of the limbs (4 × 30 = 120 bones) along with their girdles (Pectoral girdle = 4 bones; Pelvic girdle = 2 bones) constitute the appendicular skeleton (126 bones).

A. Bones of the Forelimb (Upper Limb — 30 Bones per Limb)

  • Humerus (1): Bone of the upper arm.
  • Radius (1) & Ulna (1): Bones of the forearm.
  • Carpals (8): Wrist bones.
  • Metacarpals (5): Palm bones.
  • Phalanges (14): Digits (finger bones; digital formula \(2, 3, 3, 3, 3\)).

B. Bones of the Hindlimb (Lower Limb — 30 Bones per Limb)

  • Femur (1): Thigh bone—the longest bone in the human body.
  • Patella (1): A cup-shaped bone covering the knee ventrally (knee cap). (Note: Because the patella is a 15th bone before the tarsals, the ankle has 7 tarsals compared to 8 carpals in the wrist, keeping the total per limb at exactly 30 bones).
  • Tibia (1) & Fibula (1): Bones of the shank (lower leg).
  • Tarsals (7): Ankle bones.
  • Metatarsals (5): Sole/foot bones.
  • Phalanges (14): Digits (toe bones).

C. Pectoral and Pelvic Girdles (6 Bones Total)

Pectoral and pelvic girdle bones articulate the upper and lower limbs, respectively, with the axial skeleton. Each girdle is formed of two halves.

Feature Pectoral Girdle (Shoulder Girdle) Pelvic Girdle (Hip Girdle)
Component Bones 4 bones total (2 halves; each half consists of 1 Clavicle and 1 Scapula). 2 Coxal bones total (each half is 1 coxal bone formed by the fusion of 3 bones: ilium, ischium, and pubis).
Anatomical Details • Scapula: Large triangular flat bone situated in the dorsal part of the thorax between the 2nd and 7th ribs. Its dorsal body has an elevated ridge called the spine projecting as a flat expanded process called the acromion (with which the clavicle articulates).
• Clavicle (Collar bone): Long slender bone with two curvatures. 		• Each coxal bone results from fusion of ilium, ischium, and pubis.
• The two halves of the pelvic girdle meet ventrally to form the pubic symphysis containing fibrous cartilage.
Articulation Cavity Glenoid cavity (located below the acromion of the scapula); articulates with the head of the humerus to form the shoulder joint. Acetabulum (cavity at the point of fusion of ilium, ischium, and pubis); articulates with the thigh bone (femur) to form the hip joint.

6. Joints and Musculoskeletal Disorders

Joints are points of contact between bones, or between bones and cartilages. Force generated by muscles is used to carry out movement through joints, where the joint acts as a fulcrum. Based on structural characteristics and degree of movability, joints are classified into three major forms:

Structural Class of Joint Movability & Structural Feature Anatomical Examples
1. Fibrous Joints Do not allow any movement (Immovable). Bones fuse end-to-end via dense fibrous connective tissue. Sutures between flat skull bones forming the cranium.
2. Cartilaginous Joints Permit limited movements (Slightly movable). Bones involved are joined together with the help of cartilages. • Joint between adjacent vertebrae in the vertebral column.
• Pubic symphysis between the two pubic bones in the pelvic girdle (contains fibrous cartilage).
3. Synovial Joints Allow considerable movement (Freely movable). Characterized by a fluid-filled synovial cavity between the articulating surfaces of the two bones. 1. Ball and Socket joint: Between humerus and pectoral girdle (glenoid cavity); also between femur and acetabulum of pelvic girdle.
2. Hinge joint: Knee joint, elbow joint, and joints between phalanges.
3. Pivot joint: Between atlas and axis (1st and 2nd cervical vertebrae).
4. Gliding joint: Between the carpals.
5. Saddle joint: Between carpal and metacarpal of thumb.

6.1 Disorders of the Muscular and Skeletal System

NEET UG frequently tests the exact etiology and clinical features of these six disorders:

  • Myasthenia gravis: An autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction, leading to fatigue, weakening, and paralysis of skeletal muscle.
  • Muscular dystrophy: Progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle mostly due to a genetic disorder.
  • Tetany: Rapid spasms (wild contractions) in muscle due to low \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) levels in body fluid (hypocalcemic tetany).
  • Arthritis: Inflammation of joints.
  • Osteoporosis: An age-related disorder characterized by decreased bone mass and increased chances of fractures. Decreased levels of estrogen is a common cause (especially in post-menopausal women).
  • Gout: Inflammation of joints due to the accumulation of uric acid crystals.

7. Worked Conceptual Examples & Complete Chapter Exercise Solutions

Worked Example 1: True/False Verification & Scientific Correction

Problem: Evaluate whether each of the following statements is True or False. If false, rewrite the statement accurately:

  1. Actin is present in thin filament.
  2. H-zone of striated muscle fibre represents both thick and thin filaments.
  3. Human skeleton has 206 bones.
  4. There are 11 pairs of ribs in man.
  5. Sternum is present on the ventral side of the body.

Solution:

  • (a) True. Thin filaments are composed of actin along with tropomyosin and troponin.
  • (b) False. Correction: The H-zone of a striated muscle fibre is the central portion of the thick filament (A-band) that is not overlapped by thin filaments; thus, it represents only thick (myosin) filaments.
  • (c) True. The adult human skeleton consists of 206 bones (80 axial + 126 appendicular).
  • (d) False. Correction: There are 12 pairs of ribs (24 ribs total) in humans.
  • (e) True. The sternum is a flat bone situated on the ventral midline of the thorax.

Worked Example 2: Anatomical Joint Identification

Problem: Identify the specific type of joint present at each of the following anatomical locations:

  1. Between atlas and axis
  2. Between carpal and metacarpal of thumb
  3. Between phalanges
  4. Between femur and acetabulum
  5. Between cranial bones
  6. Between pubic bones in the pelvic girdle

Solution:

  • (a) Atlas and axis: Pivot joint (a type of synovial joint).
  • (b) Carpal and metacarpal of thumb: Saddle joint (a type of synovial joint).
  • (c) Between phalanges: Hinge joint (a type of synovial joint).
  • (d) Femur and acetabulum: Ball and socket joint (a type of synovial joint).
  • (e) Between cranial bones: Fibrous joint (sutures; immovable).
  • (f) Between pubic bones in the pelvic girdle: Cartilaginous joint (pubic symphysis containing fibrous cartilage).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Choice MCQ)

Which of the following statements accurately describes the relationship between movement and locomotion across organisms?

  • (A) Locomotory structures in organisms are always anatomically separate from structures performing other physiological movements.
  • (B) All movements are considered locomotions, whereas only voluntary movements are non-locomotory.
  • (C) In Paramoecium, cilia propel food through the cytopharynx and also carry out locomotion, illustrating that all locomotions are movements but not all movements are locomotions.
  • (D) Macrophages and leucocytes in human blood exhibit ciliary movement mediated by microfilaments.
Correct Answer: (C) Worked Solution: Locomotory structures need not be different from those affecting other types of movements. In Paramoecium, cilia move food through the cytopharynx and perform locomotion; in Hydra, tentacles capture prey and aid locomotion. All locomotions are movements (those resulting in a change of place or location), but all movements are not locomotions. Macrophages and leucocytes exhibit amoeboid movement (not ciliary movement) using pseudopodia and microfilaments.
Practice Question 2 (Multiple-Statement Evaluation)

Consider the following four statements regarding human muscle tissue:

  1. Skeletal muscle fibres are syncytial because their sarcoplasm contains multiple nuclei enclosed by a single sarcolemma.
  2. Visceral muscles located in the inner walls of the reproductive tract are non-striated and under voluntary neural control.
  3. Cardiac muscle cells assemble in a branching pattern, appear striated under the microscope, and are involuntary in nature.
  4. Each muscle bundle (fascicle) is held together with other fascicles in an organized skeletal muscle by a common collagenous connective tissue layer called fascia.

Which of the above statements are correct?

  • (A) I, II, and III only
  • (B) I, III, and IV only
  • (C) II and IV only
  • (D) I, II, III, and IV
Correct Answer: (B) Worked Solution: Statements I, III, and IV are completely accurate. Statement II is incorrect because visceral (smooth) muscles are involuntary—their activities are not under the voluntary control of the nervous system.
Practice Question 3 (Single-Choice MCQ)

During the resting state of a skeletal muscle fibre, what is the specific molecular role of troponin and tropomyosin on the thin filament?

  • (A) Tropomyosin hydrolyzes ATP to ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\) while troponin anchors the thin filament to the M-line.
  • (B) A subunit of troponin distributed at regular intervals on tropomyosin masks the active binding sites for myosin on the actin filaments.
  • (C) Troponin polymerizes monomeric G-actins into helical F-actins when \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) concentration is low.
  • (D) Tropomyosin forms the cross-arm that projects outwards from the surface of the thick filament.
Correct Answer: (B) Worked Solution: Each thin filament contains two F-actins, two filaments of tropomyosin running close to the F-actins throughout their length, and a complex protein troponin distributed at regular intervals on tropomyosin. In the resting state, a subunit of troponin masks the active binding sites for myosin on the actin filaments.

  • (B) Globular head of Heavy Meromyosin (HMM)
  • (C) Monomeric Globular (G) actin

Correct Answer: (B) Worked Solution: Each meromyosin has two parts: a globular head with a short arm (Heavy Meromyosin, HMM) and a tail (Light Meromyosin, LMM). The globular head of HMM is an active ATPase enzyme and contains the binding sites for ATP as well as active sites for actin.

Practice Question 6 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): During the contraction of a striated muscle fibre, the length of the 'I' band and 'H' zone decreases, whereas the length of the 'A' band remains unchanged.

Reason (R): Muscle contraction occurs by the sliding of thin (actin) filaments over thick (myosin) filaments towards the centre of the 'A' band without any shortening of the individual thick or thin filaments.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A) Worked Solution: According to the sliding filament theory, thin filaments slide over thick filaments toward the M-line. Because the A-band width is defined by the exact length of the thick (myosin) filaments—which do not shorten—the A-band retains its constant length, while the un-overlapped regions (I-band and H-zone) shorten as overlap increases. Thus, both (A) and (R) are true and (R) directly explains (A).

9. Common NEET Pitfalls & Rapid Revision Checklist

Common Exam Traps to Avoid:

  • Trap 1 (A-band vs. I-band during contraction): Never select an option stating that the A-band shortens during contraction. The A-band remains constant; only the I-band, H-zone, and sarcomere shorten.
  • Trap 2 (Role of ATP binding vs. ATP hydrolysis): ATP hydrolysis (\(\mathrm{ATP \rightarrow ADP + P_i}\)) energizes the myosin head to form a cross-bridge, whereas the binding of a fresh ATP molecule breaks (detaches) the cross-bridge.
  • Trap 3 (Carpals vs. Tarsals count): Forelimbs and hindlimbs both have exactly 30 bones each, even though the hindlimb has a patella (1) and the forelimb has no equivalent elbow cap. This is balanced because the wrist has 8 carpals while the ankle has 7 tarsals.
  • Trap 4 (Myasthenia gravis vs. Muscular dystrophy): Remember that Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction, whereas Muscular dystrophy is a genetic disorder causing progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle.
  • Trap 5 (Matrix salts): Bone matrix is hard due to calcium salts; cartilage matrix is slightly pliable due to chondroitin salts.

Final Concise Recap Checklist:

  • Cellular Movements: Amoeboid (macrophages, leucocytes via pseudopodia & microfilaments); Ciliary (trachea dust removal, ovum passage in female reproductive tract); Flagellar (spermatozoa swimming, water current in sponges, Euglena locomotion); Muscular (limbs, jaws, tongue).
  • Muscle Properties & Origin: Mesodermal origin; 40–50% of adult human body weight; exhibits excitability, contractility, extensibility, and elasticity.
  • Sarcomere & Proteins: Functional unit between two Z-lines. Thin filament = 2 F-actins (polymers of G-actin) + 2 tropomyosins + troponin (masks active sites at rest; binds \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)). Thick filament = polymerized meromyosins (HMM = head + short arm/cross arm with ATPase; LMM = tail).
  • Skeleton Numbers: Total = 206. Axial = 80 [Skull 22 (8 cranial + 14 facial) + Hyoid 1 + Ear ossicles 6 + Vertebrae 26 (\(\mathrm{C_7, T_{12}, L_5, S_{1\text{-fused}}, Co_{1\text{-fused}}}\)) + Sternum 1 + Ribs 24 (Pairs 1–7 true, 8–10 false/vertebrochondral, 11–12 floating)]. Appendicular = 126 [Limbs 4 × 30 = 120 + Pectoral girdle 4 + Pelvic girdle 2].
  • Joints: Fibrous (immovable cranial sutures); Cartilaginous (limited movement: adjacent vertebrae, pubic symphysis); Synovial (fluid-filled cavity, considerable movement: ball & socket, hinge, pivot, gliding, saddle).
