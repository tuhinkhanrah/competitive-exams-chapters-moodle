Neural Control and Coordination

Comprehensive Self-Contained Study Module: Neural Organisation, Neuron Structure, Impulse Conduction, Synaptic Transmission, and Human Brain Anatomy

1. Learning Objectives and Overview

To maintain homeostasis in the human body, the functions of different organs and organ systems must be continuously coordinated. Coordination is the process through which two or more organs interact and complement the functions of one another.

Syllabus & Exam Focus (NEET UG)

  • Understand the physiological basis of organ coordination during activities such as physical exercise.
  • Compare neural organisation across lower invertebrates (Hydra), insects, and vertebrates.
  • Classify the divisions of the human neural system: CNS, PNS, somatic, autonomic, and visceral nervous systems.
  • Identify structural components of a neuron (cell body, dendrites, axon) and classify neurons by polarity and myelination.
  • Master the ionic mechanism of resting membrane potential, action potential (depolarisation), repolarisation, and the role of the \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}-\mathrm{K}^{+}\) pump.
  • Contrast electrical and chemical synapses and trace the steps of neurotransmission.
  • Map the structural divisions and functional centres of the human brain (forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, limbic system, and brain stem).

Physiological Example of Coordination: Physical Exercise

When we perform physical exercise, the energy demand increases to sustain heightened muscular activity. Consequently, the supply of oxygen must also increase. An increased oxygen supply necessitates a coordinated rise in:

  • The rate of respiration (lungs),
  • Heart beat (heart), and
  • Blood flow via blood vessels.

When physical exercise stops, the activities of the nerves, lungs, heart, and kidney gradually return to their normal baseline conditions. Thus, the functions of muscles, lungs, heart, blood vessels, kidney, and other organs are tightly coordinated.

In our body, the neural system and the endocrine system jointly coordinate and integrate all organ activities so that they function in a synchronised fashion:

  • Neural System: Provides an organised network of point-to-point connections for quick coordination.
  • Endocrine System: Provides chemical integration through hormones.

2. Core Concepts and Detailed Explanations

2.1 Comparative Neural System in Animals

The neural system of all animals is composed of highly specialised cells called neurons, which can detect, receive, and transmit different kinds of stimuli.

  • Lower Invertebrates (e.g., Hydra): Neural organisation is very simple and consists of a diffuse network of neurons (nerve net) without a brain.
  • Insects: The neural system is better organised; a brain is present along with a number of ganglia and neural tissues.
  • Vertebrates: Possess a much more developed and complex neural system.

2.2 Human Neural System

The human neural system is divided into two principal parts:

  1. Central Neural System (CNS): Includes the brain and the spinal cord. It is the primary site of information processing and control.
  2. Peripheral Neural System (PNS): Comprises all the nerves of the body associated with the CNS (brain and spinal cord).

Types of Nerve Fibres in the PNS

  • Afferent fibres: Transmit impulses from tissues/organs to the CNS.
  • Efferent fibres: Transmit regulatory impulses from the CNS to the concerned peripheral tissues/organs.

Divisions of the Peripheral Neural System

  • Somatic Neural System: Relays impulses from the CNS to skeletal muscles.
  • Autonomic Neural System: Transmits impulses from the CNS to the involuntary organs and smooth muscles of the body. It is further classified into:
    • Sympathetic neural system
    • Parasympathetic neural system
  • Visceral Nervous System: The part of the peripheral nervous system that comprises the whole complex of nerves, fibres, ganglia, and plexuses by which impulses travel from the central nervous system to the viscera and from the viscera to the central nervous system.

2.3 Neuron as Structural and Functional Unit of Neural System

A neuron is a microscopic structure composed of three major parts: cell body, dendrites, and axon.

  • Cell body (Soma/Cyton): Contains cytoplasm with typical cell organelles and specific granular bodies called Nissl's granules.
  • Dendrites: Short fibres that branch repeatedly and project out of the cell body. They also contain Nissl's granules and transmit impulses towards the cell body.
  • Axon: A single long fibre whose distal end is branched. It lacks Nissl's granules. Each distal branch terminates as a bulb-like structure called a synaptic knob, which possesses synaptic vesicles containing chemicals called neurotransmitters. Axons transmit nerve impulses away from the cell body to a synapse or to a neuro-muscular junction.

Classification of Axons (Nerve Fibres) Based on Myelination

  • Myelinated Nerve Fibres: Enveloped with Schwann cells, which form a myelin sheath around the axon. The gaps between two adjacent myelin sheaths are called nodes of Ranvier. Myelinated nerve fibres are found in spinal and cranial nerves.
  • Non-myelinated (Unmyelinated) Nerve Fibres: Enclosed by a Schwann cell that does not form a myelin sheath around the axon. Commonly found in autonomous and somatic neural systems.

2.4 Generation and Conduction of Nerve Impulse

1. Resting Membrane Potential (Polarised State)

Neurons are excitable cells because their membranes are in a polarised state. Different types of ion channels are present on the neural membrane, and these channels are selectively permeable to different ions.

  • When a neuron is not conducting any impulse (i.e., resting), the axonal membrane is comparatively more permeable to potassium ions (\(\mathrm{K}^{+}\)) and nearly impermeable to sodium ions (\(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\)).
  • Similarly, the membrane is impermeable to negatively charged proteins present in the axoplasm.
  • Consequently, the axoplasm inside the axon contains a high concentration of \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) and negatively charged proteins and a low concentration of \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\).
  • In contrast, the fluid outside the axon contains a low concentration of \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) and a high concentration of \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\), thus forming a concentration gradient across the membrane.
  • These ionic gradients across the resting membrane are actively maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, which transports \(3\,\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) outwards for \(2\,\mathrm{K}^{+}\) into the cell.
  • As a result, the outer surface of the axonal membrane possesses a positive charge while its inner surface becomes negatively charged, and therefore the membrane is polarised.
  • The electrical potential difference across the resting plasma membrane is called the resting potential.

2. Depolarisation and Action Potential (Excitation)

  • When a stimulus is applied at a site (e.g., site A) on the polarised membrane, the membrane at site A becomes freely permeable to \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\).
  • This leads to a rapid influx of \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) followed by the reversal of the polarity at that site: the outer surface of the membrane becomes negatively charged and the inner side becomes positively charged.
  • The polarity of the membrane at site A is thus reversed and hence depolarised.
  • The electrical potential difference across the plasma membrane at site A is called the action potential, which is in fact termed a nerve impulse.

3. Propagation (Conduction) Along the Axon

  • At sites immediately ahead on the axon (e.g., site B), the membrane still has a positive charge on the outer surface and a negative charge on its inner surface.
  • As a result, a local electrical current flows on the inner surface from site A to site B.
  • On the outer surface, current flows from site B to site A to complete the circuit of current flow.
  • Hence, the polarity at site B is reversed, and an action potential is generated at site B. Thus, the impulse (action potential) generated at site A arrives at site B. The sequence is repeated along the length of the axon and consequently the impulse is conducted.

4. Repolarisation

  • The rise in the stimulus-induced permeability to \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) is extremely short-lived.
  • It is quickly followed by a rise in permeability to \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\).
  • Within a fraction of a second, \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) diffuses outside the membrane and restores the resting potential of the membrane at the site of excitation, and the fibre becomes once more responsive to further stimulation.

2.5 Transmission of Impulses Across a Synapse

A nerve impulse is transmitted from one neuron to another through junctions called synapses. A synapse is formed by the membranes of a pre-synaptic neuron and a post-synaptic neuron, which may or may not be separated by a gap called a synaptic cleft.

Two Types of Synapses

  1. Electrical Synapses:
    • The membranes of pre- and post-synaptic neurons are in very close proximity.
    • Electrical current can flow directly from one neuron into the other across these synapses.
    • Transmission of an impulse across electrical synapses is very similar to impulse conduction along a single axon.
    • Impulse transmission across an electrical synapse is always faster than that across a chemical synapse.
    • Electrical synapses are rare in our system.
  2. Chemical Synapses:
    • The membranes of the pre- and post-synaptic neurons are separated by a fluid-filled space called the synaptic cleft.
    • Chemicals called neurotransmitters are involved in the transmission of impulses at these synapses.
    • The axon terminals contain synaptic vesicles filled with these neurotransmitters.
    • Step-by-step mechanism:
      1. When an impulse (action potential) arrives at the axon terminal, it stimulates the movement of the synaptic vesicles towards the pre-synaptic membrane.
      2. The vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release their neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft.
      3. The released neurotransmitters bind to their specific receptors present on the post-synaptic membrane.
      4. This binding opens ion channels, allowing the entry of ions which can generate a new potential in the post-synaptic neuron.
      5. The new potential developed may be either excitatory or inhibitory.

2.6 Central Neural System (Human Brain)

The brain is the central information processing organ of our body and acts as the 'command and control system'. It controls:

  • Voluntary movements and balance of the body,
  • Functioning of vital involuntary organs (e.g., lungs, heart, kidneys, etc.),
  • Thermoregulation, hunger, and thirst,
  • Circadian (24-hour) rhythms of our body,
  • Activities of several endocrine glands and human behaviour,
  • Processing of vision, hearing, speech, memory, intelligence, emotions, and thoughts.

Protection of the Brain: Cranial Meninges

The human brain is well protected by the bony skull. Inside the skull, the brain is covered by cranial meninges consisting of three layers (from outside inward):

  1. Dura mater: The outer layer.
  2. Arachnoid: A very thin middle layer.
  3. Pia mater: The inner layer (which is in direct contact with the brain tissue).

The brain is divided into three major parts: (i) Forebrain, (ii) Midbrain, and (iii) Hindbrain.

1. Forebrain

The forebrain consists of the cerebrum, thalamus, and hypothalamus.

  • Cerebrum: Forms the major part of the human brain. A deep cleft divides the cerebrum longitudinally into two halves, termed the left and right cerebral hemispheres.
    • Corpus callosum: A tract of nerve fibres that connects the left and right cerebral hemispheres.
    • Cerebral cortex (Grey Matter): The layer of cells that covers the cerebral hemisphere and is thrown into prominent folds. It is referred to as grey matter due to its greyish appearance caused by the concentration of neuron cell bodies here.
    • Functional areas of Cerebral Cortex: Contains motor areas, sensory areas, and large regions that are neither clearly sensory nor motor in function called association areas. Association areas are responsible for complex functions like intersensory associations, memory, and communication.
    • White Matter: Fibres of the tracts covered with the myelin sheath constitute the inner part of the cerebral hemisphere. They give an opaque white appearance to the layer and hence are called the white matter.
  • Thalamus: The cerebrum wraps around the thalamus, which is a major coordinating centre for sensory and motor signaling.
  • Hypothalamus: Lies at the base of the thalamus. It contains a number of centres that control body temperature, urge for eating, and drinking. It also contains several groups of neurosecretory cells, which secrete hormones called hypothalamic hormones.
  • Limbic Lobe / Limbic System: Formed by the inner parts of cerebral hemispheres and a group of associated deep structures like the amygdala, hippocampus, etc. Along with the hypothalamus, the limbic system is involved in:
    • Olfaction and autonomic responses,
    • Regulation of sexual behaviour,
    • Expression of emotional reactions (e.g., excitement, pleasure, rage, and fear), and
    • Motivation.

2. Midbrain

  • Location: Located between the thalamus/hypothalamus of the forebrain and pons of the hindbrain.
  • Cerebral aqueduct: A canal that passes through the midbrain.
  • Corpora quadrigemina: The dorsal portion of the midbrain consists mainly of four round swellings (lobes) called corpora quadrigemina.
  • Function: The midbrain receives and integrates visual, tactile, and auditory inputs.

3. Hindbrain

The hindbrain comprises the pons, cerebellum, and medulla (also called the medulla oblongata).

  • Pons: Consists of fibre tracts that interconnect different regions of the brain.
  • Cerebellum: Has a very convoluted surface in order to provide the additional space for many more neurons. It integrates information received from the semicircular canals of the ear and the auditory system (maintaining posture and body balance).
  • Medulla (Medulla Oblongata): Connected to the spinal cord. It contains centres that control respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, and gastric secretions.

Brain Stem

Three major regions make up the brain stem:

\[ \text{Brain Stem} = \text{Midbrain} + \text{Pons} + \text{Medulla Oblongata} \]

The brain stem forms the connections between the brain and the spinal cord.

3. Key Definitions, Stoichiometry, and Comparative Tables

Essential Ionic Stoichiometry & Membrane States

Active Transport by Sodium-Potassium (\(\mathrm{Na}^{+}-\mathrm{K}^{+}\)) Pump:

\[ 3\,\mathrm{Na}^{+}\;\text{(transported outwards)} \;\longleftrightarrow\; 2\,\mathrm{K}^{+}\;\text{(transported into the cell)} \]

  • Resting Axoplasm (Inside): High \([\mathrm{K}^{+}]\), high negatively charged proteins, low \([\mathrm{Na}^{+}]\). Net charge on inner surface = Negative (\(-\)).
  • Extracellular Fluid (Outside): Low \([\mathrm{K}^{+}]\), high \([\mathrm{Na}^{+}]\). Net charge on outer surface = Positive (\(+\)).
  • Depolarised State (Action Potential): Rapid \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) influx reverses polarity → Outer surface becomes Negative (\(-\)); Inner surface becomes Positive (\(+\)).
  • Repolarised State: Rapid \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) efflux restores resting potential → Outer surface becomes Positive (\(+\)); Inner surface becomes Negative (\(-\)).

4. Conceptual Connections & Physiological Pathways

Pathway 1: Sequence of Events in Impulse Generation and Conduction

  1. Polarised Resting State: \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}-\mathrm{K}^{+}\) pump exports \(3\,\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) and imports \(2\,\mathrm{K}^{+}\); resting membrane is more permeable to \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) → outer surface positive, inner surface negative.
  2. Threshold Stimulus at Site A: Voltage-gated \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) channels open → membrane becomes freely permeable to \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\).
  3. Depolarisation at Site A: Rapid influx of \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) → inner surface becomes positive, outer surface becomes negative → Action Potential generated.
  4. Local Circuit Current to Site B: Current flows from \(A \to B\) on the inner surface and \(B \to A\) on the outer surface → reverses polarity at Site B, propagating the impulse forward.
  5. Repolarisation at Site A: \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) permeability drops rapidly; \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) permeability rises → \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) diffuses outside the membrane → restores resting potential.

Pathway 2: Sequence of Events at a Chemical Synapse

  1. Arrival of action potential (nerve impulse) at the axon terminal (synaptic knob).
  2. Movement of synaptic vesicles towards the pre-synaptic membrane.
  3. Fusion of synaptic vesicles with the pre-synaptic plasma membrane.
  4. Release (exocytosis) of neurotransmitters into the fluid-filled synaptic cleft.
  5. Binding of neurotransmitters to specific receptors located on the post-synaptic membrane.
  6. Opening of ion channels on the post-synaptic membrane, permitting the entry of ions.
  7. Generation of a new potential (either excitatory or inhibitory) in the post-synaptic neuron.

5. Worked Examples (Conceptual & Analytical)

Worked Example 1: Active Transport Stoichiometry Across the Axonal Membrane

Problem: During the maintenance of resting membrane potential, if the sodium-potassium pump hydrolyses ATP to complete \(150\) pumping cycles across a segment of the axonal membrane, calculate: (a) the total number of \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) ions transported out of the axoplasm, (b) the total number of \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) ions transported into the axoplasm, and (c) the net number of positive charges moved outwards.

Solution:

In each cycle, the \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}-\mathrm{K}^{+}\) pump transports \(3\,\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) outwards for \(2\,\mathrm{K}^{+}\) into the cell.

  • (a) Total \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) transported outwards \(= 150 \times 3 = 450\,\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) ions.
  • (b) Total \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) transported inwards \(= 150 \times 2 = 300\,\mathrm{K}^{+}\) ions.
  • (c) Net positive charges moved outwards \(= 450 - 300 = 150\) positive charges (or \(1\) net positive charge outward per cycle), contributing to the electrogenic positivity on the outer surface of the resting membrane.
Worked Example 2: Tracing Local Circuit Currents During Impulse Conduction

Problem: An unmyelinated axon is stimulated at Point A, while Point B lies immediately ahead of Point A in the direction of impulse propagation. At the exact instant when Point A is maximally depolarised and Point B is still in the resting state, state the electrical charge on the inner and outer surfaces at both points and the direction of current flow.

Solution:

  • At Point A (Depolarised): Rapid influx of \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) has reversed the polarity. Therefore, the outer surface is negatively charged (\(-\)) and the inner surface is positively charged (\(+\)).
  • At Point B (Resting/Polarised): The membrane is still in its resting state. Therefore, the outer surface is positively charged (\(+\)) and the inner surface is negatively charged (\(-\)).
  • Direction of Current Flow: Conventional electrical current flows from positive potential to negative potential:
    • On the inner surface (axoplasm): Current flows from Point A (\(+\)) to Point B (\(-\)).
    • On the outer surface (extracellular fluid): Current flows from Point B (\(+\)) to Point A (\(-\)) to complete the circuit.
Worked Example 3: Anatomical & Functional Localization in the Human Brain

Problem: Identify the specific part of the human central neural system responsible for each of the following roles: (a) Most developed part of the human brain, (b) Master clock regulating 24-hour circadian rhythms and body temperature, (c) Tract of nerve fibres connecting left and right cerebral hemispheres, (d) Controlling respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, and gastric secretions.

Solution:

  • (a) Cerebrum — forms the major and most developed part of the human brain.
  • (b) Hypothalamus (specifically the suprachiasmatic nucleus region of the hypothalamus in the forebrain) — acts as the master clock for circadian rhythms and regulates thermoregulation, hunger, and thirst.
  • (c) Corpus callosum — myelinated tract of nerve fibres connecting the two cerebral hemispheres.
  • (d) Medulla (Medulla oblongata) — hindbrain region connected to the spinal cord that houses vital autonomic reflex centres for respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, and gastric secretions.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements accurately describes the neural organisation across different animal groups?

  • (1) Hydra possesses a well-organised brain along with a number of ganglia and neural tissues.
  • (2) Insects have a simple diffuse network of neurons lacking any ganglia.
  • (3) In Hydra, the neural system is composed of a network of neurons, whereas insects possess a brain along with a number of ganglia and neural tissues.

Correct Answer: (3)

Worked Solution: Neural organisation is very simple in lower invertebrates such as Hydra, where it is composed of a network of neurons. In the human peripheral neural system (PNS), the somatic neural system and the autonomic neural system transmit impulses from the CNS respectively to:

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Nissl's granules are granular bodies found in which of the following parts of a neuron?

  • (1) Cell body, dendrites, and axon
  • (2) Cell body and axon terminals only
  • (3) Cell body and dendrites only
  • (4) Axon and nodes of Ranvier only
Correct Answer: (3)

Worked Solution: The cell body contains cytoplasm with typical cell organelles and granular bodies called Nissl's granules. Short branching fibres projecting out of the cell body called dendrites also contain Nissl's granules. The axon and synaptic knobs lack Nissl's granules.

Practice Question 7 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): The stimulus-induced rise in permeability of the axonal membrane to \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) is extremely short-lived and the resting potential is quickly restored at the site of excitation.

Reason (R): The rapid influx of \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) is quickly followed by a rise in permeability to \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\), allowing \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) to diffuse outside the membrane within a fraction of a second.

  • (1) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (2) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (3) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (4) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (1)

Worked Solution: During depolarisation, the stimulus-induced permeability to \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) is extremely short-lived. It is quickly followed by a rise in permeability to \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\). Within a fraction of a second, \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) diffuses outside the membrane (repolarisation) and restores the resting potential at the site of excitation. Thus both Assertion and Reason are true and R correctly explains A.

  • (4) B → D → A → E → C
Correct Answer: (1)

Worked Solution: When an impulse (action potential) arrives at the axon terminal (B), it stimulates the movement of synaptic vesicles towards the membrane where they fuse with the plasma membrane (D) and release their neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft (E). The released neurotransmitters bind to specific receptors on the post-synaptic membrane (A), opening ion channels and generating a new potential in the post-synaptic neuron (C).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

7. Common Pitfalls & High-Yield NEET Revision Traps

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid

  1. Schwann Cells in Unmyelinated Fibres: Students often assume unmyelinated axons lack Schwann cells altogether. Fact: Unmyelinated nerve fibres in the autonomous and somatic neural systems are enclosed by a Schwann cell, but that Schwann cell does not form a myelin sheath around the axon.
  2. Location of Nissl's Granules: Nissl's granules are present in the cell body and dendrites, but are absent in the axon.
  3. \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}-\mathrm{K}^{+}\) Pump Ratio: Do not reverse the stoichiometry. It is always \(3\,\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) outwards for \(2\,\mathrm{K}^{+}\) into the cell.
  4. Direction of Local Current Flow: Between an excited (depolarised) site A and a resting site B ahead of it, current flows from A to B on the inner surface and from B to A on the outer surface.
  5. Brain Stem vs. Hindbrain:
    • Hindbrain = Pons + Cerebellum + Medulla oblongata.
    • Brain Stem = Midbrain + Pons + Medulla oblongata (excludes Cerebellum!).
  6. Thalamus vs. Hypothalamus: Sensory and motor signaling coordination is the role of the thalamus, whereas thermoregulation, hunger, thirst, and neurosecretory hormone production belong to the hypothalamus (which lies at the base of the thalamus).

8. Concise Final Recap

  • Coordination: Interaction in which two or more organs complement one another's functions to maintain homeostasis; achieved jointly by the neural system (rapid point-to-point network) and endocrine system (hormonal integration).
  • Divisions of Human Neural System: CNS (brain + spinal cord) and PNS (afferent and efferent fibres; divided into somatic neural system to skeletal muscles, autonomic neural system to involuntary organs/smooth muscles, and visceral nervous system).
  • Neuron Polarity & Impulse: Resting membrane is polarised (outer \(+\), inner \(-\); high axoplasmic \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) and negatively charged proteins, high extracellular \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\), maintained by \(3\,\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) out / \(2\,\mathrm{K}^{+}\) in pump). Stimulus causes rapid \(\mathrm{Na}^{+}\) influx (depolarisation / action potential), followed by \(\mathrm{K}^{+}\) efflux (repolarisation).
  • Synapses: Electrical synapses (close proximity, direct current flow, faster, rare in humans) vs. Chemical synapses (fluid-filled synaptic cleft, neurotransmitter release from synaptic vesicles, binding to post-synaptic receptors, opening ion channels to generate excitatory or inhibitory potential).
  • Human Brain Architecture: Protected by skull and cranial meninges (outer dura mater, middle arachnoid, inner pia mater).
    • Forebrain: Cerebrum (hemispheres joined by corpus callosum; grey matter cortex with sensory, motor, and association areas; inner myelinated white matter), Thalamus (sensory/motor relay), Hypothalamus (thermoregulation, eating, drinking, hypothalamic hormones), and Limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus; emotions, sexual behaviour, motivation, olfaction).
    • Midbrain: Cerebral aqueduct passes through it; dorsal corpora quadrigemina (4 lobes); integrates visual, tactile, and auditory inputs.
    • Hindbrain: Pons (interconnecting fibre tracts), Cerebellum (convoluted surface; integrates semicircular canal and auditory information for balance), and Medulla oblongata (respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, gastric secretions).
    • Brain stem: Midbrain + Pons + Medulla oblongata.
