Structural Organisation in Animals: Organ Systems & Amphibian Biology (Rana tigrina)

Comprehensive conceptual guide, comparative anatomy, worked examples, and exam-aligned practice assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Scope

Multicellular animals achieve physiological efficiency by distributing life-sustaining tasks across specialised structural levels. This tutorial builds a rigorous foundation for NEET UG by connecting the general principles of tissue-to-organ-system organisation with the detailed external morphology and internal anatomy of the Indian bullfrog (Rana tigrina).

Target Competencies for NEET UG
  • Explain how cells, tissues, organs, and organ systems establish division of labour and reflect evolutionary trends.
  • Distinguish between morphology (external form) and anatomy (internal organ structure) in animals.
  • Describe the adaptive physiology of Rana tigrina, including poikilothermy, camouflage, aestivation, and hibernation.
  • Identify external features and sexual dimorphism in frogs.
  • Trace anatomical pathways across the digestive, respiratory, circulatory, excretory, nervous, endocrine, sensory, and reproductive systems.

2. Levels of Organisation & Division of Labour

In unicellular organisms, a single cell performs digestion, respiration, excretion, and reproduction. In multicellular animals, however, billions of cells coordinate through hierarchical specialisation—a principle known as division of labour.

  • Tissue: A group of similar cells along with their intercellular substances that collectively perform a specific function. Even simple multicellular animals such as Hydra possess thousands of cells organised into distinct cell types.
  • Four Basic Animal Tissues: All complex animals are constructed from only four fundamental tissue types—epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural tissues.
  • Organ: Specific proportions and spatial arrangements of one or more tissue types form an organ (such as the stomach, lung, kidney, or heart). Notably, the vertebrate heart incorporates all four basic tissue types.
  • Organ System: Two or more organs interact physically and/or chemically to execute a shared physiological role, forming systems such as the digestive or respiratory system.

Across animal phyla, increasing complexity in organs and organ systems reveals a discernible evolutionary trend toward greater physiological efficiency and coordination. In zoological studies, morphology denotes the study of externally visible features of the body or its parts, whereas anatomy conventionally refers to the study of internal organs.

3. Biology & Adaptations of the Indian Bullfrog (Rana tigrina)

Frogs belong to Phylum Chordata and Class Amphibia, enabling them to inhabit both terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems. In India, the most common species is Rana tigrina.

  • Poikilothermy (Cold-Blooded Nature): Frogs lack a constant internal body temperature; instead, their body temperature fluctuates with ambient environmental temperature.
  • Camouflage & Mimicry: Frogs alter their skin colouration to blend with grasses or dry land, concealing themselves from predators. This protective colouration is referred to as mimicry.
  • Seasonal Dormancy: Because frogs cannot metabolically buffer extreme temperatures, they retreat into deep burrows during peak summer and peak winter. Summer sleep is called aestivation, and winter sleep is called hibernation. During both states, gaseous exchange occurs exclusively through the moist skin.

4. External Morphology & Sexual Dimorphism

The external body plan of Rana tigrina is streamlined for aquatic swimming and terrestrial leaping:

  • Skin & Hydration: The skin is smooth, slippery, and perpetually moist due to mucus secreted by cutaneous mucous glands. It is highly vascularised. Dorsally, the skin is olive green with dark irregular spots; ventrally, it is uniformly pale yellow. Crucially, a frog never drinks water—it absorbs water directly through its skin.
  • Body Divisions: The body is divisible strictly into a head and a trunk; both a neck and a tail are absent in the adult.
  • Head Structures: Above the mouth sits a pair of nostrils. The eyes are prominent, bulged, and protected underwater by a transparent third eyelid called the nictitating membrane. Behind each eye lies a circular membranous tympanum (ear drum) that receives sound waves; an external ear (pinna) is absent.
  • Limbs: Two pairs of limbs support swimming, walking, leaping, and burrowing. The forelimbs are shorter and terminate in 4 digits, whereas the hind limbs are larger, more muscular, and terminate in 5 digits. The feet possess webbed digits that act as paddles during swimming.

Sexual Dimorphism

Male and female frogs are externally distinguishable during the breeding season by two male-specific features:

  1. Vocal Sacs: Sound-producing pouches present in males to amplify mating calls (absent in females).
  2. Copulatory Pad (Nuptial Pad): A thickened pad on the first digit of each forelimb in males, used to grip the female during amplexus (absent in females).

5. Internal Anatomy & Organ Systems

5.1 Digestive System

Because frogs are carnivores, their alimentary canal is relatively short, with a reduced intestinal length compared to herbivores.

  • Capture & Ingestion: Prey is captured by a muscular, bilobed tongue attached anteriorly. Food passes from the mouth into the buccal cavity, through the pharynx, and down a short oesophagus into the stomach.
  • Gastric & Intestinal Digestion: Gastric walls secrete \(\mathrm{HCl}\) and gastric juices that convert food into partially digested chyme. Chyme enters the first part of the small intestine, the duodenum.
  • Accessory Glands: The liver secretes bile (stored in the gall bladder), and the pancreas secretes pancreatic juice containing digestive enzymes. Both secretions enter the duodenum via a common bile duct. Bile emulsifies fats, while pancreatic enzymes digest carbohydrates and proteins.
  • Absorption & Egestion: Final digestion and absorption occur in the intestine, whose inner wall bears numerous finger-like folds—villi and microvilli—to maximise absorptive surface area. Undigested solid waste moves into the rectum and exits via the cloaca.

5.2 Respiratory System

Frogs employ three respiratory surfaces depending on habitat and activity:

  • Cutaneous Respiration: In water (and during aestivation and hibernation), dissolved oxygen diffuses directly across the moist, vascularised skin.
  • Buccopharyngeal & Pulmonary Respiration: On land, the skin, buccal cavity, and a pair of elongated, pink, sac-like lungs located in the upper trunk (thorax) participate in gas exchange. Air travels from the nostrils into the buccal cavity and then into the lungs.

5.3 Vascular & Lymphatic Systems

Frogs possess a well-developed closed blood vascular system alongside a lymphatic system (lymph, lymph channels, and lymph nodes).

  • Three-Chambered Heart: Located in the upper body cavity and enclosed in a pericardium, the muscular heart has two atria and one ventricle.
  • Accessory Heart Chambers: A triangular sinus venosus joins the right atrium dorsally, receiving deoxygenated blood from the major veins (vena cava). Ventrally, the single ventricle opens into a sac-like conus arteriosus, which distributes blood into the arterial system.
  • Portal Systems: Frogs exhibit two specialised venous connections: the hepatic portal system (linking the intestine to the liver) and the renal portal system (linking the lower body to the kidneys).
  • Blood & Lymph Composition: Blood consists of plasma and formed elements—nucleated RBCs (erythrocytes containing haemoglobin), WBCs (leucocytes), and platelets. Lymph differs from blood by lacking RBCs and certain plasma proteins.

5.4 Excretory System

Adult frogs are ureotelic, excreting nitrogenous waste primarily as urea.

  • Kidneys: A pair of compact, dark-red, bean-shaped kidneys sit slightly posteriorly on either side of the vertebral column. Each kidney contains numerous structural and functional units called uriniferous tubules (nephrons).
  • Ureters & Urinary Bladder: Two ureters emerge from the kidneys and open into the cloaca. In males, the ureters function as urinogenital ducts (carrying both urine and sperm). In females, the ureters and oviducts open separately into the cloaca. A thin-walled urinary bladder lies ventral to the rectum and also opens into the cloaca.

5.5 Control & Coordination (Neural, Endocrine & Sensory)

Chemical coordination is mediated by hormones from prominent endocrine glands: pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, pineal body, pancreatic islets, adrenals, and gonads. Neural control is organised into three divisions:

  • Central Nervous System (CNS): Brain (enclosed in the bony cranium) and spinal cord (enclosed in the vertebral column). The medulla oblongata exits the skull through the foramen magnum to continue as the spinal cord.
  • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS): Includes 10 pairs of cranial nerves arising from the brain, plus spinal nerves.
  • Autonomic Nervous System (ANS): Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions.

Sense Organs: Frogs possess five types of sensory receptors. Only the eyes (simple eyes, possessing a single unit, housed in skull orbits) and internal ears (paired with the external tympanum; serving both hearing and equilibrium/balancing) are well-organised organs. Touch (sensory papillae), taste (taste buds), and smell (nasal epithelium) consist of cellular aggregations around nerve endings.

5.6 Reproductive Systems, Development & Ecological Role

  • Male Reproductive System: A pair of yellowish, ovoid testes adhere to the upper part of the kidneys via a double fold of peritoneum called the mesorchium. From each testis, 10–12 vasa efferentia enter the kidney on that side and open into Bidder's canal, which communicates with the urinogenital duct before emptying into the cloaca.
  • Female Reproductive System: A pair of ovaries sit near the kidneys with no functional connection to them. A pair of oviducts arise from the ovaries and open separately into the cloaca. A mature female lays 2,500 to 3,000 ova at a time.
  • Fertilisation & Metamorphosis: Fertilisation is external and occurs in water. Development includes an aquatic larval stage called a tadpole, which undergoes metamorphosis into the adult frog.
  • Ecological & Economic Importance: Frogs protect agricultural crops by consuming insects, serve as vital links in food chains and food webs, and have muscular legs consumed as human food in some regions.

6. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: External Identification & Physiological State

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): During a field survey in July, a biology student examines a frog found inside a moist burrow. The specimen has 4 digits on each forelimb, 5 webbed digits on each hind limb, a thickened pad on the inner digit of each forelimb, and prominent vocal sacs.

Questions: (a) Identify the sex of the frog. (b) Explain how it respires inside the burrow if ambient temperatures are extremely high.

Solution:

  • (a) Sex Identification: The presence of vocal sacs and a copulatory pad on the first digit of the forelimbs confirms that the specimen is a male frog. (Both males and females share the 4-digit forelimb and 5-digit webbed hind limb pattern.)
  • (b) Respiratory Mode: Seeking shelter in a deep burrow during peak summer heat is aestivation (summer sleep). During aestivation, pulmonary ventilation is suspended, and gaseous exchange occurs exclusively by diffusion across the moist skin (cutaneous respiration).
Worked Example 2: Tracing Gamete vs. Urea Pathways

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Compare the anatomical route taken by a mature sperm cell versus a urea molecule in a male frog, and contrast this with an ovum versus a urea molecule in a female frog.

Solution:

  • Male Frog: Urea is filtered in the nephrons of the kidney and enters the urinogenital duct (ureter), which opens into the cloaca. A sperm cell travels from the testis through one of the 10–12 vasa efferentia into the kidney, enters Bidder's canal, and merges into the same urinogenital duct to exit via the cloaca.
  • Female Frog: Urea passes from the kidney through the ureter directly into the cloaca. An ovum released from the ovary enters the oviduct (which has no functional connection to the kidney) and opens separately into the cloaca.
Worked Example 3: Quantitative Reproductive Output

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Assume a mature female Rana tigrina lays \(2{,}800\) ova in a freshwater pond (within the natural range of \(2{,}500\text{–}3{,}000\)). Suppose \(25\%\) of these ova are successfully fertilised externally, and \(10\%\) of the resulting tadpoles survive metamorphosis to become adult frogs. Calculate the number of adult frogs produced.

Solution:

\[ \text{Fertilised ova} = 2{,}800 \times 0.25 = 700 \] \[ \text{Adult frogs} = 700 \times 0.10 = 70 \]

Under these hypothetical assumptions, \(70\) adult frogs complete metamorphosis.

7. NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding the external morphology of Rana tigrina is incorrect?

  1. (1) The body is divisible into head and trunk, while neck and tail are absent.
  2. (2) Forelimbs terminate in five digits, whereas hind limbs terminate in four webbed digits.
  3. (3) Eyes are bulged and protected underwater by a nictitating membrane.
  4. (4) The frog absorbs water through its moist skin and never drinks water.
Correct Answer: (2)

Explanation: In frogs, the forelimbs end in four digits, whereas the larger, muscular hind limbs end in five webbed digits. Statements (1), (3), and (4) are accurate.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

In a male frog, sperms produced in the testes are transported to the urinogenital duct through which sequence of structures?

  1. (1) Mesorchium \(\rightarrow\) Bidder's canal \(\rightarrow\) Vasa efferentia
  2. (2) Vasa efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Bidder's canal \(\rightarrow\) Urinogenital duct
  3. (3) Bidder's canal \(\rightarrow\) Vasa efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Cloaca
  4. (4) Oviduct \(\rightarrow\) Bidder's canal \(\rightarrow\) Urinogenital duct
Correct Answer: (2)

Explanation: From the testes (held to the kidneys by the mesorchium), 10–12 vasa efferentia enter the kidneys and open into Bidder's canal, which then communicates with the urinogenital duct opening into the cloaca.

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): The alimentary canal of a frog is relatively short.

Reason (R): Frogs are carnivorous animals, so the length of the intestine is reduced.

  1. (1) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (2) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (3) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (4) Both (A) and (R) are false.
Correct Answer: (1)

Explanation: Animal matter is easier to digest than plant cellulose, so carnivores like frogs have a reduced intestinal length and a short alimentary canal.

Practice Question 4 Statement-Based

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: In female frogs, the ureters and oviducts open separately into the cloaca.

Statement II: The ovaries in female frogs have a direct functional connection with the kidneys via Bidder's canal.

  1. (1) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (2) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (3) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (4) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (3)

Explanation: Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because ovaries lie near the kidneys with no functional connection to them; Bidder's canal is part of the male urinogenital pathway.

Practice Question 5 Match-the-Column

Match the brain divisions of a frog in Column I with their respective structures in Column II:

Column I Column II
A. Forebrain i. Optic lobes
B. Midbrain ii. Cerebellum and medulla oblongata
C. Hindbrain iii. Olfactory lobes, cerebral hemispheres, diencephalon
  1. (1) A–iii, B–i, C–ii
  2. (2) A–i, B–iii, C–ii
  3. (3) A–iii, B–ii, C–i

Explanation: In frogs, the forebrain comprises olfactory lobes, paired cerebral hemispheres, and an unpaired diencephalon; the midbrain is characterised by a pair of optic lobes; and the hindbrain consists of the cerebellum and medulla oblongata.

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

Which combination accurately describes the blood and heart of Rana tigrina?

  1. (1) 3-chambered heart; enucleated RBCs; sinus venosus joins left atrium
  2. (2) 3-chambered heart; nucleated RBCs with haemoglobin; sinus venosus joins right atrium
  3. (3) 4-chambered heart; nucleated RBCs; conus arteriosus arises from right atrium
  4. (4) 2-chambered heart; enucleated RBCs; lymph contains RBCs
Correct Answer: (2)

Explanation: Frogs have a 3-chambered heart (two atria, one ventricle), nucleated RBCs containing haemoglobin, and a triangular sinus venosus that joins the right atrium. Conus arteriosus arises from the ventricle, and lymph lacks RBCs.

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

In frogs, which of the following sense organs are well-organised structures rather than simple cellular aggregations around nerve endings?

  1. (1) Sensory papillae and taste buds
  2. (2) Nasal epithelium and sensory papillae
  3. (3) Eyes and internal ears
  4. (4) Taste buds and nasal epithelium
Correct Answer: (3)

Explanation: Eyes (simple eyes in orbits) and internal ears (with tympanum, serving hearing and equilibrium) are well-organised structures, whereas sensory papillae (touch), taste buds (taste), and nasal epithelium (smell) are cellular aggregations around nerve endings.

Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

How many pairs of cranial nerves arise from the brain of a frog, and through which opening does the medulla oblongata continue into the spinal cord?

  1. (1) 12 pairs; foramen magnum
  2. (2) 10 pairs; foramen magnum
  3. (3) 10 pairs; cloacal aperture
  4. (4) 8 pairs; cranium
Correct Answer: (2)

Explanation: Frogs possess 10 pairs of cranial nerves (unlike mammals, which have 12 pairs). The medulla oblongata exits the cranium through the foramen magnum to continue as the spinal cord.

Practice Question 9 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Frogs are classified as ureotelic animals.

Reason (R): Their kidneys eliminate nitrogenous wastes primarily in the form of urea.

  1. (1) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (2) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (3) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (4) Both (A) and (R) are false.
Correct Answer: (1)

Explanation: Animals that excrete urea as their chief nitrogenous waste are termed ureotelic. The frog's kidneys separate urea from blood for excretion.

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following pairs correctly matches a frog's special venous portal system with the organs it connects?

  1. (1) Hepatic portal system: Kidney and lower body parts
  2. (2) Renal portal system: Liver and intestine
  3. (3) Hepatic portal system: Liver and intestine
  4. (4) Pulmonary portal system: Lungs and sinus venosus
Correct Answer: (3)

Explanation: The hepatic portal system is a special venous connection between the intestine and the liver, while the renal portal system connects the lower parts of the body to the kidneys.

8. Common NEET UG Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

High-Yield Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Digit Count Swap: Forelimbs have 4 digits; hind limbs have 5 webbed digits (not vice versa).
  • Cranial Nerve Count: Frogs have 10 pairs of cranial nerves, whereas humans have 12 pairs.
  • RBC Morphology: Frog RBCs are nucleated and contain haemoglobin (unlike mature enucleated mammalian RBCs).
  • Female vs. Male Cloacal Openings: Male ureters carry both urine and sperm (urinogenital ducts); female ureters and oviducts open separately into the cloaca.
  • Eye Structure: Frogs have simple eyes (single unit), not compound eyes.
  • Dormancy Terms: Aestivation = summer sleep; Hibernation = winter sleep; respiration during both = cutaneous.
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Four basic animal tissues: epithelial, connective, muscular, neural (all four present in the heart).
  • Rana tigrina: Chordata, Amphibia, poikilothermic, camouflage/mimicry, no neck or tail.
  • Male dimorphism: vocal sacs + copulatory pad on 1st digit of forelimb.
  • Digestive duct: liver (bile in gall bladder) + pancreas \(\rightarrow\) common bile duct \(\rightarrow\) duodenum.
  • Heart: pericardium, 2 atria + 1 ventricle, dorsal sinus venosus, ventral conus arteriosus.
  • Reproductive output: 2,500–3,000 ova per spawn, external fertilisation in water, tadpole \(\rightarrow\) metamorphosis.

9. Concise Final Recap

Complex animals achieve survival through division of labour across cells, four basic tissues, organs, and organ systems. Representing Class Amphibia, Rana tigrina exhibits seamless adaptation to aquatic and terrestrial life: moist, mucus-coated skin for water absorption and cutaneous respiration; a 3-chambered heart with hepatic and renal portal systems; ureotelic kidneys; a 10-pair cranial nerve system; and distinct male (mesorchium, 10–12 vasa efferentia, Bidder's canal, urinogenital duct) and female (independent ovaries and oviducts) reproductive anatomies culminating in external fertilisation and tadpole metamorphosis.
