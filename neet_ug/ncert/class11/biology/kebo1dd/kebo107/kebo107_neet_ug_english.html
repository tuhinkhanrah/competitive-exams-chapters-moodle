Structural Organisation in Animals: Organ Systems & Frog (Rana tigrina)

Exam Standard: NEET UG Subject: Biology (Zoology) Topic: Tissue to Organ System Level & Amphibian Morphology/Anatomy

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

In unicellular organisms, all vital life processes—such as digestion, respiration, and reproduction—are executed by a single cell. In contrast, multicellular animals possess a complex body where these fundamental functions are carried out by different groups of cells in a well-organised manner. This tutorial explores how tissues organize into organs and organ systems, and provides a comprehensive, exam-aligned study of the morphology and internal anatomy of the common Indian frog (Rana tigrina), representing vertebrates.

Key Competencies for NEET UG:
  • Understand the biological hierarchy from cells to tissues, organs, and organ systems, and how this leads to a division of labour.
  • Distinguish between morphology and anatomy in animals, plants, and microbes.
  • Master the external diagnostic features, sexual dimorphism, and behavioural adaptations (camouflage, aestivation, hibernation) of Rana tigrina.
  • Trace the complete structural and functional pathways of the digestive, respiratory, circulatory, lymphatic, excretory, neuro-endocrine, sensory, and reproductive systems in frogs.

2. Core Concepts: Tissue, Organ, and Organ System

2.1 Cellular Organisation and Division of Labour

Even simple multicellular organisms like Hydra are constructed of different types of cells, where the number of cells in each type can be in thousands. The human body is composed of billions of cells performing diverse functions. In multicellular animals, a group of similar cells along with intercellular substances performing a specific function is called a tissue.

Epithelia, for instance, are sheet-like tissues lining the body's surface and its cavities, ducts, and tubes. Epithelia possess one free surface facing either a body fluid or the outside environment, and their cells are structurally and functionally connected at junctions.

Remarkably, all complex animals consist of only four basic types of tissues:

  1. Epithelial tissue
  2. Connective tissue
  3. Muscular tissue
  4. Neural tissue

These four basic tissues are organised in specific proportions and patterns to form an organ such as the stomach, lung, heart, or kidney. Each organ in our body is made of one or more types of tissues. For example, our heart consists of all four basic types of tissues—epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural.

When two or more organs perform a common function through their physical and/or chemical interaction, they associate to form an organ system (e.g., digestive system, respiratory system). Cells, tissues, organs, and organ systems split up the physiological work in a way that exhibits a division of labour and ensures the survival of the body as a whole. Furthermore, the increasing complexity in organs and organ systems across different animal groups displays a discernible trend known as an evolutionary trend.

Cells \(\longrightarrow\) Tissues (Cells + Intercellular Substances) \(\longrightarrow\) Organs \(\longrightarrow\) Organ Systems \(\longrightarrow\) Whole Organism

2.2 Morphology vs. Anatomy

  • Morphology: Refers to the study of form or externally visible features. In the case of plants or microbes, the term morphology precisely means only this. In the case of animals, morphology refers to the external appearance of the organs or parts of the body.
  • Anatomy: Conventionally used for the study of the morphology of internal organs in animals.

3. Frogs (Rana tigrina): Habitat, Adaptations, and Morphology

3.1 Taxonomic Position and Physiological Adaptations

Frogs are capable of living both on land and in freshwater. They belong to the Class Amphibia of Phylum Chordata. The most common species of frog (the Indian bullfrog) found in India is Rana tigrina.

  • Poikilothermy (Cold-bloodedness): Frogs do not have a constant body temperature; their body temperature varies with the temperature of the environment. Such animals are called cold-blooded or poikilotherms.
  • Camouflage and Mimicry: Frogs change their body colour while in grasses and on dry land to hide from their enemies (camouflage). This protective coloration is called mimicry.
  • Dormancy in Extreme Seasons: Frogs are not seen during peak summer and winter. During these periods, they take shelter in deep burrows to protect themselves from extreme heat and cold. This is known as summer sleep (aestivation) and winter sleep (hibernation), respectively.

3.2 External Features (Morphology)

  • Skin and Water Absorption: The skin is smooth and slippery due to the presence of mucus secreted by mucous glands, and it is always maintained in a moist condition. It is also highly vascularised. The dorsal side of the body is generally olive green with dark irregular spots, while the ventral side is uniformly pale yellow. Crucially, the frog never drinks water but absorbs it through the skin.
  • Body Divisions: The body of a frog is divisible into two main parts: head and trunk. A neck and tail are absent.
  • Structures on the Head:
    • A pair of nostrils is present above the mouth.
    • Eyes are bulged and covered by a nictitating membrane that protects them while in water.
    • On either side of the eyes, a membranous tympanum (ear) receives sound signals. An external ear is absent.
  • Limbs and Locomotion: The forelimbs and hind limbs help in swimming, walking, leaping, and burrowing. The hind limbs end in five (5) digits and are larger and more muscular than the forelimbs, which end in four (4) digits. The feet have webbed digits that assist in swimming.
  • Sexual Dimorphism: Male and female frogs can be distinguished externally. Male frogs possess two diagnostic structures that are completely absent in female frogs:
    1. Sound-producing vocal sacs.
    2. A copulatory pad on the first digit of the forelimbs.

4. Internal Anatomy and Organ Systems of Frog

The body cavity of frogs accommodates well-developed organ systems: digestive, respiratory, circulatory, lymphatic, excretory, nervous, endocrine, sensory, and reproductive systems.

4.1 Digestive System

The digestive system comprises the alimentary canal and digestive glands. Because frogs are carnivores, the alimentary canal is short, and the length of the intestine is reduced.

  • Prey Capture: Food is captured by a muscular bilobed tongue (bilobed at the tip).
  • Pathway of the Alimentary Canal:
    Mouth \(\longrightarrow\) Buccal Cavity \(\longrightarrow\) Pharynx \(\longrightarrow\) Oesophagus (short tube) \(\longrightarrow\) Stomach \(\longrightarrow\) Intestine \(\longrightarrow\) Rectum \(\longrightarrow\) Cloaca \(\longrightarrow\) Cloacal Aperture
  • Digestive Glands and Ducts:
    • Liver: Secretes bile, which is stored in the gall bladder.
    • Pancreas: A digestive gland that produces pancreatic juice containing digestive enzymes.
    • Common Bile Duct: The first part of the small intestine, the duodenum, receives both bile from the gall bladder and pancreatic juices from the pancreas through a common bile duct.
  • Physiology of Digestion and Absorption:
    • In the stomach, digestion takes place by the action of \(\mathrm{HCl}\) and gastric juices secreted from the walls of the stomach.
    • Partially digested acidic food, called chyme, passes from the stomach into the duodenum.
    • In the duodenum, bile emulsifies fat, and pancreatic juices digest carbohydrates and proteins.
    • Final digestion takes place in the intestine.
    • Digested food is absorbed by numerous finger-like folds in the inner wall of the intestine called villi and microvilli.
    • Undigested solid waste moves into the rectum and passes out to the exterior through the cloaca.

4.2 Respiratory System

Frogs respire both in water and on land using different methods:

Environment / Physiological State Respiratory Organ(s) Involved Mode & Mechanism of Respiration
In Water Moist, vascularised Skin Cutaneous respiration: Dissolved oxygen in water is exchanged directly through the skin by diffusion.
On Land (Active State) Buccal cavity, Skin, and Lungs Pulmonary respiration (via lungs), along with buccopharyngeal and cutaneous gaseous exchange.
During Aestivation & Hibernation Skin only Cutaneous respiration: Gaseous exchange takes place exclusively through the moist skin while sheltered in deep burrows.

Structure of Lungs & Air Pathway: The lungs are a pair of elongated, pink-coloured, sac-like structures situated in the upper part of the trunk region (thorax). Air enters through the nostrils \(\longrightarrow\) buccal cavity \(\longrightarrow\) lungs.

4.3 Vascular (Circulatory) and Lymphatic Systems

The vascular system of the frog is a well-developed closed type with single circulation (mixed blood is pumped from the single ventricle). Frogs also possess a lymphatic system.

A. Blood Vascular System (Heart, Blood Vessels, and Blood)

  • Heart Structure: A muscular structure situated in the upper part of the body cavity, enclosed by a protective membrane called the pericardium.
  • Chambers of the Heart: It is three-chambered, consisting of two atria (right and left) and one ventricle.
  • Accessory Chambers:
    • Sinus venosus: A triangular structure that joins the right atrium (dorsally). It receives deoxygenated blood through the major veins called vena cava.
    • Conus arteriosus: The ventricle opens into a sac-like structure called the conus arteriosus on the ventral side of the heart.
  • Arterial and Venous Systems: Blood from the heart is distributed to all body parts by arteries (arterial system), while veins collect blood from body parts back to the heart (venous system).
  • Special Venous Connections (Portal Systems): Frogs possess both portal systems:
    1. Hepatic portal system: Special venous connection between the liver and intestine.
    2. Renal portal system: Special venous connection between the kidney and lower parts of the body.
  • Composition of Blood: Composed of liquid plasma and blood cells—erythrocytes (RBCs), leucocytes (WBCs), and platelets.
    • Important NEET Fact: Frog RBCs are nucleated and contain the red-coloured respiratory pigment haemoglobin (unlike mature mammalian RBCs, which are enucleated).

B. Lymphatic System

  • Consists of lymph, lymph channels, and lymph nodes.
  • Difference between Lymph and Blood: Lymph is different from blood because it lacks a few proteins and RBCs.

4.4 Excretory System

The elimination of nitrogenous wastes is performed by a well-developed excretory system consisting of a pair of kidneys, ureters, cloaca, and urinary bladder.

  • Kidneys: Compact, dark red, and bean-like structures situated a little posteriorly in the body cavity on both sides of the vertebral column. Each kidney is composed of several structural and functional units called uriniferous tubules or nephrons.
  • Ureters (Sex-Specific Function):
    • In male frogs, two ureters emerge from the kidneys and act as a urinogenital duct (carrying both urine and spermatozoa), opening into the cloaca.
    • In female frogs, the ureters (carrying only urine) and the oviducts (carrying ova) open separately into the cloaca.
  • Urinary Bladder: A thin-walled structure present ventral to the rectum, which also opens into the cloaca.
  • Nature of Nitrogenous Waste: The adult frog excretes urea and is therefore a ureotelic animal. Excretory wastes are carried by blood into the kidney, where they are separated and excreted.

4.5 System for Control and Coordination (Neural & Endocrine)

Control and coordination are highly evolved in the frog, comprising both the neural system and endocrine glands.

A. Endocrine System

Chemical coordination of various organs is achieved by hormones secreted by endocrine glands. The prominent endocrine glands found in frogs are:

  • Pituitary, Thyroid, Parathyroid, Thymus, Pineal body, Pancreatic islets, Adrenals, and Gonads.

B. Nervous System

The nervous system is organised into three divisions:

  1. Central Nervous System (CNS): Brain and spinal cord.
  2. Peripheral Nervous System (PNS): Cranial and spinal nerves. There are ten (10) pairs of cranial nerves arising from the brain.
  3. Autonomic Nervous System (ANS): Sympathetic and parasympathetic systems.

Structure of the Brain: Enclosed in a bony structure called the brain box (cranium), the brain is divided into three major regions:

Brain Division Component Structures
Fore-brain Olfactory lobes, paired cerebral hemispheres, and unpaired diencephalon.
Mid-brain Characterised by a pair of optic lobes.
Hind-brain Cerebellum and medulla oblongata.

The medulla oblongata passes out through the foramen magnum and continues into the spinal cord, which is enclosed in the vertebral column.

4.6 Sense Organs

Frogs possess five different types of sense organs. Out of these, only the eyes and internal ears are well-organised structures, whereas the remaining three are merely cellular aggregations around nerve endings:

Sensory Modality Sense Organ in Frog Level of Structural Organisation
Touch Sensory papillae Cellular aggregations around nerve endings
Taste Taste buds Cellular aggregations around nerve endings
Smell Nasal epithelium Cellular aggregations around nerve endings
Vision Eyes (pair of spherical structures in skull orbit; simple eyes possessing only one unit) Well-organised structures
Hearing & Equilibrium Tympanum with internal ears (external ear is absent; only tympanum is visible externally) Well-organised structures (functions in both hearing and balancing/equilibrium)

4.7 Reproductive Systems and Development

Frogs have well-organised male and female reproductive systems.

A. Male Reproductive System

  • Testes: A pair of yellowish, ovoid testes found adhered to the upper part of the kidneys by a double fold of peritoneum called mesorchium.
  • Vasa efferentia: 10–12 in number; they arise from the testes, enter the kidneys on their side, and open into Bidder's canal.
  • Pathway of Spermatozoa:
    Testes \(\longrightarrow\) Vasa efferentia (10–12) \(\longrightarrow\) Bidder's canal (inside kidney) \(\longrightarrow\) Urinogenital duct (Ureter) \(\longrightarrow\) Cloaca \(\longrightarrow\) Cloacal Aperture
  • Cloaca: A small, median chamber used to pass faecal matter, urine, and sperms to the exterior.

B. Female Reproductive System

  • Ovaries: A pair of ovaries situated near the kidneys. Crucially, there is no functional connection between the ovaries and the kidneys.
  • Oviducts: A pair of oviducts arising from the ovaries opens into the cloaca separately (distinct from the ureters).
  • Oviposition, Fertilisation, and Development:
    • A mature female can lay 2500 to 3000 ova at a time.
    • Fertilisation is external and takes place in water.
    • Development is indirect and involves an aquatic larval stage called a tadpole. The tadpole undergoes metamorphosis to form the adult frog.

4.8 Ecological and Economic Importance of Frogs

  • Crop Protection: Frogs are beneficial for mankind because they eat insects and protect crops from pests.
  • Ecological Balance: Frogs maintain ecological balance because they serve as an important link in the food chain and food web in the ecosystem.
  • Human Food Source: In some countries, the muscular legs of frogs are used as food by humans.

6. Worked Examples & Conceptual Discussions

Worked Example 1: Structural & Functional Role of Ureters in Frogs

Problem Statement: Mention and compare the exact physiological function of the ureters in male and female frogs.

Solution:

  • In male frogs, two ureters emerge from the kidneys and function as urinogenital ducts. Because the vasa efferentia from the testes enter the kidneys and open into Bidder's canal (which communicates with the ureter), the male ureter transports both urine and spermatozoa into the cloaca.
  • In female frogs, the ovaries have no functional connection with the kidneys. Therefore, the female ureters function solely to conduct urine from the kidneys to the cloaca, while the oviducts open separately into the cloaca to release ova.

Worked Example 2: Tracing Anatomical Pathways

Problem Statement: A red blood cell is present in the capillaries of the frog's intestine, and a spermatozoon is produced in the frog's testis. Trace (a) the special venous pathway taken by the blood from the intestine before returning to the heart, and (b) the exact sequence of ducts traversed by the spermatozoon before exiting the body.

Solution:

  1. Venous Pathway from Intestine: Frogs possess a special venous connection between the intestine and the liver called the hepatic portal system. Blood from the intestine first passes to the liver via the hepatic portal vein, and then from the liver through the systemic venous circulation (vena cava) into the triangular sinus venosus, which empties into the right atrium of the heart.
  2. Pathway of Spermatozoon: Testis \(\longrightarrow\) Vasa efferentia (\(10\text{–}12\) in number) \(\longrightarrow\) Bidder's canal (inside kidney) \(\longrightarrow\) Urinogenital duct (ureter) \(\longrightarrow\) Cloaca \(\longrightarrow\) Cloacal aperture (to exterior).

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements correctly describes the composition of tissues in the human heart and the definition of animal anatomy?

  • (A) The heart is composed exclusively of muscular and connective tissues; anatomy is the study of externally visible features in microbes.
  • (B) The heart consists of all four basic types of tissues (epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural); anatomy conventionally refers to the study of the morphology of internal organs in animals.
  • (C) The heart consists of muscular, connective, and neural tissues but lacks epithelial tissue; morphology refers to the study of internal viscera.
  • (D) Only organs of the digestive system contain all four basic tissue types; anatomy is synonymous with external morphology in plants.
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Each organ in our body is made of one or more types of tissues. Specifically, our heart consists of all four basic types of tissues: epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural. Furthermore, while morphology refers to externally visible features, the word anatomy is conventionally used for the study of the morphology of internal organs in animals.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

During peak summer and winter, Rana tigrina takes shelter in deep burrows to protect itself from extreme heat and cold. How does gaseous exchange occur during these periods of aestivation and hibernation?

  • (A) Exclusively through pulmonary respiration via elongated pink lungs
  • (B) Through the buccal cavity lining and nostrils by active ventilation
  • (C) Exclusively through the moist, vascularised skin (cutaneous respiration)

Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: During summer sleep (aestivation) and winter sleep (hibernation), frogs remain inactive in deep burrows and do not use lungs or buccal respiration. Gaseous exchange during aestivation and hibernation takes place solely through the moist skin (cutaneous respiration).

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following accurately describes the accessory chambers and portal systems of the frog's circulatory system?

  • (A) Sinus venosus joins the left atrium; conus arteriosus arises from the right atrium; renal portal system is absent.

8. Common NEET Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Pitfall 1 — Digit Count Reversal: Students frequently swap the digit counts of the limbs. Remember: Forelimbs = 4 digits (with copulatory pad on the 1st digit in males); Hind limbs = 5 webbed digits (larger and more muscular).
  • Pitfall 2 — Mammalian vs. Amphibian RBCs: Unlike human RBCs, frog RBCs are nucleated and contain haemoglobin. Also remember that frog lymph lacks a few proteins and RBCs.
  • Pitfall 3 — Sinus Venosus vs. Conus Arteriosus: Do not confuse their locations! Sinus venosus (triangular, dorsal) receives vena cava and joins the right atrium. Conus arteriosus (sac-like, ventral) receives blood pumped out of the ventricle.
  • Pitfall 4 — Cranial Nerve Number: Frogs (amphibians, anamniotes) have 10 pairs of cranial nerves—do not confuse this with the 12 pairs found in reptiles, birds, and mammals.
  • Pitfall 5 — Mesorchium vs. Vasa Efferentia: Mesorchium is the double fold of peritoneum that adheres the testis to the kidney; vasa efferentia (10–12) are the actual ducts that carry sperm from the testis into Bidder's canal inside the kidney.
  • Pitfall 6 — Ovary-Kidney Connection: While testes have a direct structural and functional ductal connection with the kidneys in males, ovaries have NO functional connection with the kidneys in females.

Pre-Exam Rapid Revision Checklist

  • ☑ All 4 basic tissue types (epithelial, connective, muscular, neural) are present in the heart.
  • ☑ Rana tigrina: Class Amphibia, Phylum Chordata; poikilothermic; shows camouflage (mimicry), aestivation (summer sleep), and hibernation (winter sleep).
  • ☑ Never drinks water; absorbs water through moist, mucous-covered skin.
  • ☑ Alimentary canal is short (carnivorous); bilobed tongue captures prey; common bile duct brings bile + pancreatic juice to duodenum.
  • ☑ Respiration: Cutaneous in water & during aestivation/hibernation; cutaneous, buccopharyngeal, and pulmonary on land.
  • ☑ Closed vascular system with single circulation; 3-chambered heart; both hepatic and renal portal systems present.
  • ☑ Ureotelic adult; kidney has nephrons (uriniferous tubules); urinary bladder is ventral to rectum.
  • ☑ Brain: Forebrain (olfactory lobes, paired cerebral hemispheres, unpaired diencephalon), Midbrain (paired optic lobes), Hindbrain (cerebellum, medulla oblongata passing through foramen magnum).
  • ☑ Well-organised sense organs: Eyes (simple) and Internal ears (hearing + equilibrium). Cellular aggregations: Sensory papillae, taste buds, nasal epithelium.
  • ☑ External fertilisation in water; \(2500\text{–}3000\) ova laid at a time; aquatic tadpole larva undergoes metamorphosis into adult.

9. Concise Final Recap

In multicellular animals, similar cells and intercellular substances form tissues, which organise in specific proportions to form organs, and interacting organs form organ systems—achieving a division of labour essential for survival. The Indian bullfrog (Rana tigrina) exemplifies vertebrate structural organisation. Adapted to both terrestrial and freshwater life, its moist vascular skin enables water absorption and cutaneous respiration, while its internal cavity houses well-coordinated digestive, pulmonary, closed single-circuit vascular, portal, lymphatic, ureotelic excretory, neuro-endocrine, sensory, and reproductive systems. Together, these systems illustrate how cellular and tissue-level specialization supports complex physiological life and ecological balance.
