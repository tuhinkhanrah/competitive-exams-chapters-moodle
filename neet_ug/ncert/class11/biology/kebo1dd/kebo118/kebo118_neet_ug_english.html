Neural Control and Coordination

Comprehensive Concept Tutorial, Schematic Mechanisms, Worked Examples, and NEET UG Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives and Overview

Multicellular organisms depend on rapid, precise communication among organs to maintain a stable internal environment (homeostasis). This tutorial builds a complete conceptual and exam-ready understanding of neural coordination in humans, aligned with the NEET UG syllabus.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between neural and endocrine modes of physiological integration.
  • Classify the divisions of the human neural system (CNS, PNS, somatic, autonomic, and visceral components).
  • Identify the structural parts of a neuron and classify neurons by polarity and myelination.
  • Explain the ionic basis of resting membrane potential, depolarisation, action potential propagation, and repolarisation.
  • Compare electrical and chemical synapses and trace neurotransmitter-mediated signal transmission.
  • Map the anatomical subdivisions of the human brain (forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain) to their specific physiological functions.

2. Physiological Coordination and Evolution of Neural Complexity

Coordination is the process through which two or more organs interact and complement one another's functions. During vigorous physical exertion, for instance, skeletal muscles require elevated oxygen and ATP delivery; consequently, breathing rate, heart rate, and blood flow increase simultaneously. Once exercise stops, neural and chemical signals return cardiac, pulmonary, and renal activities to baseline.

Two organ systems jointly orchestrate this integration:

  • Neural System: Provides an organised network of point-to-point connections for rapid, short-lived, targeted coordination via electrical impulses and neurotransmitters.
  • Endocrine System: Provides slower, widespread chemical integration via hormones transported through the bloodstream.

Comparative Neural Organisation Across Animals

All animal neural systems are built from specialised excitable cells called neurons, which detect, receive, and transmit stimuli. Complexity increases across evolutionary grades:

Animal Group / Example Level of Neural Organisation Key Structural Feature
Lower invertebrates (e.g., Hydra) Simple Diffuse network of interconnected neurons without a central brain
Arthropods (e.g., Insects) Intermediate / Organised Distinct brain accompanied by segmental ganglia and neural tissues
Vertebrates (e.g., Humans) Highly developed Centralised brain and spinal cord with extensive peripheral nerves

3. Organisation of the Human Neural System

The human neural system is divided into two principal anatomical divisions: the Central Neural System (CNS) and the Peripheral Neural System (PNS).

  • Central Neural System (CNS): Comprises the brain and the spinal cord. It serves as the primary site of information processing, integration, and command.
  • Peripheral Neural System (PNS): Comprises all nerves of the body associated with the brain (cranial nerves) and spinal cord (spinal nerves).

Functional Classification of PNS Fibres and Divisions

Based on impulse direction, PNS nerve fibres are classified into two types:

  • Afferent (Sensory) Fibres: Transmit impulses from peripheral tissues or organs towards the CNS.
  • Efferent (Motor) Fibres: Transmit regulatory impulses away from the CNS to target peripheral tissues or organs.

Based on target effectors, the PNS is subdivided into:

  1. Somatic Neural System: Relays motor impulses from the CNS to skeletal muscles (voluntary control).
  2. Autonomic Neural System (ANS): Transmits impulses from the CNS to involuntary organs and smooth muscles. It is further divided into the sympathetic neural system (typically mobilising energy during stress) and the parasympathetic neural system (typically conserving energy and promoting resting functions).
  3. Visceral Nervous System: The component of the PNS comprising the complete complex of nerves, fibres, ganglia, and plexuses through which impulses travel both from the CNS to the viscera and from the viscera back to the CNS.

4. Neuron: Structural and Functional Unit

A neuron is a microscopic, excitable cell specialised for electrical signalling. Every typical neuron consists of three main regions:

  1. Cell Body (Soma / Cyton): Contains cytoplasm with standard organelles and characteristic basophilic granular bodies called Nissl's granules (involved in protein synthesis).
  2. Dendrites: Short, repeatedly branching fibres projecting from the cell body that also contain Nissl's granules. They receive incoming signals and transmit impulses towards the cell body.
  3. Axon: A single long fibre transmitting impulses away from the cell body towards a synapse or a neuromuscular junction. Note that axons lack Nissl's granules. The distal end of an axon branches into axon terminals, each ending in a bulb-like synaptic knob packed with synaptic vesicles containing chemical messengers called neurotransmitters.

Classification of Neurons

Neurons are classified structurally by the number of processes extending from the cell body, and functionally by the presence or absence of a myelin sheath around the axon:

Neuron Type (by Polarity) Process Arrangement Typical Anatomical Location
Multipolar One axon and two or more dendrites Cerebral cortex
Bipolar One axon and one dendrite Retina of the eye
Unipolar Cell body with one axon only (no dendrites) Usually found in the embryonic stage
High-Yield NEET Distinction: Myelinated vs. Non-Myelinated Axons

Both myelinated and non-myelinated nerve fibres in the PNS are associated with Schwann cells:

  • Myelinated fibres: Enveloped by Schwann cells that wrap around the axon to form an insulating myelin sheath. Periodic uninsulated gaps between adjacent sheaths are called nodes of Ranvier. Found in spinal and cranial nerves.
  • Non-myelinated fibres: Enclosed by a Schwann cell that does not form a myelin sheath around the axon. Commonly found in the autonomic and somatic neural systems.

5. Generation and Conduction of a Nerve Impulse

Neurons are excitable because their plasma membranes maintain an asymmetric distribution of ions, creating a polarised electrical state. Selective ion channels and active transport pumps govern every phase of impulse generation and propagation.

Phase 1: Resting Potential (Polarised State)

When a neuron is not conducting an impulse, its axonal membrane is:

  • Comparatively more permeable to potassium ions (K⁺).
  • Nearly impermeable to sodium ions (Na⁺).
  • Impermeable to negatively charged proteins present inside the axoplasm.

Consequently, the axoplasm has a high concentration of K⁺ and negatively charged proteins, and a low concentration of Na⁺, whereas the extracellular fluid has a high concentration of Na⁺ and a low concentration of K⁺. This steep ionic gradient is actively maintained by the sodium–potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), which uses ATP to transport:

\[3\,\mathrm{Na}^{+}\text{ outwards} \quad : \quad 2\,\mathrm{K}^{+}\text{ inwards per ATP-driven cycle}\]

Because more positive charges are pumped out than in, and large anionic proteins remain trapped inside, the outer surface of the axonal membrane carries a net positive charge while the inner surface carries a net negative charge. This electrical potential difference across the resting plasma membrane is the resting potential (a polarised state).

Phase 2: Depolarisation and Action Potential

When a threshold stimulus is applied at a specific site (say, point A) on a polarised membrane:

  1. The membrane at site A suddenly becomes freely permeable to Na⁺.
  2. A rapid influx of Na⁺ into the axoplasm occurs down its concentration gradient.
  3. Polarity reverses at site A: the outer surface becomes negatively charged and the inner surface becomes positively charged. The membrane at site A is now depolarised.
  4. The electrical potential difference across the membrane at site A during this reversal is the action potential, which constitutes a nerve impulse.

Phase 3: Impulse Conduction Along the Axon

Immediately ahead of site A, the neighbouring resting region (say, site B) still has a positive outer surface and negative inner surface. This potential difference drives local ionic currents:

  • On the inner surface: Current flows from site A to site B.
  • On the outer surface: Current flows from site B to site A, completing the electrical circuit.

This current depolarises site B, reversing its polarity and generating a new action potential there. The sequence repeats continuously along the axon, propagating the impulse forward.

Phase 4: Repolarisation

The stimulus-induced surge in Na⁺ permeability is extremely short-lived. It is rapidly followed by a rise in permeability to K⁺. Within a fraction of a second, K⁺ diffuses out of the axoplasm across the membrane, restoring the positive outer / negative inner charge distribution (resting potential) at the excited site so the fibre becomes responsive to further stimulation.

6. Synaptic Transmission of Impulses

A nerve impulse travels from one neuron to the next across a specialised junction called a synapse, formed by the membrane of a pre-synaptic neuron and a post-synaptic neuron, which may or may not be separated by a gap called the synaptic cleft.

Feature Electrical Synapse Chemical Synapse
Membrane proximity Pre- and post-synaptic membranes are in very close proximity Separated by a fluid-filled synaptic cleft
Mechanism Electrical current flows directly from one neuron to the next Neurotransmitters diffuse across the cleft and bind to receptors
Speed of transmission Always faster (similar to conduction along a single axon) Slower due to synaptic delay (vesicle fusion and diffusion)
Occurrence in humans Rare in our neural system Predominant type in the human neural system

Step-by-Step Mechanism at a Chemical Synapse

  1. An action potential arrives at the axon terminal (synaptic knob) of the pre-synaptic neuron.
  2. The impulse stimulates synaptic vesicles (filled with neurotransmitters) to move towards and fuse with the pre-synaptic membrane.
  3. Neurotransmitters are released into the synaptic cleft.
  4. The released neurotransmitters bind to specific receptors located on the post-synaptic membrane.
  5. Receptor binding opens specific ion channels, allowing ions to enter the post-synaptic neuron and generate a new potential, which may be either excitatory or inhibitory depending on the neurotransmitter and channel type.

7. Central Neural System: The Human Brain

The brain is the central information-processing organ and acts as the body's command and control system. It governs voluntary movements, balance, vital involuntary organs (lungs, heart, kidneys), thermoregulation, hunger and thirst, circadian (24-hour) rhythms, endocrine gland activity, behaviour, vision, hearing, speech, memory, intelligence, emotions, and thoughts.

Protective Coverings: Cranial Meninges

Protected externally by the bony skull, the brain is wrapped internally by three cranial meninges (from outer to inner):

  1. Dura mater: Tough outer layer.
  2. Arachnoid: Very thin middle layer.
  3. Pia mater: Innermost layer in direct contact with brain tissue.

8. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Ion Stoichiometry of the Sodium–Potassium Pump

Scenario (Illustrative Assumption): Suppose a segment of a resting axonal membrane performs \(500\) complete ATP-driven cycles of the \(\mathrm{Na}^+/\mathrm{K}^+\) pump to maintain its resting potential. Calculate the net number of positive charges shifted across the membrane and identify which side becomes more positive.

Solution:

  1. In each active transport cycle, the \(\mathrm{Na}^+/\mathrm{K}^+\) pump exports \(3\,\mathrm{Na}^+\) ions out of the axoplasm and imports \(2\,\mathrm{K}^+\) ions into the axoplasm.
  2. For \(500\) cycles: \[ \text{Na}^+ \text{ exported} = 500 \times 3 = 1500\text{ ions (outward)} \] \[ \text{K}^+ \text{ imported} = 500 \times 2 = 1000\text{ ions (inward)} \]
  3. Since both \(\mathrm{Na}^+\) and \(\mathrm{K}^+\) carry a \(+1\) charge, the net outward movement of positive charge is: \[ 1500 - 1000 = 500\text{ elementary positive charges outward} \]

Conclusion: A net \(500\) positive charges are shifted to the outer surface of the axonal membrane, reinforcing the positive exterior and negative interior of the polarised resting state.

Worked Example 2: Localising Brain Lesions from Physiological Symptoms

Scenario: A patient presents with three distinct deficits following a focal neurological injury: (i) loss of body temperature regulation and appetite control, (ii) impaired coordination of balance despite intact muscle strength, and (iii) unstable respiratory rhythm. Identify the specific brain structure associated with each symptom.

Solution:

  • Loss of thermoregulation and appetite: Points to the hypothalamus (forebrain), which houses centres for body temperature and the urges for eating and drinking.
  • Impaired balance and coordination: Points to the cerebellum (hindbrain), which integrates vestibular inputs from the semicircular canals and coordinates motor precision.
  • Unstable respiratory rhythm: Points to the medulla oblongata (hindbrain), which contains vital centres regulating respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, and gastric secretions.

9. NEET UG Practice Questions

Attempt all questions below to test your conceptual clarity and exam readiness. Each item is an original practice question modelled on NEET UG patterns.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following correctly describes the direction of local electrical current flow during the propagation of an action potential from an excited site P to an adjacent resting site Q along an unmyelinated axon?

  • (A) Inner surface: Q to P; Outer surface: P to Q
  • (B) Inner surface: P to Q; Outer surface: Q to P
  • (C) Both inner and outer surfaces: P to Q
  • (D) Both inner and outer surfaces: Q to P
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: At the depolarised site P, the inner surface is positively charged and the outer surface is negatively charged. At the resting site Q ahead of it, the inner surface is negative and the outer surface is positive. Current flows from positive to negative: hence from P to Q on the inner surface and from Q to P on the outer surface to complete the circuit.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Select the incorrect statement regarding the structure and classification of neurons.

  • (A) Nissl's granules are present in both the cell body and the dendrites.
  • (B) Bipolar neurons possess one axon and one dendrite and are found in the retina of the eye.
  • (C) Non-myelinated nerve fibres in the peripheral neural system lack Schwann cells completely.
  • (D) Multipolar neurons are characteristic of the cerebral cortex.
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Non-myelinated nerve fibres are enclosed by Schwann cells, but those Schwann cells do not wrap around the axon to form a myelin sheath. Statements (A), (B), and (D) are factually accurate.

Practice Question 3 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Impulse transmission across an electrical synapse is always faster than across a chemical synapse.
Reason (R): At an electrical synapse, pre- and post-synaptic membranes are in very close proximity, allowing direct flow of electrical current without neurotransmitter release.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Electrical synapses lack a wide synaptic cleft and do not require vesicle movement, exocytosis, or neurotransmitter diffusion. Direct ionic current flow makes transmission nearly instantaneous, much like conduction along a single axon.

Practice Question 4 Statement I & II

Statement I: The cerebral cortex appears greyish due to the high concentration of myelinated axon tracts.
Statement II: Association areas of the cerebral cortex are neither clearly sensory nor motor, and are responsible for memory, communication, and intersensory associations.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (D)

Explanation: Statement I is incorrect because the cerebral cortex (grey matter) appears greyish due to concentrated neuron cell bodies, whereas myelinated fibre tracts form the inner opaque white matter. Statement II accurately describes association areas.

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

During repolarisation of an excited axonal membrane, restoration of the resting potential is primarily achieved by:

  • (A) Rapid influx of K⁺ ions into the axoplasm
  • (B) Rapid efflux of K⁺ ions outside the membrane
  • (C) Continuous influx of Na⁺ ions through voltage-independent channels
  • (D) Efflux of negatively charged proteins into the extracellular fluid
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Following the brief rise in Na⁺ permeability during depolarisation, membrane permeability to K⁺ increases rapidly. K⁺ diffuses out of the axoplasm within a fraction of a second, restoring the positive outer / negative inner resting polarity.

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

Which combination of structures constitutes the human brain stem?

  • (A) Thalamus, Hypothalamus, and Midbrain
  • (B) Cerebellum, Pons, and Medulla oblongata
  • (C) Midbrain, Pons, and Medulla oblongata
  • (D) Cerebrum, Midbrain, and Spinal cord
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: The brain stem consists of three regions: the midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata. Note that the cerebellum is part of the hindbrain, not the brain stem.

10. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Avoid These Frequent Exam Traps
  • Schwann cells in non-myelinated fibres: Non-myelinated fibres do have Schwann cells; they simply lack a myelin sheath.
  • Brain stem vs. Hindbrain: Hindbrain = Pons + Cerebellum + Medulla. Brain stem = Midbrain + Pons + Medulla (excludes cerebellum).
  • Pump stoichiometry: Always \(3\,\mathrm{Na}^+\) out for \(2\,\mathrm{K}^+\) in—never reverse these numbers or directions.
  • Meninges order (outer to inner): Dura mater → Arachnoid → Pia mater (mnemonic: DAP).
  • Afferent vs. Efferent: Afferent = Arriving at CNS; Efferent = Exiting CNS.

Concise Final Recap

  • Neurons are excitable cells with dendrites (towards soma), cell body (with Nissl's granules), and an axon (away from soma).
  • Resting membrane is polarised (+ outside, − inside) due to differential ion permeability, intracellular anionic proteins, and the \(3\,\mathrm{Na}^+ : 2\,\mathrm{K}^+\) pump.
  • Depolarisation is driven by rapid \(\mathrm{Na}^+\) influx; repolarisation is driven by rapid \(\mathrm{K}^+\) efflux.
  • Chemical synapses convert an electrical impulse into a neurotransmitter signal across the synaptic cleft, generating an excitatory or inhibitory post-synaptic potential.
  • The brain integrates sensory, motor, homeostatic, and cognitive functions across the forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain.
