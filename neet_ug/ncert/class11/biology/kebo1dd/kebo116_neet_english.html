NEET UG Biology • Human Physiology

Excretory Products and Their Elimination

Comprehensive, Self-Contained Exam Tutorial with Structural Diagrams, Physiological Mechanisms, Concept Exercises, and NEET UG Practice Questions

1. Learning Objectives & Syllabus Overview

Animals accumulate nitrogenous wastes (ammonia, urea, uric acid), carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)), water, and ions such as \(\mathrm{Na^+}\), \(\mathrm{K^+}\), \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), phosphate, and sulphate either through metabolic activities or by excess ingestion. These substances must be eliminated totally or partially to maintain internal homeostasis. By mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Classify animals into ammonotelic, ureotelic, and uricotelic groups based on nitrogenous waste toxicity, solubility, and water requirement.
  • Identify specialized excretory organs across the animal kingdom (protonephridia, nephridia, Malpighian tubules, antennal/green glands, and vertebrate kidneys).
  • Describe the macroscopic anatomy of the human urinary system and the microscopic structure of cortical and juxtamedullary nephrons.
  • Explain the three steps of urine formation: glomerular filtration (ultrafiltration), tubular reabsorption, and tubular secretion.
  • Analyze the specific transport functions of the Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT), Henle's Loop, Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT), and Collecting Duct.
  • Trace the counter-current mechanism operating between Henle's loop and the vasa recta that establishes the medullary osmolarity gradient (\(300\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) to \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\)).
  • Evaluate neuro-hormonal regulation of kidney function via ADH (Vasopressin), the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS), and Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF).
  • Understand the micturition reflex, accessory excretory organs (lungs, liver, skin), clinical urinalysis, hemodialysis, and renal disorders.

2. Modes of Nitrogenous Excretion & Animal Excretory Structures

2.1 Major Forms of Nitrogenous Wastes

Ammonia, urea, and uric acid are the three primary nitrogenous wastes excreted by animals. Their evolutionary selection depends primarily on habitat and the availability of water:

  • Ammonia (Ammonotelism): Ammonia is the most toxic form of nitrogenous waste and requires a large amount of water for its elimination. The process of excreting ammonia is called ammonotelism. Because ammonia is readily soluble in water, it is generally excreted by diffusion across body surfaces or through gill surfaces (in fishes) as ammonium ions (\(\mathrm{NH_4^+}\)). Kidneys do not play any significant role in its removal. Examples include many bony fishes, aquatic amphibians, and aquatic insects.
  • Urea (Ureotelism): Terrestrial adaptation required the production of less toxic nitrogenous wastes such as urea and uric acid to conserve water. Animals that mainly excrete urea are called ureotelic. Ammonia produced by cellular metabolism is converted into urea in the liver of these animals and released into the blood, which is then filtered and excreted out by the kidneys. Notably, some amount of urea may be retained in the kidney matrix of some of these animals to maintain a desired osmolarity. Examples include mammals, many terrestrial amphibians, and marine fishes.
  • Uric Acid (Uricotelism): Uric acid is the least toxic nitrogenous waste and can be removed with a minimum loss of water in the form of a semi-solid pellet or paste. Animals excreting uric acid are called uricotelic. Examples include reptiles, birds, land snails, and insects.

3. Human Excretory System

3.1 Gross Anatomy of the Urinary System and Kidneys

In humans, the excretory system consists of one pair of kidneys, one pair of ureters, a single urinary bladder, and a single urethra.

  • Shape, Colour, and Location: Kidneys are reddish-brown, bean-shaped structures situated between the levels of the last thoracic (\(\mathrm{T_{12}}\)) and third lumbar (\(\mathrm{L_3}\)) vertebra, close to the dorsal inner wall of the abdominal cavity.
  • Dimensions and Weight: Each adult human kidney measures \(10\text{–}12\,\mathrm{cm}\) in length, \(5\text{–}7\,\mathrm{cm}\) in width, and \(2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{cm}\) in thickness, with an average weight of \(120\text{–}170\,\mathrm{g}\).
  • Hilum and Renal Pelvis: Towards the centre of the inner concave surface of the kidney is a notch called the hilum, through which the ureter, blood vessels, and nerves enter. Inner to the hilum is a broad, funnel-shaped space called the renal pelvis with projections called calyces (singular: calyx).
  • Capsule, Cortex, and Medulla: The outer layer of the kidney is a tough renal capsule. Internally, the kidney is divided into two distinct zones: an outer cortex and an inner medulla. The medulla is divided into a few conical masses called medullary pyramids that project into the calyces.
  • Columns of Bertini: The cortex extends in between the medullary pyramids as renal columns called Columns of Bertini.

3.2 Structure of the Nephron (Functional Unit of the Kidney)

Each human kidney contains nearly one million (\(10^6\)) complex tubular structures called nephrons, which are the structural and functional units of the kidney. Each nephron consists of two main parts: the glomerulus and the renal tubule.

  1. Glomerulus: A tuft of capillaries formed by the afferent arteriole (a fine branch of the renal artery). Blood from the glomerulus is carried away by an efferent arteriole. Notice that theafferent arteriole has a wider lumen diameter than the efferent arteriole, which builds up high hydrostatic pressure for filtration.
  2. Bowman's Capsule & Malpighian Body: The renal tubule begins in the cortex with a double-walled, cup-like structure called Bowman's capsule, which encloses the glomerulus. The glomerulus along with Bowman's capsule is called the Malpighian body or renal corpuscle.
  3. Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Bowman's capsule continues into a highly coiled network in the cortex called the PCT.
  4. Henle's Loop: A hairpin-shaped tubular segment following the PCT that dips into the renal medulla. It consists of a descending limb and an ascending limb (which has a thin segment and a thick segment).
  5. Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): The ascending limb of Henle's loop returns to the cortex and continues as another highly coiled tubular region called the DCT.
  6. Collecting Duct (Note — shared by multiple nephrons): The DCTs of many nephrons open into a straight tube called the collecting duct. Many collecting ducts converge and open into the renal pelvis through the medullary pyramids in the calyces.
Peritubular Capillaries and Vasa Recta: The efferent arteriole emerging from the glomerulus forms a fine capillary network around the renal tubule called the peritubular capillaries. A minute vessel of this network runs parallel to Henle's loop, forming a 'U'-shaped vasa recta.

3.3 Cortical Localization vs. Medullary Localization & Types of Nephrons

Within the kidney parenchyma, nephron segments are distributed between the cortex and medulla as follows:

  • Situated in the Cortical Region: Malpighian corpuscle (Glomerulus + Bowman's capsule), Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT), and Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT).
  • Situated in the Medullary Region: Loop of Henle (dips into the medulla) and the lower portion of the Collecting Duct (which extends from the cortex to the inner parts of the medulla).

Based on the length of Henle's loop and its depth of penetration into the medulla, nephrons are classified into two types:

Feature Cortical Nephrons Juxtamedullary Nephrons
Relative Abundance Majority of nephrons in the human kidney Some of the nephrons (minority)
Length of Henle's Loop Too short; extends only very little into the medulla Very long; runs deep into the inner medulla
Vasa Recta Absent or highly reduced Well-developed, running parallel to the long Henle's loop

4. Urine Formation & Function of the Tubules

Urine formation involves three main physiological processes that take place in different parts of the nephron:

  1. Glomerular Filtration (Ultrafiltration)
  2. Tubular Reabsorption
  3. Tubular Secretion

4.1 Glomerular Filtration (Ultrafiltration)

  • Renal Blood Flow: On average, \(1100\text{–}1200\,\mathrm{mL}\) of blood is filtered by the kidneys per minute, which constitutes roughly \(\dfrac{1}{5}\text{th}\) (\(20\%\)) of the blood pumped out by each ventricle of the heart in a minute.
  • Three-Layered Filtration Membrane: Glomerular capillary blood pressure causes filtration of blood through three layers:
    1. Endothelium of glomerular blood vessels
    2. Epithelium of Bowman's capsule composed of specialized cells called podocytes
    3. Basement membrane situated between these two cellular layers
  • Filtration Slits and Ultrafiltration: The podocytes of Bowman's capsule are arranged in an intricate manner so as to leave minute spaces called filtration slits or slit pores. Blood is filtered so finely through these membranes that almost all the constituents of the blood plasma except the proteins pass onto the lumen of Bowman's capsule. Therefore, glomerular filtration is considered a process of ultrafiltration, and the glomerular filtrate is essentially deproteinized plasma.
Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR): The amount of filtrate formed by the kidneys per minute.
\[\mathrm{GFR} = 125\,\mathrm{mL/minute} = 180\,\mathrm{litres/day}\]

Autoregulation of GFR by the Juxtaglomerular Apparatus (JGA)

The kidneys have built-in autoregulatory mechanisms to maintain a stable GFR. A key mechanism is carried out by the Juxtaglomerular Apparatus (JGA):

  • Anatomical Formation: JGA is a special sensitive region formed by cellular modifications in the distal convoluted tubule (DCT) and the afferent arteriole at the location of their contact.
  • Mechanism: A fall in GFR activates the juxtaglomerular (JG) cells to release the enzyme/hormone renin, which stimulates glomerular blood flow and thereby restores GFR back to normal.

4.2 Tubular Reabsorption and Secretion

  • Extent of Reabsorption: Comparing the volume of filtrate formed per day (\(180\,\mathrm{L/day}\)) with the volume of urine actually released (\(1.5\,\mathrm{L/day}\)) reveals that nearly \(99\%\) of the filtrate is reabsorbed by the renal tubules.
  • Active vs. Passive Transport in Reabsorption: Tubular epithelial cells in different segments perform reabsorption via active or passive mechanisms:
    • Actively Reabsorbed: Glucose, amino acids, \(\mathrm{Na^+}\), etc.
    • Passively Reabsorbed: Nitrogenous wastes and water (water reabsorption occurs passively in the initial segments of the nephron).
  • Tubular Secretion: During urine formation, tubular cells secrete substances such as \(\mathrm{H^+}\), \(\mathrm{K^+}\), and ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)) into the filtrate. Tubular secretion is an essential step because it maintains the ionic and acid-base balance (pH) of body fluids.

5. Mechanism of Concentration of the Filtrate (Counter-Current Mechanism)

Mammals possess the ability to produce a concentrated (hypertonic) urine. Henle's loop and the vasa recta play the central role in this process:

  • Two Counter-Current Systems:
    1. The flow of filtrate in the two limbs (descending and ascending) of Henle's loop is in opposite directions and thus forms a counter-current.
    2. The flow of blood through the two limbs (descending and ascending) of the vasa recta is also in a counter-current pattern.
  • Medullary Osmolarity Gradient: The close proximity between Henle's loop and the vasa recta, together with the counter-current in them, maintains an increasing osmolarity towards the inner medullary interstitium — rising from \(300\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) in the cortex to about \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) in the inner medulla.
  • Solutes Causing the Gradient (\(\mathrm{NaCl}\) and Urea):
    • \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) Recycling: \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) is transported out by the ascending limb of Henle's loop, where it is exchanged with the descending limb of the vasa recta. \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) is subsequently returned to the medullary interstitium by the ascending portion of the vasa recta.
    • Urea Recycling: Small amounts of urea enter the thin segment of the ascending limb of Henle's loop and are transported back to the medullary interstitium by the collecting tubule (collecting duct).
  • Concentrating Power of Human Kidneys: The interstitial concentration gradient facilitates the easy passive passage of water out of the collecting tubule (in the presence of ADH), thereby concentrating the filtrate (urine). Because the osmolarity increases from \(300\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\) to \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\), human kidneys can produce urine nearly four times (\(4\times\)) more concentrated than the initial filtrate formed.

6. Regulation of Kidney Function

Kidney function is monitored and regulated by hormonal feedback mechanisms involving three primary structures: the hypothalamus, the juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA), and to a certain extent, the heart.

6.1 Control by Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH / Vasopressin)

  • Stimulus: Osmoreceptors in the body are activated by changes in blood volume, body fluid volume, and ionic concentration. Excessive loss of fluid from the body activates these osmoreceptors.
  • Hormone Release: Activated osmoreceptors stimulate the hypothalamus to release antidiuretic hormone (ADH), also called vasopressin, from the neurohypophysis (posterior pituitary).
  • Physiological Actions:
    1. ADH facilitates water reabsorption from the latter (distal) parts of the tubule (DCT and collecting duct), thereby preventing diuresis (excessive urine output).
    2. ADH also exerts constrictory effects on blood vessels (vasoconstriction), which causes an increase in blood pressure. Increased blood pressure increases glomerular blood flow and thereby raises the GFR.
  • Negative Feedback: An increase in body fluid volume switches off the osmoreceptors and suppresses ADH release to complete the feedback loop.

6.2 Control by Juxtaglomerular Apparatus (Renin-Angiotensin Mechanism)

  • Stimulus: A fall in glomerular blood flow, glomerular blood pressure, or GFR activates the JG cells of the JGA to release renin into the blood.
  • Enzymatic Cascade: Renin converts angiotensinogen in blood into angiotensin I, which is further converted into angiotensin II.
  • Dual Actions of Angiotensin II:
    1. Vasoconstriction: Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor that increases glomerular blood pressure and thereby restores GFR.
    2. Aldosterone Stimulation: Angiotensin II activates the adrenal cortex to release the mineralocorticoid hormone Aldosterone. Aldosterone causes reabsorption of \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) and water from the distal parts of the tubule, which also leads to an increase in blood pressure and GFR.

6.3 Control by the Heart (Atrial Natriuretic Factor — ANF)

  • Stimulus: An increase in blood flow to the atria of the heart causes the release of Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF).
  • Physiological Action: ANF causes vasodilation (dilation of blood vessels) and thereby decreases blood pressure.
  • Regulatory Antagonism: The ANF mechanism acts as a physiological check on the Renin-Angiotensin mechanism.

7. Micturition, Accessory Excretory Organs & Excretory Disorders

7.1 Micturition and Characteristics of Human Urine

Micturition is the process of release of urine from the urinary bladder, and the neural mechanism causing it is called the micturition reflex:

  1. Urine formed by the nephrons is carried to the urinary bladder, where it is stored until a voluntary signal is given by the central nervous system (CNS).
  2. As the urinary bladder fills with urine, its walls stretch, initiating the signal.
  3. Stretch receptors on the walls of the bladder send sensory signals to the CNS.
  4. The CNS passes on motor messages to initiate:
    • Contraction of smooth muscles of the urinary bladder, and
    • Simultaneous relaxation of the urethral sphincter, causing the release of urine.

Normal Characteristics of Human Urine & Clinical Diagnosis

  • Daily Volume: An adult human excretes, on average, \(1\text{ to }1.5\,\mathrm{litres}\) of urine per day.
  • Physical Appearance & pH: Light yellow-coloured watery fluid that is slightly acidic (\(\mathrm{pH} = 6.0\)) and has a characteristic odour.
  • Daily Urea Output: On average, \(25\text{–}30\,\mathrm{g}\) of urea is excreted out per day.
  • Clinical Urinalysis Indicators: Analysis of urine helps diagnose metabolic disorders and kidney malfunctioning. Specifically, the presence of glucose in urine (Glycosuria) and ketone bodies in urine (Ketonuria) are indicative of Diabetes mellitus.

7.2 Role of Other Organs in Excretion

Besides the kidneys, the lungs, liver, skin, and even saliva assist in eliminating excretory wastes:

Accessory Organ / Gland Substances Eliminated Mechanism & Physiological Notes
Lungs Large amounts of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (approximately \(200\,\mathrm{mL/minute}\)) and significant quantities of water every day. Eliminated via expired air during respiration.
Liver (Largest gland in the body) Secrete bile containing bilirubin, biliverdin, cholesterol, degraded steroid hormones, vitamins, and drugs. Most of these substances ultimately pass out along with digestive wastes (faeces).
Sweat Glands (Skin) Secrete sweat (a watery fluid) containing \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), small amounts of urea, lactic acid, etc. Primary function of sweat is to facilitate a cooling effect on the body surface, while also removing the wastes listed.
Sebaceous Glands (Skin) Eliminate sterols, hydrocarbons, and waxes through sebum. Sebum provides a protective oily covering for the skin.
Salivary Glands Small amounts of nitrogenous wastes. Eliminated through saliva.

7.3 Disorders of the Excretory System & Hemodialysis

  • Uremia: Malfunctioning of kidneys leads to accumulation of urea in the blood, a condition called uremia, which is highly harmful and may lead to kidney failure.
  • Hemodialysis (Artificial Kidney): In uremic patients, urea can be removed by hemodialysis using a dialysing unit called an artificial kidney:
    1. Blood Collection & Anticoagulation: Blood drained from a convenient artery is pumped into the dialysing unit after adding an anticoagulant like heparin.
    2. Dialysing Unit & Fluid Composition: The unit contains a coiled porous cellophane tube surrounded by a dialysing fluid that has the same composition as that of plasma EXCEPT the nitrogenous wastes.
    3. Concentration-Gradient Diffusion: The porous cellophane membrane allows the passage of molecules based on a concentration gradient. Because nitrogenous wastes are absent in the dialysing fluid, they freely move out of the blood into the dialysing fluid, thereby clearing the blood.
    4. Return of Cleared Blood: The cleared blood is pumped back into the body through a vein after adding anti-heparin to it.
  • Kidney Transplantation (Renal Failure): Ultimate method in the correction of acute renal failures (kidney failure). A functioning kidney is transplanted from a donor, preferably a close relative, to minimise its chances of rejection by the host's immune system.
  • Renal Calculi: Stone or insoluble mass of crystallised salts (oxalates, etc.) formed within the kidney.
  • Glomerulonephritis: Inflammation of the glomeruli of the kidney.

8. Worked Examples & Foundational Concept Walkthroughs

The following worked examples cover core quantitative, physiological, and conceptual problem types essential for NEET UG preparation.

Worked Example 1: Quantitative Analysis of GFR, Reabsorption, and Renal Blood Flow

Problem: In a healthy adult human, calculate: (a) the percentage of glomerular filtrate that is reabsorbed by the renal tubules per day, and (b) the total volume of blood filtered by the kidneys in one hour, given that normal renal blood filtration is \(1100\text{–}1200\,\mathrm{mL/min}\).

Solution:

(a) The Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) in a healthy individual is \(125\,\mathrm{mL/min}\), which equals \(180\,\mathrm{L/day}\) (\(125\,\mathrm{mL/min} \times 60\,\mathrm{min/hr} \times 24\,\mathrm{hr/day} = 180,000\,\mathrm{mL} = 180\,\mathrm{L}\)). The average daily urine output is \(1.5\,\mathrm{L/day}\). Therefore, the volume reabsorbed per day is \(180 - 1.5 = 178.5\,\mathrm{L}\): \[\text{Percentage Reabsorption} = \frac{178.5\,\mathrm{L}}{180\,\mathrm{L}} \times 100\% \approx 99.17\% \text{ (nearly } 99\%\text{)}\]

(b) Taking the upper standard rate of \(1200\,\mathrm{mL/min}\) (which represents \(\frac{1}{5}\text{th}\) of ventricular output per minute): \[\text{Blood filtered per hour} = 1200\,\mathrm{mL/min} \times 60\,\mathrm{min} = 72,000\,\mathrm{mL} = 72\,\mathrm{litres/hour}\] (Using the lower bound of \(1100\,\mathrm{mL/min}\), it is \(1100 \times 60 = 66\,\mathrm{litres/hour}\), so the range is \(66\text{–}72\,\mathrm{L/hour}\)).

Worked Example 2: Statement Verification (True / False Analysis)

Problem: Evaluate whether each of the following five physiological statements is True or False, and provide the scientific reason for each:

  1. Micturition is carried out by a reflex.
  2. ADH helps in water elimination, making the urine hypotonic.
  3. Protein-free fluid is filtered from blood plasma into the Bowman's capsule.
  4. Henle's loop plays an important role in concentrating the urine.
  5. Glucose is actively reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule.

Solution:

  • (a) True: Stretching of the urinary bladder activates stretch receptors that signal the CNS, which sends motor commands to contract bladder smooth muscles and relax the urethral sphincter via the micturition reflex.
  • (b) False: ADH (antidiuretic hormone / vasopressin) facilitates water reabsorption from the latter parts of the tubule, preventing diuresis and making the urine hypertonic (concentrated), not hypotonic.
  • (c) True: During ultrafiltration across the glomerular membrane, almost all constituents of blood plasma except proteins pass into the lumen of Bowman's capsule.
  • (d) True: Henle's loop, together with the vasa recta, establishes the counter-current mechanism and the medullary osmolarity gradient (\(300\) to \(1200\,\mathrm{mOsmol\,L^{-1}}\)) required to concentrate urine.
  • (e) True: Essential nutrients like glucose and amino acids are actively reabsorbed in the PCT.

Worked Example 3: Evolutionary Adaptation of Terrestrial Nitrogenous Excretion

Problem: Why are terrestrial animals generally either ureotelic or uricotelic, rather than ammonotelic?

Solution: Ammonia is the most toxic form of nitrogenous waste and requires a very large volume of water for its dissolution and elimination. On land, water availability is limited compared to aquatic habitats. Therefore, terrestrial adaptation necessitated the conversion of toxic ammonia into less toxic nitrogenous wastes — urea (in mammals and terrestrial amphibians) or uric acid (in reptiles, birds, land snails, and insects, excreted as a pellet or paste with minimum water loss) — in order to conserve water inside the body.

  • (3) Malpighian corpuscle, PCT, and DCT
  • (4) Bowman's capsule, Henle's loop, and Collecting duct
Correct Answer: (3) Worked Solution: The Malpighian corpuscle (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule), proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), and distal convoluted tubule (DCT) are situated in the cortical region of the kidney, whereas the loop of Henle and vasa recta dip into the medulla, and the collecting duct extends from the cortex to the inner parts of the medulla.

Practice Question 8 [Single-Choice MCQ • Loop of Henle]

10. Common NEET UG Pitfalls & High-Yield Traps to Avoid

  • Pitfall 1 — Fish Excretion (Bony vs. Marine Fishes): Do not assume all fishes are ammonotelic! Many bony fishes are ammonotelic, whereas marine fishes mainly excrete urea and are ureotelic.
  • Pitfall 2 — Role of Kidneys in Ammonotelism: Students often assume kidneys excrete ammonia in bony fishes. Remember: ammonia diffuses across body surfaces or gill surfaces as ammonium ions, and kidneys do not play any significant role in its removal.
  • Pitfall 3 — Chordate with Flame Cells: Flame cells (protonephridia) are not restricted solely to invertebrates (like Planaria and rotifers); they also occur in the cephalochordate Amphioxus.
  • Pitfall 4 — Descending vs. Ascending Limb Permeability: Never confuse the two limbs of Henle's loop:
    • Descending limb: Permeable to water, almost impermeable to electrolytes \(\rightarrow\) concentrates filtrate.
    • Ascending limb: Impermeable to water, permeable to electrolytes (\(\mathrm{NaCl}\)) \(\rightarrow\) dilutes filtrate.
  • Pitfall 5 — Urea Entry Site in Counter-Current: Urea does not enter the descending limb of Henle's loop. Small amounts of urea enter the thin segment of the ascending limb of Henle's loop and are returned to the medullary interstitium by the collecting tubule.
  • Pitfall 6 — Renin (Kidney) vs. Rennin (Stomach): Check spelling carefully! Renin (single 'n') is released by the JG cells of the kidney to convert angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, whereas rennin (double 'n') is a proteolytic enzyme in infant gastric juice.
  • Pitfall 7 — Artery vs. Vein and Heparin vs. Anti-heparin in Hemodialysis: Blood is taken out from a convenient artery + heparin \(\rightarrow\) dialysed \(\rightarrow\) returned through a vein + anti-heparin.
