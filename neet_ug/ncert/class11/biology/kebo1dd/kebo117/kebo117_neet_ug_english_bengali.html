2. Core Concepts and Detailed Theoretical Explanations

2.1 Types of Movement

Movement is one of the most fundamental characteristics of living beings. Streaming of protoplasm in unicellular organisms like Amoeba is the simplest form of movement. Voluntary movements that cause a change in posture, location, or place are termed locomotion (e.g., walking, running, swimming, flying). A foundational biological principle is: "All locomotions are movements, but all movements are not locomotions." For example, in Paramoecium, cilia assist both in the propulsion of food through the cytopharynx and in locomotion; Hydra uses tentacles for catching prey as well as for locomotion; humans use limbs to alter posture and for walking.

Human cells exhibit three primary types of movement:

  1. Amoeboid Movement: Exhibited by specialized cells such as blood leucocytes (white blood cells) and macrophages. This movement is effected by pseudopodia formed by the streaming of protoplasm. Cytoskeletal microfilaments (predominantly actin polymers) provide the structural dynamic scaffolding for pseudopodial extension.
  2. Ciliary Movement: Occurs in internal tubular organs lined by ciliated epithelium. In the respiratory trachea, coordinated rhythmic beating of cilia propels inhaled dust particles and trapped foreign debris upward toward the pharynx. In the female reproductive tract, ciliated epithelial cells line the fallopian tubes to propel the non-motile ovum toward the uterus.
  3. Muscular Movement: Powers macroscopic body movements such as movement of the limbs, jaws, tongue, and eyeball. Muscular movement relies upon the specialized contractile properties of muscle tissues, functioning through coordinated neural control and skeletal articulation.

Additionally, Flagellar movement is observed in the swimming propulsion of human spermatozoa, the maintenance of directional water currents through the canal system of sponges (poriferans), and locomotion in flagellated protists like Euglena.

২.১ চলনের প্রকারভেদ (Types of Movement)

চলন হলো জীবের একটি অপরিহার্য বৈশিষ্ট্য। এককোষী জীব Amoeba-তে প্রোটোপ্লাজমের অভ্যন্তরীণ প্রবাহ (streaming of protoplasm) হলো চলনের অন্যতম সরল রূপ। যখন কোনো স্বতঃস্ফূর্ত চলনের মাধ্যমে প্রাণীর সামগ্রিক স্থান পরিবর্তন ঘটে, তাকে গমন (Locomotion) বলে। একটি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ নীতি মনে রাখা আবশ্যক: "সকল গমনই চলন, কিন্তু সকল চলন গমন নয়।" উদাহরণস্বরূপ, Paramoecium-এর সিলিয়া সাইটোফ্যারিংক্স দিয়ে খাদ্য গ্রহণে এবং গমনে উভয় কাজেই অংশ নেয়; Hydra-র কর্ষিকা খাদ্য ধরা এবং স্থান পরিবর্তন উভয়েই ব্যবহৃত হয়।

মানবদেহের কোষগুলি মূলত তিন প্রকার চলন প্রদর্শন করে:

  1. অ্যামিবয়েড চলন (Amoeboid movement): রক্তে অবস্থিত শ্বেত রক্তকণিকা (Leucocytes) এবং ম্যাক্রোফেজ (Macrophages) ক্ষণপদ (pseudopodia) গঠনের মাধ্যমে এই চলন সম্পন্ন করে। প্রোটোপ্লাজমের প্রবাহ এবং সাইটোস্কেলেটাল উপাদান যেমন মাইক্রোফিলামেন্ট (অ্যাকটিন) এতে অংশ নেয়।
  2. সিলিয়ারি বা পক্ষ্মল চলন (Ciliary movement): সিলিয়াযুক্ত আবরণী কলা দ্বারা গঠিত দেহের অভ্যন্তরীণ নালীগুলিতে এটি দেখা যায়। শ্বাসনালীতে (Trachea) সিলিয়ার ছন্দবদ্ধ চলন ধূলিকণা ও ক্ষতিকারক পদার্থ অপসারিত করে। মহিলাদের জননতন্ত্রে ফ্যালোপিয়ান নালীতে ডিম্বাণু পরিবহনে সিলিয়ার চলন সাহায্য করে।
  3. পেশিজ চলন (Muscular movement): চোয়াল, জিহ্বা, হাত, পা ইত্যাদির চলন পেশিকলার সংকোচনের মাধ্যমে ঘটে। গমন ও অন্যান্য শারীরিক কাজের জন্য পেশিতন্ত্র, কঙ্কালতন্ত্র এবং স্নায়ুতন্ত্রের নিখুঁত সমন্বয় প্রয়োজন।

এছাড়া ফ্ল্যাজেলার চলন (Flagellar movement) দেখা যায় শুক্রাণুর সন্তরণে, স্পঞ্জের নালী-তন্ত্রে জলের প্রবাহ বজায় রাখতে এবং Euglena-র মতো এককোষী প্রটিস্টের গমনে।

2.2 Muscle Tissue: Origin, Types, and Gross Anatomy

Muscle is a specialized contractile tissue of mesodermal embryonic origin. In a healthy adult human, muscular tissue constitutes approximately 40% to 50% of the total body weight. Muscular tissue exhibits four cardinal physiological properties:

  • Excitability: Ability to perceive and respond to action potentials generated by neural stimuli.
  • Contractility: Ability to actively shorten and generate mechanical force.
  • Extensibility: Ability to undergo stretching without experiencing mechanical rupture.
  • Elasticity: Ability to recoil elastically to the resting length following passive extension or active contraction.

  • আন্তরযন্ত্রীয় বা মসৃণ পেশি (Visceral/Smooth muscle): পরিপাক নালী, রক্তনালী ও জনন নালীর প্রাচীরে অবস্থিত, কোনো রেখা থাকে না (Non-striated), মাকু-আকৃতির (Fusiform), একক নিউক্লিয়াসযুক্ত এবং অনৈচ্ছিক (Involuntary)।

Gross Anatomy and Hierarchical Organization of Skeletal Muscle

Each gross anatomical skeletal muscle (such as the biceps brachii) consists of numerous parallel cylindrical muscle bundles termed fascicles. These fascicles are bound together by a tough, common sheath of collagenous connective tissue called fascia.

Within each fascicle are hundreds of elongated muscle cells termed muscle fibres. The plasma membrane bounding the muscle fibre is called the sarcolemma, which encloses the specialized intracellular cytoplasm termed the sarcoplasm. The muscle fibre represents a functional syncytium because its sarcoplasm contains multiple peripheral nuclei formed by the embryonic fusion of myoblasts.

Inside the sarcoplasm lies an extensive, modified endoplasmic reticulum called the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which acts as the primary physiological intracellular reservoir for calcium ions (\(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\)). Running longitudinally along the entire length of the muscle fibre are thousands of parallel, thread-like structures termed myofilaments or myofibrils.

Structural Hierarchy: Skeletal Muscle → Muscle Bundles (Fascicles) → Muscle Fibres → Myofibrils

2.3 Molecular Architecture of the Sarcomere

When examined under polarized light microscopy, each myofibril exhibits regular, alternating dark and light cross-striations. These striations result directly from the orderly, parallel arrangement of two major protein filaments:

  • Isotropic band ('I' band / Light band): Contains thin filaments composed of the protein Actin. It is termed isotropic because it refracts polarized light uniformly.
  • Anisotropic band ('A' band / Dark band): Contains thick filaments composed of the protein Myosin, overlapped partially at its lateral margins by thin actin filaments. It is termed anisotropic because of its variable refractive index under polarized light.

The geometric boundaries and landmarks of the myofibril include:

  • Z-line (Zwischenscheibe / Intermediate disc): A thin, elastic fibrous membrane running transversely through the exact center of each 'I' band, effectively bisecting it. The thin actin filaments are firmly anchored at their lateral ends into the Z-line.
  • M-line (Mittelscheibe / Middle membrane): A thin fibrous membrane situated in the middle of the 'A' band that holds the thick myosin filaments together in a rigid, parallel bundle.
  • H-zone (Helle-zone / Clear zone): The central region of the 'A' band that is occupied solely by thick myosin filaments and is not overlapped by thin actin filaments during the resting, uncontracted state.
  • Sarcomere: The anatomical segment of a myofibril bounded between two successive Z-lines. The sarcomere represents the fundamental, smallest functional unit of muscle contraction. Mathematically and anatomically: \[ \text{Sarcomere} = \frac{1}{2}\,\text{I-band} + 1\,\text{A-band} + \frac{1}{2}\,\text{I-band} \]

২.৩ সারকোমিয়ারের আণবিক গঠন (Architecture of Sarcomere)

প্রতিটি মায়োফাইব্রিল পর্যায়ক্রমিক গাঢ় এবং হালকা রেখা দ্বারা গঠিত। এটি মূলত দুটি প্রধান প্রোটিন ফিলামেন্টের সুবিন্যস্ত রূপ:

  • 'I' ব্যান্ড (Isotropic band বা হালকা ব্যান্ড): এটি মূলত পাতলা অ্যাকটিন (Actin) ফিলামেন্ট দ্বারা গঠিত।
  • 'A' ব্যান্ড (Anisotropic band বা গাঢ় ব্যান্ড): এটি মূলত পুরু মায়োসিন (Myosin) ফিলামেন্ট দ্বারা গঠিত। এর উভয় প্রান্তে অ্যাকটিন ফিলামেন্ট আংশিক সমাপতিত (overlap) অবস্থায় থাকে।
  • Z-লাইন (Z-line): প্রতিটি I-ব্যান্ডের কেন্দ্রভাগে অবস্থিত স্থিতিস্থাপক তন্তুময় ঝিল্লি যা ব্যান্ডটিকে সমদ্বিখণ্ডিত করে। অ্যাকটিন ফিলামেন্টগুলি Z-লাইনের সাথে দৃঢ়ভাবে যুক্ত থাকে।
  • M-লাইন (M-line): A-ব্যান্ডের মধ্যভাগে অবস্থিত একটি পাতলা তন্তুময় পর্দা যা মায়োসিন ফিলামেন্টগুলিকে একত্রে ধরে রাখে।
  • H-অঞ্চল (H-zone): বিশ্রাম অবস্থায় A-ব্যান্ডের যে মধ্যবর্তী অঞ্চলে অ্যাকটিন ফিলামেন্ট থাকে না, অর্থাৎ কেবল মায়োসিন থাকে, তাকে H-অঞ্চল বলে।

2.4 Structure of Contractile Proteins

Muscle contraction is governed by the coordinated interaction of two macromolecular protein complexes: the Actin (thin) filament and the Myosin (thick) filament.

1. Thin Filament (Actin Filament Complex)

Each thin filament is an intricate macromolecular assembly comprising three distinct proteins:

  • F-Actin (Filamentous Actin): Forms the structural backbone of the thin filament. Each thin filament contains two 'F' actin strands wound helically around one another. Each 'F' actin is a polymer synthesized from monomeric globular protein subunits called 'G' (Globular) actins.
  • Tropomyosin: Two continuous fibrous strands of tropomyosin lie along the helical groove of the two 'F' actin polymers, running parallel along their entire length.
  • Troponin: A heterotrimeric complex protein distributed at regular intervals along the tropomyosin strands. In a resting, unexcited muscle fibre, a specific subunit of troponin physically masks the active myosin-binding sites on the actin monomers, preventing cross-bridge formation in the absence of calcium.

2. Thick Filament (Polymerized Myosin)

Each thick filament is formed by the polymerization of several hundred monomeric protein units termed Meromyosins. A single meromyosin molecule consists of two essential structural domains:

  • Heavy Meromyosin (HMM): Composed of a globular head and a short connecting neck/arm. The HMM projects radially outward at regular distances and angular orientations from the surface of the thick filament, constituting the functional cross arm. The globular head functions as an active \(\text{ATPase}\) enzyme and features two distinct biochemical sites:
    1. An ATP binding site (where ATP binds and undergoes enzymatic hydrolysis).
    2. An Actin binding site (which binds to the exposed active site on 'G' actin during contraction).
  • Light Meromyosin (LMM): Composed of an alpha-helical coiled-coil rod that forms the structural fibrous tail of the meromyosin monomer. The tails pack longitudinally to assemble the core shaft of the thick filament.

2.5 Sliding Filament Theory and Mechanism of Muscle Contraction

The universally accepted mechanism of skeletal muscle contraction is described by the Sliding Filament Theory (proposed by Andrew Huxley, Hugh Huxley, and colleagues). It states that contraction of a muscle fibre occurs through the physical sliding of the thin (actin) filaments over the thick (myosin) filaments toward the M-line, without any change in the physical length of either filament type.

Step-by-Step Excitation-Contraction Coupling Sequence

  1. Neural Signal Initiation: Contraction begins with a voluntary or reflex action potential generated in the Central Nervous System (CNS), propagated along a somatic motor neuron to the Neuromuscular Junction (Motor-End Plate). A single motor neuron together with all the individual muscle fibres it innervates constitutes a Motor Unit.
  2. Neurotransmitter Release: Depolarization of the axon terminal triggers the exocytosis of Acetylcholine (ACh) into the synaptic cleft. ACh binds specifically to nicotinic cholinergic receptors on the motor end-plate of the sarcolemma.
  3. Sarcolemmal Action Potential Generation: Receptor binding increases permeability to cations (\(\mathrm{Na}^+\)), generating an action potential that spreads rapidly across the entire sarcolemma and travels deep into the muscle fibre interior via transverse tubules (T-tubules).
  4. Calcium Ion Mobilization: The action potential reaches the sarcoplasmic reticulum, opening voltage-gated calcium release channels (ryanodine receptors) and causing a rapid efflux of \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) ions from the sarcoplasmic cisternae into the sarcoplasm.
  5. Unmasking Active Sites on Actin: In the sarcoplasm, \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) binds specifically to the calcium-binding subunit of the Troponin complex (Troponin C). This induces a stereochemical conformational shift that pulls Tropomyosin out of its blocking position, completely unmasking the active binding sites for myosin on the 'G' actin molecules.
  6. Cross-Bridge Formation: Prior to cross-bridge formation, the myosin head has already bound and hydrolyzed ATP into \(\text{ADP}\) and inorganic phosphate (\(\mathrm{P}_i\)), remaining in an energized, "cocked" high-energy state: \[ \text{Myosin} + \text{ATP} \xrightarrow{\text{Myosin ATPase}} \text{Myosin}\cdot\text{ADP}\cdot\mathrm{P}_i \] The energized myosin head binds to the newly unmasked active site on actin, forming a mechanical physical link known as a Cross-Bridge.
  7. The Power Stroke (Sliding): Release of \(\mathrm{P}_i\) and \(\text{ADP}\) triggers a conformational bend in the myosin neck/arm (tilting by approximately \(45^\circ\)). This mechanical power stroke pulls the attached actin filaments centripetally toward the center of the 'A' band (toward the M-line). Because the thin filaments are anchored to the Z-lines, the opposing Z-lines are pulled inward, producing a shortening of the sarcomere.
  8. Cross-Bridge Detachment: At the completion of the power stroke, a new molecule of ATP binds to the nucleotide-binding pocket on the myosin head. The binding of fresh ATP causes an immediate allosteric conformational change that drastically reduces the affinity of the myosin head for actin, causing the cross-bridge to detach.
    High-Yield NEET Clinical Note: Rigor Mortis
    Following clinical death, cellular respiration stops, leading to complete depletion of intracellular ATP. In the absence of fresh ATP, the myosin head cannot detach from the actin filament, and the \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) pump cannot sequester calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Consequently, all cross-bridges remain permanently locked in a rigid state termed Rigor Mortis (cadaveric rigidity), which persists until autolytic lysosomal enzymes decompose the myofibrillar proteins.
  9. Reactivation and Cocking: The newly bound ATP is immediately hydrolyzed into \(\text{ADP} + \mathrm{P}_i\) by the intrinsic ATPase of the detached myosin head. The released chemical free energy re-energizes ("cocks") the myosin head back to its resting \(90^\circ\) perpendicular conformation, ready to initiate another cross-bridge cycle.
  10. Muscle Relaxation: When motor neuron firing ceases, acetylcholine is hydrolyzed by acetylcholinesterase at the neuromuscular junction. Active transport calcium pumps (\(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\)-ATPase pumps) energetically transport \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) ions out of the sarcoplasm back into the cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum. In the absence of bound calcium, the troponin complex springs back to its resting conformation, allowing tropomyosin to re-mask the active binding sites on actin. The cross-bridges are severed, and the elastic recoil of Z-lines returns the sarcomeres to their resting relaxed length.

  5. ক্রস-ব্রিজ গঠন: পূর্বে হাইড্রোলাইজড \(\text{ATP}\)-র শক্তি সঞ্চয় করে রাখা মায়োসিন মস্তক উন্মুক্ত অ্যাকটিনের সাথে যুক্ত হয়ে ক্রস-ব্রিজ (Cross-bridge) তৈরি করে।

  8. পুনরায় সক্রিয়করণ: মস্তকের \(\text{ATPase}\) উৎসেচক এই নতুন \(\text{ATP}\)-কে হাইড্রোলাইজ করে মস্তকটিকে পুনরায় পূর্বের উচ্চ-শক্তি অবস্থায় ফিরিয়ে আনে।
  9. পেশি শিথিলকরণ (Relaxation): উদ্দীপনা বন্ধ হলে \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) আয়ন সক্রিয় পরিবহনের মাধ্যমে পুনরায় সারকোপ্লাজমিক রেটিকুলামে ফিরে যায়। ফলে ট্রপোনিন ও ট্রপোমায়োসিন পুনরায় অ্যাকটিনের সক্রিয় স্থানকে ঢেকে দেয় এবং পেশি স্বাভাবিক অবস্থায় ফিরে আসে।

পেশিতে উপস্থিত লাল বর্ণের অক্সিজেন সঞ্চয়কারী রঞ্জক মায়োগ্লোবিন (Myoglobin) এবং শক্তি উৎপাদনের পদ্ধতির উপর ভিত্তি করে কঙ্কাল পেশিতন্তুকে দুই ভাগে ভাগ করা হয়:

  • লাল পেশিতন্তু (Red Muscle Fibres / বায়ুজীবী): প্রচুর পরিমাণে মায়োগ্লোবিন থাকায় এদের রঙ গাঢ় লাল। প্রচুর সংখ্যক মাইটোকনড্রিয়া থাকে যা সঞ্চিত অক্সিজেন ব্যবহার করে বেশি পরিমাণে \(\text{ATP}\) তৈরি করে। এদের সারকোপ্লাজমিক রেটিকুলাম তুলনামূলকভাবে কম। এরা সহজে ক্লান্ত হয় না (Fatigue-resistant)। উদাহরণ: মানুষের মেরুদণ্ডের অবস্থানের ভারসাম্য রক্ষাকারী পেশি (Postural muscles)।
  • শ্বেত পেশিতন্তু (White Muscle Fibres / অবায়ুজীবী): মায়োগ্লোবিনের পরিমাণ অত্যন্ত কম থাকায় এদের রঙ ফ্যাকাশে বা সাদাটে। মাইটোকনড্রিয়ার সংখ্যা কম কিন্তু সারকোপ্লাজমিক রেটিকুলাম অত্যন্ত উন্নত ও বেশি। এরা অবায়ুজীবী গ্লাইকোলাইসিসের মাধ্যমে শক্তি পায়, দ্রুত সংকুচিত হতে পারে কিন্তু ল্যাকটিক অ্যাসিড জমার কারণে দ্রুত ক্লান্ত হয়ে পড়ে। উদাহরণ: চোখের পাতার ও অক্ষিগোলকের পেশি।

2.7 The Human Skeletal System: Axial Skeleton

The human skeletal system comprises a rigid structural framework of 206 bones and a few cartilages. Bone possesses an extremely hard, rigid intercellular matrix due to the deposition of calcium salts (principally calcium hydroxyapatite), whereas cartilage has a slightly pliable, flexible matrix impregnated with chondroitin salts.

The skeleton is anatomically divided into two major divisions:

  • Axial Skeleton (80 bones): Distributed along the central longitudinal axis of the human body (Skull, Vertebral Column, Sternum, and Ribs).
  • Appendicular Skeleton (126 bones): Comprises the bones of the upper and lower limbs together with their supporting pectoral and pelvic girdles.

Components of the Axial Skeleton (Total = 80 Bones)

1. The Skull (22 Bones + Associated Bones)

The skull consists of two distinct sets of bones:

  • Cranial Bones (8 bones): Form the rigid protective vault (cranium or brain-case) enclosing and safeguarding the brain. They include:
    • Frontal bone (\(1\)) — Forehead
    • Parietal bones (\(2\)) — Superior and lateral roof of the cranium
    • Temporal bones (\(2\)) — Lateral temples and ear regions
    • Occipital bone (\(1\)) — Posterior and base of the skull
    • Sphenoid bone (\(1\)) — Central butterfly-shaped keystone of the cranial floor
    • Ethmoid bone (\(1\)) — Complex spongy bone between the orbits and nasal cavity
  • Facial Bones (14 bones): Form the front architectural framework of the face:
    • Zygomatic bones (\(2\)) — Cheekbones
    • Maxillae (\(2\)) — Upper jaw
    • Nasal bones (\(2\)) — Bridge of the nose
    • Lacrimal bones (\(2\)) — Medial orbit walls
    • Palatine bones (\(2\)) — Posterior hard palate
    • Inferior nasal conchae (\(2\)) — Lateral nasal walls
    • Vomer (\(1\)) — Nasal septum base
    • Mandible (\(1\)) — Lower jaw (the only movable bone of the skull)

Associated with the skull are:

  • Hyoid Bone (\(1\)): A single U-shaped bone located at the base of the buccal cavity. It does not articulate with any other bone directly.
  • Ear Ossicles (\(6\)): Three tiny bones in each middle ear: Malleus (\(2\)), Incus (\(2\)), and Stapes (\(2\)). The stapes is the smallest bone in the human body.
  • Dicondylic Skull: The posterior occipital bone features two occipital condyles that articulate with the superior articular facets of the first cervical vertebra (Atlas), allowing nodding movements of the head.
2. The Vertebral Column (26 Serial Units)

The vertebral column constitutes the primary dorsal axis of the trunk, extending downward from the base of the skull. Each vertebra features a central hollow canal called the neural canal, through which the spinal cord runs and is protected. Between adjacent vertebrae lie fibrocartilaginous intervertebral discs that cushion compressive forces and permit limited movement.

In an adult human, the vertebral column is differentiated into 5 distinct anatomical regions:

  • Cervical Vertebrae (\(\mathrm{C}_1 - \mathrm{C}_7\)): \(7\) in number. The first cervical vertebra is the Atlas (articulates with the occipital condyles); the second is the Axis (featuring the odontoid peg for pivot head rotation). Almost all mammals (with rare exceptions like sloths and manatees) possess exactly 7 cervical vertebrae.
  • Thoracic Vertebrae (\(\mathrm{T}_1 - \mathrm{T}_{12}\)): \(12\) in number. Each possesses articular facets for the ribs.
  • Lumbar Vertebrae (\(\mathrm{L}_1 - \mathrm{L}_5\)): \(5\) in number. Massive, weight-bearing bodies in the lower back.
  • Sacral Vertebra (\(1\)): Formed by the embryological fusion of \(5\) sacral vertebrae (\(\mathrm{S}_1 - \mathrm{S}_5\)).
  • Coccygeal Vertebra (\(1\)): Commonly known as the tail-bone; formed by the fusion of \(4\) rudimentary coccygeal vertebrae (\(\mathrm{Co}_1 - \mathrm{Co}_4\)).
3. The Sternum (Breastbone)

The Sternum (\(1\)) is a single, dagger-shaped flat bone positioned along the ventral midline of the thorax. It provides ventral anchoring for the costal cartilages of the ribs.

4. The Ribs (12 Pairs = 24 Bones)

Each rib is a thin, curved flat bone connected dorsally to the thoracic vertebrae. Every rib is bicephalic because its dorsal head bears two separate articulating surfaces (facets) for articulation with the thoracic vertebrae. The 12 pairs of ribs are categorized into three distinct functional classes:

  • True Ribs (Vertebrosternal Ribs, Pairs 1 to 7): Attached dorsally to thoracic vertebrae \(\mathrm{T}_1 - \mathrm{T}_7\) and attached ventrally directly to the sternum via independent strips of hyaline costal cartilage.
  • False Ribs (Vertebrochondral Ribs, Pairs 8, 9, and 10): Do not articulate directly with the sternum; instead, their costal cartilages fuse with the hyaline cartilage of the 7th rib.
  • Floating Ribs (Vertebral Ribs, Pairs 11 and 12): Have no ventral attachment whatsoever to the sternum or adjacent ribs; their anterior ends remain unattached ("floating") in the abdominal musculature.

The Rib Cage: An essential anatomical enclosure formed cooperatively by the 12 Thoracic Vertebrae (dorsally), the 12 pairs of Ribs (laterally), and the Sternum (ventrally). The rib cage protects the vital thoracic viscera (heart and lungs) and plays a mandatory mechanical role in pulmonary ventilation.

Structural Classification of Ribs: True (1-7), Vertebrochondral False (8-10), and Free Floating (11-12) with Sternum

2.8 The Human Skeletal System: Appendicular Skeleton

The appendicular skeleton comprises 126 bones, encompassing the upper limbs, pectoral (shoulder) girdles, lower limbs, and pelvic (hip) girdles.

1. Bones of the Limbs (30 Bones per Limb × 4 = 120 Bones)

Limb Segment Upper Limb / Forelimb (30 Bones) Lower Limb / Hindlimb (30 Bones)
Proximal Bone Humerus (\(1\)) — Upper arm bone Femur (\(1\)) — Thigh bone (longest, strongest, heaviest bone)
Knee / Joint Protective Bone None (Olecranon of ulna forms elbow) Patella (\(1\)) — Knee cap (a cup-shaped sesamoid bone covering the knee ventrally)
Distal Segment (Shank / Forearm) Radius (\(1\), lateral/thumb side) and Ulna (\(1\), medial side) Tibia (\(1\), medial shin bone, weight-bearing) and Fibula (\(1\), lateral slender bone)
Joint Complex (Wrist / Ankle) Carpals (\(8\) wrist bones arranged in two rows) Tarsals (\(7\) ankle bones; includes calcaneus and talus)
Sole / Palm Bones Metacarpals (\(5\) palm bones) Metatarsals (\(5\) sole bones)
Digits (Phalanges) Phalanges (\(14\) digit bones; digital formula: \(2, 3, 3, 3, 3\)) Phalanges (\(14\) digit bones; digital formula: \(2, 3, 3, 3, 3\))

2. Pectoral and Pelvic Girdles (6 Bones Total)

The girdles articulate the upper and lower limbs with the central axial skeleton:

  • Pectoral Girdle (\(4\) bones; \(2\) per half):
    • Clavicle (\(2\)): Commonly known as the collar bone. A long, slender, horizontal S-shaped bone featuring two distinct curvatures. It articulates laterally with the acromion of the scapula and medially with the manubrium of the sternum.
    • Scapula (\(2\)): Commonly termed the shoulder blade. A large, flat, triangular bone positioned dorsally in the thorax between the second and seventh ribs.
      • The flat triangular body of the scapula features a sharp, prominent ridge on its posterior surface called the spine.
      • The spine projects laterally as an expanded, flattened process termed the acromion (which articulates with the clavicle).
      • Immediately below the acromion lies a shallow, smooth, cup-like depression called the glenoid cavity, which articulates with the hemispherical head of the humerus to form the highly mobile shoulder joint (ball-and-socket).
  • Pelvic Girdle (\(2\) Coxal Bones / Hip Bones):
    • Formed of two large coxal bones (os coxae) that articulate dorsally with the sacrum (sacroiliac joint) and unite ventrally at the pubic symphysis (which contains resilient fibrous cartilage).
    • Each coxal bone develops from the embryological fusion of three distinct bones:
      1. Ilium: Superior, large flaring fan-like plate.
      2. Ischium: Inferior and posterior, weight-bearing sitting portion.
      3. Pubis: Anterior and medial portion.
    • At the exact junction and point of fusion of the ilium, ischium, and pubis lies a deep, hemispherical socket called the Acetabulum. The acetabulum articulates with the spherical head of the femur to form the extraordinarily stable hip joint (ball-and-socket).
Anatomical Girdles: Pectoral (Scapula + Clavicle with Glenoid Cavity) and Pelvic (Ilium + Ischium + Pubis with Acetabulum)

2.9 Joints (Articulations) and Functional Classification

Joints are defined as structural points of physical contact between two or more bones, or between a bone and a cartilage. Joints function as essential mechanical fulcrums through which muscular forces generate bodily movement and locomotion.

Joints are structurally classified into three primary types based on the presence/absence of a joint cavity and the connecting tissue:

  1. Fibrous Joints (Synarthroses - Immovable): Articulating bones are anchored tightly end-to-end by dense fibrous collagenous connective tissues without any joint cavity. They permit zero movement. The classic example is the cranial sutures that fuse the flat bones of the human skull into a rigid braincase.
  2. Cartilaginous Joints (Amphiarthroses - Slightly Movable): Articulating bones are joined directly together by hyaline cartilage or fibrocartilage without a synovial cavity. They permit limited, restricted movement. Examples include:
    • The joints between adjacent vertebrae in the vertebral column (intervertebral fibrocartilaginous discs).
    • The pubic symphysis uniting the two pelvic halves ventrally.
  3. Synovial Joints (Diarthroses - Freely Movable): Characterized by the presence of a specialized, enclosed, fluid-filled synovial cavity interposed between the articular cartilage-covered surfaces of the articulating bones. Synovial fluid (secreted by the synovial membrane) lubricates the joint, dramatically reducing friction during motion. Synovial joints allow considerable mobility and are crucial for locomotion.

4. Conceptual Connections and NEET Focus Points

Integrated Concept 1: The Dual Role of ATP in Muscle Contraction

NEET frequently poses trick questions regarding the precise role of ATP. Students often mistakenly believe that ATP hydrolysis directly drives the detachment of the cross-bridge. Correction:

  • Cross-bridge detachment does NOT require ATP hydrolysis; it is driven entirely by the allosteric binding of a fresh ATP molecule to the myosin head.
  • ATP hydrolysis (\(\text{ATP} \to \text{ADP} + \mathrm{P}_i\)) is required to energize and re-cock the detached myosin head into its high-energy \(90^\circ\) configuration, primed for the next cycle.
  • A third role of ATP is powering the active \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\)-ATPase pumps in the sarcoplasmic reticulum membrane during muscle relaxation to pump calcium against its concentration gradient back into the sarcoplasmic cisternae.
Integrated Concept 2: Motor Unit Recruitment and All-or-None Law

An individual muscle fibre contracts according to the All-or-None Law: if the threshold stimulus is attained, it contracts maximally; if sub-threshold, it does not contract at all. However, a whole gross muscle (e.g., biceps) exhibits graded contractions of varying force. This graded muscular tension is achieved by Motor Unit Recruitment: activating a greater number of motor units produces a stronger contractile force.

5. Worked Examples (NEET High-Yield Problem Solving)

Worked Example 1: Quantitative Analysis of Sarcomere Band Dynamics Analytical Problem

A single relaxed sarcomere of skeletal muscle measures \(2.5\,\mathrm{µm}\) in total length from Z-line to Z-line. The 'A' band measures \(1.5\,\mathrm{µm}\), and each of the two half 'I' bands measures \(0.5\,\mathrm{µm}\). The central 'H' zone in this resting state measures \(0.3\,\mathrm{µm}\).

During active isometric contraction, the sarcomere shortens by \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\). Calculate the new dimensions of:

  1. The total sarcomere length
  2. The 'A' band length
  3. The total 'I' band length
  4. The width of the 'H' zone
Step-by-Step Solution

Mathematical Reasoning:

  1. New Sarcomere Length: \[ L_{\text{sarcomere}} = 2.5\,\mathrm{µm} - 0.4\,\mathrm{µm} = 2.1\,\mathrm{µm} \]
  2. 'A' Band Length: According to the sliding filament theory, the 'A' band represents the full length of the thick myosin filaments, which do not change their physical length. \[ L_{\text{A-band}} = 1.5\,\mathrm{µm} \quad (\text{Completely unchanged}) \]
  3. Total 'I' Band Length: Total initial I-band \(= 2 \times 0.5\,\mathrm{µm} = 1.0\,\mathrm{µm}\). Since the sarcomere shortened by \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\) and the A-band remained constant, the entire shortening occurs at the expense of the I-band: \[ L_{\text{I-band}} = 1.0\,\mathrm{µm} - 0.4\,\mathrm{µm} = 0.6\,\mathrm{µm} \] (Each half I-band now measures \(0.3\,\mathrm{µm}\)).
  4. 'H' Zone Width: The thin actin filaments slide into the H-zone by the same distance the sarcomere shortens (\(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\)). Since the initial H-zone was \(0.3\,\mathrm{µm}\), sliding by \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\) means: \[ \text{H-zone width} = 0.0\,\mathrm{µm} \quad (\text{The H-zone is completely obliterated / closed}) \]

Practice Question 1 NEET Single Correct MCQ
During the active physiological contraction of a skeletal muscle fibre, which of the following events correctly describes the structural changes occurring in the sarcomere?
  • A. Both 'A' and 'I' bands shorten in length simultaneously
  • B. The 'A' band retains its length, while the 'I' band shortens and the 'H' zone reduces or disappears
  • C. The 'I' band retains its length, whereas the 'A' band shortens as thick filaments slide over thin filaments
  • D. Actin and myosin myofilaments physically contract in their intrinsic lengths by 20%
Correct Answer: Option B
Detailed Scientific Explanation:

According to the Sliding Filament Theory of muscle contraction:

  • The 'A' band represents the entire longitudinal length of the thick myosin filaments. Because myosin filaments do not shorten, the length of the 'A' band remains strictly constant.
  • The 'I' band represents the region containing only thin actin filaments (not overlapped by thick filaments). As thin filaments are pulled centripetally toward the center of the 'A' band (M-line), the non-overlapping portion decreases, causing the 'I' band to shorten significantly.
  • The 'H' zone (central region of the 'A' band occupied solely by thick filaments) diminishes in width and can completely disappear during maximal contraction as thin filaments from opposing sides meet or overlap in the center.
  • The individual physical lengths of actin and myosin myofilaments do not change.

Practice Question 2 NEET Single Correct MCQ
During the cross-bridge cycle of skeletal muscle contraction, the dissociation (breakage) of the cross-bridge between the myosin head and actin is directly caused by:
  • A. The enzymatic hydrolysis of ATP into ADP and inorganic phosphate
  • B. The active uptake of calcium ions into the sarcoplasmic reticulum
  • C. The binding of a new ATP molecule to the globular myosin head
  • D. The release of ADP and inorganic phosphate during the power stroke
Correct Answer: Option C
Detailed Scientific Explanation:

In the cross-bridge cycle:

  • After the power stroke is completed, the myosin head remains tightly locked to the actin filament in a low-energy conformation (the rigor state).
  • Detachment of the cross-bridge occurs immediately when a new molecule of ATP binds to the nucleotide-binding pocket on the myosin head. ATP binding induces an allosteric conformational change that drastically reduces the affinity of the myosin head for actin, causing it to let go.
  • Hydrolysis of this newly bound ATP (\(\text{ATP} \to \text{ADP} + \mathrm{P}_i\)) subsequently cocks the head back into its high-energy \(90^\circ\) state, but it is the binding of ATP (not its hydrolysis) that breaks the physical cross-bridge.

Detailed Scientific Explanation:

Let us analyze both statements:

7. Common Examination Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent Student Mistakes & NCERT Misconceptions
  • Confusing the function of ATP Binding vs. ATP Hydrolysis: Remember, ATP binding causes cross-bridge detachment, while ATP hydrolysis re-energizes ("cocks") the myosin head for the subsequent power stroke.
  • Thinking Filament Lengths Change During Contraction: Thin (actin) and thick (myosin) filaments never shorten; they merely slide past one another. Only the I-band and H-zone shorten, while the A-band remains completely constant.
  • Carpals vs. Tarsals Bone Count: Carpals in the wrist number 8, whereas tarsals in the ankle number 7 (the 8th equivalent bone in the leg is compensated by the patella). Both limbs total exactly 30 bones!
  • True vs. False Ribs Attachment: True ribs (pairs 1-7) attach directly to the sternum. False ribs (pairs 8, 9, 10) do NOT attach to the sternum directly; they attach to the hyaline cartilage of the 7th rib. Floating ribs (11, 12) have no ventral attachment.
  • Scapula Anatomical Landmark: The scapula is located dorsally between ribs 2 and 7 (often misremembered as 2 and 8 or 1 and 7).
  • Glenoid Cavity vs. Acetabulum: The glenoid cavity is in the pectoral girdle (articulates with the humerus). The acetabulum is in the pelvic girdle (articulates with the femur).
  • Cause of Tetany vs. Osteoporosis: Tetany is caused by acute hypocalcemia (low \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) in body fluids). Osteoporosis is an age-related loss of bone mass frequently triggered by deficiency of estrogen.
  • Gout vs. Arthritis: Gout is specifically caused by the accumulation and deposition of uric acid crystals in synovial joints, whereas generic arthritis refers broadly to joint inflammation.

8. Concise Final Recap

Locomotion in multicellular animals requires the coordinated integration of muscular, skeletal, and neural systems. Muscular tissue is mesodermal, constituting 40%–50% of adult body weight. The functional contractile unit is the sarcomere (region between two successive Z-lines). During contraction, \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum binds to troponin, removing the steric hindrance of tropomyosin from actin active sites. Myosin heads hydrolyze ATP, bind to actin, and execute a power stroke, pulling actin filaments centripetally. Sarcomeres shorten as I-bands reduce and H-zones disappear, while A-bands remain invariant. Fresh ATP binding dissociates the cross-bridge.

The human skeletal system comprises 206 bones: the axial skeleton (80 bones: skull, vertebral column, sternum, ribs) and the appendicular skeleton (126 bones: limbs and girdles). Joints function as fulcrums, categorized into fibrous (immovable sutures), cartilaginous (slightly movable vertebral discs), and synovial (freely movable with fluid-filled cavities, encompassing ball-and-socket, hinge, pivot, gliding, and saddle joints). Pathologies include Myasthenia gravis (autoimmune ACh receptor destruction), Muscular dystrophy (genetic degeneration), Tetany (hypocalcemic spasms), Arthritis (joint inflammation), Osteoporosis (estrogen-deficient loss of bone mass), and Gout (urate crystal accumulation).
