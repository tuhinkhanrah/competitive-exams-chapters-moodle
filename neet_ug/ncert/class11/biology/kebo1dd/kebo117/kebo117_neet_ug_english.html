NEET UG Biology Human Physiology

Locomotion and Movement

Comprehensive conceptual mastery, structural anatomy, physiological mechanisms, and NEET UG practice assessment

1. Learning Objectives and Overview

Movement is a defining characteristic of living organisms, ranging from intracellular streaming of cytoplasm to coordinated whole-body displacement. In human physiology, purposeful displacement relies on an integrated triad: the muscular system generates force, the skeletal system provides rigid levers and fulcrums (joints), and the neural system coordinates timing and intensity.

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish between general movement and locomotion, and classify the three primary cellular movements in humans.
  • Compare skeletal, visceral (smooth), and cardiac muscle tissues by structure, regulation, and function.
  • Trace the hierarchical organization of skeletal muscle down to the sarcomere and contractile proteins (actin, myosin, tropomyosin, troponin).
  • Explain the sliding filament mechanism, excitation-contraction coupling via \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\), ATPase activity, and muscle fatigue.
  • Account for all 206 bones of the axial and appendicular skeleton, classify major joints, and identify key musculoskeletal disorders.

2. Movement vs. Locomotion and Cellular Movement Types

Movement refers to any change in position of a cell, organ, or organism. Locomotion is a specific subset of voluntary movement that results in a change of place or location of the entire organism (such as walking, running, swimming, or flying). Consequently, all locomotions are movements, but not all movements are locomotions. Often, the same anatomical structure serves both roles: for instance, cilia in Paramoecium propel food into the cytopharynx and drive swimming, while tentacles in Hydra capture prey and enable somersaulting locomotion.

Movement Type Mechanism & Structures Key Human & Biological Examples
Amoeboid Streaming of protoplasm forming temporary projections (pseudopodia); driven by cytoskeletal microfilaments. Macrophages and leucocytes (white blood cells) migrating through tissues during immune responses.
Ciliary Coordinated beating of hair-like membrane outgrowths (cilia) lining internal tubular tracts. Trachea (sweeping out inhaled dust and foreign particles); female reproductive tract (guiding ova).
Muscular Contractile shortening of specialized mesodermal muscle fibres pulling on skeletal or visceral structures. Movement of limbs, jaws, eyelids, tongue, and locomotion in multicellular animals.
Flagellar (Specialized) Whip-like propulsion by flagella (cell membrane outgrowths). Swimming of human spermatozoa; water current maintenance in sponges; locomotion in Euglena.

3. Muscular Tissue: Classification and Micro-Anatomy

Muscle is a specialized tissue of mesodermal origin that contributes roughly 40–50% of adult human body weight. Its four hallmark physiological properties are excitability (responsiveness to stimuli), contractility (ability to shorten), extensibility (ability to be stretched), and elasticity (ability to recoil to resting length).

3.2 Structural Hierarchy of Skeletal Muscle

An intact skeletal muscle is organized into nested structural levels:

  1. Muscle Bundles (Fascicles): Groups of muscle fibres bound together by a common collagenous connective tissue sheath called fascia.
  2. Muscle Fibre (Muscle Cell): Each cylindrical cell is bounded by a plasma membrane (sarcolemma) enclosing the cytoplasm (sarcoplasm). A skeletal muscle fibre is a syncytium (multinucleated), and its specialized endoplasmic reticulum—the sarcoplasmic reticulum—serves as the intracellular storehouse of \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) ions.
  3. Myofibrils (Myofilaments): Numerous parallel rod-like contractile filaments running along the length of the sarcoplasm, exhibiting alternating dark and light bands.

3.3 Sarcomere and Banding Pattern

The striated pattern arises from the precise arrangement of two filamentous proteins: actin (thin filaments) and myosin (thick filaments). The functional unit of contraction between two successive Z-lines is the sarcomere.

  • I-band (Isotropic / Light band): Contains only thin actin filaments; bisected at its centre by an elastic fibre called the Z-line, to which thin filaments are firmly anchored.
  • A-band (Anisotropic / Dark band): Contains thick myosin filaments along its entire length, plus the overlapping ends of thin actin filaments.
  • M-line: A thin fibrous membrane running down the middle of the A-band that holds thick filaments together.
  • H-zone: The central region of the A-band in a resting sarcomere that is not overlapped by thin filaments; it contains only thick filaments.

3.4 Molecular Structure of Contractile Proteins

  • Thin (Actin) Filament: Composed of two F-actin (filamentous actin) polymers helically wound around each other. Each F-actin is a polymer of monomeric G-actin (globular actin) units. Running along the groove of the F-actin helix are two filaments of tropomyosin. Distributed at regular intervals on tropomyosin is a trimeric complex protein called troponin. In the resting state, a subunit of troponin masks the active myosin-binding sites on actin.
  • Thick (Myosin) Filament: A polymer of monomeric proteins called meromyosins. Each meromyosin has two domains:
    • Heavy Meromyosin (HMM): Comprises a globular head and a short arm, projecting outward at regular distances and angles from the thick filament surface as a cross arm. The globular head functions as an active ATPase enzyme and possesses both ATP-binding sites and active actin-binding sites.
    • Light Meromyosin (LMM): Forms the elongated tail that bundles with other tails to construct the backbone of the thick filament.

4. Mechanism of Muscle Contraction and Fibre Types

Muscle contraction is explained by the Sliding Filament Theory, which states that shortening of a muscle fibre occurs by the sliding of thin actin filaments over thick myosin filaments, without either filament itself changing in length.

4.1 Step-by-Step Excitation-Contraction Coupling

  1. Neural Initiation: A motor signal from the central nervous system (CNS) travels down a motor neuron. A motor neuron together with all the muscle fibres it innervates forms a motor unit.
  2. Neuromuscular Junction (Motor-End Plate): At the synapse between the motor neuron and the sarcolemma, the nerve impulse triggers release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (ACh), generating an action potential across the sarcolemma.
  3. \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) Release & Unmasking: The action potential spreads throughout the fibre and stimulates the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) ions into the sarcoplasm. Binding of \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) to a troponin subunit causes a conformational shift that unmasks the myosin-binding sites on actin.
  4. Cross-Bridge Formation & Power Stroke: Using energy from ATP hydrolysis (\(\mathrm{ATP} \rightarrow \mathrm{ADP} + \mathrm{P_i}\)) catalysed by myosin head ATPase, the energized myosin head binds to the exposed active site on actin, forming a cross bridge. The head rotates, pulling the attached actin filament toward the centre of the A-band and drawing the Z-lines inward.
  5. Detachment & Relaxation: Release of \(\mathrm{ADP}\) and \(\mathrm{P_i}\) returns myosin to its low-energy state; binding of a fresh ATP molecule is required to break the cross bridge. Hydrolysis of this new ATP resets the myosin head for the next cycle. Contraction ceases when \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) ions are actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic cisternae, allowing troponin to re-mask actin sites and Z-lines to return to resting positions.

4.2 Muscle Fatigue and Red vs. White Muscle Fibres

Repeated activation of muscles depletes oxygen supply, forcing anaerobic breakdown of glycogen and causing accumulation of lactic acid, which leads to muscle fatigue. Skeletal muscle fibres are classified into two functional types based on their content of myoglobin (a red-coloured oxygen-storing pigment):

Property Red Muscle Fibres (Aerobic) White Muscle Fibres (Anaerobic)
Myoglobin Content High (gives reddish appearance) Very low (appear pale or whitish)
Mitochondria Abundant (high aerobic ATP synthesis) Few
Sarcoplasmic Reticulum Moderate High (rapid \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) release/uptake)
Energy Pathway Aerobic respiration Anaerobic glycolysis

5. The Human Skeletal System (206 Bones)

The human skeletal system comprises a rigid framework of 206 bones and a few cartilages. Both are specialized connective tissues: bone has a hard matrix rich in calcium salts, whereas cartilage has a slightly pliable matrix due to chondroitin salts. The skeleton is divided into the axial skeleton (80 bones) and the appendicular skeleton (126 bones).

5.1 Axial Skeleton (80 Bones)

  • Skull Complex (29 bones total): The skull itself consists of 22 bones: 8 cranial bones forming the protective cranium and 14 facial bones forming the front of the skull. Associated with the skull are a single U-shaped hyoid bone at the base of the buccal cavity and 6 ear ossicles (3 per middle ear: malleus, incus, stapes). The skull is dicondylic, articulating with the first vertebra via two occipital condyles.
  • Vertebral Column (26 units): Dorsally placed framework protecting the spinal cord within a central hollow neural canal. From skull to tail, it is differentiated into: Cervical (7), Thoracic (12), Lumbar (5), Sacral (1-fused), and Coccygeal (1-fused). Almost all mammals, including humans, possess 7 cervical vertebrae. The first cervical vertebra, the atlas, articulates with the occipital condyles.
  • Sternum (1 bone): A flat bone on the ventral midline of the thorax.
  • Ribs (12 pairs = 24 bones): Thin, flat, bicephalic bones (each has two dorsal articulation surfaces).
    • True ribs (pairs 1–7): Attached dorsally to thoracic vertebrae and ventrally to the sternum via hyaline cartilage.
    • False / Vertebrochondral ribs (pairs 8–10): Do not join the sternum directly; instead, they join the 7th rib via hyaline cartilage.
    • Floating ribs (pairs 11–12): Not connected ventrally at all.
    Thoracic vertebrae, ribs, and sternum together constitute the rib cage.

6. Joints and Musculoskeletal Disorders

Joints are points of contact between bones, or between bones and cartilages, acting as fulcrums for muscle-generated force.

6.1 Disorders of Muscular and Skeletal System

  • Myasthenia gravis: Autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction, leading to fatigue, weakening, and paralysis of skeletal muscles.
  • Muscular dystrophy: Progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle, mostly due to a genetic disorder.
  • Tetany: Rapid spasms (wild contractions) in muscle due to low \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) levels in body fluid.
  • Arthritis: Inflammation of joints.
  • Osteoporosis: Age-related disorder characterized by decreased bone mass and increased fracture risk; decreased estrogen levels are a common cause.
  • Gout: Inflammation of joints due to accumulation of uric acid crystals.

7. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 — Sarcomere Geometry (Hypothetical Values)

Problem: Suppose a resting sarcomere has a total length of \(2.4\,\mathrm{µm}\), with an A-band of \(1.6\,\mathrm{µm}\) and an H-zone of \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\). If the sarcomere contracts such that each Z-line moves inward by \(0.2\,\mathrm{µm}\) (assumed value), calculate the new sarcomere length, new A-band length, and new H-zone width.

Solution:

  • Because both Z-lines move inward by \(0.2\,\mathrm{µm}\), total shortening is \(2 \times 0.2\,\mathrm{µm} = 0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\).
  • New sarcomere length: \(2.4\,\mathrm{µm} - 0.4\,\mathrm{µm} = 2.0\,\mathrm{µm}\).
  • A-band length: Thick filaments do not shorten, so the A-band remains constant at \(1.6\,\mathrm{µm}\).
  • H-zone width: Thin filaments slide inward by \(0.2\,\mathrm{µm}\) from each side, reducing the non-overlapped central H-zone by \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm}\). Thus, new H-zone = \(0.4\,\mathrm{µm} - 0.4\,\mathrm{µm} = 0\,\mathrm{µm}\) (H-zone disappears).
Worked Example 2 — Skeletal Bone Accounting

Problem: A biology student counts all bones in the human body that belong exclusively to the wrist, ankle, and middle ear. What is the total count?

Solution:

  • Wrist (carpals): \(8\) per forelimb \(\times 2 = 16\) bones.
  • Ankle (tarsals): \(7\) per hindlimb \(\times 2 = 14\) bones.
  • Middle ear (ear ossicles — malleus, incus, stapes): \(3\) per ear \(\times 2 = 6\) bones.
  • Total = \(16 + 14 + 6 = 36\) bones.

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following combinations of cellular movement and human anatomical site is incorrectly matched?

  • (A) Amoeboid movement — Macrophages in blood
  • (B) Ciliary movement — Passage of ova in the female reproductive tract
  • (C) Muscular movement — Movement of jaws and tongue
  • (D) Flagellar movement — Removal of dust particles in the trachea
Answer: (D)

Explanation: The trachea is lined by ciliated epithelium, so coordinated ciliary movement—not flagellar movement—sweeps dust and foreign particles upward. Flagellar movement in humans is exhibited by spermatozoa.

Practice Question 2 Statement I & II

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Cardiac muscle cells assemble in a branching pattern, appear striated, and are involuntary in nature.
Statement II: Visceral muscles located in the inner walls of the alimentary canal are striated and under voluntary neural control.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Cardiac muscles are indeed striated, branched, and involuntary (Statement I is correct). Visceral (smooth) muscles lack striations and are involuntary (Statement II is incorrect).

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

During maximal contraction of a skeletal muscle fibre, which of the following sarcomeric zones retains its original length?

  • (A) I-band
  • (B) H-zone
  • (C) A-band
  • (D) Distance between two successive Z-lines
Answer: (C)

Explanation: According to the sliding filament theory, thin filaments slide over thick filaments. Neither filament shortens, so the A-band (which corresponds to the length of thick myosin filaments) remains constant, while the I-band, H-zone, and sarcomere length (Z-line to Z-line) decrease.

Practice Question 4 Multiple-Statement MCQ

Identify the correct statements regarding contractile proteins:
(i) Each F-actin is a polymer of monomeric G-actins.
(ii) In the resting state, tropomyosin subunits mask the ATP-binding sites on myosin.
(iii) The globular head of heavy meromyosin (HMM) acts as an active ATPase enzyme.
(iv) Binding of \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) to troponin unmasks the myosin-binding sites on actin.

  • (A) (i), (iii) and (iv) only
  • (B) (i) and (ii) only
  • (C) (ii), (iii) and (iv) only
  • (D) (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv)
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Statement (ii) is false because in the resting state, a subunit of troponin masks the myosin-binding sites on actin (not on myosin). Statements (i), (iii), and (iv) are accurate.

Practice Question 5 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): A fresh molecule of ATP must bind to the myosin head for muscle relaxation to proceed.
Reason (R): Binding of a new ATP molecule breaks the cross bridge between myosin and actin.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: After the power stroke releases \(\mathrm{ADP}\) and \(\mathrm{P_i}\), the myosin head remains tightly bound to actin until a new ATP molecule binds, which detaches the cross bridge and allows the cycle to reset or the muscle to relax (when \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum).

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following is characteristic of red muscle fibres compared to white muscle fibres?

  • (A) Fewer mitochondria and high sarcoplasmic reticulum
  • (B) Dependence primarily on anaerobic glycolysis
  • (C) High myoglobin content and abundant mitochondria
  • (D) Pale appearance due to low oxygen storage
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Red (aerobic) fibres are rich in oxygen-storing myoglobin and contain numerous mitochondria to sustain aerobic ATP production. White fibres have few mitochondria, high sarcoplasmic reticulum, and rely on anaerobic energy.

Explanation: Humans have 12 pairs of bicephalic ribs: pairs 1–7 are true ribs (direct sternal attachment), pairs 8–10 are false/vertebrochondral ribs (join the 7th rib), and pairs 11–12 are floating ribs.

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

A patient presents with rapid, involuntary muscle spasms (wild contractions) caused by abnormally low \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) levels in body fluids. Which disorder is this?

  • (A) Myasthenia gravis
  • (B) Tetany
  • (C) Muscular dystrophy
  • (D) Gout
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Tetany is characterized by rapid spasms due to hypocalcaemia (low \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\) in body fluid). Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune NMJ disorder; muscular dystrophy is genetic degeneration; gout is uric acid crystal accumulation in joints.

9. Common NEET Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent Exam Traps
  • Carpals vs. Tarsals: Wrist has 8 carpals, whereas the ankle has 7 tarsals (because the patella accounts for the 30th bone of the hindlimb!). Both forelimb and hindlimb total exactly 30 bones each.
  • Skull Bone Counts: Cranial (8) + Facial (14) = 22 skull bones; adding 1 hyoid and 6 ear ossicles gives 29 bones in the skull region.
  • A-band vs. I-band: During contraction, the A-band never shortens—only the I-band and H-zone narrow.
  • False Ribs vs. Floating Ribs: Pairs 8, 9, 10 are false (vertebrochondral) ribs; only pairs 11 and 12 are floating ribs.
Concise Final Recap

Human locomotion integrates muscular force (sliding of actin over myosin driven by \(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}\)-mediated troponin unmasking and myosin ATPase hydrolysis of ATP), skeletal leverage (80 axial + 126 appendicular = 206 bones), and joint fulcrums (fibrous, cartilaginous, and synovial). Mastery of sarcomere band dynamics, bone counts, joint examples, and disorder mechanisms ensures high accuracy on NEET UG questions.
