Biomolecules: Chemical Composition, Structure, and Enzymes

Comprehensive Self-Contained Study Module, Structural Illustrations, Worked Examples, and Original NEET UG Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Despite the vast diversity of living organisms in our biosphere, their underlying chemical composition and metabolic pathways show remarkable unity. Whether we analyse a plant tissue, an animal tissue, or a microbial paste, we encounter the same fundamental elements and classes of organic and inorganic compounds. This tutorial systematically builds your conceptual and factual command over cellular chemistry for the NEET UG examination.

Key Learning Objectives

  • Compare the elemental composition of living matter with non-living matter (Earth's crust) and understand wet weight, dry weight, and ash analysis.
  • Distinguish between the trichloroacetic acid (\(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH}\)) soluble filtrate (micromolecules) and acid-insoluble retentate (biomacromolecules), and explain why lipids appear in the acid-insoluble fraction despite their low molecular weight.
  • Recognize the chemical structures and classifications of amino acids (including zwitterionic forms), lipids (fatty acids, glycerol, glycerides, phospholipids, cholesterol), sugars, nitrogenous bases, nucleosides, and nucleotides.
  • Differentiate primary metabolites from secondary metabolites and memorize key examples of secondary metabolites by category.
  • Master the structural organization of proteins (primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary), polysaccharides (cellulose, starch, glycogen, inulin, chitin), and nucleic acids (DNA and RNA).
  • Understand enzyme catalysis, activation energy profiles, the catalytic cycle, factors affecting enzyme activity (\(T\), \(\mathrm{pH}\), substrate concentration, competitive inhibition), the 6-class enzyme classification system, and co-factors (prosthetic groups, co-enzymes, metal ions).

2. Core Concepts and Detailed Explanations

2.1 Elemental Composition: Living Tissue vs. Non-Living Matter

When elemental analysis is performed on living tissue (such as a plant leaf, animal liver, or microbial paste) and compared against a sample of non-living matter (such as the Earth's crust), we obtain lists of elements like carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulphur, sodium, calcium, magnesium, and silicon.

  • Qualitative Similarity: In absolute terms, there is no difference in the types of elements found; all elements present in a sample of the Earth's crust are also present in a sample of living tissue.
  • Quantitative Difference: A closer examination reveals that the relative abundance of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen with respect to other elements is significantly higher in any living organism than in the Earth's crust.

2.2 How to Analyse Chemical Composition?

A. Analysis of Organic Constituents (Trichloroacetic Acid Fractionation)

All carbon compounds obtained from living tissues are called biomolecules. To separate and study them:

  1. Take any living tissue (e.g., a vegetable or a piece of liver) and grind it in trichloroacetic acid (\(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH}\)) using a mortar and a pestle to obtain a thick slurry.
  2. Strain the slurry through cheesecloth or cotton to obtain two distinct fractions:
    • The Filtrate (Acid-Soluble Pool): Contains thousands of small organic compounds (molecular weight ranging from \(18\) to around \(800\,\mathrm{Da}\)) as well as inorganic ions/compounds like sulphate (\(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\)) and phosphate (\(\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}\)). It roughly represents the cytoplasmic composition.
    • The Retentate (Acid-Insoluble Fraction): Contains macromolecules from the cytoplasm and cellular organelles—specifically proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, and lipids (in the form of disrupted membrane vesicles).
  3. Extracts are then subjected to separation techniques to isolate and purify individual compounds. Analytical techniques determine the molecular formula and probable structure of each purified compound.

B. Analysis of Inorganic Constituents (Destructive Ash Analysis)

To identify inorganic elements and compounds in living organisms, a destructive experiment is performed:

  1. Wet Weight: Weigh a small amount of fresh living tissue (such as a leaf or liver).
  2. Dry Weight: Dry the tissue completely so that all water evaporates. The remaining material gives the dry weight.
  3. Ash: Burn the dried tissue completely. All carbon compounds are oxidised to gaseous forms (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and water vapour) and are removed. The remaining residue is called ash. This ash contains inorganic elements such as calcium (\(\mathrm{Ca}\)), magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg}\)), etc.

2.3 Small Molecular Weight Organic Compounds (Biomicromolecules)

From a chemical perspective, one identifies functional groups such as aldehydes, ketones, and aromatic rings. From a biological perspective, small organic biomolecules are classified into amino acids, fatty acids/lipids, sugars, and nucleotide bases/nucleosides/nucleotides.

A. Amino Acids

  • Structure: Amino acids are organic compounds containing an amino group (\(\mathrm{-NH_2}\)) and an acidic carboxyl group (\(\mathrm{-COOH}\)) as substituents on the same carbon atom—the \(\alpha\)-carbon. Hence, they are called \(\alpha\)-amino acids.
  • Substituted Methanes: They are substituted methanes with four substituent groups occupying the four valency positions of the \(\alpha\)-carbon:
    1. Hydrogen (\(\mathrm{-H}\))
    2. Carboxyl group (\(\mathrm{-COOH}\))
    3. Amino group (\(\mathrm{-NH_2}\))
    4. Variable group designated as the \(\mathrm{R}\) group
  • Proteinaceous Amino Acids: While many amino acids exist in nature based on the \(\mathrm{R}\) group, those occurring in proteins are of only 20 types:
    • If \(\mathrm{R = -H}\), the amino acid is Glycine (simplest amino acid).
    • If \(\mathrm{R = -CH_3}\) (methyl group), the amino acid is Alanine.
    • If \(\mathrm{R = -CH_2-OH}\) (hydroxy methyl group), the amino acid is Serine.
  • Classification by Functional Groups: The chemical and physical properties of amino acids depend on the amino, carboxyl, and \(\mathrm{R}\) functional groups:
    • Acidic amino acids (extra carboxyl group): e.g., Glutamic acid.
    • Basic amino acids (extra amino group): e.g., Lysine.
    • Neutral amino acids (equal amino and carboxyl groups): e.g., Valine.
    • Aromatic amino acids (possess aromatic ring): e.g., Tyrosine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan.
  • Ionizable Nature & Zwitterionic Form: Because the \(\mathrm{-NH_2}\) and \(\mathrm{-COOH}\) groups are ionizable, the structure of amino acids changes in solutions of different \(\mathrm{pH}\). The dipolar ion carrying both a positive charge (\(\mathrm{-NH_3^+}\)) and a negative charge (\(\mathrm{-COO^-}\)) is called the zwitterionic form.

B. Lipids

Lipids are generally water-insoluble compounds. They include:

  • Simple Fatty Acids: A fatty acid has a carboxyl group (\(\mathrm{-COOH}\)) attached to an \(\mathrm{R}\) group. The \(\mathrm{R}\) group can be a methyl (\(\mathrm{-CH_3}\)), an ethyl (\(\mathrm{-C_2H_5}\)), or a higher number of \(\mathrm{-CH_2}\) groups (\(1\) carbon to \(19\) carbons).
    • Palmitic acid: Has 16 carbons including the carboxyl carbon (\(\mathrm{CH_3-(CH_2)_{14}-COOH}\)).
    • Arachidonic acid: Has 20 carbon atoms including the carboxyl carbon.
    • Fatty acids can be saturated (without double bonds) or unsaturated (with one or more \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds).
  • Glycerol: A simple lipid chemically known as trihydroxy propane (\(\mathrm{CH_2OH-CHOH-CH_2OH}\)).
  • Glycerides (Fats and Oils): Many lipids have both glycerol and fatty acids, where fatty acids are esterified with glycerol. Depending on the number of fatty acids attached (1, 2, or 3), they are called monoglycerides, diglycerides, or triglycerides.
    • Based on melting point, glycerides are called fats or oils. Oils have a lower melting point (e.g., gingelly oil) and hence remain as liquid oil in winters, whereas fats solidify at room/winter temperatures (e.g., butter, ghee).
  • Phospholipids: Lipids containing phosphorus and a phosphorylated organic compound. They are found in the cell membrane. Lecithin is a prime example.
  • Complex & Steroid Lipids: Neural tissues contain lipids with more complex structures. Cholesterol is another important lipid with a four-ring fused steroid nucleus and a hydroxyl (\(\mathrm{-OH}\)) group.

C. Sugars, Nitrogenous Bases, Nucleosides, and Nucleotides

  • Monosaccharide Sugars: Key examples include Glucose (\(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\), a hexose forming a 6-membered pyranose ring) and Ribose (\(\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O_5}\), a pentose forming a 5-membered furanose ring) or 2'-deoxyribose.
  • Nitrogenous Bases: Heterocyclic carbon-nitrogen ring compounds:
    • Substituted Purines (dicyclic/two-ring structure): Adenine (A) and Guanine (G).
    • Substituted Pyrimidines (monocyclic/single-ring structure): Cytosine (C), Uracil (U), and Thymine (T).
  • Nucleosides (Base + Sugar): When a nitrogenous base is found attached to a sugar, it is called a nucleoside. Examples: Adenosine, Guanosine, Thymidine, Uridine, and Cytidine.
  • Nucleotides (Base + Sugar + Phosphate): If a phosphate group (phosphoric acid) is also found esterified to the sugar of a nucleoside, it is called a nucleotide. Examples: Adenylic acid, Thymidylic acid, Guanylic acid, Uridylic acid, and Cytidylic acid. Nucleic acids like DNA and RNA consist solely of nucleotides.

2.4 Primary and Secondary Metabolites

All biomolecules involved in cellular reactions can be called metabolites.

  • Primary Metabolites: Organic compounds such as amino acids, sugars, fatty acids, glycerol, nitrogen bases, nucleosides, and nucleotides that are present in animal, plant, and microbial tissues. They have identifiable functions and play known roles in normal physiological processes.
  • Secondary Metabolites: When plant, fungal, and microbial cells are analysed, thousands of compounds other than primary metabolites are found—such as alkaloids, flavonoids, rubber, essential oils, antibiotics, coloured pigments, scents, gums, and spices.
    • We do not at the moment understand the exact role or functions of all secondary metabolites in the host organisms.
    • However, many of them are useful to human welfare (e.g., rubber, drugs, spices, scents, and pigments), and some have ecological importance.

2.5 Biomacromolecules and the Lipid Paradox

Biomolecules in living organisms are divided into two groups based on molecular weight and solubility in trichloroacetic acid:

  1. Micromolecules (or simply Biomolecules): Found in the acid-soluble pool; molecular weights range from \(18\) to around \(800\,\mathrm{Da}\) (less than \(1000\,\mathrm{Da}\)).
  2. Macromolecules (Biomacromolecules): Found in the acid-insoluble fraction. This fraction has only four types of organic compounds: proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, and lipids. With the exception of lipids, these compounds are polymeric substances with molecular weights in the range of \(10,000\,\mathrm{Da}\) and above.

Why Do Lipids Come Under the Acid-Insoluble (Macromolecular) Fraction?

Lipids have molecular weights that do not exceed \(800\,\mathrm{Da}\) and are not polymeric substances. Therefore, lipids are not strictly macromolecules.

However, lipids are present not only as individual molecules but are also arranged into structures like the cell membrane and other organelle membranes. When we grind a tissue, we disrupt the cell structure. Cell membranes and other membranes are broken into pieces and form vesicles which are not water-soluble. Consequently, these water-insoluble membrane fragments in the form of vesicles get separated along with the acid-insoluble pool (retentate) and hence appear in the macromolecular fraction.

When we arrange the chemical composition of living tissue in order of abundance, water is the most abundant chemical in living organisms (\(70\text{–}90\%\) of total cellular mass), followed by proteins (\(10\text{–}15\%\)), nucleic acids (\(5\text{–}7\%\)), carbohydrates (\(3\%\)), lipids (\(2\%\)), and ions (\(1\%\)).

2.6 Proteins and Their Levels of Structure

Proteins are polypeptides—linear chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Because there are 20 different types of amino acids (e.g., alanine, cysteine, proline, tryptophan, lysine, etc.) that build proteins, a protein is a heteropolymer and not a homopolymer (a homopolymer has only one type of monomer repeating \(n\) times).

  • Essential vs. Non-Essential Amino Acids:
    • Essential amino acids: Essential for health and cannot be synthesized by our body; they must be supplied through our diet/food (dietary proteins).
    • Non-essential amino acids: Those which our body can synthesize on its own.
  • Most Abundant Proteins:
    • Collagen is the most abundant protein in the animal world.
    • Ribulose bisphosphate Carboxylase-Oxygenase (RuBisCO) is the most abundant protein in the whole of the biosphere.

Four Levels of Protein Structure

While inorganic chemists express structure as molecular formulae (e.g., \(\mathrm{NaCl}\), \(\mathrm{MgCl_2}\)), organic chemists write 2-dimensional views (e.g., benzene, naphthalene), and physicists focus on 3-dimensional views, biologists describe protein structure at four levels:

  1. Primary Structure: The sequence of amino acids, i.e., the positional information in a protein (which is the first amino acid, which is second, and so on). Imagined as a line:
    • The left end is represented by the first amino acid, called the N-terminal amino acid.
    • The right end is represented by the last amino acid, called the C-terminal amino acid.
  2. Secondary Structure: A protein thread does not exist throughout as an extended rigid rod. Portions of the thread are folded in the form of a helix (similar to a revolving staircase) or a \(\beta\)-pleated sheet. In proteins, only right-handed helices are observed.
  3. Tertiary Structure: The long protein chain is also folded upon itself like a hollow woolen ball, giving rise to a 3-dimensional view stabilized by interactions such as hydrogen bonds and disulphide bonds. Tertiary structure is absolutely necessary for the many biological activities of proteins (such as enzymatic activity).
  4. Quaternary Structure: Some proteins are an assembly of more than one polypeptide or subunit. The manner in which these individual folded polypeptides or subunits are arranged with respect to each other (e.g., linear string of spheres, spheres arranged one upon each other in the form of a cube or plate) is the architecture or quaternary structure.
    • Example: Adult human haemoglobin (\(\mathrm{Hb}\)) consists of 4 subunits—two identical subunits of \(\alpha\) type and two identical subunits of \(\beta\) type (\(\alpha_2\beta_2\)).

2.7 Polysaccharides (Carbohydrates)

Polysaccharides are another class of macromolecules in the acid-insoluble pellet. They are long chains of sugars—threads containing monosaccharides as building blocks linked by glycosidic bonds.

  • Cellulose: A polymeric polysaccharide consisting of only one type of monosaccharide—glucose. Hence, cellulose is a homopolymer. Plant cell walls are made of cellulose; paper made from plant pulp and cotton fibre is cellulosic. Cellulose does not contain complex helices and hence cannot hold \(\mathrm{I_2}\) molecules.
  • Starch: A homopolymer of glucose present as a storehouse of energy in plant tissues. Starch forms helical secondary structures and can hold \(\mathrm{I_2}\) (iodine) molecules in the helical portion, giving a characteristic blue colour (starch-\(\mathrm{I_2}\) complex).
  • Glycogen: The animal variant of glucose homopolymer, serving as the energy storehouse in animal tissues. It is highly branched. In a polysaccharide chain like glycogen, the right end is called the reducing end and the left end is called the non-reducing end.
  • Inulin: A homopolymer of fructose.
  • Complex Polysaccharides (Chitin): In nature, complex polysaccharides have amino-sugars and chemically modified sugars as building blocks (e.g., glucosamine, N-acetyl galactosamine). The exoskeletons of arthropods have a complex polysaccharide called chitin. Complex polysaccharides are mostly homopolymers.

2.8 Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids are polynucleotides found in the acid-insoluble fraction. Together with polysaccharides and polypeptides, they comprise the true macromolecular fraction of any living tissue or cell.

  • Building Block (Nucleotide): Every nucleotide has three chemically distinct components:
    1. A heterocyclic compound (nitrogenous base):
      • Substituted Purines: Adenine and Guanine.
      • Substituted Pyrimidines: Uracil, Cytosine, and Thymine.
    2. A monosaccharide pentose sugar: Either ribose or 2'-deoxyribose.
    3. A phosphoric acid or phosphate group.
  • DNA vs. RNA: A nucleic acid containing deoxyribose is called deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), while that which contains ribose is called ribonucleic acid (RNA). Both function as genetic material, carrying hereditary information from parental generation to progeny.

2.9 Enzymes: Biological Catalysts

  • Chemical Nature: Almost all enzymes are proteins. However, there are some nucleic acids that behave like enzymes; these are called ribozymes.
  • Active Site: Like any protein, an enzyme has primary, secondary, and tertiary structures. In the tertiary structure, the polypeptide chain folds upon itself and criss-crosses, creating many crevices or pockets. One such pocket is the active site—a crevice or pocket into which the substrate fits.
  • Comparison with Inorganic Catalysts: Inorganic catalysts work efficiently at high temperatures and high pressures, whereas most enzymes get damaged (denatured) at high temperatures (above \(40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)).
    • Exception (Thermophiles): Enzymes isolated from organisms living under extremely high temperatures (e.g., hot vents and sulphur springs) are thermally stable and retain their catalytic power even at high temperatures (up to \(80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\text{–}90\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\)).

A. Physical Changes vs. Chemical Reactions & Reaction Rates

  • Physical Process: A change in shape without breaking bonds, or a change in state of matter (e.g., ice melting into water, or water becoming vapour).
  • Chemical Reaction: A transformation in which old chemical bonds are broken and new bonds are formed.
    • Inorganic example: \(\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2 + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow BaSO_4 + 2H_2O}\)
    • Organic example: Hydrolysis of starch into glucose.
  • Rate (or Velocity) of a Process: Refers to the amount of product formed per unit time: \[\text{rate} = \frac{\delta P}{\delta t}\] Rate is called velocity if the direction is specified.
  • Temperature Rule of Thumb: Rates of physical and chemical processes are influenced by temperature. A general rule of thumb is that the rate doubles or decreases by half for every \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) change in either direction.
  • Dramatic Acceleration by Carbonic Anhydrase: Consider the hydration of carbon dioxide: \[\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}} H_2CO_3}\]
    • Uncatalysed (absence of enzyme): Very slow—about \(200\) molecules of \(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\) formed per hour.
    • Catalysed (with cytoplasmic carbonic anhydrase): About \(600,000\) molecules formed every second.
    • Acceleration Factor: The enzyme accelerates the reaction rate by about \(10\text{ million}\) times (\(10^7\) times)!
  • Metabolic Pathways: A multistep chemical reaction where each step is catalysed by the same enzyme complex or different enzymes is called a metabolic pathway. For example, the conversion of glucose to pyruvic acid (\(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + O_2 \rightarrow 2C_3H_4O_3 + 2H_2O}\)) occurs through 10 different enzyme-catalysed metabolic reactions (glycolysis). Depending on conditions, one or two additional reactions yield different end products:
    • Normal aerobic conditions: Pyruvic acid is formed.
    • Human skeletal muscle (anaerobic conditions): Lactic acid is formed.
    • Yeast (fermentation): Ethanol (alcohol) is produced.

B. Activation Energy and Transition State

In an enzyme-catalysed reaction (\(\mathrm{S \rightarrow P}\)), the substrate (\(\mathrm{S}\)) diffuses toward and binds to the active site of the enzyme (\(\mathrm{E}\)), forming an obligatory, transient \(\mathrm{ES}\) complex. While bound, a new, unstable high-energy structure of the substrate called the transition state structure is formed before bond breaking/making completes and product (\(\mathrm{P}\)) is released.

  • If the potential energy level of \(\mathrm{P}\) is lower than that of \(\mathrm{S}\), the reaction is exothermic (spontaneous; no external heating needed to form product). If \(\mathrm{P}\) is higher than \(\mathrm{S}\), it is endothermic (energy-requiring).
  • In both exothermic and endothermic reactions, \(\mathrm{S}\) must pass through the higher-energy transition state. The difference in average energy content of \(\mathrm{S}\) from that of this transition state is called activation energy.
  • Enzymes lower this activation energy barrier, making the transition from \(\mathrm{S}\) to \(\mathrm{P}\) much easier and faster.

C. Nature of Enzyme Action (The 4-Step Catalytic Cycle)

The reaction sequence is represented as:

\[\mathrm{E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \longrightarrow EP \longrightarrow E + P}\]
  1. First, the substrate (\(\mathrm{S}\)) binds to the active site of the enzyme (\(\mathrm{E}\)), fitting into the active site.
  2. The binding of the substrate induces the enzyme to alter its shape, fitting more tightly around the substrate.
  3. The active site of the enzyme, now in close proximity to the substrate, breaks the chemical bonds of the substrate and the new enzyme-product (\(\mathrm{EP}\)) complex is formed.
  4. The enzyme releases the products (\(\mathrm{P}\)) of the reaction, and the free enzyme (\(\mathrm{E}\)) is ready to bind to another molecule of the substrate and run through the catalytic cycle once again.

D. Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

  • Temperature and \(\mathrm{pH}\): Enzymes function in a narrow range of temperature and \(\mathrm{pH}\), showing highest activity at a specific optimum temperature and optimum \(\mathrm{pH}\) (bell-shaped curves). Activity declines both below and above the optimum value:
    • Low temperature preserves the enzyme in a temporarily inactive state.
    • High temperature destroys enzymatic activity because proteins are denatured by heat (disrupting tertiary structure).
  • Concentration of Substrate (\(\mathrm{[S]}\)): With an increase in substrate concentration, the velocity of the enzymatic reaction rises at first. The reaction ultimately reaches a maximum velocity (\(V_{\max}\)) which is not exceeded by any further rise in substrate concentration.
    • Reason: Enzyme molecules are fewer than substrate molecules; after saturation of all enzyme molecules, there are no free enzyme molecules left to bind with additional substrate molecules.
    • Michaelis Constant (\(K_m\)): The substrate concentration at which the chemical reaction attains half its maximum velocity (\(\frac{V_{\max}}{2}\)).
  • Inhibition and Competitive Inhibitors: When the binding of a specific chemical shuts off enzyme activity, the process is called inhibition and the chemical is an inhibitor.
    • Competitive Inhibitor: When the inhibitor closely resembles the substrate in its molecular structure, it competes with the substrate for the substrate-binding (active) site of the enzyme. Consequently, the substrate cannot bind and enzyme action declines.
    • Classic Example: Inhibition of succinic dehydrogenase by malonate, which closely resembles the substrate succinate in structure.
    • Application: Competitive inhibitors are often used in the control of bacterial pathogens.

E. Classification and Nomenclature of Enzymes

Enzymes are divided into 6 classes based on the type of reactions they catalyse, each with 4–13 subclasses, and named accordingly by a four-digit number:

  1. Class 1 — Oxidoreductases / Dehydrogenases: Catalyse oxidoreduction between two substrates \(\mathrm{S}\) and \(\mathrm{S'}\): \[\mathrm{S\text{ reduced} + S'\text{ oxidised} \longrightarrow S\text{ oxidised} + S'\text{ reduced}}\]
  2. Class 2 — Transferases: Catalyse transfer of a group \(\mathrm{G}\) (other than hydrogen) between a pair of substrates \(\mathrm{S}\) and \(\mathrm{S'}\): \[\mathrm{S - G + S' \longrightarrow S + S' - G}\]
  3. Class 3 — Hydrolases: Catalyse hydrolysis of ester, ether, peptide, glycosidic, \(\mathrm{C-C}\), \(\mathrm{C}\)-halide, or \(\mathrm{P-N}\) bonds.
  4. Class 4 — Lyases: Catalyse removal of groups from substrates by mechanisms other than hydrolysis, leaving double bonds: \[\mathrm{\overset{\overset{\text{X}}{|}}{C} - \overset{\overset{\text{Y}}{|}}{C} \longrightarrow X - Y + C = C}\]
  5. Class 5 — Isomerases: Include all enzymes catalysing inter-conversion of optical, geometric, or positional isomers.
  6. Class 6 — Ligases: Catalyse the linking together of 2 compounds (e.g., enzymes which catalyse joining of \(\mathrm{C-O}\), \(\mathrm{C-S}\), \(\mathrm{C-N}\), \(\mathrm{P-O}\), etc. bonds).

F. Co-Factors

In many enzymes, non-protein constituents called co-factors are bound to the enzyme to make it catalytically active. In these instances, the protein portion of the enzyme is called the apoenzyme (Holoenzyme = Apoenzyme + Co-factor). Catalytic activity is lost when the co-factor is removed from the enzyme.

Three kinds of co-factors are identified:

3. Key Definitions, Equations, and High-Yield Results

  • Rate of Reaction: \(\text{rate} = \dfrac{\delta P}{\delta t}\) (called velocity when direction is specified).
  • Temperature Rule of Thumb (\(Q_{10}\) concept): Rate doubles or decreases by half for every \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) change in either direction.
  • Carbonic Anhydrase Acceleration: \[\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}} H_2CO_3}\] Uncatalysed rate \(= 200\text{ molecules/hour}\); Catalysed rate \(= 600,000\text{ molecules/second}\) (\(\text{Acceleration} = 10\text{ million times}\)).
  • Enzyme-Substrate Catalytic Sequence: \[\mathrm{E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \longrightarrow EP \longrightarrow E + P}\]
  • Glycolysis (10-Step Metabolic Pathway): \[\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + O_2 \longrightarrow 2C_3H_4O_3\text{ (Pyruvic acid)} + 2H_2O}\]
  • Michaelis Constant (\(K_m\)): Substrate concentration \(\mathrm{[S]}\) at which reaction velocity \(V = \dfrac{V_{\max}}{2}\).

5. Worked Examples (Step-by-Step Application)

Worked Example 1: Quantitative Comparison of Catalysed vs. Uncatalysed Reaction Rates

Problem: In the absence of any enzyme, \(200\) molecules of carbonic acid (\(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\)) are formed per hour from \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). In the presence of cytoplasmic carbonic anhydrase, \(600,000\) molecules of \(\mathrm{H_2CO_3}\) are formed every second. Calculate the exact ratio of the catalysed rate to the uncatalysed rate when both are expressed in molecules formed per hour.

Solution:

  1. Uncatalysed rate per hour: \[R_{\text{uncatalysed}} = 200\text{ molecules/hour}\]
  2. Convert catalysed rate from molecules/second to molecules/hour: Since \(1\text{ hour} = 60\text{ minutes} \times 60\text{ seconds} = 3,600\text{ seconds}\): \[R_{\text{catalysed}} = 600,000\text{ molecules/s} \times 3,600\text{ s/hour} = 2.16 \times 10^9\text{ molecules/hour}\]
  3. Ratio of catalysed to uncatalysed rate: \[\text{Ratio} = \frac{2.16 \times 10^9}{200} = 1.08 \times 10^7 \approx 10\text{ million times}\]

Conclusion: Carbonic anhydrase accelerates the hydration of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) by approximately \(10\text{ million}\) times (\(1.08 \times 10^7\)).

Worked Example 2: Identifying Fractions in Trichloroacetic Acid (\(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH}\)) Extraction

Problem: A student grinds a piece of fresh liver tissue in trichloroacetic acid and strains the slurry through cotton. Predict whether each of the following components will be found predominantly in the filtrate (acid-soluble pool) or the retentate (acid-insoluble fraction), and state why: (a) Alanine, (b) Lecithin, (c) RuBisCO/Collagen, (d) Adenylic acid, (e) Glycogen, (f) \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) and \(\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}\).

Solution:

  • (a) Alanine: Filtrate (acid-soluble pool) — It is a monomeric amino acid with molecular weight well within \(18\text{–}800\,\mathrm{Da}\).
  • (b) Lecithin: Retentate (acid-insoluble fraction) — Although lecithin is a phospholipid with molecular weight \(< 800\,\mathrm{Da}\), it is a component of cell membranes. Upon grinding, membranes break into water-insoluble vesicles that are retained in the acid-insoluble fraction.
  • (c) RuBisCO / Collagen: Retentate (acid-insoluble fraction) — Polypeptide macromolecules with molecular weight \(\ge 10,000\,\mathrm{Da}\).
  • (d) Adenylic acid: Filtrate (acid-soluble pool) — It is a single nucleotide monomer (molecular weight \(< 1000\,\mathrm{Da}\)), unlike polymeric DNA or RNA.
  • (e) Glycogen: Retentate (acid-insoluble fraction) — Polymeric polysaccharide macromolecule.
  • (f) \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}\) and \(\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}\): Filtrate (acid-soluble pool) — Inorganic compounds/ions dissolve in the acid-soluble fraction.
Worked Example 3: Resolving Textbook Conceptual Exercises

Problem: Provide concise, exam-ready explanations for: (a) Composition of a triglyceride, (b) Why tertiary structure of proteins is vital, and (c) How gums differ from synthetic adhesives like Fevicol.

Solution:

  • (a) Composition of a Triglyceride: A triglyceride is a glyceride lipid composed of one molecule of glycerol (trihydroxy propane) esterified with three molecules of fatty acids (\(\mathrm{R_1COOH}\), \(\mathrm{R_2COOH}\), \(\mathrm{R_3COOH}\)) via three ester bonds.
  • (b) Importance of Tertiary Structure: In the tertiary structure, the long polypeptide chain folds upon itself like a hollow woolen ball (3D conformation), bringing distant amino acid side chains together to form crevices/pockets called active sites. This 3D tertiary structure is absolutely necessary for the biological and enzymatic activities of proteins.
  • (c) Natural Gums vs. Fevicol: Natural gums are polymeric secondary metabolites (carbohydrates/polysaccharides) produced by plants, whereas Fevicol is a synthetic resin adhesive (polyvinyl acetate polymer) manufactured industrially from petrochemicals.

7. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Trap 1 — Lipids as Macromolecules: Never mark "Lipids have molecular weight \(> 10,000\,\mathrm{Da}\)" as true! Lipids have molecular weight \(\le 800\,\mathrm{Da}\) and are not polymers or true macromolecules; they only appear in the acid-insoluble fraction because disrupted membranes form water-insoluble vesicles.
  • Trap 2 — Homopolymer vs. Heteropolymer: Proteins and nucleic acids are heteropolymers (built from 20 amino acids and 4 nucleotides, respectively). Cellulose, starch, glycogen, inulin, and chitin are homopolymers.
  • Trap 3 — Starch vs. Cellulose Iodine Test: Starch gives a blue colour with \(\mathrm{I_2}\) because it forms helical secondary structures that trap \(\mathrm{I_2}\). Cellulose lacks complex helices and does not trap \(\mathrm{I_2}\).
  • Trap 4 — Glycogen Ends vs. Protein Ends:
    • In Polysaccharides (Glycogen): Left end = Non-reducing end; Right end = Reducing end.
    • In Proteins: Left end (first amino acid) = N-terminal; Right end (last amino acid) = C-terminal.
  • Trap 5 — Prosthetic Group vs. Co-enzyme: Both are organic compounds! The distinction is binding affinity: prosthetic groups (e.g., haem in catalase/peroxidase) are tightly bound, whereas co-enzymes (e.g., NAD, NADP containing vitamin niacin) bind transiently during catalysis.

8. Concise Final Recap

  • Elemental Abundance: \(\mathrm{C, H, O}\) are relatively much higher in living tissues than in the Earth's crust. Water is the most abundant chemical in living cells (\(70\text{–}90\%\)).
  • Acid-Soluble vs. Acid-Insoluble: Grinding tissue in \(\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH}\) yields the acid-soluble filtrate (biomicromolecules, \(18\text{–}800\,\mathrm{Da}\), plus inorganic ions like \(\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}, \mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}\)) and the acid-insoluble retentate (proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides \(\ge 10,000\,\mathrm{Da}\), plus membrane lipid vesicles).
  • Proteins: Heteropolymers of 20 \(\alpha\)-amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Collagen is the most abundant protein in the animal world; RuBisCO is the most abundant protein in the entire biosphere.
  • Polysaccharides & Nucleic Acids: Cellulose, starch, and glycogen are homopolymers of glucose; inulin is a homopolymer of fructose; chitin is a complex homopolymer in arthropod exoskeletons. DNA (2'-deoxyribose) and RNA (ribose) are polynucleotides serving as genetic material.
  • Enzymes: Mostly proteins (except catalytic RNA ribozymes) that lower activation energy via active sites in their tertiary structure. Classified into 6 classes (Oxidoreductases, Transferases, Hydrolases, Lyases, Isomerases, Ligases) and often require co-factors (prosthetic groups, co-enzymes, or metal ions) for catalytic activity.
