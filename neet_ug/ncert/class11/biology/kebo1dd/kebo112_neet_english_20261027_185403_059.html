NEET UG Standard — Biology (Plant Physiology)

Respiration in Plants

Cellular Oxidation, Glycolysis, Fermentation, Krebs' Cycle, Electron Transport System, Amphibolic Pathway, and Respiratory Quotient

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

All living organisms require energy to perform vital daily life activities such as absorption, transport, movement, reproduction, and breathing. This energy is obtained by the enzymatic oxidation of macromolecules called food. While green plants and cyanobacteria trap light energy during photosynthesis and convert it into chemical energy stored in the bonds of carbohydrates (such as glucose, sucrose, and starch), non-green parts of plants—as well as heterotrophic animals and saprophytic fungi—depend on translocated or ingested photosynthetic products. Ultimately, all food respired for life processes originates from photosynthesis.

Core Learning Objectives for NEET UG
  • Understand the definition of cellular respiration, respiratory substrates, and why ATP functions as the energy currency of the cell.
  • Explain how plants manage gaseous exchange through stomata and lenticels without specialised respiratory organs.
  • Trace the complete ten-step enzymatic pathway of Glycolysis (EMP Pathway) in the cytoplasm, identifying exact steps of ATP utilisation, \( \mathrm{NADH} + \mathrm{H}^+ \) formation, and substrate-level phosphorylation.
  • Compare anaerobic pathways—alcoholic fermentation and lactic acid fermentation—with aerobic respiration.
  • Analyse mitochondrial aerobic events: oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate, the Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) / Krebs' Cycle in the mitochondrial matrix, and the Electron Transport System (ETS) on the inner mitochondrial membrane.
  • Evaluate the chemiosmotic synthesis of ATP via Complex V (\( \mathrm{F}_0\text{–}\mathrm{F}_1 \) ATP synthase) and the theoretical assumptions behind the respiratory balance sheet (net gain of 38 ATP).
  • Justify why the respiratory pathway is designated as an amphibolic pathway and calculate the Respiratory Quotient (RQ) for carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.

2. Core Concepts and Detailed Explanations

2.1 Cellular Respiration and Respiratory Substrates

In eukaryotes, photosynthesis takes place inside chloroplasts, whereas the breakdown of complex molecules to yield energy occurs in the cytoplasm and in the mitochondria. The breaking of the carbon–carbon (\(\mathrm{C{-}C}\)) bonds of complex organic compounds through oxidation within the cells, leading to the release of a considerable amount of energy, is called cellular respiration.

The organic compounds that are oxidised during this process are known as respiratory substrates:

  • Carbohydrates are the most favoured and commonly oxidised substrates to release energy.
  • Proteins, fats, and organic acids can also be used as respiratory substrates in some plants under specific physiological conditions.

During intracellular oxidation, the entire energy contained within a respiratory substrate is never released free into the cell at once or in a single step. Instead, it is released in a series of slow, step-wise reactions controlled by specific enzymes and is trapped as chemical energy in the form of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP). Because energy released by oxidation cannot be used directly by cellular machinery, it is first used to synthesise ATP, which is later broken down whenever and wherever energy is required. Therefore, ATP acts as the energy currency of the cell. In addition to providing energy, the carbon skeletons produced as intermediates during respiration serve as precursors for the biosynthesis of other essential cellular molecules.

2.2 Do Plants Breathe? Gaseous Exchange in Plants

Plants require \(\mathrm{O}_2\) for aerobic respiration and release \(\mathrm{CO}_2\). Unlike animals, plants possess no specialised organs for gaseous exchange; instead, they rely on stomata (in leaves and young green stems) and lenticels (in woody stems and roots) for gaseous diffusion. Plants thrive without specialised respiratory organs for three primary physiological and anatomical reasons:

  1. Autonomous Part-wise Gas Exchange: Each plant part takes care of its own gas-exchange needs. There is very little transport of gases from one plant part to another.
  2. Low Respiratory Rates: Plants do not present great demands for gas exchange. Roots, stems, and leaves respire at rates far lower than animals do. Large volumes of gases are exchanged only during photosynthesis, and each leaf is well adapted to meet its own needs during these periods. Furthermore, when cells actively photosynthesise, \(\mathrm{O}_2\) availability is not a limiting factor because \(\mathrm{O}_2\) is evolved right within those cells.
  3. Short Diffusion Distance and Interconnected Air Spaces: Even in large, bulky plants, the distance that gases must diffuse is small because nearly every living cell in a plant is located close to the surface. In thick, woody stems, the living cells are organised in thin layers inside and beneath the bark, which contains openings called lenticels, while the cells in the deep interior are dead and provide only mechanical support. Furthermore, the loose packing of parenchyma cells in leaves, stems, and roots provides an interconnected network of air spaces, ensuring that most living cells have at least a part of their surface in direct contact with air.
Complete Oxidation (Combustion) of Glucose: \[ \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \longrightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O + Energy} \]

In uncontrolled physical combustion, most of the liberated energy is lost as heat. Plant cells avoid this by catabolising glucose in several small, enzyme-catalysed steps so that individual steps release just enough energy to be coupled to ADP phosphorylation (ATP synthesis).

Although aerobic respiration requires oxygen, the earliest cells on Earth evolved in an oxygen-free atmosphere. Even today, several organisms are adapted to anaerobic conditions—some are facultative anaerobes (can respire with or without \(\mathrm{O}_2\)), whereas others are obligate anaerobes (require strictly anaerobic conditions). Crucially, all living organisms retain the enzymatic machinery to partially oxidise glucose into pyruvic acid without the help of oxygen. This universal pathway is called glycolysis.

2.3 Glycolysis (EMP Pathway)

The term glycolysis originates from the Greek words glycos (sugar) and lysis (splitting). The scheme was elucidated by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof, and J. Parnas, and is therefore known as the EMP pathway.

  • Site: Cytoplasm (cytosol) of the cell.
  • Distribution: Present in all living organisms. In anaerobic organisms, it is the sole process in respiration.
  • Oxygen Requirement: Does not require oxygen (occurs in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions).
  • Source of Glucose in Plants: Derived either from sucrose (the end product of photosynthesis) or from storage carbohydrates (like starch). The enzyme invertase hydrolyses sucrose into glucose and fructose, both of which readily enter the glycolytic pathway.

Step-by-Step Analysis of Glycolysis

In glycolysis, a chain of ten reactions under the control of different enzymes converts one molecule of hexose sugar (glucose, 6C) into two molecules of pyruvic acid (pyruvate, 3C):

  1. Phosphorylation of Glucose (ATP Utilisation #1): Glucose (and fructose) are phosphorylated to form glucose-6-phosphate by the enzyme hexokinase using one molecule of ATP.
  2. Isomerisation: Glucose-6-phosphate isomerises to produce fructose-6-phosphate. (Subsequent steps of metabolism of glucose and fructose are identical.)
  3. Second Phosphorylation (ATP Utilisation #2): Fructose-6-phosphate is phosphorylated into fructose 1,6-bisphosphate using a second molecule of ATP.
  4. Cleavage (Splitting): Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (6C) is split into two 3-carbon triose phosphate isomers: 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL / glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP).
  5. Isomerisation of Triose Phosphates: DHAP is converted into PGAL so that two molecules of PGAL proceed through the remainder of the pathway.
  6. Oxidation and Phosphorylation (\( \mathrm{NADH + H^+} \) Formation): Each PGAL is converted into 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA / 1,3-bisphosphoglyceric acid). Two redox-equivalents (in the form of two hydrogen atoms) are removed from PGAL and transferred to a molecule of \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\), reducing it to \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\). Simultaneously, PGAL is oxidised and combines with inorganic phosphate (\(\mathrm{Pi}\)) to form BPGA. (For 2 molecules of PGAL, \(2\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) are formed.)
  7. Substrate-Level Phosphorylation #1 (ATP Synthesis #1): The conversion of BPGA to 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA) is an energy-yielding reaction; the released energy is trapped by synthesising ATP from ADP (2 ATP formed per glucose).
  8. Intramolecular Rearrangement: 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA) is converted to 2-phosphoglycerate.
  9. Dehydration: 2-phosphoglycerate loses a molecule of water (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) to form phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP).
  10. Substrate-Level Phosphorylation #2 (ATP Synthesis #2): PEP is converted into pyruvic acid, coupled with the synthesis of a second set of ATP molecules (2 ATP formed per glucose).
Energetics of Glycolysis per Glucose Molecule
  • ATP Utilised (Invested): \(2\text{ ATP}\) (Step 1: Glucose \(\rightarrow\) Glucose-6-phosphate; Step 3: Fructose-6-phosphate \(\rightarrow\) Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate).
  • ATP Directly Synthesised: \(4\text{ ATP}\) (\(2\text{ ATP}\) during BPGA \(\rightarrow\) PGA, and \(2\text{ ATP}\) during PEP \(\rightarrow\) Pyruvic acid).
  • Net Direct Gain of ATP: \(4\text{ ATP} - 2\text{ ATP} = \mathbf{2\text{ ATP}}\).
  • Reducing Equivalents Formed: \(\mathbf{2\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})}\) (formed during PGAL \(\rightarrow\) BPGA).

2.4 Metabolic Fate of Pyruvate and Fermentation

Pyruvic acid is the key product of glycolysis. Depending on cellular needs and the availability of \(\mathrm{O}_2\), different cells handle pyruvic acid in three major ways:

  1. Lactic acid fermentation (anaerobic)
  2. Alcoholic fermentation (anaerobic)
  3. Aerobic respiration (Krebs' cycle) (requires \(\mathrm{O}_2\) supply for complete oxidation of glucose to \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) and \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\))

Fermentation takes place under anaerobic conditions in many prokaryotes, unicellular eukaryotes (such as yeast), and germinating seeds. It involves the incomplete oxidation of glucose:

  • Alcoholic Fermentation (e.g., in Yeast): Pyruvic acid is converted into \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) and ethanol in a two-step reaction catalysed sequentially by the enzymes pyruvic acid decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase.
  • Lactic Acid Fermentation (e.g., in Lactobacillus bacteria and animal skeletal muscles during vigorous exercise): When oxygen is inadequate for cellular respiration, pyruvic acid is directly reduced to lactic acid (without \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) release) by the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase.
Tutorial Diagram 2: Major Pathways of Anaerobic Respiration (Lactic Acid and Alcoholic Fermentation)
Key Characteristics & Limitations of Fermentation (High-Yield NEET Facts)
  • Role of Reducing Agent: In both lactic acid and alcoholic fermentation, \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) (generated during glycolysis when PGAL is oxidised to BPGA) acts as the reducing agent and is re-oxidised to \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\), allowing glycolysis to continue in the absence of oxygen.
  • Low Energy Yield: Less than 7 per cent (\(7\%\)) of the energy in glucose is released during fermentation, and not all of it is trapped as high-energy bonds of ATP.
  • Net ATP Gain: Only \(2\text{ ATP}\) molecules are synthesised net per glucose molecule fermented to alcohol or lactic acid (\(4\text{ ATP}\) synthesised minus \(2\text{ ATP}\) utilised in glycolysis).
  • Hazardous Products & Yeast Toxicity: Fermentation is hazardous because either acid or alcohol is produced. Yeasts poison themselves to death when the concentration of alcohol reaches about 13 per cent (\(13\%\)). Consequently, the maximum concentration of alcohol in naturally fermented beverages is about \(13\%\). Alcoholic beverages with an alcohol content greater than \(13\%\) are obtained by distillation.

2.5 Aerobic Respiration

Aerobic respiration is the process that leads to the complete oxidation of organic substances in the presence of oxygen, releasing \(\mathrm{CO}_2\), water, and a large amount of energy. In eukaryotes, these post-glycolytic steps take place inside the mitochondria. Pyruvate formed in the cytosol during glycolysis is actively transported across the mitochondrial membranes into the mitochondrial matrix.

The two crucial events in aerobic respiration are:

  1. Complete oxidation of pyruvate by the stepwise removal of all hydrogen atoms, leaving three molecules of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) (per pyruvate). Location: Mitochondrial Matrix.
  2. Passing on of the electrons removed as part of the hydrogen atoms to molecular \(\mathrm{O}_2\) with simultaneous synthesis of ATP. Location: Inner Membrane of the Mitochondria.

Link Reaction: Oxidative Decarboxylation of Pyruvate

Upon entering the mitochondrial matrix, pyruvate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation through a complex set of reactions catalysed by the enzyme complex pyruvic dehydrogenase (pyruvate dehydrogenase). This reaction requires the participation of several cofactors and coenzymes, including \(\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}\), \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\), and Coenzyme A (\(\mathrm{CoA}\)):

\[ \mathrm{Pyruvic\ acid + CoA + NAD^+} \xrightarrow[\text{Pyruvate dehydrogenase}]{\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}} \mathrm{Acetyl\ CoA + CO_2 + NADH + H^+} \]

Because one molecule of glucose produces two molecules of pyruvic acid during glycolysis, the oxidative decarboxylation of two pyruvates yields \(2\text{ Acetyl CoA}\), \(2\,\mathrm{CO}_2\), and \(2\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\).

2.5.1 Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) Cycle / Krebs' Cycle

Acetyl CoA (2C) enters a cyclic metabolic pathway in the mitochondrial matrix called the Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) cycle, more commonly called Krebs' cycle after Hans Krebs, who first elucidated it (also called the Citric Acid Cycle because citric acid with three carboxyl groups is the first stable product).

Tutorial Diagram 3: The Citric Acid Cycle (Krebs' Cycle) and Link Reaction

Step-by-Step Reactions of the TCA Cycle

  1. Condensation: The TCA cycle begins with the condensation of the acetyl group (2C) (from Acetyl CoA) with oxaloacetic acid (OAA, 4C) and water (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) to yield citric acid (6C). This reaction is catalysed by the enzyme citrate synthase, and a molecule of \(\mathrm{CoA}\) is released.
  2. Isomerisation: Citrate (6C) is isomerised to isocitrate (6C).
  3. First Oxidative Decarboxylation: Isocitrate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation to form \(\alpha\)-ketoglutaric acid (5C), releasing the first molecule of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) and reducing \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) to \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\).
  4. Second Oxidative Decarboxylation: \(\alpha\)-ketoglutaric acid (5C) undergoes a second successive step of oxidative decarboxylation to form succinyl-CoA (4C), releasing a second molecule of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) and reducing another \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) to \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\).
  5. Substrate-Level Phosphorylation (Conversion of Succinyl-CoA to Succinic Acid): Succinyl-CoA (4C) is oxidised/converted to succinic acid (4C), during which a molecule of GTP (Guanosine Triphosphate) is synthesised from GDP and Pi. This is a substrate-level phosphorylation. In a coupled reaction, GTP is converted to GDP with the simultaneous synthesis of ATP from ADP.
  6. Oxidation of Succinate: Succinic acid (4C) is oxidised to fumarate/malic acid via Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase), where \(\mathrm{FAD^+}\) is reduced to \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\).
  7. Regeneration of OAA: Malic acid (4C) is oxidised back to oxaloacetic acid (OAA, 4C), reducing a third molecule of \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) to \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) and allowing the cycle to continue.
Summary Equation for Mitochondrial Matrix Oxidation (Link Reaction + TCA Cycle per Pyruvic Acid): \[ \mathrm{Pyruvic\ acid + 4\,NAD^+ + FAD^+ + 2\,H_2O + ADP + Pi} \xrightarrow{\text{Mitochondrial Matrix}} \mathrm{3\,CO_2 + 4\,NADH + 4\,H^+ + FADH_2 + ATP} \]
Key Accounting for TCA Cycle & Link Reaction
  • Per Single Turn of TCA Cycle (per 1 Acetyl CoA): \(3\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\), \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\), \(1\text{ GTP (or ATP)}\), and \(2\,\mathrm{CO}_2\).
  • Per 1 Pyruvate (Link Reaction + 1 Turn of TCA): \(4\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\) (\(1\) in Link + \(3\) in TCA), \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\), \(1\text{ ATP (via GTP)}\), and \(3\,\mathrm{CO}_2\).
  • Per 1 Glucose Molecule (2 Pyruvates = 2 Link Reactions + 2 Turns of TCA): \(8\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\), \(2\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\), \(2\text{ ATP}\), and \(6\,\mathrm{CO}_2\).
  • Essential Requirements for Continued TCA Operation: Continued replenishment of oxaloacetic acid (OAA) (the first member of the cycle) and continuous regeneration of \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) and \(\mathrm{FAD^+}\) from \(\mathrm{NADH}\) and \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\) respectively via the Electron Transport System.

2.5.2 Electron Transport System (ETS) and Oxidative Phosphorylation

To release and utilise the energy stored in \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) and \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\), these reduced coenzymes are oxidised through the Electron Transport System (ETS) located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, and the electrons are ultimately passed on to \(\mathrm{O}_2\), resulting in the formation of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\).

Tutorial Diagram 4: Electron Transport System (ETS) and ATP Synthase on the Inner Mitochondrial Membrane

Organisation of the Five ETS Complexes and Mobile Carriers

  • Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase): Oxidises electrons from \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) (produced in the mitochondrial matrix during the citric acid cycle) via FMN and \(\mathrm{Fe\text{-}S}\) centres, transferring electrons to ubiquinone (UQ) located within the inner membrane.
  • Complex II (Succinate dehydrogenase): Receives reducing equivalents via \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\) generated during the oxidation of succinate to fumarate in the citric acid cycle (via \(\mathrm{FAD}\) and \(\mathrm{Fe\text{-}S}\)) and transfers them directly to ubiquinone (UQ).
  • Ubiquinone (UQ / Coenzyme Q): Located within the inner mitochondrial membrane; receives electrons from both Complex I and Complex II, becoming reduced ubiquinone (ubiquinol, \(\mathrm{UQH}_2\)).
  • Complex III (Cytochrome \(bc_1\) complex): Oxidises reduced ubiquinone (ubiquinol, \(\mathrm{UQH}_2\)) and transfers electrons (via \(\mathrm{Cyt\ }b \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe\text{-}S} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cyt\ }c_1\)) to cytochrome \(c\).
  • Cytochrome \(c\): A small peripheral protein attached to the outer surface of the inner mitochondrial membrane; acts as a mobile carrier for the transfer of electrons between Complex III and Complex IV.
  • Complex IV (Cytochrome \(c\) oxidase complex): Contains cytochromes \(a\) and \(a_3\), and two copper centres (\(\mathrm{Cu_A}\) and \(\mathrm{Cu_B}\)). It passes electrons to molecular oxygen (\(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{O}_2 + 2\mathrm{H}^+ \rightarrow \mathrm{H_2O}\)).
  • Complex V (ATP synthase): Couples the proton gradient generated by electron transport (Complexes I to IV) to the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate (\(\mathrm{Pi}\)).
Tutorial Diagram 5: Diagrammatic Representation of ATP Synthesis in Mitochondria via Complex V (\(\mathrm{F_0\text{–}F_1}\))

Role of Oxygen, Oxidative Phosphorylation, and Chemiosmosis

  • Yield per Electron Donor: The number of ATP molecules synthesised depends on the nature of the electron donor:
    • Oxidation of \(1\text{ molecule of }\mathrm{NADH}\) gives rise to \(3\text{ molecules of ATP}\).
    • Oxidation of \(1\text{ molecule of }\mathrm{FADH}_2\) produces \(2\text{ molecules of ATP}\).
  • Vital Role of Oxygen: Although the aerobic process of respiration takes place only in the presence of oxygen, the direct role of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) is limited to the terminal stage of the process. Nevertheless, \(\mathrm{O}_2\) is vital because it drives the entire process by removing hydrogen from the system; oxygen acts as the final hydrogen (and electron) acceptor.
  • Why Called Oxidative Phosphorylation? Unlike photophosphorylation (where light energy is utilised to produce the proton gradient required for phosphorylation), in respiration it is the energy of oxidation–reduction that is utilised to create the proton gradient. Hence, the process is termed oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Structure and Mechanism of Complex V (ATP Synthase): Consists of two major components, \(\mathrm{F}_1\) and \(\mathrm{F}_0\):
    • \(\mathrm{F}_1\) headpiece: A peripheral membrane protein complex protruding toward the matrix that contains the catalytic site for synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate (\(\mathrm{Pi}\)).
    • \(\mathrm{F}_0\) component: An integral membrane protein complex embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane that forms the transmembrane channel through which protons (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) cross the inner membrane.
    • Proton Stoichiometry: For each ATP produced, \(4\mathrm{H}^+\) pass through \(\mathrm{F}_0\) from the intermembrane space to the matrix down the electrochemical proton gradient.

2.6 The Respiratory Balance Sheet

Calculating the theoretical net gain of ATP for every glucose molecule oxidised aerobically yields a net gain of \(38\text{ ATP}\) molecules. However, this calculation is strictly theoretical and rests on four key assumptions:

  1. There is a sequential, orderly pathway functioning, with one substrate forming the next, and with glycolysis, TCA cycle, and ETS pathway following one after another.
  2. The \(\mathrm{NADH}\) synthesised in glycolysis is transferred into the mitochondria and undergoes oxidative phosphorylation.
  3. None of the intermediates in the pathway are utilised to synthesise any other compound.
  4. Only glucose is being respired—no other alternative substrates are entering the pathway at any of the intermediary stages.

Why these assumptions are not valid in a living system: In reality, all metabolic pathways work simultaneously rather than sequentially one after another; substrates enter and are withdrawn from the pathways as and when necessary; ATP is utilised as and when needed; and enzymatic rates are controlled by multiple regulatory mechanisms.

2.7 Amphibolic Pathway

While glucose is the favoured substrate for respiration (and all other carbohydrates are usually first converted into glucose before entering the pathway), fats and proteins can also be respired, though they do not enter at the first step of glycolysis:

  • Fats: First broken down into glycerol and fatty acids.
    • Fatty acids are degraded to acetyl CoA before entering the pathway.
    • Glycerol is converted into PGAL (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate / DHAP) to enter the glycolytic pathway.
  • Proteins: Degraded by proteases into individual amino acids. After deamination, depending on their carbon skeleton structure, amino acids enter the pathway at some stage within the Krebs' cycle, or as pyruvate, or as acetyl CoA.
Tutorial Diagram 6: Interrelationship Among Metabolic Pathways Showing Entry Points of Fats, Carbohydrates, and Proteins

Why is the Respiratory Pathway Called Amphibolic rather than Purely Catabolic?

Traditionally, respiration was considered strictly a catabolic (breakdown) pathway. However, the exact same respiratory intermediates at which substrates enter for breakdown are also withdrawn from the respiratory pathway for the synthesis (anabolism) of those biomacromolecules. For instance, fatty acids are broken down to acetyl CoA before entering the pathway for oxidation, but when the organism needs to synthesise fatty acids, acetyl CoA is withdrawn from the respiratory pathway. Similarly, respiratory intermediates serve as the link during both the breakdown and synthesis of proteins. Because the respiratory pathway is involved in both catabolism (breakdown) and anabolism (synthesis), it is accurately termed an amphibolic pathway.

2.8 Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

During aerobic respiration, \(\mathrm{O}_2\) is consumed and \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) is released. The ratio of the volume of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) evolved to the volume of \(\mathrm{O}_2\) consumed in respiration is called the respiratory quotient (RQ) or respiratory ratio:

\[ \mathrm{RQ} = \frac{\text{Volume of }\mathrm{CO_2}\text{ evolved}}{\text{Volume of }\mathrm{O_2}\text{ consumed}} \]

The value of RQ depends directly on the chemical nature (degree of oxidation/reduction) of the respiratory substrate used:

  1. Carbohydrates (\(\mathrm{RQ} = 1.0\)): When carbohydrates are used as substrate and are completely oxidised, equal volumes of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) and \(\mathrm{O}_2\) are evolved and consumed: \[ \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2 \longrightarrow 6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O + Energy} \] \[ \mathrm{RQ} = \frac{6\,\mathrm{CO_2}}{6\,\mathrm{O_2}} = 1.0 \]
  2. Fats (\(\mathrm{RQ} < 1\), specifically \(\approx 0.7\)): Fats contain relatively less oxygen and more hydrogen/carbon than carbohydrates, so they require more \(\mathrm{O}_2\) for complete oxidation. For the fatty acid/fat tripalmitin (\(\mathrm{C_{51}H_{98}O_6}\)): \[ \mathrm{2\,(C_{51}H_{98}O_6) + 145\,O_2 \longrightarrow 102\,CO_2 + 98\,H_2O + Energy} \] \[ \mathrm{RQ} = \frac{102\,\mathrm{CO_2}}{145\,\mathrm{O_2}} = 0.7 \]
  3. Proteins (\(\mathrm{RQ} \approx 0.9\)): When proteins are used as respiratory substrates, the RQ is about \(0.9\) (less than 1).

Important Physiological Note: In living organisms, respiratory substrates are often more than one; pure proteins or pure fats are never used as respiratory substrates.

4. Step-by-Step Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Stoichiometric ATP & Proton Calculation in Mitochondria

Problem: Two molecules of pyruvic acid enter the mitochondrial matrix and undergo complete aerobic oxidation via the link reaction, Krebs' cycle, and the Electron Transport System. Calculate: (a) the total number of \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) and \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\) molecules generated inside the mitochondria, (b) the number of ATP molecules synthesised specifically through oxidative phosphorylation from these mitochondrial coenzymes, and (c) the total number of protons (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) that must pass through the \(\mathrm{F}_0\) channel of Complex V to synthesise this oxidative phosphorylation ATP.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Coenzymes per Pyruvate in Mitochondria: According to the summary equation for mitochondrial matrix oxidation, 1 molecule of pyruvic acid produces \(4\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\) (\(1\) in oxidative decarboxylation + \(3\) in TCA cycle) and \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\).
    For \(2\text{ molecules of pyruvic acid}\): \[ \text{Total }\mathrm{NADH + H^+} = 2 \times 4 = 8\text{ molecules} \] \[ \text{Total }\mathrm{FADH}_2 = 2 \times 1 = 2\text{ molecules} \]
  2. ATP via Oxidative Phosphorylation (ETS): Each \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) yields \(3\text{ ATP}\) and each \(\mathrm{FADH}_2\) yields \(2\text{ ATP}\): \[ \text{ATP from ETS} = (8 \times 3) + (2 \times 2) = 24 + 4 = 28\text{ ATP} \] (Note: An additional \(2\text{ ATP/GTP}\) are formed by substrate-level phosphorylation during succinyl-CoA \(\rightarrow\) succinic acid, bringing total mitochondrial ATP to \(30\text{ ATP}\), of which \(28\text{ ATP}\) come from oxidative phosphorylation.)
  3. Proton (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) Requirement at Complex V: For each ATP produced by ATP synthase, \(4\mathrm{H}^+\) pass through \(\mathrm{F}_0\) from the intermembrane space to the matrix: \[ \text{Total }\mathrm{H^+}\text{ required} = 28\text{ ATP} \times 4\,\mathrm{H^+/ATP} = 112\,\mathrm{H^+} \]
Worked Example 2: Carbon Skeleton Tracking & Decarboxylation Steps

Problem: Trace the carbon count of intermediates starting from a single molecule of sucrose (\(12\mathrm{C}\)) hydrolysed by invertase down to the regeneration of oxaloacetic acid in the TCA cycle. How many total decarboxylation steps occur when one molecule of sucrose is completely oxidised aerobically?

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Hydrolysis of Sucrose: \(1\text{ Sucrose } (12\mathrm{C}) \xrightarrow{\text{Invertase}} 1\text{ Glucose } (6\mathrm{C}) + 1\text{ Fructose } (6\mathrm{C})\). Both monosaccharides are phosphorylated by hexokinase and enter glycolysis.
  2. Glycolysis: Each hexose (\(6\mathrm{C}\)) splits into two triose phosphates (\(3\mathrm{C}\): PGAL and DHAP) and yields \(2\text{ Pyruvate } (3\mathrm{C})\). Thus, \(1\text{ sucrose}\) yields \(4\text{ Pyruvate } (3\mathrm{C})\) molecules. Zero decarboxylation steps occur in glycolysis.
  3. Mitochondrial Decarboxylations per Pyruvate (\(3\mathrm{C}\)):
    • Decarboxylation 1 (Link Reaction): Pyruvate (\(3\mathrm{C}\)) \(\rightarrow\) Acetyl CoA (\(2\mathrm{C}\)) + \(1\,\mathrm{CO}_2\).
    • Condensation: Acetyl CoA (\(2\mathrm{C}\)) + OAA (\(4\mathrm{C}\)) \(\rightarrow\) Citric acid (\(6\mathrm{C}\)) \(\rightarrow\) Isocitrate (\(6\mathrm{C}\)).
    • Decarboxylation 2 (TCA Step): Isocitrate (\(6\mathrm{C}\)) \(\rightarrow\) \(\alpha\)-ketoglutaric acid (\(5\mathrm{C}\)) + \(1\,\mathrm{CO}_2\).
    • Decarboxylation 3 (TCA Step): \(\alpha\)-ketoglutaric acid (\(5\mathrm{C}\)) \(\rightarrow\) Succinyl-CoA (\(4\mathrm{C}\)) + \(1\,\mathrm{CO}_2\).
  4. Total Decarboxylation Steps for 1 Sucrose: Each pyruvate undergoes \(3\) decarboxylation reactions. Since \(1\text{ sucrose}\) produces \(4\text{ pyruvates}\), total decarboxylations \(= 4 \times 3 = \mathbf{12\text{ decarboxylation steps}}\) (releasing \(12\,\mathrm{CO}_2\) molecules).
Worked Example 3: Respiratory Quotient (RQ) Analysis

Problem: During an experiment on germinating seeds, two seed lots were studied. Seed Lot A evolved \(30\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) while consuming \(30\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O}_2\). Seed Lot B evolved \(51\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) while consuming \(72.5\,\mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{O}_2\). Calculate the RQ of both seed lots and identify the predominant respiratory substrate in each.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • For Seed Lot A: \[ \mathrm{RQ_A} = \frac{\text{Volume of }\mathrm{CO_2}\text{ evolved}}{\text{Volume of }\mathrm{O_2}\text{ consumed}} = \frac{30\,\mathrm{mL}}{30\,\mathrm{mL}} = 1.0 \] An \(\mathrm{RQ}\) of \(1.0\) indicates that carbohydrates (e.g., starch/glucose) are being completely oxidised.
  • For Seed Lot B: \[ \mathrm{RQ_B} = \frac{51\,\mathrm{mL}}{72.5\,\mathrm{mL}} = \frac{102}{145} = 0.7 \] An \(\mathrm{RQ}\) of \(0.7\) matches the stoichiometric oxidation of fats / fatty acids (such as tripalmitin).

Practice Question 1 — Single-Choice MCQ (Glycolysis)

During the conversion of one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvic acid via the EMP pathway, at which of the following pairs of reactions does substrate-level phosphorylation (direct synthesis of ATP) take place?

  • (1) Glucose to Glucose-6-phosphate and Fructose-6-phosphate to Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate
  • (2) 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL) to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA) and BPGA to 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA)
  • (3) 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA) to 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA) and Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to Pyruvic acid
  • (4) 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA) to 2-phosphoglycerate and 2-phosphoglycerate to Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)
Correct Answer: (3)

Worked Explanation: In glycolysis, ATP is utilised at two steps (Glucose \(\rightarrow\) Glucose-6-phosphate and Fructose-6-phosphate \(\rightarrow\) Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate). Direct synthesis of ATP (substrate-level phosphorylation) occurs at two subsequent energy-yielding steps: first, when 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA) is converted to 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA), and second, when phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) is converted to pyruvic acid.

Practice Question 2 — Single-Choice MCQ (Gaseous Exchange in Plants)

Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding why plants can manage gaseous exchange without specialised respiratory organs?

  • (1) Each plant part takes care of its own gas-exchange needs, and there is very little transport of gases from one plant part to another.
  • (2) Roots, stems, and leaves respire at rates far higher than animals do, requiring rapid bulk flow of gases through vascular bundles.
  • (3) In thick, woody stems, living cells are organised in thin layers inside and beneath the bark, while cells in the interior are dead and provide mechanical support.
  • (4) Loose packing of parenchyma cells in leaves, stems, and roots provides an interconnected network of air spaces.
Correct Answer: (2)

Worked Explanation: Plants do not present great demands for gas exchange because roots, stems, and leaves respire at rates far lower than animals do, and there is very little transport of gases from one plant part to another. Hence statement (2) is incorrect.

Practice Question 3 — Single-Choice MCQ (Fermentation)

When yeast carries out incomplete oxidation of glucose under anaerobic conditions, which of the following accurately describes the enzymes involved, the net ATP gained per glucose molecule, and the alcohol concentration at which yeasts poison themselves to death?

  • (1) Lactate dehydrogenase; net 2 ATP; about 7 per cent alcohol
  • (2) Pyruvic acid decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase; net 2 ATP; about 13 per cent alcohol
  • (3) Pyruvate dehydrogenase and alcohol dehydrogenase; net 4 ATP; about 13 per cent alcohol
  • (4) Invertase and hexokinase; net 2 ATP; about 20 per cent alcohol
Correct Answer: (2)

Worked Explanation: In alcoholic fermentation by yeast, pyruvic acid is converted to \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) and ethanol by the enzymes pyruvic acid decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase. During glycolysis, \(4\text{ ATP}\) are synthesised and \(2\text{ ATP}\) are utilised, giving a net gain of \(2\text{ ATP}\). Yeasts poison themselves to death when the concentration of alcohol reaches about \(13\text{ per cent}\) (less than \(7\%\) refers to the percentage of energy in glucose released during fermentation).

Practice Question 4 — Single-Choice MCQ (Link Reaction & Krebs' Cycle)

Correct Answer: (1)

Worked Explanation: Pyruvate is a \(3\mathrm{C}\) compound. Oxidative decarboxylation removes one \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) to yield Acetyl CoA (\(2\mathrm{C}\)). 6. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Pitfall 1 — Confusing \(7\%\) and \(13\%\) in Fermentation: Less than \(7\%\) of the energy in glucose is released in fermentation, whereas \(13\%\) is the alcohol concentration at which yeasts poison themselves to death. Never swap these two numbers!
  • Pitfall 2 — Counting \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) in Lactic Acid vs. Alcoholic Fermentation: \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) is evolved in alcoholic fermentation (pyruvate \(3\mathrm{C} \rightarrow\) ethanol \(2\mathrm{C} + \mathrm{CO}_2\)), but no \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) is evolved in lactic acid fermentation (pyruvate \(3\mathrm{C} \rightarrow\) lactic acid \(3\mathrm{C}\)).
  • Pitfall 3 — Per Glucose vs. Per Pyruvate / Acetyl CoA in Krebs' Cycle: Always check whether a question asks for yields per Acetyl CoA (1 turn: \(3\,\mathrm{NADH}\), \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\), \(1\text{ GTP}\), \(2\,\mathrm{CO}_2\)), per Pyruvate including the link reaction (\(4\,\mathrm{NADH}\), \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\), \(1\text{ GTP}\), \(3\,\mathrm{CO}_2\)), or per Glucose (\(2\times\) pyruvate yields).
  • Pitfall 4 — Location of Cytochrome \(c\) vs. Ubiquinone: Ubiquinone (UQ) is a mobile carrier located within the inner mitochondrial membrane, whereas Cytochrome \(c\) is a mobile protein attached to the outer surface of the inner membrane (facing the intermembrane space).
  • Pitfall 5 — \(\mathrm{F}_0\) vs. \(\mathrm{F}_1\) Orientation: \(\mathrm{F}_0\) is the integral transmembrane proton channel; \(\mathrm{F}_1\) is the peripheral headpiece protruding into the mitochondrial matrix where ATP is synthesised.

9-Point Final Revision Recap

  1. Cellular Respiration: Enzymatic oxidation of \(\mathrm{C{-}C}\) bonds of respiratory substrates (mainly glucose) in cytoplasm and mitochondria to trap energy as ATP.
  2. Plant Gas Exchange: Occurs via diffusion through stomata and lenticels; aided by low respiratory rates, autonomous organ-level gas exchange, surface-proximate living cells, and interconnected parenchymatous air spaces.
  3. Glycolysis (EMP Pathway): Universal 10-step cytoplasmic pathway converting \(1\text{ Glucose } (6\mathrm{C}) \rightarrow 2\text{ Pyruvic acid } (3\mathrm{C})\). Consumes \(2\text{ ATP}\), directly synthesises \(4\text{ ATP}\) (net \(2\text{ ATP}\)), and generates \(2\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\).
  4. Fermentation: Anaerobic incomplete oxidation in cytoplasm; re-oxidises \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) to \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) slowly; yields only \(2\text{ ATP}\) net per glucose.
  5. Link Reaction: In mitochondrial matrix; \(\mathrm{Pyruvate + CoA + NAD^+} \xrightarrow{\text{Pyruvate dehydrogenase, }\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}} \mathrm{Acetyl\ CoA + CO_2 + NADH + H^+}\).
  6. Krebs' (TCA) Cycle: In mitochondrial matrix; Acetyl CoA (\(2\mathrm{C}\)) + OAA (\(4\mathrm{C}\)) + \(\mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{Citrate synthase}}\) Citric acid (\(6\mathrm{C}\)). Yields \(3\,(\mathrm{NADH + H^+})\), \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2\), and \(1\text{ GTP (ATP)}\) per turn via substrate-level phosphorylation (Succinyl-CoA \(\rightarrow\) Succinic acid).
  7. Electron Transport System (ETS): On inner mitochondrial membrane (Complexes I–IV). \(\mathrm{O}_2\) is the final hydrogen/electron acceptor, forming water. \(1\,\mathrm{NADH} \rightarrow 3\text{ ATP}\); \(1\,\mathrm{FADH}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{ ATP}\). Complex V (\(\mathrm{F_0\text{–}F_1}\)) synthesises \(1\text{ ATP}\) per \(4\mathrm{H}^+\) moving from intermembrane space to matrix.
  8. Amphibolic Pathway: Respiratory pathway serves both catabolism and anabolism; fatty acids enter/exit at Acetyl CoA, glycerol at PGAL, and deaminated amino acids at pyruvate, Acetyl CoA, or Krebs' cycle intermediates.
  9. Respiratory Quotient (\(\mathrm{RQ} = \mathrm{CO_2\text{ evolved} / O_2\text{ consumed}}\)): Carbohydrates \(= 1.0\); Fats (tripalmitin) \(= 0.7\); Proteins \(\approx 0.9\). Pure proteins or fats are never used alone as respiratory substrates.
