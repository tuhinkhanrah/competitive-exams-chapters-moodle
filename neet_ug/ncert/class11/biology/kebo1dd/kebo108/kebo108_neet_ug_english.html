NEET UG Biology • Cell Structure and Functions

Cell: The Unit of Life

A complete, exam-oriented tutorial covering cellular organization, prokaryotic and eukaryotic architecture, organelle coordination, and NEET UG practice assessments.

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Overview

Living organisms exhibit extraordinary structural diversity, yet all life shares a unified foundation: cellular organization. Studying physiological and behavioral phenomena through physics and chemistry in cell-free systems is known as reductionist biology, an approach that explains gross biological processes in molecular terms.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Explain the historical development and modern tenets of Cell Theory, along with the structural basis of independent living.
  • Distinguish between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, and between plant and animal eukaryotic cells.
  • Describe the bacterial cell envelope, membrane modifications (mesosomes, chromatophores), surface appendages, plasmids, and 70S ribosomes.
  • Analyze the Fluid Mosaic Model of the plasma membrane and differentiate passive, facilitated, and active transport mechanisms.
  • Trace the coordinated functions of the endomembrane system (ER, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and vacuoles) and contrast them with semi-autonomous organelles (mitochondria and plastids).
  • Compare the 9+2 axonemal arrangement of eukaryotic cilia/flagella with the 9+0 cartwheel architecture of centrioles.
  • Classify eukaryotic chromosomes based on centromere position and identify key nuclear structures such as nuclear pores, chromatin, and nucleoli.

2. Foundations of Cellularity and Cell Theory

What Makes a Cell the Basic Unit of Life?

A unicellular organism can exist independently and perform every essential function of life—metabolism, growth, response, and reproduction. Any structure less complete than an intact cell cannot sustain independent living. Therefore, the cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms.

Historical Milestones in Cell Biology

  • Antonie van Leeuwenhoek: First observed and described a live cell under a microscope.
  • Robert Brown (1831): Discovered and described the nucleus as a distinct cellular organelle.
  • Matthias Schleiden (1838): A German botanist who examined numerous plant species and concluded that all plants are composed of different kinds of cells forming plant tissues.
  • Theodore Schwann (1839): A German zoologist who studied animal cells and identified a thin outer boundary (now called the plasma membrane). Examining plant tissues as well, he noted that a cell wall is a unique feature of plant cells and proposed that animals and plants consist of cells and cell products.
  • Rudolf Virchow (1855): Resolved the major gap in Schleiden and Schwann's formulation—how new cells arise—by demonstrating that cells divide and new cells form from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula-e cellula).
Modern Cell Theory (Final Form)
  1. All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
  2. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Cell Size, Shape, and Functional Specialization

Cells vary dramatically in dimension and geometry depending on their physiological roles:

  • Smallest cells: Mycoplasma (about \(0.3\,\mathrm{µm}\) in length; related PPLO are around \(0.1\,\mathrm{µm}\)).
  • Typical bacteria: \(1\text{–}2\,\mathrm{µm}\) (some reach \(3\text{–}5\,\mathrm{µm}\)).
  • Human red blood cells (RBCs): Round and biconcave, approximately \(7.0\,\mathrm{µm}\) in diameter.
  • Typical eukaryotic cells: \(10\text{–}20\,\mathrm{µm}\).
  • Largest isolated single cell: Egg of an ostrich.
  • Specialized shapes: Nerve cells are long and branched for signal transmission; white blood cells are amoeboid; columnar epithelial cells are long and narrow; tracheids are elongated; mesophyll cells are round and oval.

3. Prokaryotic Cell Architecture

Prokaryotes—represented by bacteria, cyanobacteria (blue-green algae), Mycoplasma, and PPLO (Pleuro Pneumonia Like Organisms)—lack a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. They are generally smaller and multiply faster than eukaryotic cells. Bacteria occur in four basic shapes: bacillus (rod-like), coccus (spherical), vibrio (comma-shaped), and spirillum (spiral).

Cell Envelope and Its Modifications

Most bacteria possess a chemically complex, tightly bound three-layered cell envelope acting as a single protective unit:

  1. Glycocalyx (Outermost): Varies in thickness and composition—either a loose sheath (slime layer) or a thick, tough coat (capsule).
  2. Cell Wall (Middle): Determines cell shape and provides strong mechanical support to prevent bursting or collapsing. (Absent in Mycoplasma.)
  3. Plasma Membrane (Innermost): Selectively permeable and structurally similar to the eukaryotic plasma membrane.

Based on cell envelope differences and response to Gram staining, bacteria are classified as Gram-positive (retain the Gram stain) or Gram-negative (do not retain the stain).

Membranous Extensions and Surface Structures

  • Mesosomes: Specialized infoldings of the plasma membrane into the cell in the form of vesicles, tubules, and lamellae. They assist in cell wall formation, DNA replication and distribution to daughter cells, respiration, secretion, and increasing membrane surface area and enzymatic content.
  • Chromatophores: Pigment-bearing membranous extensions into the cytoplasm found in photosynthetic prokaryotes such as cyanobacteria.
  • Flagella: Thin filamentous extensions for motility, composed of three parts—filament (longest outer portion), hook, and basal body.
  • Pili and Fimbriae: Non-motile surface structures. Pili are elongated tubular protein structures; fimbriae are small bristle-like fibres that help bacteria attach to rocks in streams or to host tissues.

Genetic Material, Ribosomes, and Inclusion Bodies

  • Genomic DNA and Plasmids: Genetic material is naked (no nuclear envelope) and consists of a single circular chromosome. Many bacteria also carry small circular extrachromosomal DNA molecules called plasmids, which confer unique phenotypic traits such as antibiotic resistance and serve as tools to monitor bacterial transformation.
  • Ribosomes: Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S (\(15\,\mathrm{nm} \times 20\,\mathrm{nm}\)), composed of 50S and 30S subunits, and are associated with the plasma membrane. Multiple ribosomes translating a single mRNA form a polyribosome (polysome).
  • Inclusion Bodies: Non-membrane-bound reserve materials lying free in the cytoplasm, including phosphate granules, cyanophycean granules, and glycogen granules. Gas vacuoles occur in blue-green, purple, and green photosynthetic bacteria.

4. Eukaryotic Cells, Plasma Membrane, and Cell Wall

Eukaryotes (protists, fungi, plants, and animals) exhibit extensive compartmentalization of the cytoplasm via membrane-bound organelles, an organized nucleus with a nuclear envelope, chromosomes, and complex cytoskeletal and locomotory structures. Plant cells differ from animal cells by possessing cell walls, plastids, and a large central vacuole, whereas animal cells contain centrioles (absent in almost all plant cells).

Plasma Membrane: The Fluid Mosaic Model

Chemical analyses of human erythrocyte (RBC) membranes and electron microscopy in the 1950s revealed that membranes consist primarily of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates. In human RBCs, the membrane contains approximately 52% protein and 40% lipid. The major lipids are phospholipids arranged in a bilayer with hydrophilic polar heads facing outward and hydrophobic saturated hydrocarbon tails oriented inward, shielded from water. Cholesterol is also embedded in the bilayer.

In 1972, Singer and Nicolson proposed the Fluid Mosaic Model. According to this model, the quasi-fluid nature of the lipid bilayer permits lateral movement of proteins within the membrane; this mobility is measured as membrane fluidity. Fluidity is vital for cell growth, formation of intercellular junctions, secretion, endocytosis, and cell division.

Based on ease of extraction, membrane proteins are classified as:

  • Peripheral proteins: Lie on the outer or inner surface of the membrane and are easily extracted.
  • Integral proteins: Partially or completely buried within the lipid bilayer.

Transport Across the Plasma Membrane

The plasma membrane is selectively permeable. Solutes cross it by three principal mechanisms:

  • Passive Transport (No ATP required): Neutral solutes move down their concentration gradient (high to low) via simple diffusion. Water moves across the membrane down its concentration gradient by osmosis.
  • Facilitated Diffusion (No ATP required): Polar molecules cannot cross the nonpolar lipid interior directly and require specific membrane carrier proteins to move down their concentration gradient.
  • Active Transport (ATP-dependent): Ions or molecules are pumped against their concentration gradient (low to high) using cellular energy (ATP), as exemplified by the \(\mathrm{Na}^+/\mathrm{K}^+\) pump.

Cell Wall

Fungi and plants possess a non-living, rigid outer cell wall that maintains cell shape, protects against mechanical injury and infection, mediates cell-to-cell interaction, and blocks undesirable macromolecules.

  • Composition: Algal cell walls contain cellulose, galactans, mannans, and minerals like calcium carbonate. Land plant cell walls consist of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectins, and proteins.
  • Primary vs. Secondary Wall: A young plant cell has a primary wall capable of growth; as the cell matures, growth ceases and a secondary wall forms on the inner side (towards the plasma membrane).
  • Middle Lamella and Plasmodesmata: The middle lamella—composed mainly of calcium pectate—cements neighboring plant cells together. Cytoplasmic channels called plasmodesmata traverse the cell wall and middle lamella to connect adjacent cells.

5. The Endomembrane System

Although each membranous organelle has distinct structure and function, four organelles—the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and vacuoles—are grouped together as the endomembrane system because their functions are tightly coordinated. Mitochondria, chloroplasts, and peroxisomes are excluded because their functions are not coordinated with this pathway.

  • Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER): A network of tiny tubular structures dividing the intracellular space into luminal (inside ER) and extra-luminal (cytoplasmic) compartments.
    • Rough ER (RER): Bears ribosomes on its outer surface and is continuous with the outer nuclear membrane. Prominent in cells active in protein synthesis and secretion.
    • Smooth ER (SER): Lacks ribosomes; major site for lipid synthesis and, in animal cells, synthesis of lipid-like steroidal hormones.
  • Golgi Apparatus (Camillo Golgi, 1898): Consists of concentrically stacked, flat, disc-shaped sacs called cisternae (\(0.5\text{–}1.0\,\mathrm{µm}\) diameter) near the nucleus. Each stack has a convex cis (forming) face and a concave trans (maturing) face—distinct yet interconnected. Vesicles from the ER fuse with the cis face, undergo chemical modification inside the cisternae, and exit from the trans face for intracellular or extracellular delivery. It is the major site for forming glycoproteins and glycolipids.
  • Lysosomes: Single-membrane-bound vesicles packaged by the Golgi apparatus, enriched with hydrolytic enzymes (hydrolases—lipases, proteases, carbohydrases) that are optimally active at acidic pH and capable of digesting carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids.
  • Vacuoles: Cytoplasmic spaces enclosed by a single membrane called the tonoplast, containing water, sap, and excretory/non-useful materials. In mature plant cells, vacuoles occupy up to 90% of cell volume; the tonoplast actively transports ions against concentration gradients into the vacuole. In Amoeba, the contractile vacuole performs osmoregulation and excretion, while protists form food vacuoles by engulfing food particles.

6. Semi-Autonomous Organelles: Mitochondria and Plastids

Mitochondria and chloroplasts are double-membrane-bound organelles that retain their own circular DNA, RNA, and 70S ribosomes, enabling them to synthesize some of their own proteins and divide by fission.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Longitudinal section of a mitochondrion (left) showing cristae and matrix, compared with a chloroplast (right) showing grana, thylakoids, stroma lamellae, and stroma.

Mitochondria

  • Dimensions: Typically sausage-shaped or cylindrical, \(0.2\text{–}1.0\,\mathrm{µm}\) in diameter (average \(0.5\,\mathrm{µm}\)) and \(1.0\text{–}4.1\,\mathrm{µm}\) in length.
  • Compartments: The smooth outer membrane and folded inner membrane divide the lumen into an outer compartment and an inner compartment filled with a dense homogeneous matrix.
  • Cristae: Infoldings of the inner membrane into the matrix that greatly increase surface area for enzymatic reactions.
  • Function: Sites of aerobic respiration and oxidative phosphorylation, generating ATP ("powerhouses" of the cell).

Plastids

Found in all plant cells and euglenoids, plastids are classified by pigment content:

  • Chloroplasts: Contain chlorophyll and carotenoid pigments to trap light energy for photosynthesis. Lens-shaped, oval, spherical, discoid, or ribbon-like (\(5\text{–}10\,\mathrm{µm}\) long, \(2\text{–}4\,\mathrm{µm}\) wide). Number varies from \(1\) per cell in Chlamydomonas to \(20\text{–}40\) per leaf mesophyll cell.
  • Chromoplasts: Contain fat-soluble carotenoids (carotene, xanthophylls), imparting yellow, orange, or red colors to plant parts.
  • Leucoplasts: Colorless plastids storing nutrients—amyloplasts (carbohydrates/starch, e.g., potato), elaioplasts (oils and fats), and aleuroplasts (proteins).

Inside a chloroplast, the inner membrane (less permeable than the outer) encloses the stroma, which houses enzymes for carbohydrate and protein synthesis, double-stranded circular DNA, and 70S ribosomes. Flattened membranous sacs called thylakoids (housing chlorophyll in their membranes and enclosing a lumen) stack like coins into grana, interconnected by flat stroma lamellae.

7. Ribosomes, Cytoskeleton, Cilia/Flagella, and Centrosome

Ribosomes

First observed by George Palade (1953), ribosomes are non-membrane-bound granular particles made of RNA and proteins. Eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S (subunits: 60S + 40S), while prokaryotic, mitochondrial, and chloroplast ribosomes are 70S (subunits: 50S + 30S). Here, S (Svedberg's unit) is the sedimentation coefficient—an indirect measure of density and size.

Cytoskeleton

An elaborate cytoplasmic network of proteinaceous filaments—microtubules, microfilaments, and intermediate filaments—providing mechanical support, motility, and maintenance of cell shape.

Cilia, Flagella, and Centrosome/Centrioles

Cilia (short, oar-like) and flagella (longer, cell locomotion) are plasma-membrane-covered outgrowths emerging from basal bodies. Unlike bacterial flagella, eukaryotic cilia and flagella share a conserved microtubule core called the axoneme.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Cross-sectional comparison of a eukaryotic cilium/flagellum axoneme (9+2 array: 9 peripheral doublets + 2 central singlets) and a centriole (9+0 cartwheel: 9 peripheral tubulin triplets + central proteinaceous hub).

  • Axoneme (9+2 Array): Nine peripheral microtubule doublets interconnected by linkers, plus one pair of central singlet microtubules joined by a bridge and enclosed by a central sheath. Nine radial spokes connect the central sheath to one tubule of each peripheral doublet.
  • Centrosome and Centrioles (9+0 Cartwheel): A centrosome typically houses two cylindrical centrioles lying perpendicular to each other, surrounded by amorphous pericentriolar material. Each centriole has nine evenly spaced peripheral fibrils of tubulin, where each fibril is a triplet. The central proximal region forms a proteinaceous hub linked to the peripheral triplets by radial spokes. Centrioles form basal bodies of cilia/flagella and spindle fibres during animal cell division.

8. Nucleus, Chromosomes, and Microbodies

Interphase Nucleus

  • Nuclear Envelope: Two parallel membranes separated by a \(10\text{–}50\,\mathrm{nm}\) perinuclear space. The outer membrane is continuous with the ER and bears ribosomes. Minute nuclear pores (formed by fusion of the two membranes) allow bidirectional movement of RNA and proteins between nucleoplasm and cytoplasm.
  • Nucleoplasm and Nucleolus: The nuclear matrix (nucleoplasm) contains chromatin and one or more spherical nucleoli. A nucleolus is not membrane-bound; its contents are continuous with the nucleoplasm, and it is the site of active ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis. Cells actively synthesizing proteins have larger, more numerous nucleoli.
  • Chromatin: Named by Flemming for its staining with basic dyes, chromatin contains DNA, basic proteins called histones, non-histone proteins, and RNA. A single human cell packs roughly \(2\,\mathrm{m}\) of DNA across \(46\) (\(23\) pairs) chromosomes.
  • Enucleate Living Cells: Mature mammalian erythrocytes (RBCs) and sieve tube cells of vascular plants lack a nucleus yet remain functional.

Chromosome Classification

During cell division, chromatin condenses into structured chromosomes. Each chromosome has a primary constriction called the centromere holding two sister chromatids together, flanked by disc-shaped kinetochores. Some chromosomes also possess a non-staining secondary constriction at a constant site, producing a small terminal fragment called a satellite.

Tutorial Diagram 5: Classification of chromosomes by centromere position (metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, and telocentric), illustrating kinetochores and a satellite formed at a secondary constriction.

Microbodies

Microbodies are membrane-bound minute vesicles containing diverse enzymes and are present in both plant and animal cells.

9. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Organelle Compartmentalization & Ribosome Types

Scenario (Hypothetical): A student isolates two distinct cell fractions from a spinach leaf sample and a bacterial culture. Sample A contains 80S ribosomes in the cytosol and 70S ribosomes inside two double-membrane-bound organelles. Sample B contains only 70S ribosomes associated with the plasma membrane and lacks a nuclear envelope.

Question: Identify the cell types of Sample A and Sample B, and explain why the two organelles in Sample A possess 70S ribosomes rather than 80S ribosomes.

Solution:

  1. Sample A is a eukaryotic plant cell (mesophyll cell), because it contains 80S cytoplasmic ribosomes along with two double-membrane-bound organelles—mitochondria and chloroplasts—both of which contain their own 70S (50S + 30S) ribosomes and circular DNA.
  2. Sample B is a prokaryotic cell (bacterium), characterized by 70S ribosomes associated with the plasma membrane and the absence of a nuclear envelope or membrane-bound organelles.
Worked Example 2: Membrane Transport Logic

Scenario (Hypothetical): Assume the concentration of a polar amino acid \(X\) inside an animal cell is \(12\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\), while its extracellular concentration is \(2\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\). Meanwhile, a neutral lipid-soluble molecule \(Y\) is at \(8\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\) outside the cell and \(1\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\) inside.

Question: Predict the transport mechanisms required to move \(X\) into the cell and \(Y\) into the cell.

Solution:

  • Molecule \(X\) (polar, moving from \(2\) to \(12\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\)): Movement is against the concentration gradient (low to high). Therefore, it requires active transport mediated by a membrane protein with expenditure of ATP. (Note: even if \(X\) moved down its gradient, its polarity would prevent simple diffusion through the nonpolar lipid core and would require facilitated diffusion.)
  • Molecule \(Y\) (neutral/nonpolar, moving from \(8\) to \(1\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}\)): Movement is along the concentration gradient (high to low) and the solute is neutral/nonpolar, so it crosses by simple diffusion (passive transport) without ATP.

10. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All items below are original practice questions designed for NEET UG preparation.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding the historical development of Cell Theory is incorrect?

  • (A) Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was the first to observe and describe a live cell.
  • (B) Theodore Schwann concluded that the presence of a cell wall is a unique character of plant cells.
  • (C) Schleiden and Schwann's original theory explained that new cells arise from pre-existing cells.
  • (D) Robert Brown discovered the nucleus in 1831.
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Schleiden (1838) and Schwann (1839) formulated the cell theory stating that all organisms are composed of cells and cell products, but their theory did not explain how new cells were formed. It was Rudolf Virchow (1855) who first explained that cells divide and new cells arise from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula-e cellula).

Practice Question 2 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the following two statements carefully and choose the correct option:

Statement I: All prokaryotic cells possess a rigid cell wall surrounding the plasma membrane.

Statement II: Plasmid DNA in bacteria is smaller than genomic DNA and confers unique phenotypic traits such as antibiotic resistance.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: Statement I is incorrect because Mycoplasma is a prokaryote that completely lacks a cell wall. Statement II is correct: plasmids are small circular extrachromosomal DNA molecules that confer phenotypic characters like antibiotic resistance.

Practice Question 3 Single-Correct MCQ

In a prokaryotic cell, membranous infoldings called mesosomes occur in the form of vesicles, tubules, and lamellae. Which of the following is not a function of mesosomes?

  • (A) Cell wall formation
  • (B) DNA replication and distribution to daughter cells
  • (C) Increasing plasma membrane surface area and enzymatic content
  • (D) Oxygenic photosynthesis using chlorophyll-containing thylakoids
Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: Mesosomes assist in cell wall formation, DNA replication/distribution, respiration, secretion, and increasing membrane surface area. Pigment-bearing membranous extensions in cyanobacteria are called chromatophores, not mesosomes, and thylakoid-stacked chloroplasts are eukaryotic organelles.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

According to the Fluid Mosaic Model of the plasma membrane, the ability of proteins to move laterally within the lipid bilayer depends primarily on:

  • (A) Covalent bonding of peripheral proteins to the cell wall

Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Singer and Nicolson (1972) proposed that the quasi-fluid nature of lipids enables lateral movement of proteins within the overall bilayer, a property measured as membrane fluidity.

11. Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Svedberg Units Are Not Additive: \(50\mathrm{S} + 30\mathrm{S} = 70\mathrm{S}\) (not \(80\mathrm{S}\)), and \(60\mathrm{S} + 40\mathrm{S} = 80\mathrm{S}\) (not \(100\mathrm{S}\)). Svedberg's unit reflects sedimentation rate (density + shape + size), not simple mass addition.
  • Non-Membrane-Bound Structures: Remember all key structures lacking a lipid membrane: ribosomes (all cells), centrosome/centrioles (animal cells), nucleolus (inside nucleus), and prokaryotic inclusion bodies.
  • Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Flagella: Both mediate motility, but bacterial flagella are structurally different (filament, hook, basal body) and do not have the eukaryotic 9+2 microtubule axoneme.
  • Middle Lamella Composition: Middle lamella is primarily calcium pectate (not cellulose), gluing neighboring plant cells together.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Cell Theory: Schleiden (1838), Schwann (1839), Virchow (1855, Omnis cellula-e cellula).
  • RBC membrane ratio: ~52% protein, ~40% lipid; Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicolson, 1972).
  • Endomembrane system: ER + Golgi + Lysosomes + Vacuoles (excludes mitochondria, chloroplasts, peroxisomes).
  • Mitochondria: cristae (inner membrane), matrix with 70S ribosomes & circular DNA, divides by fission.
  • Chloroplasts: grana (light reaction site), stroma (dark reaction/carbohydrate synthesis enzymes, 70S, ds circular DNA).
  • Chromosomes: Metacentric (middle), Sub-metacentric (slightly off-center), Acrocentric (near end), Telocentric (terminal).

12. Concise Final Recap

The cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of life, unified by Cell Theory. Prokaryotes lack a nuclear envelope and membrane-bound organelles, relying on a three-layered cell envelope, mesosomes, 70S ribosomes, and naked circular DNA (often with plasmids). Eukaryotes compartmentalize metabolism into coordinated endomembrane organelles (ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles), semi-autonomous energy-converting organelles (mitochondria and plastids), cytoskeletal networks (including 9+2 cilia/flagella and 9+0 centrioles), and a double-membraned nucleus housing chromatin and nucleoli. Mastering these structural–functional relationships is the key to scoring high on Cell Biology questions in NEET UG.
