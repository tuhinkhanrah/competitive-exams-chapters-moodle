NEET UG Biology • Diversity in the Living World

Biological Classification

Taxonomy Frameworks, Prokaryotes, Protists, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia, and Acellular Infectious Agents

1. Learning Objectives and Chapter Scope

Organizing Earth's vast biodiversity requires systematic criteria that reflect cellular architecture, nutrition, reproduction, and evolutionary history. For NEET UG, this topic tests precise distinctions among the five kingdoms, diagnostic features of microbial and fungal groups, exceptions to general rules, and the nature of acellular agents.

Key Learning Targets
  • Trace the shift from early morphological groupings and the Two-Kingdom system to R.H. Whittaker's Five-Kingdom framework.
  • Compare cell wall chemistry, nuclear organization, body plan, and nutrition across all five kingdoms.
  • Differentiate Archaebacteria, Eubacteria (including Cyanobacteria), and Mycoplasma within Kingdom Monera.
  • Master the five protistan groups (Chrysophytes, Dinoflagellates, Euglenoids, Slime Moulds, Protozoans) and their ecological roles.
  • Analyze fungal mycelium types, the three-step sexual cycle (plasmogamy, karyogamy, meiosis), dikaryophase, and the four fungal classes.
  • Distinguish viruses, viroids, prions, and lichens by structure, genetic material, host impact, and ecological significance.

2. Evolution of Classification Systems

2.1 Early Attempts and the Two-Kingdom Model

Human classification began with practical needs such as food, shelter, and clothing. The earliest scientific attempt came from Aristotle, who relied on easily observable morphology: he grouped plants into trees, shrubs, and herbs, and divided animals into those possessing red blood and those lacking it.

Later, Carolus Linnaeus established a Two-Kingdom system comprising Plantae and Animalia. Although simple and intuitive, this binary scheme proved inadequate because it grouped organisms strictly by presence or absence of a cell wall, overlooking fundamental biological differences:

  • Prokaryotes vs. Eukaryotes: Prokaryotic bacteria and blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) were placed alongside eukaryotic plants.
  • Unicellular vs. Multicellular: Single-celled organisms such as Chlamydomonas and multicellular forms such as Spirogyra were both placed under algae within Plantae.
  • Autotrophs vs. Heterotrophs: Non-photosynthetic, heterotrophic fungi (with chitinous walls) were grouped with photosynthetic green plants (with cellulosic walls).

2.2 R.H. Whittaker's Five-Kingdom Classification (1969)

To resolve these inconsistencies, R.H. Whittaker (1969) proposed five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia. His classification relied on five major criteria:

  1. Cell structure (prokaryotic vs. eukaryotic).
  2. Body organisation (unicellular/cellular vs. multicellular/tissue/organ/organ-system).
  3. Mode of nutrition (autotrophic—photosynthetic or chemosynthetic—vs. heterotrophic—saprophytic, parasitic, or holozoic).
  4. Reproduction (vegetative, asexual, and sexual strategies).
  5. Phylogenetic relationships (evolutionary ancestry).

By separating all prokaryotes into Monera and all unicellular eukaryotes into Protista, Whittaker's system united organisms that shared cellular organization even if earlier systems split them—for instance, Protista brought together walled photosynthetic unicells (Chlamydomonas, Chlorella) with wall-less heterotrophic unicells (Amoeba, Paramoecium). A subsequent Three-Domain (Six-Kingdom) approach further divides Monera into two distinct prokaryotic domains while keeping all four eukaryotic kingdoms in a third domain.

3. Kingdom Monera: The Prokaryotic Domain

Bacteria are the sole members of Kingdom Monera. They are the most abundant microorganisms and inhabit virtually every niche, from fertile soil to extreme environments such as hot springs, deserts, snow, and deep oceans. Despite simple cellular structure, bacteria exhibit the most extensive metabolic diversity of any group of organisms.

3.1 Bacterial Shapes and Division

Based on cell geometry, bacteria are classified into four morphological categories: Coccus (spherical), Bacillus (rod-shaped), Vibrium (comma-shaped), and Spirillum (spiral-shaped). Reproduction occurs predominantly by binary fission; under unfavourable conditions, some form resistant spores, and they can also exchange genetic material via a primitive form of DNA transfer.

3.2 Archaebacteria

Archaebacteria thrive in extreme habitats because they possess a distinct cell wall structure compared to other bacteria. Three major groups are recognized:

  • Halophiles: Inhabit extreme salty areas.
  • Thermoacidophiles: Survive in hot, acidic springs.
  • Methanogens: Live in marshy areas and in the gut of ruminant animals (such as cows and buffaloes), where they produce methane (biogas) from animal dung.

3.3 Eubacteria (True Bacteria) and Mycoplasma

Eubacteria are characterised by a rigid cell wall and, when motile, a flagellum. They are subdivided by their mode of nutrition:

  • Photosynthetic Autotrophs (Cyanobacteria / Blue-Green Algae): Possess chlorophyll a (similar to green plants). They may be unicellular, colonial, or filamentous, living in freshwater, marine, or terrestrial habitats, often enclosed in a gelatinous (mucilaginous) sheath. They frequently form blooms in polluted water bodies. Certain filamentous forms such as Nostoc and Anabaena fix atmospheric nitrogen inside specialised thick-walled cells called heterocysts.
  • Chemosynthetic Autotrophs: Oxidise inorganic substances such as nitrates, nitrites, and ammonia, using the released chemical energy to synthesise ATP. They play a vital ecological role in recycling nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and sulphur.
  • Heterotrophic Bacteria: The most abundant group in nature, functioning primarily as decomposers. Economically, they aid in curd production from milk, antibiotic synthesis, and nitrogen fixation in legume roots. Pathogenic strains cause human and crop diseases including cholera, typhoid, tetanus, and citrus canker.
High-Yield Exception: Mycoplasma

Mycoplasma are the smallest known living cells. They completely lack a cell wall, can survive without oxygen, and are pathogenic in both plants and animals. Because they lack a cell wall, antibiotics that target cell-wall synthesis are ineffective against them.

4. Kingdom Protista: Unicellular Eukaryotes

All single-celled eukaryotes belong to Kingdom Protista, though its boundaries remain fluid because some photosynthetic protists overlap with plant-like traits. Protists are primarily aquatic and serve as an evolutionary link connecting plants, animals, and fungi. Their cells contain a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles; some bear cilia or flagella. Reproduction occurs both asexually and sexually (via cell fusion and zygote formation).

5. Kingdom Fungi: Absorptive Heterotrophs

Fungi are cosmopolitan eukaryotic heterotrophs that thrive in warm, humid habitats (which is why refrigeration slows food spoilage). Except for unicellular yeasts, fungi are filamentous: slender thread-like hyphae intertwine to form a mycelium. Hyphae may be coenocytic (continuous multinucleated tubes lacking cross-walls) or septate (divided by cross-walls). Their cell walls consist of chitin and polysaccharides.

Nutritionally, fungi act as saprophytes (absorbing soluble organic matter from dead substrates), parasites (e.g., Puccinia causing wheat rust, or white spots on mustard leaves), or symbionts (forming lichens with algae and mycorrhiza with roots of higher plants).

5.1 Fungal Reproduction and Dikaryophase

Fungi reproduce vegetatively (fragmentation, fission, budding), asexually (conidia, sporangiospores, or zoospores), and sexually (oospores, ascospores, or basidiospores produced in fruiting bodies). Sexual reproduction proceeds in three sequential steps:

  1. Plasmogamy: Fusion of protoplasms between two compatible motile or non-motile gametes/hyphae.
  2. Karyogamy: Fusion of the two haploid nuclei to form a diploid (\(2n\)) zygote nucleus.
  3. Meiosis: Reduction division in the zygote yielding haploid (\(n\)) spores.

In lower fungi, plasmogamy is immediately followed by karyogamy (\(n + n \rightarrow 2n\)). However, in Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes, an intervening dikaryotic stage (\(n + n\), two unfused haploid nuclei per cell) persists; this cell condition is a dikaryon and the phase is the dikaryophase.

5.2 The Four Classes of Kingdom Fungi

Fungi are divided into four classes based on mycelium morphology, spore formation mode, and fruiting body architecture.

6. Brief Overview of Kingdoms Plantae and Animalia

  • Kingdom Plantae: Eukaryotic, chlorophyll-containing organisms with cellulosic cell walls and prominent chloroplasts (comprising algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms, and angiosperms). While overwhelmingly autotrophic, rare members are partially heterotrophic: insectivorous plants (Bladderwort, Venus fly trap) and parasitic plants (Cuscuta). Their life cycle displays alternation of generations between a diploid sporophyte and a haploid gametophyte.
  • Kingdom Animalia: Multicellular, heterotrophic eukaryotes lacking cell walls. Nutrition is holozoic (ingestion followed by internal cavity digestion), with food reserves stored as glycogen or fat. Animals follow a definite growth pattern, possess sensory and neuromotor mechanisms in higher forms, mostly exhibit locomotion, and reproduce sexually via copulation and embryological development.

7. Acellular Agents and Lichens

Whittaker's Five-Kingdom classification omits acellular infectious agents (viruses, viroids, prions) because they lack cellular organisation, as well as composite symbiotic organisms (lichens).

7.1 Viruses

Viruses ("venom" or poisonous fluid) exist as inert crystalline structures outside a host cell, but once inside, they hijack the host's cellular machinery to replicate, often killing the host. Thus, they are obligate intracellular parasites bridging living and non-living states.

  • Dmitri Ivanowsky (1892): Identified microbes causing tobacco mosaic disease that were smaller than bacteria and passed through bacteria-proof filters.
  • M.W. Beijerinck (1898): Showed that extract from infected tobacco plants could infect healthy plants, coining the term Contagium vivum fluidum (infectious living fluid).
  • W.M. Stanley (1935): Demonstrated that viruses could be crystallised and that crystals consist largely of proteins.

Structurally, a virus is a nucleoprotein: an infectious nucleic acid core (either RNA or DNA, never both) enclosed by a protective protein coat called a capsid, built from small subunits called capsomeres arranged in helical or polyhedral geometry.

  • Plant viruses: Generally contain single-stranded RNA (ssRNA); cause mosaic formation, leaf rolling/curling, yellowing, vein clearing, and dwarfing.
  • Animal viruses: Contain ssRNA, dsRNA, or dsDNA; cause mumps, smallpox, herpes, influenza, and AIDS.
  • Bacteriophages (viruses infecting bacteria): Usually contain double-stranded DNA (dsDNA).

7.2 Viroids, Prions, and Lichens

  • Viroids: Discovered by T.O. Diener (1971) as infectious agents smaller than viruses causing potato spindle tuber disease. A viroid consists solely of free, low-molecular-weight RNA and lacks a protein coat.
  • Prions: Infectious agents similar in size to viruses but composed exclusively of abnormally folded proteins (no nucleic acid). They cause fatal neurological disorders: Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) ("mad cow disease") in cattle and its human variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob Disease (CJD).
  • Lichens: Mutualistic associations between an algal partner (phycobiont, autotrophic, synthesises food) and a fungal partner (mycobiont, heterotrophic, provides shelter and absorbs water and mineral nutrients). Lichens do not grow in polluted areas and therefore serve as reliable pollution indicators.

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Diagnostic Identification of a Newly Isolated Microbe

Scenario (Hypothetical): A researcher isolates a unicellular aquatic organism from a stagnant freshwater pond. Microscopy reveals a membrane-bound nucleus, chloroplasts containing pigments identical to higher plants, two flagella of unequal length, and no rigid cell wall; instead, a flexible proteinaceous outer layer is observed. In prolonged darkness, the organism preys on smaller microbes. Assign this organism to its correct kingdom and group.

Solution:

  1. Because the organism is unicellular and eukaryotic (possesses a membrane-bound nucleus), it belongs to Kingdom Protista rather than Monera.
  2. The absence of a cell wall combined with a flexible protein-rich pellicle, two unequal flagella, freshwater stagnant habitat, and mixotrophic nutrition (photosynthetic in light, predaceous heterotroph in darkness) uniquely matches Euglenoids (e.g., Euglena).
Worked Example 2: Ploidy Tracking Through the Fungal Sexual Cycle

Scenario (Hypothetical): Assume a species of Ascomycetes has a haploid vegetative mycelium with chromosome number \(n = 7\). Determine the chromosome count per cell/nucleus during: (a) the dikaryophase, (b) the diploid zygote nucleus inside the ascus, and (c) each resulting ascospore.

Solution:

  1. Dikaryophase: After plasmogamy between two compatible haploid hyphae, the nuclei do not fuse immediately. Each dikaryotic cell contains two separate haploid nuclei: \(n + n = 7 + 7\) chromosomes (two distinct haploid genomes per cell).
  2. Diploid Zygote: Karyogamy fuses the two nuclei inside the ascus, yielding a single diploid nucleus with \(2n = 2 \times 7 = 14\) chromosomes.
  3. Ascospores: Meiosis of the \(2n = 14\) nucleus restores the haploid state, giving \(n = 7\) chromosomes in each ascospore.
Worked Example 3: Biochemical Differentiation of Acellular Pathogens

Scenario (Hypothetical): Three infectious filtrates (X, Y, Z) pass through a \(0.2\,\mathrm{µm}\) bacteria-proof filter. Treatment with protease (protein-digesting enzyme) destroys the infectivity of X and Z, whereas Y remains fully infectious. Treatment with RNase destroys the infectivity of Y, while Z remains unaffected. Identify X, Y, and Z as a virus, viroid, or prion.

Solution:

  • Agent Y resists protease (indicating no essential protein coat) and is inactivated by RNase (indicating exposed RNA). Thus, Y is a viroid (free infectious RNA).
  • Agent Z is inactivated by protease (essential protein) but unaffected by RNase (no RNA). Given its protein-only nature, Z is a prion (abnormally folded infectious protein).
  • Agent X requires its protein capsid to protect/deliver its nucleic acid and is inactivated by protease; therefore, X is a virus.

9. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All items below are original practice questions modelled on the NEET UG examination pattern.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following pairs of organisms were grouped together under Plantae in Linnaeus's Two-Kingdom system solely because both possessed a cell wall, despite differing in cellular organization or nutrition?

  1. (A) Amoeba and Paramoecium
  2. (B) Bacteria and Fungi
  3. (C) Trypanosoma and Plasmodium
  4. (D) Mycoplasma and Euglena

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Linnaeus placed all walled organisms in Plantae. Bacteria (prokaryotic) and fungi (eukaryotic heterotrophs with chitinous walls) were both placed under Plantae alongside green plants. Amoeba, Paramoecium, Trypanosoma, Plasmodium, Mycoplasma, and Euglena all lack a true cell wall.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

A prokaryote isolated from the rumen of a cow produces methane gas from organic matter. Its survival in such an anaerobic, harsh niche is primarily attributed to:

  1. (A) Presence of chlorophyll a and heterocysts
  2. (B) Complete absence of a cell wall
  3. (C) A cell wall structure different from eubacteria
  4. (D) Possession of a proteinaceous pellicle

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Methane-producing bacteria in ruminant guts are methanogens, which belong to Archaebacteria. Archaebacteria survive extreme habitats due to their unique cell wall structure that differs from eubacteria.

Practice Question 3 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: Mycoplasma can survive without oxygen and are the smallest known living cells.
Statement II: Mycoplasma possess a rigid non-cellulosic cell wall made of polysaccharides and amino acids.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Statement I is correct—Mycoplasma are the smallest living cells and are anaerobic (survive without oxygen). Statement II is incorrect because Mycoplasma completely lack a cell wall.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Which protistan group is correctly matched with its cell-wall feature and ecological significance?

  1. (A) Dinoflagellates — silica-impregnated overlapping shells; chief producers in oceans
  2. (B) Chrysophytes — stiff cellulose plates; cause red tides
  3. (C) Diatoms — indestructible silica-embedded walls; form diatomaceous earth
  4. (D) Slime moulds — proteinaceous pellicle; nitrogen fixation in polluted water

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Diatoms (Chrysophytes) have two overlapping silica-embedded shells that resist decay and accumulate over billions of years as diatomaceous earth; they are also the chief oceanic producers. Dinoflagellates have stiff cellulose plates and cause red tides.

Practice Question 5 Match-the-Following MCQ

Match the protozoan or protist in Column I with its characteristic feature in Column II:

Column I: (i) Trypanosoma, (ii) Paramoecium, (iii) Gonyaulax, (iv) Plasmodium
Column II: (p) Coordinated cilia steering food into gullet, (q) Infectious spore-like stage causing malaria, (r) Flagellated parasite causing sleeping sickness, (s) Red dinoflagellate causing red tides

  1. (A) (i)-r, (ii)-p, (iii)-s, (iv)-q
  2. (B) (i)-p, (ii)-r, (iii)-q, (iv)-s
  3. (C) (i)-r, (ii)-s, (iii)-p, (iv)-q
  4. (D) (i)-q, (ii)-p, (iii)-s, (iv)-r

Answer: (A)

Explanation: Trypanosoma is a flagellated protozoan causing sleeping sickness (r); Paramoecium is a ciliated protozoan with a gullet (p); Gonyaulax is a red dinoflagellate responsible for red tides (s); Plasmodium is a sporozoan causing malaria (q).

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

Select the correct combination of features for Kingdom Fungi classes:

  1. (A) Phycomycetes: septate mycelium; exogenous basidiospores
  2. (B) Ascomycetes: coenocytic mycelium; endogenous conidia
  3. (C) Basidiomycetes: sex organs absent; exogenous basidiospores on basidium
  4. (D) Deuteromycetes: aseptate mycelium; sexual ascospores in ascocarps

Answer: (C)

Explanation: In Basidiomycetes, sex organs are absent; plasmogamy occurs by fusion of somatic cells, leading to a dikaryon and ultimately a basidium where four basidiospores are produced exogenously. Phycomycetes have aseptate coenocytic mycelium; Ascomycetes have septate mycelium and exogenous conidia; Deuteromycetes have septate mycelium and lack a known sexual stage.

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

If a fungal species originally placed in Deuteromycetes is later found to produce endogenous sexual spores inside sac-like asci, to which class should it be transferred?

  1. (A) Phycomycetes
  2. (B) Ascomycetes
  3. (C) Basidiomycetes
  4. (D) Slime moulds

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Deuteromycetes ("imperfect fungi") contain species whose sexual stages are unknown. Once a sexual stage producing endogenous ascospores in sac-like asci is discovered, the fungus is reassigned to Ascomycetes.

Practice Question 8 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): Lichens are rarely found in heavily industrialised, polluted cities.
Reason (R): In lichens, the mycobiont provides shelter and absorbs water and minerals, while the phycobiont synthesises food.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (D) Both (A) and (R) are false.

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Both statements are scientifically true: lichens are sensitive pollution indicators that do not grow in polluted areas, and they represent a mutualism between phycobiont and mycobiont. However, the mutualistic division of labour in (R) does not explain why lichens are sensitive to pollution.

Practice Question 9 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding viruses, viroids, and prions is incorrect?

  1. (A) A single virus particle can contain either DNA or RNA, never both.
  2. (B) Viroids consist of low-molecular-weight free RNA without a protein coat.
  3. (C) Prions are abnormally folded proteins that cause bovine spongiform encephalopathy.
  4. (D) Bacteriophages typically contain single-stranded RNA as their genetic material.

Answer: (D)

Explanation: Bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) are usually double-stranded DNA viruses, whereas plant-infecting viruses typically contain single-stranded RNA. Statements (A), (B), and (C) are all accurate.

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following organisms is/are partially heterotrophic despite belonging to Kingdom Plantae?

  1. (A) Nostoc and Anabaena
  2. (B) Cuscuta and Venus fly trap
  3. (C) Neurospora and Claviceps
  4. (D) Chlamydomonas and Chlorella

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Within Kingdom Plantae, Cuscuta (parasitic) and insectivorous plants such as Bladderwort and Venus fly trap are partially heterotrophic. Nostoc and Anabaena are Monera; Neurospora and Claviceps are Fungi; Chlamydomonas and Chlorella are unicellular eukaryotic algae placed in Protista under Whittaker's system.

10. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Unicellular Yeast in Fungi: Not all fungi are multicellular; Saccharomyces (yeast) is unicellular yet remains in Ascomycetes (Kingdom Fungi) rather than Protista.
  • Exogenous vs. Endogenous Fungal Spores: Conidia and basidiospores are produced exogenously, whereas sporangiospores (Phycomycetes) and ascospores (Ascomycetes) are produced endogenously.
  • Cyanobacteria vs. Green Algae: Cyanobacteria ("blue-green algae") are prokaryotic (Monera), not eukaryotic algae.
  • Plasmodium Confusion: Plasmodium (italicised genus) is the malarial sporozoan parasite, whereas "plasmodium" (unitalicised stage) is the multinucleated vegetative aggregation of slime moulds.
  • Viroids vs. Prions: Viroids = free RNA only (no protein); Prions = abnormally folded protein only (no nucleic acid).

High-Yield Revision Checklist

  • Whittaker's 5 criteria: cell structure, body organisation, nutrition, reproduction, phylogeny.
  • Monera cell wall: non-cellulosic (polysaccharide + amino acid); absent in Mycoplasma; modified in Archaebacteria.
  • Nostoc / Anabaena: chlorophyll a, mucilaginous sheath, heterocysts for N₂ fixation.
  • Diatoms: silica soap-box walls → diatomaceous earth; chief oceanic producers.
  • Gonyaulax: red tides; Euglena: pellicle + mixotrophy; Trypanosoma: sleeping sickness; Paramoecium: cilia + gullet.
  • Fungal sexual cycle: Plasmogamy → (Dikaryophase in Ascomycetes & Basidiomycetes) → Karyogamy → Meiosis.
  • Viral milestones: Ivanowsky (1892, filterable), Beijerinck (1898, Contagium vivum fluidum), Stanley (1935, crystallisation); Diener (1971, viroids).

11. Concise Final Recap

Whittaker's Five-Kingdom framework organises cellular life into prokaryotic Monera, unicellular eukaryotic Protista, chitin-walled absorptive Fungi (Phycomycetes, Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes, Deuteromycetes), cellulose-walled autotrophic Plantae, and wall-less holozoic Animalia. Outside this cellular hierarchy lie obligate acellular parasites—nucleoprotein viruses, naked-RNA viroids, and infectious-protein prions—alongside mutualistic lichens (phycobiont + mycobiont) that serve as sensitive indicators of clean air.
