NEET UG Biology Standard

Biological Classification

Comprehensive Study Guide, Visual Concept Atlas, Worked Examples, and Original NEET UG Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Biological classification is the scientific procedure of arranging organisms into hierarchical groups based on their similarities and dissimilarities. For the NEET UG examination, this topic forms the bedrock of diversity in the living world. Questions frequently test comparative Kingdom traits, exceptions to general rules, microbial nutrition, fungal life cycles and spore types, and the molecular nature of acellular infectious agents.

Key Learning Objectives
  • Trace the historical shift from instinctive classification to Aristotle's morphological system, Linnaeus's Two Kingdom system, and R.H. Whittaker's Five Kingdom system.
  • Compare Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia across five core criteria: cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction, and phylogenetic relationships.
  • Differentiate Archaebacteria and Eubacteria, including cyanobacteria, chemosynthetic autotrophs, heterotrophic bacteria, and wall-less Mycoplasma.
  • Examine the five major groups of Kingdom Protista: Chrysophytes, Dinoflagellates, Euglenoids, Slime moulds, and Protozoans.
  • Master the vegetative, asexual, and sexual reproductive cycles of Kingdom Fungi and distinguish between Phycomycetes, Ascomycetes, Basidiomycetes, and Deuteromycetes.
  • Understand the defining characteristics of Kingdoms Plantae and Animalia, and analyze acellular entities (Viruses, Viroids, Prions) and symbiotic Lichens.

2. Evolution of Classification Systems

2.1 Early Attempts and Aristotle's Classification

In the earliest days of human civilisation, classification was done instinctively rather than scientifically, driven by the practical human need to use organisms for food, shelter, and clothing.

Aristotle was the earliest pioneer to attempt a more scientific basis for classification. He relied on simple morphological characters:

  • Plants: Classified into three groups based on habit—Trees, Shrubs, and Herbs.
  • Animals: Divided into two major groups—those which had red blood and those that did not.

2.2 Linnaeus's Two Kingdom System and Its Limitations

During the time of Carolus Linnaeus, a Two Kingdom system of classification was developed, dividing all living organisms into Kingdom Plantae (all plants) and Kingdom Animalia (all animals). In earlier classification schemes, bacteria, blue-green algae, fungi, mosses, ferns, gymnosperms, and angiosperms were all placed together under 'Plants' based on a single unifying character: all of them had a cell wall in their cells.

While easy to understand and apply at a superficial level, the Two Kingdom system proved inadequate because a large number of organisms did not fall neatly into either category, and it brought together groups that differed widely in fundamental biology:

  1. No distinction between Prokaryotes and Eukaryotes: It grouped prokaryotic bacteria and blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) together with eukaryotic groups (mosses, ferns, gymnosperms, angiosperms).
  2. No distinction between Unicellular and Multicellular organisms: For example, unicellular Chlamydomonas and multicellular filamentous Spirogyra were placed together under algae.
  3. No distinction between Photosynthetic and Non-photosynthetic organisms: Heterotrophic fungi were grouped with autotrophic green plants, despite showing a fundamental difference in cell wall composition—fungi possess chitinous cell walls, whereas green plants possess cellulosic cell walls.

Consequently, biologists recognised the need to incorporate characteristics beyond gross morphology, such as cell structure, nature of the cell wall, mode of nutrition, habitat, methods of reproduction, and evolutionary (phylogenetic) relationships.

2.3 R.H. Whittaker's Five Kingdom Classification (1969)

In 1969, R.H. Whittaker proposed the Five Kingdom Classification, defining the kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia.

Five Main Criteria Used by R.H. Whittaker (1969)
  1. Cell structure (Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic; presence/absence and composition of cell wall)
  2. Body organisation (Cellular, multicellular/loose tissue, tissue/organ, or organ system)
  3. Mode of nutrition (Autotrophic—photosynthetic or chemosynthetic; Heterotrophic—saprophytic, parasitic, or holozoic)
  4. Reproduction
  5. Phylogenetic relationships (Evolutionary relationships)

Key Reorganisations in Whittaker's System:

  • All prokaryotic organisms were separated and grouped under Kingdom Monera.
  • All unicellular eukaryotic organisms were placed in Kingdom Protista. Notably, Kingdom Protista brought together Chlamydomonas and Chlorella (earlier placed in Algae within Plants, and both having cell walls) with Paramoecium and Amoeba (earlier placed in the Animal kingdom, and both lacking cell walls).
  • Heterotrophic eukaryotic organisms with chitinous cell walls were placed in a dedicated kingdom—Kingdom Fungi.

Note on the Three-Domain System: A three-domain system has also been proposed later, which divides Kingdom Monera into two domains while leaving all remaining eukaryotic kingdoms in the third domain, thereby resulting in a six-kingdom classification.

3. Kingdom Monera

Bacteria are the sole members of Kingdom Monera. They are the most abundant microorganisms on Earth and are cosmopolitan in distribution—hundreds of bacteria are present in a single handful of soil. They also thrive in extreme habitats where very few other life forms can survive, such as hot springs, deserts, snow, and deep oceans. Many live in or on other organisms as parasites.

3.1 Morphological Categories of Bacteria

Based on their shape, bacteria are grouped into four categories:

  1. Coccus (plural: cocci): Spherical-shaped.
  2. Bacillus (plural: bacilli): Rod-shaped (often seen with endospores under certain conditions).
  3. Vibrium (plural: vibrio): Comma-shaped.
  4. Spirillum (plural: spirilla): Spiral-shaped (often bearing tufts of flagella at one or both ends).

3.2 Bacterial Structure vs. Metabolic Diversity

Although bacterial structure is very simple, they are very complex in behaviour. Compared to all other groups of organisms, bacteria as a group show the most extensive metabolic diversity:

  • Autotrophic bacteria: Synthesise their own organic food from inorganic substrates. They may be photosynthetic autotrophic or chemosynthetic autotrophic.
  • Heterotrophic bacteria: The vast majority of bacteria are heterotrophs; they do not synthesise their own food but depend on other living organisms (parasites) or on dead organic matter (saprophytes) for nutrition.

3.3 Archaebacteria

Archaebacteria are a special group of ancient bacteria capable of surviving in some of the most harsh habitats:

  • Halophiles: Live in extreme salty areas.
  • Thermoacidophiles: Live in hot springs (high temperature and acidity).
  • Methanogens: Live in marshy (anaerobic) areas.
High-Yield NEET Concept: Why do Archaebacteria survive extreme conditions?

Archaebacteria differ from other bacteria in having a different cell wall structure, and this specific feature is responsible for their survival in extreme conditions.

Methanogens in Ruminants: Methanogens are present in the gut of several ruminant animals such as cows and buffaloes. They are responsible for the production of methane (biogas) from the dung of these animals.

3.4 Eubacteria ('True Bacteria')

Eubacteria are characterised by the presence of a rigid cell wall, and if motile, a flagellum.

A. Cyanobacteria (Blue-Green Algae)

  • They possess chlorophyll a (similar to green plants) and are photosynthetic autotrophs.
  • Habitat & Habit: Unicellular, colonial, or filamentous; found in freshwater, marine, or terrestrial environments.
  • Their colonies are generally surrounded by a gelatinous (mucilaginous) sheath.
  • They often form dense growths called blooms in polluted water bodies.
  • Nitrogen Fixation: Certain filamentous cyanobacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen in specialised, thick-walled cells called heterocysts. Classic examples: Nostoc and Anabaena.

B. Chemosynthetic Autotrophic Bacteria

  • These bacteria oxidise various inorganic substances such as nitrates, nitrites, and ammonia, and use the released chemical energy for their ATP production.
  • They play a vital ecological role in recycling nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and sulphur.

C. Heterotrophic Bacteria

  • Heterotrophic bacteria are the most abundant in nature, and the majority of them are important decomposers.
  • Beneficial Roles: Making curd from milk (e.g., Lactobacillus), production of antibiotics, and fixing atmospheric nitrogen symbiotically in legume roots (e.g., Rhizobium).
  • Pathogenic Roles: Cause damage to human beings, crops, farm animals, and pets. Well-known bacterial diseases include cholera, typhoid, tetanus, and citrus canker.

3.5 Reproduction in Bacteria

  1. Fission (Primary method): Bacteria reproduce mainly by binary fission (cell division following DNA replication).
  2. Spore formation: Under unfavourable environmental conditions, bacteria produce resistant spores.
  3. Primitive Sexual Reproduction: They also exhibit a sort of sexual reproduction by adopting a primitive type of DNA transfer from one bacterium to another.

3.6 Mycoplasma

Four Must-Know Facts About Mycoplasma for NEET UG
  1. They completely lack a cell wall (making them naturally resistant to cell-wall-targeting antibiotics like penicillin).
  2. They are the smallest living cells known.
  3. They can survive without oxygen (facultative/obligate anaerobes).
  4. Many mycoplasma are pathogenic in both animals and plants.

4. Kingdom Protista

All single-celled eukaryotes are placed under Kingdom Protista. However, the boundaries of this kingdom are not well defined—what may be considered a "photosynthetic protistan" by one biologist may be considered a "plant" by another.

  • Habitat: Members of Protista are primarily aquatic.
  • Evolutionary Link: Kingdom Protista forms a connecting link with the kingdoms dealing with plants, animals, and fungi.
  • Cellular Structure: Being eukaryotes, their cell body contains a well-defined nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. Some possess flagella or cilia for locomotion.
  • Reproduction: They reproduce both asexually and sexually by a process involving cell fusion and zygote formation.
  • Major Groups Included: Chrysophytes, Dinoflagellates, Euglenoids, Slime moulds, and Protozoans.

4.1 Chrysophytes

  • Members: Includes diatoms and golden algae (desmids).
  • Habitat & Habit: Found in both freshwater and marine environments. They are microscopic and float passively in water currents (plankton). Most of them are photosynthetic.
  • Cell Wall Structure in Diatoms: The cell walls form two thin overlapping shells that fit together like a soap box (an upper epitheca over a lower hypotheca). The walls are embedded with silica, making them indestructible.
  • Diatomaceous Earth: Because their siliceous walls do not decay, diatoms have left behind vast amounts of cell wall deposits in their habitat over billions of years. This accumulation is called diatomaceous earth. Being gritty, this soil is used commercially in polishing and in the filtration of oils and syrups.
  • Ecological Role: Diatoms are the chief 'producers' in the oceans.

4.2 Dinoflagellates

  • Habitat & Nutrition: Mostly marine and photosynthetic.
  • Pigmentation: They appear yellow, green, brown, blue, or red depending on the main pigments present in their cells.
  • Cell Wall: The outer surface of the cell wall is covered with stiff cellulose plates.
  • Flagellation: Most dinoflagellates have two flagella—one lies longitudinally and the other lies transversely in a furrow between the wall plates.
  • Red Tides: Red dinoflagellates (Example: Gonyaulax) frequently undergo rapid multiplication, making the sea appear red—a phenomenon known as red tides. Toxins released by such huge numbers may kill other marine animals such as fishes.

4.3 Euglenoids

  • Habitat: Majority are freshwater organisms found in stagnant water.
  • Body Covering (No Cell Wall): Instead of a cell wall, they possess a protein-rich layer called a pellicle, which makes their body flexible.
  • Flagellation: They possess two flagella—a short one and a long one.
  • Mixotrophic Nutrition: They are photosynthetic in the presence of sunlight, but when deprived of sunlight, they behave like heterotrophs by predating on other smaller organisms.
  • Pigments: Remarkably, the photosynthetic pigments of euglenoids are identical to those present in higher plants (chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b).
  • Example: Euglena.

4.4 Slime Moulds

  • Nutrition: Slime moulds are saprophytic protists. Their vegetative body moves along decaying twigs and leaves, engulfing organic material.
  • Favourable Conditions (Plasmodium): Under suitable conditions, they form a multinucleate aggregation called a plasmodium, which may grow and spread over several feet.
  • Unfavourable Conditions (Fruiting Bodies & Spores): During unfavourable conditions, the plasmodium differentiates and forms fruiting bodies bearing spores at their tips.
  • Spore Characteristics: The spores possess true walls, are extremely resistant, can survive for many years even under adverse conditions, and are dispersed by air currents.

4.5 Protozoans

All protozoans are heterotrophs and live as predators or parasites. They are believed to be primitive relatives of animals. Based on locomotory structures and life cycle features, there are four major groups of protozoans:

Comparison of the Four Major Groups of Protozoans
Group Habitat & Lifestyle Locomotory / Special Features Examples & Diseases
1. Amoeboid protozoans Freshwater, sea water, or moist soil; free-living predators or parasites. Move and capture prey by putting out pseudopodia (false feet). Marine forms have silica shells on their surface. Amoeba (free-living); Entamoeba (parasitic).
2. Flagellated protozoans Either free-living or parasitic. Possess flagella for locomotion. Trypanosoma (parasitic; causes sleeping sickness).
3. Ciliated protozoans Aquatic, actively moving organisms. Possess thousands of cilia. Have a cavity (gullet) opening to the outside of the cell surface; coordinated ciliary movement steers food-laden water into the gullet. Paramoecium.
4. Sporozoans Diverse parasitic organisms. Possess an infectious spore-like stage in their life cycle. Plasmodium (malarial parasite; causes malaria).

5. Kingdom Fungi

Fungi constitute a unique kingdom of heterotrophic eukaryotic organisms showing great diversity in morphology and habitat. Familiar examples include mould on moist bread and rotten fruits, edible mushrooms, poisonous toadstools, white spots on mustard leaves (caused by the parasitic fungus Albugo), unicellular yeast (Saccharomyces, used to make bread and beer), wheat rust-causing Puccinia, and antibiotic-producing Penicillium.

5.1 Habitat, Structure, and Nutrition

  • Habitat: Fungi are cosmopolitan and occur in air, water, soil, and on animals and plants. They prefer to grow in warm and humid places (which is why we store food in a refrigerator—to prevent bacterial or fungal spoilage).
  • Thallus Organisation: With the exception of yeasts, which are unicellular, fungi are filamentous. Their bodies consist of long, slender thread-like structures called hyphae. The network of hyphae is known as a mycelium.
    • Coenocytic (Aseptate) Hyphae: Continuous tubes filled with multinucleated cytoplasm lacking cross walls.
    • Septate Hyphae: Hyphae that possess septae or cross walls.
  • Cell Wall Composition: Composed of chitin and polysaccharides.
  • Modes of Heterotrophic Nutrition:
    1. Saprophytes: Most fungi absorb soluble organic matter from dead substrates.
    2. Parasites: Depend on living plants and animals.
    3. Symbionts: Live in mutually beneficial associations—with algae as lichens, and with the roots of higher plants as mycorrhiza.

5.2 Reproduction in Fungi

  • Vegetative Reproduction: Takes place by fragmentation, fission, and budding.
  • Asexual Reproduction: Occurs through spores called conidia, sporangiospores, or zoospores.
  • Sexual Reproduction: Occurs through sexual spores called oospores, ascospores, and basidiospores. Various spores are produced in distinct structures called fruiting bodies.
The Three Sequential Steps of the Fungal Sexual Cycle
  1. Plasmogamy: Fusion of protoplasms between two motile or non-motile gametes.
  2. Karyogamy: Fusion of two nuclei to form a diploid (\(2n\)) zygotic nucleus.
  3. Meiosis in Zygote: Reduction division in the zygote resulting in haploid (\(n\)) spores.

Dikaryon and Dikaryophase: When two haploid hyphae of compatible mating types fuse, in some fungi (like Phycomycetes) the fusion of two haploid cells immediately results in diploid cells (\(2n\)). However, in Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes, an intervening dikaryotic stage (\(n + n\), i.e., two nuclei per cell) occurs between plasmogamy and karyogamy. This condition is called a dikaryon and the phase is called the dikaryophase of the fungus. Later, the parental nuclei fuse (karyogamy) to make the cell diploid (\(2n\)), followed by meiosis inside fruiting bodies to produce haploid spores.

5.3 Basis of Classification of Kingdom Fungi

Kingdom Fungi is divided into four classes based on three main criteria:

  1. Morphology of the mycelium (aseptate/coenocytic vs. branched and septate)
  2. Mode of spore formation (endogenous vs. exogenous; asexual vs. sexual)
  3. Fruiting bodies (presence, absence, and type—e.g., ascocarp, basidiocarp)

5.4 Detailed Study of the Four Fungal Classes

1. Phycomycetes (Lower / Algal Fungi)

  • Habitat: Found in aquatic habitats, on decaying wood in moist and damp places, or as obligate parasites on plants.
  • Mycelium: Aseptate and coenocytic.
  • Asexual Reproduction: By zoospores (motile) or aplanospores (non-motile). These spores are produced endogenously inside a sporangium.
  • Sexual Reproduction: A diploid zygospore is formed by the fusion of two gametes. Gametes may be morphologically similar (isogamous) or dissimilar (anisogamous or oogamous).
  • Examples: Mucor, Rhizopus (the bread mould), and Albugo (the parasitic fungus causing white rust/spots on mustard).

2. Ascomycetes (Sac-Fungi)

  • Habit: Mostly multicellular (e.g., Penicillium) or rarely unicellular (e.g., yeast, Saccharomyces).
  • Nutrition: Saprophytic, decomposers, parasitic, or coprophilous (growing on dung).
  • Mycelium: Branched and septate.
  • Asexual Reproduction: By conidia produced exogenously on special mycelial stalks called conidiophores. Conidia germinate to produce mycelium.
  • Sexual Reproduction: By sexual spores called ascospores, which are produced endogenously in sac-like asci (singular: ascus). These asci are arranged in fruiting bodies called ascocarps.
  • Examples & Importance: Aspergillus, Claviceps, and Neurospora (used extensively in biochemical and genetic work). Many members like morels and truffles are edible and considered delicacies.

3. Basidiomycetes (Club Fungi)

  • Common Forms & Habitat: Includes mushrooms, bracket fungi, or puffballs. They grow in soil, on logs and tree stumps, and in living plant bodies as parasites (e.g., rusts and smuts).
  • Mycelium: Branched and septate.
  • Asexual Reproduction: Asexual spores are generally not found, but vegetative reproduction by fragmentation is common.
  • Sexual Reproduction: Sex organs are absent, but plasmogamy is brought about by the fusion of two vegetative or somatic cells of different strains or genotypes. The resulting structure is dikaryotic (\(n + n\)), which ultimately gives rise to the basidium. Karyogamy and meiosis take place in the basidium, producing four basidiospores. Basidiospores are exogenously produced on the basidium (plural: basidia), and basidia are arranged in fruiting bodies called basidiocarps.
  • Examples: Agaricus (mushroom), Ustilago (smut), and Puccinia (rust fungus).

4. Deuteromycetes (Imperfect Fungi)

  • Why 'Imperfect Fungi'? Only the asexual or vegetative phases of these fungi are known; their sexual (perfect) stages are absent or undiscovered.
  • Taxonomic Reassignment: Once the perfect (sexual) stages of members of Deuteromycetes were discovered, they were moved into the classes they rightly belong to—most often Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes.
  • Reproduction: Reproduce only by asexual spores known as conidia.
  • Mycelium: Septate and branched.
  • Nutrition & Ecological Role: Some are saprophytes or parasites, while a large number of them are decomposers of litter and help in mineral cycling.
  • Examples: Alternaria, Colletotrichum, and Trichoderma.

6. Overview of Kingdoms Plantae and Animalia

6.1 Kingdom Plantae

  • General Definition: Includes all eukaryotic chlorophyll-containing organisms commonly called plants.
  • Cellular Structure: Eukaryotic cells with prominent chloroplasts and a cell wall mainly made of cellulose.
  • Exceptions in Nutrition (Partially Heterotrophic Plants):
    • Insectivorous plants: Bladderwort (Utricularia) and Venus fly trap (Dionaea).
    • Parasitic plants: Cuscuta (dodder).
  • Major Groups Included: Algae, Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, Gymnosperms, and Angiosperms.
  • Alternation of Generations: The life cycle of plants exhibits two distinct phases—the diploid (\(2n\)) sporophytic phase and the haploid (\(n\)) gametophytic phase—that alternate with each other. The relative lengths of the haploid and diploid phases, and whether these phases are free-living or dependent on others, vary among different plant groups.

6.2 Kingdom Animalia

  • General Definition: Characterised by heterotrophic, eukaryotic, multicellular organisms whose cells completely lack cell walls.
  • Nutrition & Digestion: They directly or indirectly depend on plants for food. They digest their food in an internal cavity and store food reserves as glycogen or fat. Their mode of nutrition is holozoic (by ingestion of food).
  • Growth & Coordination: They follow a definite growth pattern and grow into adults that have a definite shape and size. Higher forms exhibit elaborate sensory and neuromotor mechanisms, and most are capable of locomotion.
  • Reproduction: Sexual reproduction occurs by copulation of male and female followed by embryological development.

7. Viruses, Viroids, Prions, and Lichens

In R.H. Whittaker's Five Kingdom classification, there is no mention of lichens and acellular organisms like viruses, viroids, and prions. Viruses were excluded because they are not considered truly 'living' if we define living organisms as those possessing a cellular structure.

7.1 Viruses

  • Non-cellular & Obligate Parasites: Viruses are non-cellular organisms characterised by having an inert crystalline structure outside the living cell. Once they infect a cell, they take over the biosynthetic machinery of the host cell to replicate themselves, killing the host. Thus, viruses are obligate parasites.
Historical Milestones in Virology (High-Yield for NEET Matching Questions)
  • Meaning of 'Virus': Means venom or poisonous fluid.
  • Dmitri Ivanowsky (1892): Recognised certain microbes as the causal organism of the mosaic disease of tobacco. He found that they were smaller than bacteria because they passed through bacteria-proof filters.
  • M.W. Beijerinck (1898): Demonstrated that the extract of infected tobacco plants could cause infection in healthy plants; he named the new pathogen "virus" and called the fluid Contagium vivum fluidum (infectious living fluid).
  • W.M. Stanley (1935): Showed that viruses could be crystallised and that these crystals consist largely of proteins.

Molecular Structure and Genetic Material of Viruses

  • Nucleoprotein Nature: A virus is a nucleoprotein (composed of protein + nucleic acid), and its genetic material is infectious.
  • Rule of Nucleic Acids: In addition to proteins, viruses contain genetic material that can be either RNA or DNA. No virus contains both RNA and DNA.
    • Viruses that infect plants: Generally have single-stranded RNA (ssRNA).
    • Viruses that infect animals: Have either single- or double-stranded RNA (ssRNA or dsRNA) or double-stranded DNA (dsDNA).
    • Bacterial viruses or Bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria): Usually double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses.
  • Capsid & Capsomeres: The protein coat called the capsid, made of small subunits called capsomeres, protects the nucleic acid. These capsomeres are arranged in helical (e.g., Tobacco Mosaic Virus) or polyhedral geometric forms (e.g., Bacteriophage head).
  • Viral Diseases in Humans: Mumps, smallpox, herpes, influenza, common cold ('flu'), and AIDS.
  • Viral Symptoms in Plants: Mosaic formation, leaf rolling and curling, yellowing and vein clearing, dwarfing, and stunted growth.

7.2 Viroids

  • Discovery: Discovered in 1971 by T.O. Diener.
  • Size & Composition: Smaller than viruses; consists of a free RNA of low molecular weight and completely lacks the protein coat (capsid) found in viruses—hence the name viroid.
  • Disease Caused: Causes potato spindle tuber disease.

7.3 Prions

  • Nature & Size: Infectious agents transmitted in certain infectious neurological diseases, consisting of abnormally folded protein (lacking any nucleic acid) and similar in size to viruses.
  • Notable Diseases Caused by Prions:
    1. Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE), commonly called mad cow disease in cattle.
    2. Its analogous variant in humans: Creutzfeldt–Jacob disease (CJD).

7.4 Lichens

  • Symbiotic Association: Lichens are mutually useful symbiotic associations between algae and fungi.
  • Two Components:
    • Phycobiont (Algal component): Autotrophic; prepares organic food via photosynthesis for the fungus.
    • Mycobiont (Fungal component): Heterotrophic; provides shelter and absorbs mineral nutrients and water for its algal partner.
  • Ecological Indicator: Lichens are very good pollution indicators—they do not grow in polluted areas (especially areas polluted with sulphur dioxide).

8. Worked Conceptual Examples & Textbook Problem Walkthroughs

The following worked examples address core conceptual applications and analytical questions essential for mastering the chapter.

Worked Example 1: Economic Importance of Heterotrophic Bacteria & Archaebacteria

Problem: State two economically important uses of (a) heterotrophic bacteria and (b) archaebacteria.

Solution:

  • (a) Uses of Heterotrophic Bacteria:
    1. Conversion of milk into curd and production of antibiotics.
    2. Symbiotic nitrogen fixation in the roots of leguminous plants, enriching soil fertility, as well as acting as natural decomposers.
  • (b) Uses of Archaebacteria:
    1. Methanogens present in the gut of ruminant animals (cows and buffaloes) produce methane gas (biogas) from animal dung, which is used as a clean domestic fuel.
    2. Thermoacidophiles provide heat-stable enzymes capable of functioning at high temperatures and acidic conditions in biotechnology.
Worked Example 2: Ecological Significance of 'Algal Bloom' and 'Red Tides'

Problem: Distinguish between the terms 'algal bloom' and 'red tides' with respect to their causative organisms and ecological effects.

Solution:

  • Algal Bloom: Refers to the rapid, excessive proliferation of photosynthetic microorganisms—particularly cyanobacteria (blue-green algae)—in nutrient-polluted freshwater or marine bodies, covering the water surface and depleting dissolved oxygen.
  • Red Tides: Refers specifically to the rapid multiplication of red dinoflagellates (such as Gonyaulax, Kingdom Protista) in marine waters, making the sea appear red. These large populations release potent toxins into the water that kill fishes and other marine animals.
Worked Example 3: Ploidy Analysis in the Fungal Life Cycle

Problem: In a mushroom (Agaricus, class Basidiomycetes), determine the ploidy level (\(n\), \(n+n\), or \(2n\)) of the following structures: (i) Primary somatic hypha, (ii) Mycelium formed immediately after plasmogamy, (iii) Basidium just after karyogamy, and (iv) Basidiospores.

Solution:

  1. Primary somatic hypha: Haploid (\(n\)).
  2. Mycelium formed immediately after plasmogamy: Dikaryotic (\(n + n\), two haploid nuclei per cell), representing the extended dikaryophase characteristic of Basidiomycetes.
  3. Basidium just after karyogamy: Diploid (\(2n\)), formed when the two nuclei fuse inside the club-shaped basidium.
  4. Basidiospores: Haploid (\(n\)), as meiosis immediately follows karyogamy inside the basidium to produce four exogenous haploid basidiospores.
Worked Example 4: Are Viruses Living or Non-Living?

Problem: Justify why viruses occupy the borderline between living and non-living entities.

Solution:

  • Non-living / Inert Properties: Viruses lack a cellular structure (proplams, cell membrane, and metabolic enzymes). Outside a specific living host cell, they exist as inert crystalline structures and can be crystallised like chemicals (demonstrated by W.M. Stanley, 1935).
  • Living / Biological Properties: They possess genetic material (either DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein capsid. Once inside a specific host cell, the infectious genetic material takes over the host's biosynthetic machinery to replicate and produce progeny virions.

Practice Question 1 [Single-Choice MCQ — History & Systems of Classification]

In Whittaker's Five Kingdom classification, which of the following pairs of organisms were brought together into the same kingdom despite having been placed in two completely different kingdoms (Plantae and Animalia) under earlier classification systems?

  • (A) Chlamydomonas and Spirogyra
  • (B) Chlorella and Amoeba
  • (C) Nostoc and Chlamydomonas
  • (D) Agaricus and Chlorella

Practice Question 5 [Single-Choice MCQ — Chrysophytes & Dinoflagellates]

Which of the following correctly compares Diatoms and Dinoflagellates?

Consider the following statements regarding Euglenoids and Slime Moulds:

  1. Euglenoids are mostly marine organisms that possess a stiff cellulose cell wall.
  2. The pigments of euglenoids are identical to those present in higher plants.
  3. When deprived of sunlight, euglenoids behave like heterotrophs by predating on other smaller organisms.
  4. Under unfavourable conditions, saprophytic slime moulds form an aggregation called a plasmodium that spreads over several feet.

Which of the above statements are correct?

  • (A) II, III, and V only
  • (B) I, II, and IV only
  • (C) II, III, IV, and V only

Correct Answer: (A)

Practice Question 8 [Single-Choice MCQ — Fungal Sexual Cycle & Spores]

In which of the following pairs of fungal classes does an intervening dikaryotic stage (\(n + n\)) occur during the sexual cycle, and how do their sexual spores differ in their mode of formation?

  • (A) Phycomycetes and Ascomycetes; both produce sexual spores endogenously.
  • (B) Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes; ascospores are produced endogenously in asci, whereas basidiospores are produced exogenously on basidia.

Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: An intervening dikaryotic stage (\(n + n\), two nuclei per cell, called a dikaryon / dikaryophase) occurs specifically in Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes. Assertion (A): Deuteromycetes are commonly known as 'imperfect fungi', and many of their former members have been moved to Ascomycetes and Basidiomycetes over time.

10. Common NEET UG Pitfalls & Rapid Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These 7 Classic NEET Exam Traps!
  1. Endogenous vs. Exogenous Fungal Spores:
    • Endogenous (formed inside a sac/sporangium): Zoospores & Aplanospores (in sporangium of Phycomycetes) and Ascospores (in ascus of Ascomycetes).
    • Exogenous (formed externally on stalks/basidia): Conidia (on conidiophores in Ascomycetes & Deuteromycetes) and Basidiospores (on basidium in Basidiomycetes).
  2. Unicellular Fungus Exception: While almost all fungi are filamentous (hyphal), yeast (Saccharomyces) is unicellular and belongs to class Ascomycetes.
  3. Silica in Protista: Silica is found in two places mentioned in this chapter—(i) embedded in the soap-box cell walls of Diatoms (Chrysophytes), and (ii) as surface silica shells in marine Amoeboid protozoans.
  4. Plasmodium (Slime Mould) vs. Plasmodium (Sporozoan):
    • plasmodium (lowercase, non-italicised): The multinucleate vegetative aggregation formed by saprophytic slime moulds under favourable conditions.
    • Plasmodium (capitalised, italicised genus): The parasitic sporozoan protozoan that causes malaria.
  5. Cell Wall Exceptions Across Kingdoms:
    • In Monera: Most have a non-cellulosic peptidoglycan wall, Archaebacteria have a distinct wall structure, and Mycoplasma completely lack a cell wall.
    • In Protista: Diatoms (siliceous wall), Dinoflagellates (cellulose plates), and Slime mould spores (true walls) have walls, whereas Euglenoids (protein pellicle) and most Protozoans lack cell walls.
  6. Scientist Chronology in Virology: Ivanowsky (1892) discovered filterable microbes causing tobacco mosaic disease → Beijerinck (1898) coined Contagium vivum fluidum and named "virus" → Stanley (1935) crystallised viruses → Diener (1971) discovered viroids.
  7. Phycobiont vs. Mycobiont: Always associate Phyco = Algae (Autotrophic) and Myco = Fungi (Heterotrophic).

Final One-Page Summary & Revision Checklist

  • Aristotle: Plants (trees, shrubs, herbs); Animals (with red blood vs. without red blood).
  • Whittaker (1969): Five kingdoms (Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) based on cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction, and phylogenetic relationships.
  • Monera: Solely bacteria; most extensive metabolic diversity. Archaebacteria (halophiles, thermoacidophiles, methanogens in ruminant gut). Eubacteria: Cyanobacteria (chlorophyll a, gelatinous sheath, heterocysts in Nostoc & Anabaena), Chemosynthetic autotrophs (oxidise nitrates, nitrites, ammonia), Heterotrophs (decomposers, curd, antibiotics, legume \(\mathrm{N_2}\)-fixation; pathogens of cholera, typhoid, tetanus, citrus canker), and wall-less anaerobic Mycoplasma.
  • Protista: Single-celled aquatic eukaryotes. Chrysophytes (diatoms + desmids; chief ocean producers; diatomaceous earth), Dinoflagellates (2 flagella, cellulose plates; Gonyaulax causes red tides), Euglenoids (Euglena; pellicle, mixotrophic, higher-plant pigments), Slime moulds (plasmodium in favourable, fruiting bodies with true-walled spores in unfavourable), Protozoans (Amoeboid, Flagellated, Ciliated, Sporozoans).
  • Fungi: Chitinous walls; saprophytes, parasites, symbionts (lichens, mycorrhiza). Sexual cycle: Plasmogamy → Karyogamy → Meiosis. Classes: Phycomycetes (Mucor, Rhizopus, Albugo), Ascomycetes (Penicillium, Saccharomyces, Aspergillus, Claviceps, Neurospora, morels, truffles), Basidiomycetes (Agaricus, Ustilago, Puccinia), Deuteromycetes (Alternaria, Colletotrichum, Trichoderma).
  • Acellular Agents & Lichens: Viruses (obligate intracellular nucleoprotein parasites; either DNA or RNA), Viroids (free low-MW RNA, no capsid; potato spindle tuber disease), Prions (abnormally folded protein; BSE & CJD), Lichens (symbiotic phycobiont + mycobiont; \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) pollution indicators).
