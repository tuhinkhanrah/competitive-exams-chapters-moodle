Respiration in Plants: Bioenergetics, Pathways, and Regulation

A complete, exam-oriented study guide covering cellular oxidation, glycolysis, fermentation, the citric acid cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and respiratory quotients.

1. Learning Objectives and Overview

Every living cell requires a continuous supply of usable chemical energy to drive active transport, biosynthesis, cell division, and maintenance. While green plant cells and cyanobacteria capture solar energy through photosynthesis, non-green tissues—such as roots, stems, flowers, and developing seeds—depend on translocated sugars to sustain metabolism. Cellular respiration is the enzyme-controlled, step-wise oxidation of organic food molecules inside cells to release energy and trap it in adenosine triphosphate (ATP).

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Explain how plants exchange respiratory gases without specialized breathing organs.
  • Trace the ten reactions of glycolysis, including energy investment, cleavage, and ATP/NADH payoff.
  • Compare alcoholic and lactic acid fermentation with complete aerobic oxidation.
  • Account for carbon atoms, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) release, \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\), and GTP/ATP in the link reaction and Krebs' cycle.
  • Describe electron flow through mitochondrial Complexes I–IV and chemiosmotic ATP synthesis via Complex V (\(\mathrm{F_0}\text{–}\mathrm{F_1}\)).
  • Evaluate the theoretical 38 ATP balance sheet, the amphibolic nature of respiration, and Respiratory Quotient (RQ) values for different substrates.

2. Core Concepts and Mechanisms

2.1 Cellular Respiration and Plant Gas Exchange

Cellular respiration is the controlled cleavage of carbon–carbon (\(\mathrm{C\text{–}C}\)) bonds of complex organic compounds through intracellular oxidation, liberating energy that is coupled to ATP synthesis. The organic molecules oxidised during this process are termed respiratory substrates. Carbohydrates (especially glucose) are the preferred substrates, though fats, proteins, and organic acids can also be oxidised under specific physiological conditions.

Unlike uncontrolled combustion—which oxidises glucose in a single violent step and dissipates nearly all energy as heat—cellular respiration proceeds through a series of small, enzyme-catalysed steps. This ensures that individual energy packets are appropriately sized to phosphorylate ADP into ATP, while intermediate carbon skeletons can be diverted to synthesize amino acids, lipids, and other biomolecules.

\[\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2 \longrightarrow 6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O + Energy\;(\text{trapped partly as ATP})}\]

Plants lack lungs, gills, or circulatory transport systems for gases, yet they respire efficiently through stomata (in leaves and young stems) and lenticels (in woody bark). Three structural and physiological features make this possible:

  • Local autonomy: Each plant organ meets its own gas-exchange demand with minimal long-distance gas transport.
  • Lower metabolic demand: Roots, stems, and leaves respire at considerably slower rates than active animal tissues; during intense photosynthesis, leaves generate \(\mathrm{O_2}\) internally.
  • Short diffusion distances: Living cells in thick stems and roots lie in thin peripheral layers near the surface (interior woody cells are dead mechanical tissue), and loosely packed parenchyma creates an interconnected network of intercellular air spaces.

2.2 Glycolysis (The EMP Pathway)

Derived from the Greek words glycos (sugar) and lysis (splitting), glycolysis was elucidated by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof, and J. Parnas, and is therefore known as the EMP pathway. It occurs in the cytoplasm of all living organisms—both aerobic and anaerobic—and does not require molecular oxygen.

In plants, glucose entering glycolysis is commonly derived from sucrose (the major translocated sugar) via the enzyme invertase, which hydrolyses sucrose into glucose and fructose, or from stored starch. Both glucose and fructose are phosphorylated by hexokinase using ATP to enter the same ten-step sequence, yielding two molecules of 3-carbon pyruvic acid (pyruvate) per 6-carbon glucose.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Two-phase organisation of glycolysis in the cytoplasm, showing ATP investment, triose phosphate cleavage, and substrate-level ATP synthesis.

Key regulatory and energetic checkpoints in glycolysis:

  • ATP consumption (2 ATP): Step 1 (glucose → glucose-6-phosphate) and Step 3 (fructose-6-phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate).
  • Cleavage: Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (6C) splits into two interconvertible 3-carbon triose phosphates: 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). Both proceed through the payoff phase as two molecules of PGAL.
  • Redox step: Oxidation of PGAL to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA) using inorganic phosphate (\(\mathrm{P_i}\)) transfers two hydrogen atoms (redox equivalents) to \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\), generating \(2\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) per glucose.
  • Substrate-level phosphorylation (4 ATP formed): BPGA → 3-phosphoglyceric acid (3-PGA) yields 2 ATP, and phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) → pyruvic acid yields 2 ATP. Net direct ATP gain = \(4 - 2 = 2\,\mathrm{ATP}\).

2.3 Fermentation (Anaerobic Fate of Pyruvate)

When oxygen is absent or insufficient, cells cannot operate the mitochondrial electron transport chain. To prevent glycolysis from halting due to depletion of \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\), cells must reoxidise \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) back to \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) via fermentation:

  • Alcoholic Fermentation (e.g., Yeast): Pyruvic acid is decarboxylated to acetaldehyde and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) by pyruvic acid decarboxylase, and acetaldehyde is then reduced to ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase using \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\). Yeasts poison themselves to death once ethanol concentration reaches approximately 13%; therefore, naturally fermented beverages cannot exceed this concentration without distillation.
  • Lactic Acid Fermentation (e.g., Certain Bacteria, Exercising Skeletal Muscle): Pyruvic acid is directly reduced to lactic acid by lactate dehydrogenase using \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), with no release of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).

Both fermentation pathways release less than 7% of the total chemical energy stored in glucose, yielding a net gain of only 2 ATP per glucose molecule while accumulating potentially toxic end products (ethanol or lactic acid).

2.4 Aerobic Respiration: Link Reaction and the Citric Acid (Krebs') Cycle

In eukaryotic cells with adequate \(\mathrm{O_2}\), pyruvate is transported from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrial matrix. Here it undergoes oxidative decarboxylation (the link reaction) catalysed by the multi-enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, requiring \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\), Coenzyme A (CoA), and \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\):

\[\mathrm{Pyruvic\;acid + CoA + NAD^+ \xrightarrow[\text{Mg}^{2+}]{\text{Pyruvate dehydrogenase}} Acetyl\;CoA + CO_2 + NADH + H^+}\]

Because one glucose molecule produces two pyruvate molecules, the link reaction generates \(2\,\mathrm{Acetyl\;CoA}\), \(2\,\mathrm{CO_2}\), and \(2\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) per glucose.

Each 2-carbon Acetyl CoA then enters the Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) Cycle—also called the Citric Acid Cycle or Krebs' Cycle (after Hans Krebs)—in the mitochondrial matrix:

  1. Condensation: Acetyl CoA (2C) condenses with oxaloacetic acid (OAA, 4C) and water, catalysed by citrate synthase, to form citric acid (6C) and release CoA.
  2. Isomerisation & Two Decarboxylations: Citrate isomerises to isocitrate, which undergoes two successive oxidative decarboxylations: first to α-ketoglutaric acid (5C) (releasing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\)) and then to succinyl-CoA (4C) (releasing a second \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\)).
  3. Substrate-Level Phosphorylation: Conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinic acid (4C) synthesises GTP from GDP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\); in a coupled reaction, GTP transfers its phosphate to ADP to form ATP.
  4. Regeneration of OAA: Succinic acid is oxidised to fumarate/malic acid (4C), reducing \(\mathrm{FAD^+}\) to \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\). Malate is then oxidised back to OAA (4C), generating a third \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\).

\[\mathrm{Pyruvic\;acid + 4\,NAD^+ + FAD^+ + 2\,H_2O + ADP + P_i \xrightarrow{\text{Matrix}} 3\,CO_2 + 4\,NADH + 4\,H^+ + FADH_2 + ATP}\]

2.5 Electron Transport System (ETS) and Oxidative Phosphorylation

The \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) and \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\) generated during glycolysis, the link reaction, and the TCA cycle hold high-energy electrons. These electrons are transferred through the Electron Transport System (ETS) embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane to molecular oxygen, driving ATP synthesis by oxidative phosphorylation:

  • Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase): Contains FMN and Fe-S centres; oxidises \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) and transfers electrons to Ubiquinone (UQ) within the membrane.
  • Complex II (Succinate dehydrogenase): Oxidises succinate to fumarate in the TCA cycle, transferring electrons via FAD and Fe-S to Ubiquinone (forming \(\mathrm{UQH_2}\)).
  • Complex III (Cytochrome \(\mathrm{bc_1}\) complex): Oxidises \(\mathrm{UQH_2}\) and passes electrons to Cytochrome c, a small mobile protein attached to the outer surface of the inner membrane.
  • Complex IV (Cytochrome c oxidase): Contains cytochromes \(a\) and \(a_3\) plus two copper centres; transfers electrons to the terminal acceptor \(\mathrm{O_2}\), reducing it with matrix protons to form \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). Although \(\mathrm{O_2}\) acts only at the final step, its presence is essential to pull electrons through the entire chain.
  • Complex V (ATP Synthase): Driven by the electrochemical proton gradient across the inner membrane (chemiosmosis), protons flow from the intermembrane space back into the matrix through the integral \(\mathrm{F_0}\) channel. This powers the peripheral \(\mathrm{F_1}\) catalytic headpiece to synthesise ATP from ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\). Passing \(4\,\mathrm{H^+}\) through \(\mathrm{F_0}\) yields \(1\,\mathrm{ATP}\).

Tutorial Diagram 3: Electron transport through Complexes I–IV to \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and chemiosmotic proton flow through Complex V (\(\mathrm{F_0\text{–}F_1}\)).

In this standard respiratory accounting, oxidation of 1 molecule of \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) yields 3 ATP, whereas oxidation of 1 molecule of \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\) yields 2 ATP.

2.6 The Respiratory Balance Sheet and Theoretical Assumptions

Calculating a theoretical net yield of 38 ATP per glucose molecule oxidised aerobically relies on four idealized assumptions that rarely hold strictly in living cells:

  1. Glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and the ETS operate strictly sequentially rather than simultaneously.
  2. Cytosolic \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) generated in glycolysis is transferred into mitochondria and yields 3 ATP each via oxidative phosphorylation.
  3. No pathway intermediates are withdrawn for biosynthesis of other cellular compounds.
  4. Only glucose serves as the respiratory substrate, with no alternative substrates entering mid-pathway.

2.7 Respiration as an Amphibolic Pathway

Because respiration breaks down organic substrates, it was historically viewed purely as a catabolic pathway. However, respiratory intermediates are also withdrawn to synthesize biomolecules (anabolism): for instance, acetyl CoA is both a breakdown product of fatty acids and the precursor for fatty acid synthesis, while TCA cycle intermediates serve as carbon skeletons for amino acid synthesis. Since the respiratory pathway participates simultaneously in breakdown and synthesis, it is accurately described as an amphibolic pathway.

2.8 Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

The Respiratory Quotient (RQ) is the ratio of the volume of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) evolved to the volume of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) consumed during respiration:

\[\mathrm{RQ} = \frac{\text{Volume of }\mathrm{CO_2}\text{ evolved}}{\text{Volume of }\mathrm{O_2}\text{ consumed}}\]

Because different macromolecules have different hydrogen-to-oxygen ratios, their complete oxidation requires different amounts of \(\mathrm{O_2}\), giving characteristic RQ values (1.0 for carbohydrates, ~0.7 for fats such as tripalmitin, and ~0.9 for proteins).

4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Proton Flow and ATP Yield in a Hypothetical Mitochondrion

Problem (Assumed Scenario): Suppose a respiring plant root cell oxidises \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of acetyl CoA completely via the Krebs' cycle and ETS. Assuming standard theoretical coupling (\(1\,\mathrm{NADH} \rightarrow 3\,\mathrm{ATP}\), \(1\,\mathrm{FADH_2} \rightarrow 2\,\mathrm{ATP}\), and \(4\,\mathrm{H^+}\) per ATP through \(\mathrm{F_0\text{–}F_1}\)), calculate: (a) the total ATP molecules (in moles) generated, and (b) the moles of protons (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) that must pass through Complex V for the oxidative phosphorylation portion alone.

Solution:

  1. Each mole of acetyl CoA entering the Krebs' cycle yields \(1\,\mathrm{GTP}\) (1 ATP via substrate-level phosphorylation), \(3\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), and \(1\,\mathrm{FADH_2}\).
  2. For \(3\,\mathrm{mol}\) of acetyl CoA:
    • Substrate-level phosphorylation: \(3 \times 1 = 3\,\mathrm{mol\;ATP}\).
    • From \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\): \(3 \times 3 = 9\,\mathrm{mol\;NADH} \rightarrow 9 \times 3 = 27\,\mathrm{mol\;ATP}\).
    • From \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\): \(3 \times 1 = 3\,\mathrm{mol\;FADH_2} \rightarrow 3 \times 2 = 6\,\mathrm{mol\;ATP}\).
  3. Total ATP: \(3 + 27 + 6 = 36\,\mathrm{mol\;ATP}\).
  4. Oxidative phosphorylation ATP only: \(27 + 6 = 33\,\mathrm{mol\;ATP}\). Since \(4\,\mathrm{H^+}\) cross \(\mathrm{F_0}\) per ATP synthesised, proton flow = \(33 \times 4 = 132\,\mathrm{mol\;H^+}\).
Worked Example 2: Calculating Respiratory Quotient for an Assumed Lipid Substrate

Problem (Assumed Scenario): Consider a hypothetical lipid molecule with the empirical formula \(\mathrm{C_{40}H_{76}O_6}\) undergoing complete aerobic oxidation to \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). Determine its balanced equation and Respiratory Quotient (RQ).

Solution:

  1. Balance carbon and hydrogen on the product side for 1 molecule of \(\mathrm{C_{40}H_{76}O_6}\): \[40\,\mathrm{CO_2} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{76}{2} = 38\,\mathrm{H_2O}\]
  2. Count total oxygen atoms on the product side: \((40 \times 2) + 38 = 118\text{ O atoms}\).
  3. Subtract the 6 oxygen atoms already present in \(\mathrm{C_{40}H_{76}O_6}\): \(118 - 6 = 112\text{ O atoms}\) required from \(\mathrm{O_2}\), which equals \(\frac{112}{2} = 56\,\mathrm{O_2}\) molecules.
  4. Balanced equation: \(\mathrm{C_{40}H_{76}O_6 + 56\,O_2 \longrightarrow 40\,CO_2 + 38\,H_2O}\).
  5. RQ: \(\mathrm{RQ} = \frac{40}{56} \approx 0.71\), confirming that lipid oxidation yields an RQ less than 1.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following conversions in glycolysis involves both oxidation of the substrate and generation of a high-energy phosphorylated intermediate without consuming ATP?

  • (A) Glucose → Glucose-6-phosphate
  • (B) Fructose-6-phosphate → Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate
  • (C) 3-Phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL) → 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA)
  • (D) Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) → Pyruvic acid
Correct Answer: (C)
Explanation: During the conversion of PGAL to 1,3-BPGA, two hydrogen atoms are removed and transferred to \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) (forming \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\)), while inorganic phosphate (\(\mathrm{P_i}\)) is incorporated into the molecule without spending ATP. Options (A) and (B) consume ATP, and (D) is a substrate-level phosphorylation step.
Practice Question 2 (Statement-Based)

Read the following two statements carefully and choose the correct option:

Statement I: Yeast cells can continue alcoholic fermentation indefinitely until all glucose in a medium is exhausted, regardless of ethanol accumulation.
Statement II: In both lactic acid fermentation and alcoholic fermentation, \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) is reoxidised to \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (D)
Explanation: Statement I is incorrect because yeasts poison themselves to death when ethanol concentration reaches about 13%. Statement II is correct because regenerating \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) from \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) is essential in both fermentation types to keep glycolysis running.
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following is a mobile electron carrier located on the outer surface of the inner mitochondrial membrane that transfers electrons between Complex III and Complex IV?

  • (A) Ubiquinone
  • (B) Cytochrome c
  • (C) Cytochrome \(a_3\)
  • (D) Succinate dehydrogenase
Correct Answer: (B)
Explanation: Cytochrome c is a small mobile protein attached to the outer surface of the inner mitochondrial membrane, shuttling electrons from Complex III (cytochrome \(bc_1\)) to Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase). Ubiquinone is within the membrane, cytochrome \(a_3\) is part of Complex IV, and succinate dehydrogenase is Complex II.
Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): The respiratory pathway is better described as an amphibolic pathway rather than a purely catabolic pathway.
Reason (R): Intermediates of the respiratory pathway, such as acetyl CoA and α-ketoglutarate, are withdrawn for the synthesis of fatty acids and amino acids, respectively, in addition to being formed during substrate breakdown.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Correct Answer: (A)
Explanation: An amphibolic pathway involves both catabolism (breakdown) and anabolism (synthesis). Because respiratory intermediates are both produced from macromolecules and withdrawn as biosynthetic precursors, Reason (R) accurately explains Assertion (A).
Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the mitochondrial matrix, how many decarboxylation steps occur during the complete oxidation of one molecule of pyruvic acid (including the link reaction and one turn of the Krebs' cycle)?

  • (A) 1
  • (B) 2
  • (C) 3
  • (D) 6
Correct Answer: (C)
Explanation: Pyruvic acid (3C) loses 1 \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) during the link reaction (pyruvate → acetyl CoA) and 2 \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) during one turn of the Krebs' cycle (isocitrate → α-ketoglutarate, and α-ketoglutarate → succinyl-CoA), totaling 3 decarboxylations per pyruvate.

Practice Question 7 (Numerical MCQ)

Suppose a hypothetical plant tissue oxidises \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of glucose completely via aerobic respiration under the standard theoretical balance-sheet assumptions. How many moles of \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\) are produced exclusively in the Krebs' cycle?

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements about plant gas exchange is incorrect?

  • (A) Plants lack specialised respiratory organs and rely on stomata and lenticels.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

  • (B) 0.9

Explanation: Complete oxidation of tripalmitin consumes \(145\,\mathrm{O_2}\) and releases \(102\,\mathrm{CO_2}\) for every 2 molecules, giving \(\mathrm{RQ} = 102/145 = 0.7\). Carbohydrates have \(\mathrm{RQ} = 1.0\), and proteins have \(\mathrm{RQ} \approx 0.9\).

6. Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Link reaction vs. Krebs' cycle: Do not count the \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) formed by pyruvate dehydrogenase as part of the TCA cycle itself; the TCA cycle alone produces 3 \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), 1 \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\), and 1 GTP per acetyl CoA (total 4 \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\) per pyruvate if the link reaction is included).
  • \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) in fermentation: Alcoholic fermentation releases \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), whereas lactic acid fermentation does not release \(\mathrm{CO_2}\).
  • Role of \(\mathrm{O_2}\): Oxygen is required only at the terminal step (Complex IV) as the final electron/hydrogen acceptor, yet without it, NADH and \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\) cannot be reoxidised and the TCA cycle stops.
  • Proton stoichiometry: \(4\,\mathrm{H^+}\) pass through \(\mathrm{F_0}\) for each ATP molecule synthesised at \(\mathrm{F_1}\).
Final Recap

Cellular respiration oxidises organic substrates in controlled, enzyme-mediated steps to capture energy as ATP. Glycolysis in the cytoplasm splits glucose into two pyruvates with a net gain of 2 ATP and \(2\,\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\). Under anaerobic conditions, fermentation regenerates \(\mathrm{NAD^+}\) with a net yield of just 2 ATP. In the presence of \(\mathrm{O_2}\), pyruvate enters the mitochondrial matrix for the link reaction and Krebs' cycle, generating \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), GTP, \(\mathrm{NADH + H^+}\), and \(\mathrm{FADH_2}\). The inner mitochondrial membrane ETS transfers electrons to \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and establishes a proton gradient that drives Complex V to yield a theoretical total of 38 ATP per glucose. Because its intermediates are both derived from and used to build macromolecules, respiration is an amphibolic pathway whose substrate mix is reflected by the Respiratory Quotient.
