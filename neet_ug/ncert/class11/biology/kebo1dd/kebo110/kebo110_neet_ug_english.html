Cell Cycle and Cell Division

A comprehensive, exam-focused tutorial covering interphase regulation, mitosis, meiosis, ploidy dynamics, and NEET UG problem-solving.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Context

Every multicellular organism begins life as a single cell—a zygote—and grows through coordinated cycles of cell growth, genome duplication, and division. In the NEET UG Biology syllabus, this chapter is a high-yield bridge between cell structure and genetics: examiners frequently test exact stage-specific events (especially Prophase I sub-stages), organelle disappearance and reformation, and quantitative tracking of chromosome number (\(n\)) and DNA content (\(C\)).

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish the preparative phases of interphase (\(G_1\), \(S\), \(G_2\)) and the quiescent stage (\(G_0\)) by metabolic, organelle, and DNA events.
  • Sequence karyokinesis and cytokinesis in mitosis, contrasting animal and plant mechanisms.
  • Explain all five sub-stages of Prophase I, homologous segregation in Anaphase I, and sister-chromatid separation in Anaphase II.
  • Compute chromosome counts (\(n\)), chromatid counts, and DNA mass (\(C\)) at any stage of mitosis or meiosis.

2. The Eukaryotic Cell Cycle & Interphase

A cell cannot simply split in half repeatedly without first duplicating its genetic blueprint and cytoplasmic machinery. The cell cycle is the genetically regulated sequence of events in which a cell grows, replicates its genome, synthesizes essential proteins and organelles, and divides into two daughter cells.

While cytoplasmic growth is largely continuous throughout the cycle, DNA synthesis occurs exclusively during one restricted window (the \(S\) phase). Cell-cycle duration varies widely across species and tissues: human cells in culture complete one cycle roughly every 24 hours, whereas budding yeast completes a cycle in approximately 90 minutes.

2.1 Two Major Divisions: Interphase vs. M Phase

  • Interphase (Preparatory Phase): Occupies more than 95% of the total cycle duration (about 23 hours in a 24-hour human cell cycle). Historically misnamed the "resting phase," it is actually a period of intense metabolic activity, growth, and DNA replication.
  • M Phase (Mitotic / Division Phase): Occupies less than 5% of the cycle (roughly 1 hour in human culture cells). It begins with nuclear division (karyokinesis) and usually concludes with cytoplasmic division (cytokinesis).

2.2 Sub-Phases of Interphase and the \(G_0\) Exit

  1. \(G_1\) Phase (Gap 1): The interval between the end of mitosis and the start of DNA replication. The cell is metabolically active, grows in size, and duplicates most organelles, but does not replicate DNA.
  2. \(S\) Phase (Synthesis): DNA replication takes place in the nucleus, doubling the DNA content from \(2C\) to \(4C\) in a diploid cell. Crucially, the chromosome number does not change: a diploid cell with \(2n\) chromosomes before \(S\) phase still has \(2n\) chromosomes after \(S\) phase, because each chromosome now consists of two sister chromatids joined at a single centromere. In animal cells, centriole duplication occurs in the cytoplasm during \(S\) phase.
  3. \(G_2\) Phase (Gap 2): The cell continues growing and synthesizes proteins (such as tubulin for spindle assembly) required for mitosis.
  4. \(G_0\) Phase (Quiescent Stage): Cells that do not divide further (such as adult mammalian cardiac muscle cells) or divide only rarely to replace damaged tissue exit \(G_1\) into an inactive proliferative state called \(G_0\). Cells in \(G_0\) remain metabolically active; they simply suspend proliferation unless signaled to re-enter the cycle.
Overview of interphase sub-stages, the \(G_0\) quiescent exit, and conservation of chromosome number (\(2n\)) despite DNA doubling (\(2C \to 4C\)) in \(S\) phase.

3. Mitosis (Equational Division) & Cytokinesis

Mitosis is termed equational division because the parent cell and both resulting daughter cells possess the exact same chromosome number and genetic complement. Although karyokinesis is a continuous, progressive process, it is studied in four sequential stages: Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, and Telophase.

3.1 Stages of Mitotic Karyokinesis

  • Prophase: Follows \(G_2\). Intertwined chromatin fibers untangle and condense into compact mitotic chromosomes, each visibly composed of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. Duplicated centrosomes migrate toward opposite poles; each radiates microtubules called asters, and the two asters plus spindle fibers constitute the mitotic apparatus. By the end of prophase, the nucleolus, nuclear envelope, Golgi complex, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) disappear.
  • Metaphase: Initiated by complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope, releasing chromosomes into the cytoplasm. Chromosomal condensation reaches its maximum, making metaphase the best stage to study chromosome morphology. Spindle fibers attach to disc-shaped protein structures on the centromere surface called kinetochores. All chromosomes align along the spindle equator, forming the metaphase plate, with one sister chromatid attached to one pole and the other to the opposite pole.
  • Anaphase: Each centromere splits simultaneously, separating sister chromatids into independent daughter chromosomes. As spindle fibers shorten, daughter chromosomes migrate toward opposite poles with the centromere at the leading edge and the chromosomal arms trailing behind.
  • Telophase: Daughter chromosomes cluster at opposite poles, decondense, and lose their discrete individuality. The nuclear envelope reforms around each chromatin cluster, and the nucleolus, Golgi complex, and ER reappear, yielding two daughter nuclei.

3.2 Cytokinesis: Animal vs. Plant Cells

Nuclear division is normally followed by cytoplasmic division (cytokinesis), which also distributes organelles such as mitochondria and plastids to the two daughter cells:

  • Animal Cells (Centripetal Cleavage): A cleavage furrow appears in the plasma membrane at the cell equator and deepens inward until it meets at the center, pinching the cell into two.
  • Plant Cells (Centrifugal Cell Plate): Because plant cells are enclosed by a rigid, inextensible cell wall, a furrow cannot form. Instead, wall formation begins at the center of the cell as a precursor called the cell-plate (representing the future middle lamella) and grows outward to fuse with the existing lateral walls.
Key Exception — Syncytium Formation

If karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis, a single cell accumulates multiple nuclei, producing a syncytium. A classic botanical example is the liquid endosperm of coconut.

Contrasting directional growth of cytokinesis in animal cells (centripetal furrow) versus walled plant cells (centrifugal cell plate).

3.3 Significance & Ploidy Exceptions in Mitosis

  • Growth & Nucleo-Cytoplasmic Ratio: Cell growth increases cytoplasmic volume relative to nuclear control, disturbing the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio; mitosis divides the cell to restore this balance.
  • Repair & Continuous Growth: Constantly replaces worn-out cells (skin epidermis, gut lining, blood cells) and drives lifelong plant growth via apical and lateral meristems.
  • Ploidy Exceptions: In animals, mitosis is generally restricted to diploid somatic cells, but haploid cells divide by mitosis in male honeybees (drones). In plants, mitosis occurs in both diploid and haploid cells (for example, haploid gametophytes of bryophytes, pteridophytes, and algae).

4. Meiosis (Reductional Division)

Sexual reproduction relies on the fusion of two haploid gametes to restore the diploid chromosome number in the zygote. Meiosis is the specialized division of diploid meiocytes that halves the chromosome number to produce four haploid daughter cells. It involves two sequential nuclear and cell divisions (Meiosis I and Meiosis II) preceded by only one round of DNA replication in the \(S\) phase.

4.1 Meiosis I: Reductional Division & Recombination

Prophase I (Five Sub-Stages)

Prophase I is markedly longer and more complex than mitotic prophase, and is subdivided by chromosomal behavior:

  1. Leptotene: Chromosomes gradually become visible under the light microscope as compaction progresses.
  2. Zygotene: Homologous chromosomes pair side-by-side in a process called synapsis, stabilized by a proteinaceous synaptonemal complex. Each paired homologous unit is called a bivalent (or tetrad).
  3. Pachytene: The four chromatids of each bivalent become clearly distinct as a tetrad. Recombination nodules appear, marking sites of crossing over—the enzyme-mediated exchange of genetic segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes catalyzed by recombinase. Recombination is completed by the end of pachytene.
  4. Diplotene: The synaptonemal complex dissolves, and recombined homologs begin separating except at crossover points, forming characteristic X-shaped structures called chiasmata. In oocytes of some vertebrates, diplotene can remain arrested for months or years.
  5. Diakinesis: Marked by terminalisation of chiasmata (chiasmata shift toward chromosome tips). Chromosomes reach full condensation, the meiotic spindle assembles, and the nucleolus and nuclear envelope break down—transitioning to Metaphase I.

Metaphase I, Anaphase I, Telophase I & Interkinesis

  • Metaphase I: Bivalents align along the equatorial plate in two parallel rows; spindle microtubules from opposite poles attach to the kinetochores of homologous chromosomes (not sister chromatids of the same chromosome).
  • Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate and move to opposite poles, reducing the chromosome number per pole from \(2n\) to \(n\). Crucially, centromeres do not split, so sister chromatids remain attached together.
  • Telophase I & Interkinesis: Nuclear membrane and nucleolus reappear, and cytokinesis produces a dyad of cells. The brief interval between Meiosis I and Meiosis II is interkinesis; no DNA replication occurs during interkinesis.

4.2 Meiosis II: Equational Separation of Sister Chromatids

Meiosis II mechanically mirrors mitosis, separating the sister chromatids of each chromosome in the two haploid dyad cells:

  • Prophase II: Initiated immediately after cytokinesis I; chromosomes re-condense and the nuclear envelope disappears.
  • Metaphase II: Chromosomes align at the equator; spindle microtubules from opposite poles attach to the kinetochores of sister chromatids.
  • Anaphase II: Centromeres split simultaneously, allowing sister chromatids (now daughter chromosomes) to move to opposite poles.
  • Telophase II: Nuclear envelopes enclose the four chromosome sets, and cytokinesis yields a tetrad of four haploid daughter cells.
Mechanistic contrast between homologous chromosome separation in Anaphase I and centromere splitting in Anaphase II.

4.3 Biological Significance of Meiosis

  • Chromosome Number Conservation: Halves the chromosome count in gametes so that fertilization restores the species-specific diploid number across generations.
  • Genetic Variability: Crossing over (pachytene) and independent assortment of homologs (metaphase I / anaphase I) generate new genetic combinations, providing raw material for evolution.

6. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Tracking Chromosomes and DNA Mass in Mitosis

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumptions): A newly discovered diploid plant species has \(2n = 24\) chromosomes in its root meristem cells. Assume that at the \(G_1\) phase, the total nuclear DNA mass of one cell is \(12\,\mathrm{pg}\) (picograms). Determine the chromosome number, chromatid count, and DNA mass at: (a) \(G_2\) phase, (b) mitotic Metaphase, and (c) each daughter cell immediately after cytokinesis.

Solution:

  1. At \(G_2\) phase: The cell has passed through \(S\) phase. DNA mass doubles from \(2C = 12\,\mathrm{pg}\) to \(4C = 24\,\mathrm{pg}\), while chromosome number remains \(2n = 24\). Each chromosome now has 2 sister chromatids, giving \(24 \times 2 = 48\) chromatids.
  2. At mitotic Metaphase: No DNA replication or centromere splitting has occurred yet. Therefore, chromosome number = \(24\), chromatid count = \(48\), and DNA mass = \(24\,\mathrm{pg}\).
  3. In each daughter cell after cytokinesis: Sister chromatids separated at Anaphase and partitioned equally into two nuclei. Each daughter cell receives \(24\) chromosomes (each with 1 chromatid, so \(24\) chromatids) and \(24 / 2 = 12\,\mathrm{pg}\) of DNA.

Worked Example 2: Bivalents, Kinetochores, and DNA Content in Meiosis

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumptions): A diploid animal meiocyte has \(2n = 18\) chromosomes and an assumed \(G_1\) DNA content of \(20\,\mathrm{pg}\). Calculate: (a) the number of bivalents (tetrads) visible in Pachytene, (b) the DNA mass in each dyad cell after Telophase I, and (c) the number of chromosomes and DNA mass in each gamete after Meiosis II.

Solution:

  1. Bivalents in Pachytene: Homologous chromosomes pair in 1:1 fashion. With \(2n = 18\), there are \(n = 18 / 2 = 9\) homologous pairs, so 9 bivalents (and \(9 \times 4 = 36\) chromatids) are visible.
  2. DNA mass after Telophase I: Before Meiosis I, \(S\) phase doubles DNA from \(20\,\mathrm{pg}\) to \(40\,\mathrm{pg}\). Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes into two dyad cells, halving both chromosome number (\(n = 9\)) and DNA mass (\(40 / 2 = 20\,\mathrm{pg}\)) per dyad cell.
  3. Gamete after Meiosis II: Meiosis II separates sister chromatids without further DNA replication. Each of the four gametes receives \(n = 9\) chromosomes and \(20 / 2 = 10\,\mathrm{pg}\) of DNA.

7. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All items below are original practice questions designed for NEET UG preparation.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

During which sub-stage of Prophase I does the enzyme recombinase mediate crossing over between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes?

  • (A) Zygotene
  • (B) Pachytene
  • (C) Diplotene
  • (D) Diakinesis
Correct Answer: (B) Pachytene
Explanation: Synapsis occurs in Zygotene, but recombination nodules and recombinase-mediated crossing over occur during Pachytene. Diplotene shows chiasmata after synaptonemal complex dissolution, and Diakinesis marks terminalisation of chiasmata.
Practice Question 2 Ploidy & DNA Calculation

Suppose a hypothetical diploid plant cell has \(2n = 20\) chromosomes and \(2C = 16\,\mathrm{pg}\) of DNA in the \(G_1\) phase. What will be the chromosome number and DNA content of this cell in the \(G_2\) phase?

  • (A) \(20\) chromosomes, \(16\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
  • (B) \(40\) chromosomes, \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
  • (C) \(20\) chromosomes, \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
  • (D) \(40\) chromosomes, \(16\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
Correct Answer: (C) \(20\) chromosomes, \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\) DNA
Explanation: During \(S\) phase, DNA replicates so DNA mass doubles from \(16\,\mathrm{pg}\) to \(32\,\mathrm{pg}\). However, sister chromatids remain joined at a single centromere, so the chromosome count stays at \(20\).
Practice Question 3 Statement I & II

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Cells in the \(G_0\) (quiescent) stage are metabolically inactive and undergo rapid apoptosis.
Statement II: In animal cells, centriole duplication takes place in the cytoplasm during the \(S\) phase of interphase.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Explanation: Cells in \(G_0\) exit the proliferative cycle from \(G_1\) but remain metabolically active (for example, adult cardiac cells). Statement II is true: in animal cells, nuclear DNA replication and cytoplasmic centriole duplication both occur during \(S\) phase.
Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following events distinguishes Anaphase of mitosis from Anaphase I of meiosis?

  • (A) Chromosomes condense to maximum thickness.
  • (B) Centromeres split and sister chromatids move to opposite poles.
  • (C) Homologous chromosomes separate while sister chromatids remain joined.
  • (D) The nuclear envelope reforms around daughter chromatin.
Correct Answer: (B) Centromeres split and sister chromatids move to opposite poles.
Explanation: In mitotic Anaphase (and Anaphase II), centromeres split so sister chromatids separate. In Anaphase I, homologous chromosomes separate while centromeres remain intact.

Practice Question 6 Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Cytokinesis in higher plant cells occurs via cell-plate formation rather than a cleavage furrow.
Reason (R): Plant cells are surrounded by a relatively inextensible cell wall, so new wall material is laid down centrifugally from the center.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
Explanation: Because the rigid plant cell wall prevents inward furrowing of the plasma membrane, cytokinesis proceeds outward (centrifugally) from a central cell-plate that becomes the middle lamella.
Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

In some organisms, karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis, resulting in a multinucleate condition called a syncytium. Which of the following is a classic example of this phenomenon?

  • (A) Onion root tip meristem
  • (B) Liquid endosperm of coconut
  • (C) Male honeybee somatic cells
  • (D) Mature mammalian erythrocytes
Correct Answer: (B) Liquid endosperm of coconut
Explanation: Free nuclear divisions without immediate cytokinesis in the coconut endosperm produce a multinucleate liquid syncytium.
Practice Question 8 Single-Correct MCQ

What is the correct sequence of the five sub-stages of Prophase I in meiosis?

  • (A) Leptotene → Pachytene → Zygotene → Diplotene → Diakinesis
  • (B) Zygotene → Leptotene → Pachytene → Diakinesis → Diplotene
  • (C) Leptotene → Zygotene → Pachytene → Diplotene → Diakinesis
  • (D) Leptotene → Zygotene → Diplotene → Pachytene → Diakinesis
Correct Answer: (C) Leptotene → Zygotene → Pachytene → Diplotene → Diakinesis
Explanation: Chromosome compaction starts in Leptotene, synapsis in Zygotene, crossing over in Pachytene, chiasmata visibility in Diplotene, and terminalisation in Diakinesis.
Practice Question 9 Single-Correct MCQ

Which statement accurately describes interkinesis during meiosis?

  • (A) It is a long resting phase during which DNA replicates before Meiosis II.
  • (B) It is a short-lived stage between Meiosis I and Meiosis II with no DNA replication.
  • (C) It occurs between \(G_1\) and \(S\) phase to check DNA damage.
  • (D) It is the stage where bivalents align at the equatorial plate.
Correct Answer: (B) It is a short-lived stage between Meiosis I and Meiosis II with no DNA replication.
Explanation: Meiosis involves only one round of DNA replication (prior to Meiosis I). Interkinesis is brief and lacks DNA synthesis, leading directly into Prophase II.
Practice Question 10 Numerical Reasoning MCQ

Suppose a hypothetical diploid flowering plant has \(2n = 14\) chromosomes. How many bivalents are formed during Zygotene, and how many chromosomes will each pollen grain (haploid microspore) contain after Meiosis II?

  • (A) 14 bivalents; 14 chromosomes
  • (B) 7 bivalents; 14 chromosomes
  • (C) 7 bivalents; 7 chromosomes
  • (D) 14 bivalents; 7 chromosomes
Correct Answer: (C) 7 bivalents; 7 chromosomes
Explanation: Each bivalent consists of 2 homologous chromosomes, so \(2n = 14\) yields \(14 / 2 = 7\) bivalents. Meiosis reduces the chromosome number by half, giving \(n = 7\) chromosomes per haploid microspore.

8. Common NEET Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • DNA doubles, chromosome count does NOT: After \(S\) phase, DNA is \(4C\) instead of \(2C\), but chromosome number stays \(2n\).
  • Centromere splitting timing: Centromeres split in mitotic Anaphase and Meiosis II Anaphase II—never in Meiosis I Anaphase I.
  • Pachytene vs. Diplotene vs. Diakinesis: Crossing over occurs in Pachytene; X-shaped chiasmata become visible upon synaptonemal complex dissolution in Diplotene; chiasmata terminalise in Diakinesis.
  • Interkinesis has NO \(S\) phase: DNA is never replicated between Meiosis I and Meiosis II.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Interphase (>95% of cycle): \(G_1\) (growth), \(S\) (DNA \(2C \to 4C\), centriole duplication in animals), \(G_2\) (mitotic protein synthesis); \(G_0\) is metabolically active but non-dividing.
  • Mitosis: Prophase (condensation; ER, Golgi, nucleolus, nuclear envelope vanish), Metaphase (kinetochore attachment, morphology study), Anaphase (centromere splits), Telophase (nuclear reformation).
  • Cytokinesis: Animal (centripetal furrow) vs. Plant (centrifugal cell-plate); syncytium lacks cytokinesis after karyokinesis.
  • Meiosis: Two divisions, one \(S\) phase → four genetically distinct haploid cells; conserves species chromosome number and drives evolutionary variation.
Concise Final Recap

Cell division is tightly coordinated with genome replication: mitosis preserves ploidy (\(2n \to 2n\)) for growth, repair, and asexual continuity, whereas meiosis halves ploidy (\(2n \to n\)) and shuffles alleles via crossing over and independent assortment to sustain sexual life cycles and genetic diversity.
