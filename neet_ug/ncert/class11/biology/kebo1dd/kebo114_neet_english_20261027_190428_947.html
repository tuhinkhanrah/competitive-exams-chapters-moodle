Human Physiology: Breathing and Exchange of Gases

Target Examination: NEET UG (Biology — Human Physiology)

Scope: Respiratory Organs, Mechanism of Breathing, Respiratory Volumes and Capacities, Exchange of Gases, Transport of Gases, Regulation of Respiration, and Respiratory Disorders

1. Unit Context, Pioneers, and Learning Objectives

Systems Biology vs. Reductionist Approach in Physiology

Historically, the study of living organisms relied heavily on a reductionist approach, which applies physico-chemical concepts and techniques to surviving tissue models or cell-free systems. This approach caused an explosion of knowledge in molecular biology, making molecular physiology nearly synonymous with biochemistry and biophysics. However, neither a purely organismic approach nor a purely reductionist molecular approach reveals the complete truth about biological processes.

Systems biology demonstrates that living phenomena are emergent properties arising from interactions among components of a system. Regulatory networks of molecules, supramolecular assemblies, cells, tissues, organisms, populations, and communities each generate emergent properties. In human physiology, processes such as gas exchange and blood circulation are understood at cellular and molecular levels while being coordinated at the whole-organism level.

Historical Pioneer in Mammalian Physiology: Alfonso Corti (1822–1888)

Alfonso Corti, an Italian anatomist born in 1822, began his scientific career studying the cardiovascular systems of reptiles. Later, he turned his attention to the mammalian auditory system. In 1851, he published a landmark paper describing a structure located on the basilar membrane of the cochlea containing hair cells that convert sound vibrations into nerve impulses—now known as the organ of Corti. He died in 1888.

2. Core Concepts and Detailed Explanations

2.1 Introduction to Breathing and Respiration

Organisms utilise oxygen (\( \mathrm{O_2} \)) to indirectly break down simple molecules such as glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids to derive energy for cellular activities. During these catabolic reactions, carbon dioxide (\( \mathrm{CO_2} \)), which is harmful to cells, is released. Therefore, \( \mathrm{O_2} \) must be continuously supplied to cells and \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) must be continuously removed. The process of exchanging \( \mathrm{O_2} \) from the atmosphere with \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) produced by the cells is called breathing, commonly known as respiration.

Five Sequential Steps of Human Respiration:
  1. Breathing or pulmonary ventilation: Drawing in atmospheric air and releasing \( \mathrm{CO_2} \)-rich alveolar air.
  2. Diffusion across alveolar membrane: Exchange of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) and \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) across the alveolar-capillary barrier.
  3. Transport of gases: Carriage of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) and \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) by the circulating blood.
  4. Diffusion between blood and tissues: Uptake of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) by tissues and release of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) into blood.
  5. Cellular respiration: Utilisation of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) by cells for catabolic reactions and resultant production of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \).

2.2 Respiratory Organs Across the Animal Kingdom

Breathing mechanisms vary among animal groups depending primarily on their habitats and levels of organisation:

Animal Group Respiratory Structure / Mode Key Mechanism Details
Lower invertebrates (Sponges, Coelenterates, Flatworms) Entire body surface Simple diffusion of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) and \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) directly across the general body surface.
Earthworms Moist cuticle (Cutaneous respiration) Gas exchange through the moist outer cuticle covering the body.
Insects Tracheal tubes (Tracheal respiration) A network of tubes transports atmospheric air directly within the body to tissues.
Aquatic arthropods, Molluscs, Fishes Gills (Branchial respiration) Special vascularised structures adapted for gas exchange in aquatic habitats.
Amphibians (e.g., Frogs) Lungs (Pulmonary) and Moist skin (Cutaneous) Respire through vascularised bags (lungs) as well as their moist skin.
Reptiles, Birds, Mammals Lungs (Pulmonary respiration) Terrestrial forms using highly vascularised lungs for gas exchange.

2.3 Human Respiratory System

The human respiratory pathway follows a strict anatomical sequence from the exterior to the gas-exchange surfaces:

External nostrils (pair opening above the upper lips) → Nasal passage → Nasal chamber → Pharynx (a portion serves as the common passage for food and air) → Larynx (through the glottis) → Trachea → Right and Left Primary Bronchi → Secondary Bronchi → Tertiary Bronchi → Initial Bronchioles → Terminal Bronchioles → Alveoli and alveolar ducts.

  • Larynx and Epiglottis: The larynx is a cartilaginous box that helps in sound production and is therefore called the sound box. During swallowing, the glottis can be covered by a thin elastic cartilaginous flap called the epiglottis to prevent food from entering the larynx.
  • Trachea and Bronchial Tree: The trachea is a straight tube extending up to the mid-thoracic cavity, where it divides at the level of the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left primary bronchi.
  • Cartilaginous Support: The trachea, primary, secondary, and tertiary bronchi, and initial bronchioles are supported by incomplete cartilaginous rings to prevent collapse.
  • Alveoli: Each terminal bronchiole gives rise to a number of very thin, irregular-walled, and vascularised bag-like structures called alveoli. The branching network of bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli comprises the lungs.
  • Pleura: Humans have two lungs covered by a double-layered pleura, with pleural fluid between the layers. This fluid reduces friction on the lung surface. The outer pleural membrane is in close contact with the thoracic lining, whereas the inner pleural membrane is in contact with the lung surface.

Anatomical Extent External nostrils up to the terminal bronchioles. Alveoli and their ducts.

2. Clears air from foreign particles.

The Thoracic Chamber

The lungs are situated in the thoracic chamber, which is anatomically an air-tight chamber. Its boundaries are formed by:

  • Dorsally: Vertebral column
  • Ventrally: Sternum
  • Laterally: Ribs
  • Lower side: Dome-shaped diaphragm

Because we cannot directly alter pulmonary (lung) volume, this anatomical setup is essential: any change in the volume of the thoracic cavity is immediately reflected in the lung (pulmonary) cavity.

2.4 Mechanism of Breathing

Breathing involves two stages: inspiration (drawing atmospheric air in) and expiration (releasing alveolar air out). Air moves into and out of the lungs due to a pressure gradient generated between the lungs and the atmosphere by the diaphragm and a specialised set of muscles—the external and internal intercostals between the ribs.

Parameter Inspiration (Active Process) Normal Expiration (Passive Relaxation)
Intra-pulmonary Pressure vs. Atmospheric Pressure Intra-pulmonary pressure is less than atmospheric pressure (negative pressure in lungs with respect to atmosphere). Intra-pulmonary pressure is slightly higher than atmospheric pressure (positive pressure with respect to atmosphere).
Diaphragm Action Contracts (flattens), increasing thoracic volume in the antero-posterior axis. Relaxes and arches upwards to its normal dome-shaped position.
External Intercostal Muscles Contract, lifting up the ribs and sternum, increasing thoracic volume in the dorso-ventral axis. Relax, returning the ribs and sternum to their original positions.
Thoracic & Pulmonary Volume Thoracic volume increases → Pulmonary volume increases → Intra-pulmonary pressure decreases. Thoracic volume decreases → Pulmonary volume decreases → Intra-pulmonary pressure increases.
Net Air Movement Air moves from outside into the lungs. Air is expelled from the lungs to the outside.
  • Forceful Breathing: Humans have the ability to increase the strength of inspiration and expiration with the help of additional muscles in the abdomen.
  • Breathing Rate: On average, a healthy human breathes 12–16 times/minute.
  • Spirometer: The volume of air involved in breathing movements can be estimated using a spirometer, which helps in the clinical assessment of pulmonary functions. (Note: Residual volume cannot be directly measured by simple spirometry because it is never expelled from the lungs).

3. Respiratory Volumes, Capacities, and Partial Pressures

3.1 Respiratory Volumes

Respiratory Volume Abbreviation Standard Definition Normal Average Value
Tidal Volume \( \mathrm{TV} \) Volume of air inspired or expired during a normal respiration. (Minute volume: a healthy human inspires/expires approx. \( 6000\text{ to }8000\,\mathrm{mL/min} \)). \( \approx 500\,\mathrm{mL} \)
Inspiratory Reserve Volume \( \mathrm{IRV} \) Additional volume of air a person can inspire by a forcible inspiration. \( 2500\text{ to }3000\,\mathrm{mL} \)
Expiratory Reserve Volume \( \mathrm{ERV} \) Additional volume of air a person can expire by a forcible expiration. \( 1000\text{ to }1100\,\mathrm{mL} \)
Residual Volume \( \mathrm{RV} \) Volume of air remaining in the lungs even after a forcible expiration. \( 1100\text{ to }1200\,\mathrm{mL} \)

3.2 Pulmonary Capacities (Derived for Clinical Diagnosis)

By adding specific respiratory volumes, various pulmonary capacities are derived for clinical assessment:

  • Inspiratory Capacity (\( \mathrm{IC} \)): Total volume of air a person can inspire after a normal expiration. \[ \mathrm{IC} = \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV} \approx 500 + (2500\text{ to }3000) = 3000\text{ to }3500\,\mathrm{mL} \]
  • Expiratory Capacity (\( \mathrm{EC} \)): Total volume of air a person can expire after a normal inspiration. \[ \mathrm{EC} = \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{ERV} \approx 500 + (1000\text{ to }1100) = 1500\text{ to }1600\,\mathrm{mL} \]
  • Functional Residual Capacity (\( \mathrm{FRC} \)): Volume of air that will remain in the lungs after a normal expiration. \[ \mathrm{FRC} = \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{RV} \approx (1000\text{ to }1100) + (1100\text{ to }1200) = 2100\text{ to }2300\,\mathrm{mL} \]
  • Vital Capacity (\( \mathrm{VC} \)): Maximum volume of air a person can breathe in after a forced expiration, OR the maximum volume of air a person can breathe out after a forced inspiration. \[ \mathrm{VC} = \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV} \approx 4000\text{ to }4600\,\mathrm{mL} \]
  • Total Lung Capacity (\( \mathrm{TLC} \)): Total volume of air accommodated in the lungs at the end of a forced inspiration. \[ \mathrm{TLC} = \mathrm{RV} + \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV} = \mathrm{VC} + \mathrm{RV} \approx 5100\text{ to }5800\,\mathrm{mL} \]

3.3 Exchange of Gases and Partial Pressures

Alveoli are the primary sites of gas exchange; exchange also occurs between blood and tissues. \( \mathrm{O_2} \) and \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) are exchanged at these sites by simple diffusion mainly driven by pressure/concentration gradients.

The pressure contributed by an individual gas in a mixture of gases is called its partial pressure, represented as \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) for oxygen and \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \) for carbon dioxide.

Partial Pressures (in \( \mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)) of Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide at Different Parts Involved in Diffusion
Respiratory Gas Atmospheric Air Alveoli Blood (Deoxygenated) Blood (Oxygenated) Tissues
\( \mathrm{O_2} \) (\( \mathrm{pO_2} \)) \( 159 \) \( 104 \) \( 40 \) \( 95 \) \( 40 \)
\( \mathrm{CO_2} \) (\( \mathrm{pCO_2} \)) \( 0.3 \) \( 40 \) \( 45 \) \( 40 \) \( 45 \)

Factors Affecting Rate of Diffusion

  1. Partial Pressure / Concentration Gradient:
    • For \( \mathrm{O_2} \): Continuous gradient from Alveoli (\( 104\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)) → Deoxygenated blood (\( 40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)), and from Oxygenated blood (\( 95\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)) → Tissues (\( 40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)).
    • For \( \mathrm{CO_2} \): Continuous gradient in the opposite direction, from Tissues (\( 45\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)) → Blood (\( 40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)), and from Deoxygenated blood (\( 45\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)) → Alveoli (\( 40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)).
  2. Solubility of the Gases: The solubility of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) is 20–25 times higher than that of \( \mathrm{O_2} \). Consequently, the amount of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) that can diffuse through the diffusion membrane per unit difference in partial pressure is much higher compared to that of \( \mathrm{O_2} \).
  3. Thickness of the Diffusion Membrane: The alveolar-capillary diffusion membrane consists of three major layers, yet its total thickness is much less than a millimetre:
    • Thin squamous epithelium of alveoli (one-celled thick).
    • Endothelium of alveolar capillaries (single layer of endothelial cells).
    • Basement substance in between them (composed of a thin basement membrane supporting the alveolar squamous epithelium and the basement membrane surrounding the capillary endothelial cells).

4. Transport of Gases, Regulation, and Disorders

4.1 Quantitative Overview of Gas Transport in Blood

Blood is the medium of transport for both \( \mathrm{O_2} \) and \( \mathrm{CO_2} \):

Gas Mode of Transport Percentage Carried
Oxygen (\( \mathrm{O_2} \)) By RBCs (bound to haemoglobin as oxyhaemoglobin) \( \approx 97\% \)
Dissolved state through blood plasma \( \approx 3\% \)
Carbon Dioxide (\( \mathrm{CO_2} \)) As bicarbonate (\( \mathrm{HCO_3^-} \)) \( \approx 70\% \)
By RBCs (bound to haemoglobin as carbamino-haemoglobin) \( \approx 20\text{–}25\% \)
Dissolved state through blood plasma \( \approx 7\% \)

4.2 Transport of Oxygen and the Oxygen Dissociation Curve

  • Haemoglobin (\( \mathrm{Hb} \)): A red-coloured, iron-containing pigment present in RBCs. \( \mathrm{O_2} \) binds with haemoglobin in a reversible manner to form oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Stoichiometry: Each haemoglobin molecule can carry a maximum of four molecules of \( \mathrm{O_2} \).
  • Oxygen Dissociation Curve: When percentage saturation of haemoglobin with \( \mathrm{O_2} \) is plotted against \( \mathrm{pO_2} \), a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve is obtained. Its sigmoidal pattern arises from cooperative binding (binding of the first \( \mathrm{O_2} \) molecule facilitates the binding of subsequent \( \mathrm{O_2} \) molecules to the remaining subunits of the haemoglobin tetramer).
  • Physiological Delivery Volume: Every \( 100\,\mathrm{mL} \) of oxygenated blood can deliver around \( 5\,\mathrm{mL} \) of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) to the tissues under normal physiological conditions.

4.3 Transport of Carbon Dioxide

  1. As Carbamino-haemoglobin (\( 20\text{–}25\% \)):
    • \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) binds reversibly with haemoglobin to form carbamino-haemoglobin. This binding depends on \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \), while \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) is a major factor affecting it.
    • In tissues (high \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \), low \( \mathrm{pO_2} \)): More binding of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) with haemoglobin occurs.
    • In alveoli (low \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \), high \( \mathrm{pO_2} \)): Dissociation of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) from carbamino-haemoglobin takes place, delivering \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) to the alveoli.
  2. As Bicarbonate Ions (\( 70\% \)):
    • RBCs contain a very high concentration of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, and minute quantities of the same enzyme are present in the plasma too.
    • This enzyme facilitates the following reversible reaction in both directions:
\[ \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightleftharpoons[\text{Carbonic anhydrase}]{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}} \mathrm{H_2CO_3} \xrightleftharpoons[\text{Carbonic anhydrase}]{\text{Carbonic anhydrase}} \mathrm{HCO_3^-} + \mathrm{H^+} \]
  • At the tissue site: Partial pressure of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) is high due to catabolism; \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) diffuses into blood (RBCs and plasma) and reacts with water to form \( \mathrm{HCO_3^-} \) and \( \mathrm{H^+} \).
  • At the alveolar site: \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \) is low; the reaction proceeds in the opposite direction, forming \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) and \( \mathrm{H_2O} \). Thus, \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) trapped as bicarbonate at the tissue level is transported to the alveoli and released out as gaseous \( \mathrm{CO_2} \).
  • Physiological Delivery Volume: Every \( 100\,\mathrm{mL} \) of deoxygenated blood delivers approximately \( 4\,\mathrm{mL} \) of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) to the alveoli.

4.4 Regulation of Respiration

Humans have a significant ability to maintain and moderate the respiratory rhythm to suit the demands of body tissues via the neural system:

  • Respiratory Rhythm Centre (Medulla): A specialised centre present in the medulla region of the brain that is primarily responsible for regulating respiratory rhythm.
  • Pneumotaxic Centre (Pons): Located in the pons region of the brain, it moderates the functions of the respiratory rhythm centre. Neural signals from this centre reduce the duration of inspiration and thereby alter (increase) the respiratory rate.
  • Central Chemosensitive Area (Adjacent to Rhythm Centre in Medulla): Highly sensitive to \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) and hydrogen ions (\( \mathrm{H^+} \)). An increase in these substances activates this centre, which signals the respiratory rhythm centre to make adjustments so these substances can be eliminated.
  • Peripheral Chemoreceptors (Aortic Arch and Carotid Artery): Receptors associated with the aortic arch and carotid artery also recognise changes in \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) and \( \mathrm{H^+} \) concentration and send necessary signals to the rhythm centre for remedial actions.
  • Role of Oxygen: The role of oxygen (\( \mathrm{O_2} \)) in the regulation of respiratory rhythm is quite insignificant.

4.5 Disorders of the Respiratory System

  • Asthma: Difficulty in breathing causing wheezing due to inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles.
  • Emphysema: A chronic disorder in which alveolar walls are damaged, due to which the respiratory surface is decreased. One of the major causes is cigarette smoking.
  • Occupational Respiratory Disorders: Occur in industries involving grinding or stone-breaking, where so much dust is produced that the body's defense mechanism cannot fully cope. Long exposure causes inflammation leading to fibrosis (proliferation of fibrous tissues), resulting in serious lung damage. Workers in such industries should wear protective masks.
  • Hypoxia & High-Altitude Physiology: Hypoxia is a condition of inadequate oxygen supply at the tissue level. When a person ascends a high hill (high altitude), atmospheric pressure and atmospheric \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) decrease, reducing alveolar \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) and causing arterial hypoxia. To compensate, the body initially increases breathing rate (hyperventilation) and heart rate, and over several days increases red blood cell (RBC) and haemoglobin production while decreasing the binding affinity of haemoglobin for \( \mathrm{O_2} \).

5. Comprehensive Worked Examples (Conceptual & Numerical)

Worked Example 1: Hourly Tidal Volume Calculation

Problem: Define Tidal Volume (\( \mathrm{TV} \)). Calculate the approximate total Tidal Volume inspired or expired by a healthy human over the course of \( 1\,\mathrm{hour} \).

Solution:

  • Definition: Tidal Volume (\( \mathrm{TV} \)) is the volume of air inspired or expired during a normal, quiet respiration. For a healthy adult, \( \mathrm{TV} \approx 500\,\mathrm{mL} \).
  • Step 1 (Minute Respiratory Volume): A healthy human breathes \( 12\text{ to }16\text{ times/minute} \). \[ \text{Volume per minute} = 500\,\mathrm{mL} \times (12\text{ to }16)\,\mathrm{min^{-1}} = 6000\text{ to }8000\,\mathrm{mL/min} \]
  • Step 2 (Hourly Tidal Volume): Multiply the per-minute volume by \( 60\,\mathrm{minutes} \): \[ \text{Volume per hour (lower bound)} = 6000\,\mathrm{mL/min} \times 60\,\mathrm{min} = 360,000\,\mathrm{mL} = 360\,\mathrm{L} \] \[ \text{Volume per hour (upper bound)} = 8000\,\mathrm{mL/min} \times 60\,\mathrm{min} = 480,000\,\mathrm{mL} = 480\,\mathrm{L} \]
  • Conclusion: A healthy human inspires or expires approximately \( 360,000\,\mathrm{mL}\text{ to }480,000\,\mathrm{mL} \) (or \( 360\,\mathrm{L}\text{ to }480\,\mathrm{L} \)) of air per hour.

Worked Example 2: Deriving Pulmonary Capacities from Spirometric Data

Problem: A clinical spirometry report along with helium-dilution assessment for an adult subject gives the following measurements: Tidal Volume (\( \mathrm{TV} \)) \( = 500\,\mathrm{mL} \), Inspiratory Reserve Volume (\( \mathrm{IRV} \)) \( = 2800\,\mathrm{mL} \), Expiratory Reserve Volume (\( \mathrm{ERV} \)) \( = 1050\,\mathrm{mL} \), and Residual Volume (\( \mathrm{RV} \)) \( = 1150\,\mathrm{mL} \). Calculate: (a) Inspiratory Capacity (\( \mathrm{IC} \)), (b) Functional Residual Capacity (\( \mathrm{FRC} \)), (c) Vital Capacity (\( \mathrm{VC} \)), and (d) Total Lung Capacity (\( \mathrm{TLC} \)).

Solution:

  • (a) Inspiratory Capacity (\( \mathrm{IC} \)): Volume that can be inspired after a normal expiration: \[ \mathrm{IC} = \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV} = 500\,\mathrm{mL} + 2800\,\mathrm{mL} = 3300\,\mathrm{mL} \]
  • (b) Functional Residual Capacity (\( \mathrm{FRC} \)): Volume remaining in the lungs after a normal expiration: \[ \mathrm{FRC} = \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{RV} = 1050\,\mathrm{mL} + 1150\,\mathrm{mL} = 2200\,\mathrm{mL} \]
  • (c) Vital Capacity (\( \mathrm{VC} \)): Maximum volume of air breathed in after a forced expiration: \[ \mathrm{VC} = \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{TV} + \mathrm{IRV} = 1050\,\mathrm{mL} + 500\,\mathrm{mL} + 2800\,\mathrm{mL} = 4350\,\mathrm{mL} \]
  • (d) Total Lung Capacity (\( \mathrm{TLC} \)): Total air accommodated at the end of a forced inspiration: \[ \mathrm{TLC} = \mathrm{VC} + \mathrm{RV} = 4350\,\mathrm{mL} + 1150\,\mathrm{mL} = 5500\,\mathrm{mL} \]

Worked Example 3: Quantitative Gas Transport per Cardiac Output

Problem: Under normal resting physiological conditions, if the left ventricle pumps \( 5000\,\mathrm{mL} \) (\( 5\,\mathrm{L} \)) of oxygenated blood per minute to systemic tissues and the right ventricle pumps \( 5000\,\mathrm{mL} \) of deoxygenated blood per minute to the lungs, calculate: (a) the volume of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) delivered to the tissues per minute, and (b) the volume of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) delivered to the alveoli per minute.

Solution:

  • (a) \( \mathrm{O_2} \) delivered to tissues: Every \( 100\,\mathrm{mL} \) of oxygenated blood delivers around \( 5\,\mathrm{mL} \) of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) to tissues under normal physiological conditions. \[ \text{Total } \mathrm{O_2} \text{ delivered per minute} = \frac{5\,\mathrm{mL}}{100\,\mathrm{mL}} \times 5000\,\mathrm{mL/min} = 250\,\mathrm{mL/min} \]
  • (b) \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) delivered to alveoli: Every \( 100\,\mathrm{mL} \) of deoxygenated blood delivers approximately \( 4\,\mathrm{mL} \) of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) to the alveoli. \[ \text{Total } \mathrm{CO_2} \text{ delivered per minute} = \frac{4\,\mathrm{mL}}{100\,\mathrm{mL}} \times 5000\,\mathrm{mL/min} = 200\,\mathrm{mL/min} \]

Worked Example 4: Anatomical Restriction of Gas Diffusion

Problem: Explain why diffusion of gases (\( \mathrm{O_2} \) and \( \mathrm{CO_2} \)) occurs only in the alveolar region and not in the other parts of the human respiratory system.

Solution:

For rapid simple diffusion of gases between air and blood, the barrier must be extremely thin, moist, and richly supplied with blood capillaries. The alveolar region (alveoli and their ducts) forms the exchange part because its diffusion membrane—composed of one-cell-thick alveolar squamous epithelium, capillary endothelium, and the basement substance between them—has a total thickness of much less than a millimetre and is highly vascularised. In contrast, the conducting part (external nostrils up to terminal bronchioles) has thick walls (many supported by incomplete cartilaginous rings) and lacks the ultra-thin alveolar-capillary interface; its role is restricted to transporting, clearing, humidifying, and warming the air to body temperature.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Choice MCQ)

In the human respiratory system, the trachea is a straight tube extending up to the mid-thoracic cavity where it divides into right and left primary bronchi at the level of which vertebra, and which of the following structures lacks incomplete cartilaginous rings?

  • (A) 5th cervical vertebra; initial bronchioles
  • (B) 5th thoracic vertebra; terminal bronchioles and alveoli
  • (C) 7th thoracic vertebra; tertiary bronchi
  • (D) 2nd lumbar vertebra; secondary bronchi
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: The trachea extends up to the mid-thoracic cavity and divides at the level of the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left primary bronchi. Incomplete cartilaginous rings support the trachea, primary, secondary, and tertiary bronchi, and initial bronchioles, whereas terminal bronchioles and alveoli are very thin-walled and lack cartilaginous rings.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Choice MCQ)

What will be the partial pressures of oxygen (\( \mathrm{pO_2} \)) and carbon dioxide (\( \mathrm{pCO_2} \)) in atmospheric air compared to those in alveolar air?

  • (A) \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) lesser, \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \) higher
  • (B) \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) higher, \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \) lesser
  • (C) \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) higher, \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \) higher
  • (D) \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) lesser, \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \) lesser
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: In atmospheric air, \( \mathrm{pO_2} = 159\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \) and \( \mathrm{pCO_2} = 0.3\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \). In alveolar air, \( \mathrm{pO_2} = 104\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \) and \( \mathrm{pCO_2} = 40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \). Therefore, in atmospheric air compared to alveolar air, \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) is higher (\( 159 > 104 \)) and \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \) is lesser (\( 0.3 < 40 \)).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Choice MCQ)

During normal inspiration in a healthy human, which of the following muscular contractions and volume changes occur?

  • (A) Diaphragm contracts to increase thoracic volume in the dorso-ventral axis; internal intercostals contract to increase thoracic volume in the antero-posterior axis.
  • (B) Diaphragm contracts to increase thoracic volume in the antero-posterior axis; external intercostals contract to increase thoracic volume in the dorso-ventral axis.
  • (C) Diaphragm relaxes and arches upwards; intra-pulmonary pressure becomes higher than atmospheric pressure.
  • (D) External intercostal muscles relax; intra-pulmonary pressure drops below atmospheric pressure.
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Inspiration is initiated by the contraction of the diaphragm, which increases the volume of the thoracic chamber in the antero-posterior axis. Simultaneously, contraction of the external intercostal muscles lifts up the ribs and sternum, causing an increase in the volume of the thoracic chamber in the dorso-ventral axis. This overall increase in thoracic and pulmonary volume decreases intra-pulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure.

Practice Question 4 (Single-Choice Numerical MCQ)

7. Common NEET Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Pitfall 1 — Cartilaginous Rings in Bronchioles: Students often assume all bronchioles lack cartilaginous rings or all have them. Remember: Initial bronchioles have incomplete cartilaginous rings, whereas terminal bronchioles do not.
  • Pitfall 2 — Normal Expiration vs. Forced Expiration Volume: Air remaining in the lungs after a normal expiration is \( \mathrm{FRC} \) (\( \mathrm{ERV} + \mathrm{RV} \)), whereas air remaining after a forcible expiration is \( \mathrm{RV} \).
  • Pitfall 3 — Dissolved \( \mathrm{O_2} \) vs. Dissolved \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) in Plasma: \( 3\% \) of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) is carried dissolved in plasma, whereas \( 7\% \) of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) is carried dissolved in plasma. Do not swap \( 7\% \) (dissolved \( \mathrm{CO_2} \)) with \( 20\text{–}25\% \) (carbamino-haemoglobin).
  • Pitfall 4 — Alveolar \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) vs. Oxygenated Blood \( \mathrm{pO_2} \): Alveolar \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) is \( 104\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \), while oxygenated systemic arterial blood \( \mathrm{pO_2} \) is \( 95\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \) (not \( 104\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)). However, \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \) is \( 40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \) in both alveoli and oxygenated blood.
  • Pitfall 5 — Role of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) in Respiratory Rhythm: A frequent trap is stating that falling \( \mathrm{O_2} \) directly stimulates the medullary chemosensitive area. The central chemosensitive area and aortic/carotid receptors respond to \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) and \( \mathrm{H^+} \); the role of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) in respiratory rhythm regulation is quite insignificant.

High-Yield Revision Checklist

  • Trachea bifurcates at the 5th thoracic vertebra into right and left primary bronchi.
  • Contraction of diaphragm increases thoracic volume in the antero-posterior axis; contraction of external intercostals increases thoracic volume in the dorso-ventral axis.
  • Healthy breathing rate: 12–16 times/minute; Minute volume: \( 6000\text{–}8000\,\mathrm{mL/min} \).
  • Standard Volumes: \( \mathrm{TV} = 500\,\mathrm{mL} \), \( \mathrm{IRV} = 2500\text{–}3000\,\mathrm{mL} \), \( \mathrm{ERV} = 1000\text{–}1100\,\mathrm{mL} \), \( \mathrm{RV} = 1100\text{–}1200\,\mathrm{mL} \).
  • \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) solubility is 20–25 times higher than \( \mathrm{O_2} \) solubility; diffusion membrane has 3 layers with total thickness \( < 1\,\mathrm{mm} \).
  • \( 1\text{ Hb} \) molecule carries a maximum of \( 4\,\mathrm{O_2} \) molecules; \( 100\,\mathrm{mL} \) oxygenated blood delivers \( 5\,\mathrm{mL}\,\mathrm{O_2} \) to tissues; \( 100\,\mathrm{mL} \) deoxygenated blood delivers \( 4\,\mathrm{mL}\,\mathrm{CO_2} \) to alveoli.
  • Medulla = Respiratory rhythm centre & Chemosensitive area; Pons = Pneumotaxic centre.

8. Concise Final Recap

Respiration integrates pulmonary ventilation, alveolar diffusion, blood gas transport, tissue diffusion, and cellular catabolism. In humans, atmospheric air passes through the conducting zone (nostrils to terminal bronchioles) to reach the exchange zone (alveoli). Breathing is driven by negative and positive intra-pulmonary pressure gradients created by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles. Gas exchange across the sub-millimetre, three-layered alveolar-capillary membrane follows partial pressure gradients (\( \mathrm{pO_2} \): \( 104 \to 40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \); \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \): \( 45 \to 40\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg} \)). Blood transports \( 97\% \) of \( \mathrm{O_2} \) as oxyhaemoglobin (displaying a cooperative sigmoidal dissociation curve sensitive to \( \mathrm{pCO_2} \), \( \mathrm{H^+} \), and temperature) and \( 70\% \) of \( \mathrm{CO_2} \) as bicarbonate via carbonic anhydrase (along with \( 20\text{–}25\% \) as carbamino-haemoglobin and \( 7\% \) dissolved in plasma). Finally, the medullary respiratory rhythm centre, modulated by the pontine pneumotaxic centre and \( \mathrm{CO_2}/\mathrm{H^+} \)-sensitive central and peripheral chemoreceptors, adjusts ventilation to match metabolic demand.
