Photosynthesis in Higher Plants

Complete Concept Guide, Mechanistic Pathways, Worked Examples & Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Overview

Photosynthesis is the primary physico-chemical process sustaining life on Earth: green plants capture solar radiation to synthesise organic carbohydrates from inorganic precursors while liberating molecular oxygen. For NEET UG, this topic tests both mechanistic precision (electron carriers, proton gradients, ATP/NADPH stoichiometry) and physiological comparisons (C₃ versus C₄ anatomy, photorespiration, and limiting factors).

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Explain the experimental milestones that established the requirements and oxygen source of photosynthesis.
  • Map the division of labour inside the chloroplast and distinguish chlorophyll a from accessory pigments.
  • Trace non-cyclic (Z-scheme) and cyclic photophosphorylation, and apply the chemiosmotic hypothesis to ATP synthesis.
  • Calculate exact ATP and NADPH requirements for the Calvin (C₃) cycle and the Hatch–Slack (C₄) pathway.
  • Explain why C₄ plants with Kranz anatomy avoid photorespiration and outperform C₃ plants under high light and temperature.
  • Apply Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors to interpret light, CO₂, temperature, and water effects.

2. Foundations & Historical Discoveries

Green plants are autotrophs because they manufacture their own organic food, whereas animals and non-photosynthetic organisms are heterotrophs that depend directly or indirectly on plants. Photosynthesis is vital for two global reasons: it is the ultimate source of all food on Earth, and it replenishes atmospheric oxygen (O₂).

Overall Stoichiometry and the Source of Oxygen

Early nineteenth-century observations established that plants combine carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water (H₂O) in the presence of light to form carbohydrates and O₂. However, microbiologist Cornelius van Niel studied purple and green sulphur bacteria, which reduce CO₂ using hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) instead of water and release elemental sulphur or sulphate rather than O₂:

\[ 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{CO}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} 2\mathrm{A} + [\mathrm{CH}_2\mathrm{O}] + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \]

From this generalisation, van Niel inferred that oxygen evolved by green plants originates from H₂O, not from CO₂—a conclusion later confirmed using heavy-oxygen (¹⁸O) radioisotopic labelling. Because six molecules of O₂ (12 oxygen atoms) are released per glucose molecule formed, 12 molecules of H₂O must be split as the hydrogen donor:

\[ 6\mathrm{CO}_2 + 12\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} \mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6 + 6\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} + 6\mathrm{O}_2 \]

3. Site of Photosynthesis & Photosynthetic Pigments

Photosynthesis occurs in all green tissues, predominantly within the mesophyll cells of leaves, which house numerous chloroplasts. Chloroplasts dynamically reorient along mesophyll cell walls: they align their flat surfaces parallel to the cell wall in low light to maximise photon capture, and perpendicular to incident light under intense illumination to prevent photodamage.

Division of Labour Inside the Chloroplast

  • Membrane System (Grana & Stroma Lamellae): Traps light energy and synthesises ATP and NADPH. Because these reactions are directly light-driven, they are called light reactions (photochemical phase).
  • Stroma (Aqueous Matrix): Houses soluble enzymes that use ATP and NADPH to reduce CO₂ into sugars (stored as starch). These are called carbon reactions (or by convention, dark reactions).

Leaf Pigments & Action vs. Absorption Spectra

Paper chromatography separates leaf pigments into four distinct bands:

  • Chlorophyll a: Bright or blue-green; the chief pigment directly converting light into chemical energy.
  • Chlorophyll b: Yellow-green; accessory pigment.
  • Xanthophylls: Yellow; oxygenated carotenoid accessory pigments.
  • Carotenoids: Yellow to yellow-orange; accessory pigments.

Chlorophyll a absorbs maximally in the blue (~430 nm) and red (~660–680 nm) regions of the visible spectrum, which coincides with the highest rates of photosynthesis (the action spectrum). However, the action spectrum does not show a strict one-to-one overlap with chlorophyll a alone because accessory pigments (chlorophyll b, xanthophylls, carotenoids) absorb light at intermediate wavelengths and funnel that energy to chlorophyll a. Additionally, carotenoids protect chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation.

4. The Light Reactions & Chemiosmosis

The photochemical phase includes light absorption, water splitting, O₂ release, and formation of ATP and NADPH. Pigments are organised into two discrete Light Harvesting Complexes (LHC) within Photosystem I (PS I) and Photosystem II (PS II)—named in order of discovery, not functional sequence.

In each photosystem, hundreds of protein-bound pigment molecules form the antennae that funnel excitation energy to a single specialised chlorophyll a molecule called the reaction centre:

  • PS I Reaction Centre: Absorbs maximally at 700 nm (P700).
  • PS II Reaction Centre: Absorbs maximally at 680 nm (P680).

Non-Cyclic Electron Transport (The Z-Scheme) & Water Splitting

When P680 in PS II absorbs 680 nm red light, an electron is excited to a higher redox state and captured by a primary electron acceptor. It then travels downhill along an electron transport system (ETS) containing plastoquinone, cytochrome b₆f, and plastocyanin to reach P700 of PS I. Simultaneously, P700 absorbs 700 nm light, excites its electron to a higher acceptor, and passes it downhill via ferredoxin and NADP reductase to reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH + H⁺. Plotted on a redox potential scale, this sequence traces a characteristic Z-shape.

Electrons lost by P680 are continuously replenished by the photolysis (splitting) of water catalysed by the water-splitting complex located on the inner (lumen) side of the PS II thylakoid membrane:

\[ 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \longrightarrow 4\mathrm{H}^+ + \mathrm{O}_2 + 4\mathrm{e}^- \]

Chemiosmotic Hypothesis of ATP Synthesis

ATP synthesis in chloroplasts is driven by a proton (H⁺) gradient across the thylakoid membrane, with protons accumulating inside the thylakoid lumen (unlike mitochondria, where protons accumulate in the intermembrane space). Three events build this gradient:

  1. Water splitting on the inner (lumen) side of the membrane releases H⁺ directly into the lumen.
  2. Proton translocation occurs as the primary electron acceptor of PS II passes electrons to an H-carrier (plastoquinone) on the outer side, which picks up H⁺ from the stroma and releases it into the lumen upon transferring electrons to cytochrome b₆f.
  3. NADP⁺ reduction by NADP reductase on the stroma side consumes stromal H⁺ to form NADPH + H⁺.

Consequently, stromal [H⁺] drops while lumen [H⁺] rises sharply (lowering lumen pH). Breakdown of this electrochemical gradient occurs as H⁺ diffuses back into the stroma through the transmembrane CF₀ channel of ATP synthase, inducing a conformational change in the stromal CF₁ particle that catalyses ATP formation from ADP + Pᵢ.

5. The Biosynthetic Phase: Calvin Cycle (C₃ Pathway)

The ATP and NADPH generated by light reactions are immediately consumed in the stroma to fix CO₂ into sugars. Calling this phase the "dark reaction" is a misnomer: although it does not require light directly, it depends strictly on light-reaction products (ATP and NADPH) and halts shortly after illumination stops. Using radioactive ¹⁴C in algal photosynthesis, Melvin Calvin demonstrated that the first stable product of CO₂ fixation is a 3-carbon organic acid, 3-phosphoglyceric acid (3-PGA), and that the primary CO₂ acceptor is a 5-carbon ketose sugar, ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP).

Crucially, the Calvin cycle operates in all photosynthetic plants (both C₃ and C₄). It proceeds in three stages:

  1. Carboxylation: RuBP combines with CO₂, catalysed by RuBP carboxylase-oxygenase (RuBisCO), yielding two molecules of 3-PGA per CO₂ fixed.
  2. Reduction: A series of reactions converts 3-PGA into triose phosphates, consuming 2 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂ molecule fixed.
  3. Regeneration: RuBP is regenerated from triose phosphates to keep the cycle continuous, consuming 1 ATP per CO₂ fixed.
\[ \text{Per } \mathrm{CO}_2 \text{ fixed: } 3\,\mathrm{ATP} + 2\,\mathrm{NADPH} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \text{Per Glucose } (6\,\mathrm{CO}_2): 18\,\mathrm{ATP} + 12\,\mathrm{NADPH} \]

Because each CO₂ requires 3 ATP but only 2 NADPH, cyclic photophosphorylation is thought to supply the extra ATP needed to balance this 3 : 2 ratio.

6. The C₄ Pathway (Hatch–Slack) & Photorespiration

Plants adapted to dry tropical climates (such as maize and sorghum) employ the C₄ pathway, in which the first stable CO₂ fixation product is a 4-carbon acid, oxaloacetic acid (OAA). These plants exhibit Kranz anatomy ("wreath" arrangement): large bundle sheath cells form concentric layers around vascular bundles. Bundle sheath cells possess abundant chloroplasts, thick walls impervious to gaseous exchange, and no intercellular spaces.

Mechanism of the C₄ Shuttle

  1. Initial Fixation (Mesophyll Cells): Atmospheric CO₂ (as HCO₃⁻) is fixed by PEP carboxylase (PEPcase) onto a 3-carbon acceptor, phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), forming 4C OAA. Mesophyll cells lack RuBisCO.
  2. Transport: OAA is converted to malic acid or aspartic acid and transported via plasmodesmata into bundle sheath cells.
  3. Decarboxylation & Calvin Cycle (Bundle Sheath Cells): C₄ acids are decarboxylated to release CO₂ and a 3C acid (pyruvate). Bundle sheath cells are rich in RuBisCO (and lack PEPcase), so the released CO₂ enters the Calvin cycle.
  4. Regeneration: The 3C acid returns to the mesophyll cell and is phosphorylated back to PEP (consuming ATP equivalents).
Tutorial Diagram 5: Spatial separation of initial CO₂ fixation (mesophyll) and the Calvin cycle (bundle sheath) in C₄ plants.

Photorespiration: Why C₄ Plants Are More Productive

RuBisCO is the most abundant enzyme on Earth, and its active site binds both CO₂ and O₂ competitively depending on their relative concentrations. In C₃ plants, when O₂ binds to RuBisCO, RuBP is converted into one molecule of 3-PGA (3C) and one molecule of phosphoglycolate (2C) instead of two 3-PGA molecules. This wasteful pathway—photorespiration—synthesises no sugar, no ATP, and no NADPH; instead, it consumes ATP and releases fixed CO₂.

In C₄ plants, decarboxylation of C₄ acids inside gas-tight bundle sheath cells elevates intracellular [CO₂] around RuBisCO, ensuring RuBisCO acts exclusively as a carboxylase and suppressing photorespiration. Consequently, C₄ plants show higher biomass productivity and greater tolerance to high temperatures and high light intensities.

7. Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is governed by internal (plant) factors (leaf number, size, age, orientation, mesophyll/chloroplast density, internal CO₂, chlorophyll content—all under genetic and developmental control) and external factors (light, CO₂, temperature, water).

Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors (1905): When a biochemical process is conditioned as to its rapidity by a number of separate factors, the rate of the process is limited by the pace of the "slowest" factor—the factor nearest to its minimal value, which directly alters the rate if its quantity is changed.
  • Light: At low intensities, rate increases linearly with incident light. Beyond ~10% of full sunlight, light saturation occurs and another factor (usually CO₂) becomes limiting; excessive light causes chlorophyll breakdown.
  • Carbon Dioxide Concentration: The major limiting factor in nature (atmospheric CO₂ is 0.03–0.04%). Increasing CO₂ up to 0.05% boosts fixation, though prolonged higher levels can be damaging. C₄ plants saturate around 360 µL L⁻¹, whereas C₃ plants respond up to >450 µL L⁻¹ (exploited in CO₂-enriched greenhouses for tomatoes and bell peppers).
  • Temperature: Dark reactions are enzymatic and strongly temperature-sensitive; light reactions are much less affected. Tropical (and C₄) plants have a higher optimum (30–40 °C) than temperate C₃ plants (20–25 °C).
  • Water: Affects photosynthesis primarily indirectly—water stress induces stomatal closure (restricting CO₂ entry) and leaf wilting (reducing surface area and metabolic activity).

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: ATP & NADPH Budget for Sucrose Synthesis (Hypothetical Scenario)

Scenario (Assumed): Suppose a leaf synthesises 1 molecule of sucrose (a disaccharide composed of 2 hexose units, equivalent to fixing 12 CO₂ molecules). Compare the minimum ATP and NADPH required if this occurs in a C₃ plant versus a C₄ plant (assuming C₄ PEP regeneration consumes 2 additional ATP per CO₂ fixed).

Solution:

  • In a C₃ plant: Each CO₂ fixed in the Calvin cycle requires \(3\,\mathrm{ATP} + 2\,\mathrm{NADPH}\). For 12 CO₂ molecules: \[ \mathrm{ATP} = 12 \times 3 = 36\,\mathrm{ATP}, \qquad \mathrm{NADPH} = 12 \times 2 = 24\,\mathrm{NADPH} \]
  • In a C₄ plant: The Calvin cycle in bundle sheath cells still requires \(36\,\mathrm{ATP} + 24\,\mathrm{NADPH}\). Additionally, regenerating PEP from pyruvate in mesophyll cells costs \(2\,\mathrm{ATP}\) per CO₂ (\(12 \times 2 = 24\,\mathrm{ATP}\)). Thus: \[ \mathrm{ATP} = 36 + 24 = 60\,\mathrm{ATP}, \qquad \mathrm{NADPH} = 24\,\mathrm{NADPH} \]
Worked Example 2: Interpreting CO₂ Enrichment in a Greenhouse (Hypothetical Scenario)

Scenario (Assumed): A botanist grows tomato (C₃) and sorghum (C₄) in two identical chambers at 32 °C and saturating light. Chamber X is maintained at 360 µL L⁻¹ CO₂, while Chamber Y is enriched to 500 µL L⁻¹ CO₂. Predict which species shows a significant increase in photosynthetic rate in Chamber Y.

Solution:

Sorghum (C₄) already saturates around 360 µL L⁻¹ CO₂ because its bundle sheath CO₂-concentrating mechanism keeps RuBisCO saturated; therefore, raising CO₂ to 500 µL L⁻¹ yields negligible further increase. Tomato (C₃) saturates only beyond 450 µL L⁻¹ CO₂, so raising CO₂ from 360 to 500 µL L⁻¹ relieves CO₂ limitation, suppresses photorespiration, and significantly increases its photosynthetic rate.

Worked Example 3: Water Splitting & Oxygen Evolution Accounting (Hypothetical Scenario)

Scenario (Assumed): An isolated chloroplast suspension evolves 3.0 moles of O₂ during non-cyclic photophosphorylation under optimal illumination. How many moles of water were split, and how many moles of electrons passed through PS II?

Solution:

From the photolysis equation \(2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{H}^+ + \mathrm{O}_2 + 4\mathrm{e}^-\), evolving \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of O₂ requires splitting \(2\,\mathrm{mol}\) of H₂O and transfers \(4\,\mathrm{mol}\) of electrons.

For \(3.0\,\mathrm{mol}\) of O₂: \[ \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\text{ split} = 3.0 \times 2 = 6.0\,\mathrm{mol}, \qquad \mathrm{e}^-\text{ transferred} = 3.0 \times 4 = 12.0\,\mathrm{mol} \]

Practice Question 2 Statement-Based

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: In stroma lamellae, cyclic photophosphorylation occurs because these membranes lack both PS II and NADP reductase enzyme.

  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.

Answer: (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.

Explanation: Stroma lamellae lack PS II and NADP reductase, so excited electrons from PS I cycle back through the ETS to generate only ATP—never NADPH + H⁺.

Practice Question 4 Numerical / Stoichiometry

How many turns of the Calvin cycle, ATP molecules, and NADPH molecules are required respectively to synthesise two molecules of glucose in a C₃ plant?

  • (A) 6 turns, 18 ATP, 12 NADPH
  • (B) 12 turns, 36 ATP, 24 NADPH
  • (C) 12 turns, 24 ATP, 36 NADPH
  • (D) 6 turns, 36 ATP, 24 NADPH
Answer: (B) 12 turns, 36 ATP, 24 NADPH

Explanation: One glucose molecule requires 6 turns (6 CO₂), consuming 18 ATP and 12 NADPH. Therefore, 2 glucose molecules require \(2 \times 6 = 12\) turns, \(2 \times 18 = 36\,\mathrm{ATP}\), and \(2 \times 12 = 24\,\mathrm{NADPH}\).

10. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

High-Frequency NEET UG Traps to Avoid:
  • PS II acts before PS I: Despite their numbering (order of discovery), electrons flow from PS II (P680) → ETS → PS I (P700) → NADP⁺.
  • Proton accumulation site: In chloroplasts, H⁺ accumulates in the thylakoid lumen; in mitochondria, H⁺ accumulates in the intermembrane space.
  • Primary CO₂ acceptor in C₃ is 5C RuBP, not 2C: RuBP (5C) + CO₂ (1C) → 2 × 3-PGA (3C).
  • Photorespiration yields zero energy: No ATP, no NADPH, and no sugar are formed—it wastes ATP and releases CO₂.
  • CO₂ saturation thresholds: C₄ saturates at ~360 µL L⁻¹; C₃ saturates only beyond 450 µL L⁻¹.
  • I can state why 12 H₂O appear in the balanced photosynthesis equation and cite van Niel's proof.
  • I can distinguish the four chromatographic leaf pigments and explain why accessory pigments prevent photo-oxidation.
  • I can trace electron flow in both Z-scheme and cyclic photophosphorylation and explain where each occurs.
  • I can list all three mechanisms generating the thylakoid proton gradient.
  • I can compute ATP (18) and NADPH (12) per glucose in the Calvin cycle and explain Kranz anatomy in C₄ plants.

11. Concise Final Recap

Photosynthesis couples thylakoid light reactions (splitting H₂O to evolve O₂ and generating ATP via chemiosmosis plus NADPH via the Z-scheme) with stromal carbon reactions (the Calvin cycle, fixing CO₂ onto RuBP via RuBisCO at a cost of 3 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂). While C₃ plants lose efficiency to photorespiration when O₂ binds RuBisCO, C₄ plants use Kranz anatomy and PEPcase in mesophyll cells to pump CO₂ into RuBisCO-rich bundle sheath cells, eliminating photorespiration and thriving at higher temperatures and light intensities. Ultimately, photosynthetic rate at any moment is governed by the factor nearest its minimum value (Blackman's Law).
