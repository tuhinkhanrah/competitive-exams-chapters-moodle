NEET UG Standard • Plant Physiology

Photosynthesis in Higher Plants

Unit Context: Plant Physiology examines biological phenomena at the cellular and molecular levels while connecting them to organismic activities and environmental interactions. This tutorial covers the complete photosynthetic machinery, energy transformation mechanisms, carbon assimilation pathways, and limiting factors required for NEET UG mastery.

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

All animals, including human beings, depend on plants for food. Green plants synthesise the organic food they need through photosynthesis and are therefore classified as autotrophs, whereas all other organisms that depend on green plants for nutrition are heterotrophs.

Core Definition & Ecological Significance

Photosynthesis is a physico-chemical process by which green plants use light energy to drive the synthesis of organic compounds. It is the fundamental basis of life on Earth for two primary reasons:

  1. It is the primary source of all food on Earth.
  2. It is responsible for the release of molecular oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)) into the atmosphere.

Key Learning Objectives for NEET UG:

  • Understand classical experiments establishing the roles of light, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), chlorophyll, and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) in photosynthesis.
  • Relate chloroplast ultrastructure to the division of labour between photochemical (light) and biosynthetic (dark) reactions.
  • Analyze absorption and action spectra of photosynthetic pigments and the organization of Light Harvesting Complexes (PS I and PS II).
  • Trace non-cyclic (Z-scheme) and cyclic electron transport, water splitting, and chemiosmotic ATP synthesis.
  • Compare carbon fixation via the Calvin cycle (\(\mathrm{C_3}\)), Hatch and Slack pathway (\(\mathrm{C_4}\)), and photorespiration (\(\mathrm{C_2}\)).
  • Apply Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors to evaluate the effects of light, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), temperature, and water on photosynthetic rates.

2. Foundational & Historical Experiments

2.1 Basic Classroom Demonstrations

  • Variegated Leaf / Black Paper Experiment: Testing a variegated leaf or a leaf partially covered with black paper after exposure to light shows that starch formation occurs only in the green parts of the leaf in the presence of light.
  • Moll's Half-Leaf (\(\mathrm{KOH}\)) Experiment: Part of a leaf is enclosed in a test tube containing \(\mathrm{KOH}\)-soaked cotton (which absorbs \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)), while the other half remains exposed to air in light. Upon starch testing with iodine, the exposed half tests positive for starch, whereas the enclosed half inside the tube tests negative. This proves that \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is essential for photosynthesis.

2.3 Chemical Equations of Photosynthesis

By the mid-nineteenth century, the empirical equation representing oxygen-evolving photosynthesis was written as:

\[\mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} [\mathrm{CH_2O}] + \mathrm{O_2}\]

where \([\mathrm{CH_2O}]\) represents a carbohydrate (e.g., glucose, a 6-carbon sugar). Based on studies of purple and green sulphur bacteria, Cornelius van Niel formulated the generalised light-dependent reaction:

\[2\mathrm{H_2A} + \mathrm{CO_2} \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} 2\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{CH_2O} + \mathrm{H_2O}\]
  • In green plants, \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) is the hydrogen donor and is oxidised to \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  • In purple and green sulphur bacteria, \(\mathrm{H_2S}\) is the hydrogen donor, so the oxidation product is sulphur or sulphate (depending on the organism) and not \(\mathrm{O_2}\).
  • This proved that \(\mathrm{O_2}\) evolved by green plants comes from \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), not from \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (later confirmed using radioisotopic techniques with \(^{18}\mathrm{O}\)).

Because all \(6\,\mathrm{O_2}\) molecules (12 oxygen atoms) released per molecule of glucose synthesised must originate exclusively from water, 12 molecules of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) are required as substrate on the left-hand side of the balanced multistep overall equation:

\[6\mathrm{CO_2} + 12\mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\mathrm{H_2O} + 6\mathrm{O_2}\]

3. Site of Photosynthesis & Division of Labour

Photosynthesis occurs mainly in green leaves, as well as in other green parts of plants (such as green stems, unripe green fruits, and green sepals). Within leaves, mesophyll cells contain a large number of chloroplasts that align themselves along the cell walls to receive the optimum quantity of incident light:

  • Low / Optimum Light Intensity (Parastrophe): Chloroplasts align with their flat surfaces perpendicular to the incident light (parallel to the periclinal/surface wall) to maximise light absorption.
  • Very High Light Intensity (Epistrophe): Chloroplasts align with their flat surfaces parallel to the incident light (along the anticlinal/lateral walls) to protect chlorophyll from photo-oxidation.
Tutorial Diagram 2: Sectional view of a chloroplast showing the membranous system (grana and stroma lamellae) and the fluid stroma.
Division of Labour Within the Chloroplast
  • Membranous System (Grana + Stroma Lamellae): Responsible for trapping light energy and synthesising ATP and NADPH. Because these reactions are directly light-driven, they are called light reactions (photochemical reactions).
  • Matrix Stroma: Site of enzymatic reactions that synthesise sugar (which in turn forms starch). Because these reactions are not directly light-driven but depend on the products of light reactions (ATP and NADPH, besides \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)), they are conventionally called dark reactions (carbon reactions / biosynthetic phase). Note: Calling them "dark reactions" is a misnomer because they do not require darkness and are indirectly light-dependent.

Tutorial Diagram 3: (a) Absorption spectra of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids; (b) Action spectrum of photosynthesis superimposed on the absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a.

Key Conclusions from Absorption & Action Spectra

  • Chlorophyll a shows maximum absorption in the blue region (highest peak) and a second major absorption peak in the red region.
  • Maximum rate of photosynthesis (measured by \(\mathrm{O_2}\) release) also occurs in the blue and red regions of the spectrum.
  • There is not a complete one-to-one overlap between the absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a and the action spectrum of photosynthesis because accessory pigments (chlorophyll b, xanthophylls, carotenoids) absorb light at intermediate wavelengths and transfer the energy to chlorophyll a.
  • Functions of Accessory Pigments: (1) Enable a wider range of incoming wavelengths to be utilised for photosynthesis, and (2) Protect chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation.

5. Light Reaction, Photophosphorylation & Chemiosmosis

5.1 Organization of Photosystems (LHC)

The photochemical phase includes: (1) light absorption, (2) water splitting, (3) oxygen release, and (4) formation of high-energy chemical intermediates (ATP and NADPH). Pigments are organised into two discrete photochemical Light Harvesting Complexes (LHC) within Photosystem I (PS I) and Photosystem II (PS II). They are named in the sequence of their discovery, not in the sequence in which they function during the light reaction.

  • Antennae (LHC): Hundreds of pigment molecules bound to proteins (all pigments except one molecule of chlorophyll a) that absorb different wavelengths of light and funnel energy to the reaction centre.
  • Reaction Centre: A single molecule of chlorophyll a.
    • In PS I, the reaction centre chlorophyll a has an absorption peak at \(700\,\mathrm{nm}\) and is called P700.
    • In PS II, the reaction centre chlorophyll a has an absorption maximum at \(680\,\mathrm{nm}\) and is called P680.
Tutorial Diagram 4: Organization of the Light Harvesting Complex (left) and the Z-scheme of electron transport in non-cyclic photophosphorylation (right).

5.2 The Electron Transport (Z-Scheme) & Splitting of Water

  1. Excitation at PS II: Reaction centre P680 absorbs \(680\,\mathrm{nm}\) red light, exciting electrons to jump to a farther atomic orbit.
  2. Uphill Transfer & Downhill ETS: Excited electrons are picked up by a primary electron acceptor (uphill on the redox scale) and passed downhill along an oxidation-reduction (redox potential) scale through an Electron Transport System (ETS) consisting of cytochromes to PS I.
  3. Excitation at PS I & Reduction of \(\mathrm{NADP^+}\): Simultaneously, electrons in P700 are excited by \(700\,\mathrm{nm}\) red light, transferred uphill to another acceptor with a greater redox potential, and then moved downhill to reduce energy-rich \(\mathrm{NADP^+}\) to \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\).
  4. Splitting of Water (Photolysis): Associated exclusively with PS II and physically located on the inner side of the thylakoid membrane (facing the lumen). It continuously supplies electrons to replace those lost by PS II (which in turn replace those removed from PS I), while releasing protons (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) into the thylakoid lumen:
    \[2\mathrm{H_2O} \longrightarrow 4\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{O_2} + 4\mathrm{e^-}\]

5.3 Non-Cyclic vs. Cyclic Photophosphorylation

Photophosphorylation is the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate (\(\mathrm{P_i}\)) in the presence of light.

Feature Non-Cyclic Photophosphorylation Cyclic Photophosphorylation
Photosystems Involved Both PS II and PS I working in series (connected via ETS in the Z-scheme). Only PS I (P700) is functional.
Location in Chloroplast Grana lamellae (thylakoids), which possess both PS I, PS II, and \(\mathrm{NADP}\) reductase. Stroma lamellae, which lack PS II and lack the \(\mathrm{NADP}\) reductase enzyme.
Electron Flow Path Unidirectional: \(\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \text{PS II} \rightarrow \text{ETS} \rightarrow \text{PS I} \rightarrow \mathrm{NADP^+}\). Cyclic: Excited \(\mathrm{e^-}\) from P700 acceptor cycles back to PS I through the ETS.
Wavelength Requirement Requires wavelengths supporting both \(680\,\mathrm{nm}\) and \(700\,\mathrm{nm}\). Occurs when only light of wavelengths beyond \(680\,\mathrm{nm}\) is available for excitation.
Products Synthesised Both \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\) (plus \(\mathrm{O_2}\) evolved from water splitting). Only \(\mathrm{ATP}\) (no \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\) synthesis; no \(\mathrm{O_2}\) evolution).

5.4 Chemiosmotic Hypothesis of ATP Synthesis

ATP synthesis in chloroplasts is linked to the development of a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane. Unlike respiration (where protons accumulate in the intermembrane space of mitochondria), in photosynthesis proton accumulation occurs towards the inside of the membrane, i.e., in the thylakoid lumen.

Tutorial Diagram 5: Mechanism of ATP synthesis through chemiosmosis across the thylakoid membrane.
Three Steps Causing the Proton Gradient Across the Thylakoid Membrane
  1. Water Splitting in the Lumen: Splitting of water takes place on the inner side of the thylakoid membrane, so the \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ions produced accumulate directly within the lumen.
  2. Proton Transport via Hydrogen Carrier (Plastoquinone): As electrons move through the photosystems, the primary electron acceptor located towards the outer side of the membrane transfers its electron not to an electron carrier, but to an \(\mathrm{H}\)-carrier (plastoquinone). It removes a proton from the stroma while transporting an electron, and releases this proton into the lumen when passing its electron to the electron carrier (cytochrome \(b_6f\)) on the inner side of the membrane.
  3. Proton Removal by \(\mathrm{NADP}\) Reductase in the Stroma: The \(\mathrm{NADP}\) reductase enzyme (FNR) is located on the stroma side of the membrane. Protons are removed from the stroma to reduce \(\mathrm{NADP^+}\) to \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\).

Result: Protons decrease in number in the stroma and accumulate in the lumen, creating a strong proton gradient (electrochemical potential gradient) and a measurable decrease in pH in the thylakoid lumen.

Requirements & Action of Chemiosmosis: Chemiosmosis requires (1) a membrane, (2) a proton pump, (3) a proton gradient, and (4) ATP synthase. The ATP synthase enzyme consists of two parts:

  • \(\mathrm{CF_0}\): Embedded in the thylakoid membrane; forms a transmembrane channel that carries out facilitated diffusion of protons across the membrane from lumen to stroma.
  • \(\mathrm{CF_1}\): Protrudes on the outer surface of the thylakoid membrane facing the stroma. Breakdown of the proton gradient releases energy that causes a conformational change in the \(\mathrm{CF_1}\) particle, activating the enzyme to synthesise ATP.

6. Biosynthetic Phase: Calvin Cycle (\(\mathrm{C_3}\)), \(\mathrm{C_4}\) Pathway & Photorespiration

Of the light reaction products (\(\mathrm{ATP}\), \(\mathrm{NADPH}\), and \(\mathrm{O_2}\)), \(\mathrm{O_2}\) diffuses out of the chloroplast while \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) drive the synthesis of sugars in the stroma. If light becomes unavailable, the biosynthetic process continues for a short time (until the accumulated \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) are exhausted) and then stops; it resumes when light is restored.

  • \(\mathrm{C_3}\) Pathway: First stable product of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixation is a 3-carbon organic acid, 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA).
  • \(\mathrm{C_4}\) Pathway: First stable product of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixation is a 4-carbon organic acid, oxaloacetic acid (OAA).

6.1 The Calvin Cycle (\(\mathrm{C_3}\) Pathway)

Universal Occurrence: The Calvin cycle operates in a cyclic manner in all photosynthetic plants, regardless of whether they have \(\mathrm{C_3}\) or \(\mathrm{C_4}\) (or any other) pathways. The primary acceptor of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is a 5-carbon ketose sugar called ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP) (scientists initially searched in vain for a 2-carbon acceptor before discovering the 5-carbon RuBP).

Tutorial Diagram 6: The three stages of the Calvin cycle: (1) Carboxylation, (2) Reduction, and (3) Regeneration.

Three Stages of the Calvin Cycle:

  1. Carboxylation: Fixation of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) into a stable organic intermediate. This is the most crucial step of the Calvin cycle, where \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) carboxylates RuBP to form two molecules of 3-PGA, catalysed by RuBP carboxylase-oxygenase (RuBisCO).
  2. Reduction: A series of reactions leading to the formation of glucose. It utilises 2 molecules of ATP (for phosphorylation) and 2 molecules of NADPH (for reduction) per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) molecule fixed.
  3. Regeneration: Regeneration of the \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) acceptor molecule RuBP is crucial for the cycle to continue uninterrupted. It requires 1 molecule of ATP for phosphorylation to form RuBP per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed.
Stoichiometric Basis \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) Fixed ATP Consumed NADPH Consumed
Per 1 Turn of Calvin Cycle \(1\,\mathrm{CO_2}\) \(3\,\mathrm{ATP}\) (2 in Reduction + 1 in Regeneration) \(2\,\mathrm{NADPH}\) (in Reduction)
For 1 Glucose Molecule (6 Turns) \(6\,\mathrm{CO_2}\) \(18\,\mathrm{ATP} \rightarrow 18\,\mathrm{ADP}\) \(12\,\mathrm{NADPH} \rightarrow 12\,\mathrm{NADP^+}\)

6.2 The \(\mathrm{C_4}\) Pathway (Hatch and Slack Pathway)

Plants adapted to dry tropical regions (such as maize and sorghum) exhibit the \(\mathrm{C_4}\) pathway. Although oxaloacetic acid (4C) is their first \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixation product, they still use the Calvin cycle (\(\mathrm{C_3}\) pathway) as their main biosynthetic pathway to make sugars.

  • Special Characteristics of \(\mathrm{C_4}\) Plants: Possess special leaf anatomy (Kranz anatomy), tolerate higher temperatures, show a response to high light intensities, lack photorespiration, and have greater productivity of biomass.
  • Kranz Anatomy ('Kranz' = 'wreath'): Particularly large cells around the vascular bundles are called bundle sheath cells, which may form several layers around the vascular bundles. Bundle sheath cells are characterised by:
    1. A large number of chloroplasts,
    2. Thick walls impervious to gaseous exchange, and
    3. No intercellular spaces.
Tutorial Diagram 7: Diagrammatic representation of the Hatch and Slack (C₄) pathway operating across mesophyll and bundle sheath cells.

Step-by-Step Mechanism of the Hatch and Slack Pathway:

  1. Initial Fixation in Mesophyll Cells: The primary \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) acceptor is a 3-carbon molecule, phosphoenol pyruvate (PEP), present in mesophyll cells. Fixation is catalysed by PEP carboxylase (PEPcase) to form the 4-carbon oxaloacetic acid (OAA). Mesophyll cells of \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants lack the RuBisCO enzyme.
  2. Conversion & Transport: OAA is converted into other 4-carbon compounds such as malic acid or aspartic acid in the mesophyll cells, which are then transported via plasmodesmata to the bundle sheath cells.
  3. Decarboxylation & Calvin Cycle in Bundle Sheath Cells: In the bundle sheath cells, these \(\mathrm{C_4}\) acids are broken down (decarboxylated) to release \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and a 3-carbon molecule. The released \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) enters the Calvin cycle. Bundle sheath cells are rich in RuBisCO but lack PEPcase.
  4. Regeneration of PEP: The 3-carbon molecule is transported back to the mesophyll cell, where it is converted back to PEP (consuming additional ATP), completing the cycle.

6.3 Photorespiration (\(\mathrm{C_2}\) Pathway)

RuBisCO is the most abundant enzyme in the world. Its active site can bind to both \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{O_2}\) competitively. RuBisCO has a much greater affinity for \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) than for \(\mathrm{O_2}\) when the \(\mathrm{CO_2 : O_2}\) ratio is nearly equal, and it is the relative concentration of \(\mathrm{O_2}\) and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) that determines which gas binds to the enzyme:

  • Normal Carboxylation: \(\mathrm{RuBP} + \mathrm{CO_2} \xrightarrow{\text{RuBisCO}} 2 \times \text{3-PGA (3C)}\).
  • Photorespiration (Oxygenation in \(\mathrm{C_3}\) Plants): In \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants, some \(\mathrm{O_2}\) binds to RuBisCO, decreasing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixation. Instead of forming two molecules of 3-PGA, one molecule of RuBP (5C) binds with \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to form one molecule of phosphoglycerate (3C) and one molecule of phosphoglycolate (2C):
    \[\mathrm{RuBP\,(5C)} + \mathrm{O_2} \xrightarrow{\text{RuBisCO (Oxygenase)}} 1\;\text{Phosphoglycerate (3C)} + 1\;\text{Phosphoglycolate (2C)}\]
  • Why Photorespiration is a Wasteful Process: In the photorespiratory pathway, there is neither synthesis of sugars nor synthesis of ATP or NADPH. Instead, it results in the release of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) with the utilisation of ATP. Its biological function is not yet known.
  • Why \(\mathrm{C_4}\) Plants Lack Photorespiration: \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants have a mechanism that increases the intracellular concentration of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) at the RuBisCO enzyme site when \(\mathrm{C_4}\) acids from the mesophyll are broken down in the bundle sheath cells to release \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) (and thick bundle sheath walls prevent gas leakage). This ensures that RuBisCO functions almost exclusively as a carboxylase, minimising its oxygenase activity.

7. Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

  • Internal (Plant) Factors: Number, size, age, and orientation of leaves, mesophyll cells, and chloroplasts; internal \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) concentration; and the amount of chlorophyll. These depend on the genetic predisposition and the growth of the plant.
  • External Factors: Availability of sunlight, temperature, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) concentration, and water.
Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors (1905)

"If a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, then its rate will be determined by the factor which is nearest to its minimal value: it is the factor which directly affects the process if its quantity is changed."

For example, a green leaf with optimal light and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) will not photosynthesise if the temperature is very low; raising the temperature to optimum immediately allows photosynthesis to proceed.

Tutorial Diagram 8: Effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis. Region A shows a linear relationship where light is limiting; Region B is the transition zone; Region C represents maximum rate (E) where other factors become limiting; Point D is the light saturation point (10% of full sunlight).

Detailed Analysis of External Factors

  1. Light (Quality, Intensity, and Duration):
    • At low light intensities (Region A), there is a linear relationship between incident light and \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixation rates (light is the limiting factor).
    • At higher light intensities (Region C), the rate shows no further increase because other factors (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\), temperature) become limiting.
    • Light saturation (Point D) occurs at 10% of full sunlight. Consequently, except for plants in shade or dense forests, light is rarely a limiting factor in nature.
    • Increase in incident light beyond a point causes the breakdown of chlorophyll (solarisation/photo-oxidation) and a decrease in photosynthesis.
  2. Carbon Dioxide Concentration:
    • \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) is the major limiting factor for photosynthesis. Atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) concentration is very low (between 0.03% and 0.04%, i.e., \(300\text{–}400\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\)).
    • Increasing concentration up to 0.05% increases \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixation rates; beyond this level, it can become damaging over longer periods.
    • At low light conditions, neither \(\mathrm{C_3}\) nor \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants respond to high \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) conditions.
    • At high light intensities, both show increased rates, but \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants show saturation at about \(360\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\), whereas \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants respond to increased \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and show saturation only beyond \(450\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\). Thus, current atmospheric \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) availability is limiting to \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants.
    • Greenhouse Application: \(\mathrm{C_3}\) crops such as tomatoes and bell pepper are grown in a \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)-enriched atmosphere to achieve higher productivity and yields.
  3. Temperature:
    • The dark reactions, being enzymatic, are temperature-controlled. Light reactions are also temperature-sensitive, but are affected to a much lesser extent.
    • \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants respond to higher temperatures (\(30\text{–}40\,\mathrm{°C}\)) and show higher rates of photosynthesis, while \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants have a much lower temperature optimum (\(20\text{–}25\,\mathrm{°C}\)).
    • Tropical plants have a higher temperature optimum than plants adapted to temperate climates.
  4. Water:
    • Although water is a reactant in the light reaction, the effect of water as a limiting factor is more through its effect on the plant as a whole rather than directly on the photosynthetic reaction.
    • Water stress causes: (a) stomata to close, reducing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) availability, and (b) leaves to wilt, reducing leaf surface area and overall metabolic activity.

8. Worked Examples & Conceptual Applications

Worked Example 1: External vs. Internal Identification of \(\mathrm{C_3}\) and \(\mathrm{C_4}\) Plants

Problem: Can you tell whether a plant is \(\mathrm{C_3}\) or \(\mathrm{C_4}\) merely by looking at it externally? Which internal structure definitively distinguishes them?

Solution:

  • External Observation: Generally, one cannot conclusively tell whether a plant is \(\mathrm{C_3}\) or \(\mathrm{C_4}\) purely by external morphology, although \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants are well adapted to dry tropical climates and do not show wilting/photorespiratory stress under high temperatures and bright sunlight.
  • Internal Anatomical Structure: A vertical section of the leaf under a microscope definitively distinguishes them by the presence of Kranz anatomy in \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants. \(\mathrm{C_4}\) leaves possess large bundle sheath cells forming wreath-like layers around the vascular bundles, with a large number of chloroplasts, thick walls impervious to gaseous exchange, and no intercellular spaces. \(\mathrm{C_3}\) leaves lack Kranz anatomy.
Worked Example 2: High Productivity in \(\mathrm{C_4}\) Plants Despite Few Calvin-Cycle Cells

Problem: Even though very few cells (only the bundle sheath cells) in a \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plant carry out the biosynthetic Calvin pathway, why are \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants highly productive?

Solution: In \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants, mesophyll cells rapidly fix \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) via PEPcase into \(\mathrm{C_4}\) acids, which are pumped into the bundle sheath cells and decarboxylated. This acts as an intracellular \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) pump that elevates the \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) concentration at the RuBisCO site inside bundle sheath cells. Consequently, RuBisCO acts exclusively as a carboxylase and photorespiration is completely eliminated, resulting in high photosynthetic rates and biomass yield.

Worked Example 3: Pigment Stability, Shade Adaptation, and Accessory Pigments

Problem: (a) Would a plant with a high concentration of chlorophyll b but lacking chlorophyll a carry out photosynthesis? (b) Why does a leaf kept in the dark turn yellow or pale green, and which pigment is more stable? (c) Why are leaves kept in the shade darker green than leaves on the sunny side?

Solution:

  • (a) No. Chlorophyll a forms the reaction centre (P680 and P700) where light energy is converted into chemical energy via electron excitation. Without chlorophyll a, photosynthesis cannot occur. Chlorophyll b and other accessory pigments are present to harvest a wider range of wavelengths, transfer energy to chlorophyll a, and protect chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation.
  • (b) Chlorophyll synthesis requires light, and existing chlorophyll degrades in the dark, unmasking the yellow colour of carotenoids and xanthophylls, which are more stable pigments.
  • (c) Leaves in the shade are darker green because they synthesise a higher concentration of chlorophyll pigments (and orient their chloroplasts flat/perpendicular to incident rays) to maximise capture of the limited available light.
Worked Example 4: Bioenergetics Calculation for Hexose Synthesis

Problem: Calculate the total number of \(\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(\mathrm{NADPH}\) molecules required in the stroma to synthesise \(2\) molecules of glucose in a \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plant, and specify how many \(\mathrm{ATP}\) molecules are consumed solely in the regeneration stage.

Solution:

  • For \(1\) molecule of glucose (\(6\,\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed = \(6\) turns of Calvin cycle):
    • Reduction stage uses \(2\,\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(2\,\mathrm{NADPH}\) per \(\mathrm{CO_2} \Rightarrow 12\,\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(12\,\mathrm{NADPH}\).
    • Regeneration stage uses \(1\,\mathrm{ATP}\) per \(\mathrm{CO_2} \Rightarrow 6\,\mathrm{ATP}\).
    • Total per glucose = \(18\,\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(12\,\mathrm{NADPH}\).
  • For \(2\) molecules of glucose: Total required = \(2 \times 18 = \mathbf{36\,\mathrm{ATP}}\) and \(2 \times 12 = \mathbf{24\,\mathrm{NADPH}}\), of which \(2 \times 6 = \mathbf{12\,\mathrm{ATP}}\) are used in the regeneration of RuBP.

Practice Question 3 (Assertion–Reason — Photophosphorylation)

Given below are two statements: one is labelled as Assertion (A) and the other is labelled as Reason (R).
Assertion (A): Cyclic photophosphorylation operating in the stroma lamellae results only in the synthesis of ATP and not of \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\).
Reason (R): The stroma lamellae membranes lack both Photosystem II and the \(\mathrm{NADP}\) reductase enzyme.
In the light of the above statements, choose the correct answer from the options given below:

  • (1) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (2) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (3) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (4) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (1)
Worked Solution: Unlike grana lamellae (which have both PS I and PS II), the stroma lamellae membranes lack PS II as well as the \(\mathrm{NADP}\) reductase enzyme. Therefore, excited electrons from PS I (P700) cannot be passed on to \(\mathrm{NADP^+}\) and are instead cycled back to the PS I complex through the electron transport chain, generating only ATP. Thus, both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the exact structural explanation of (A).

  • (2) II and IV only
  • (3) II, III and IV only
  • (4) I, II and IV only

Practice Question 5 (Single-Choice MCQ — Water Splitting & Z-Scheme)

During non-cyclic electron transport (Z-scheme), the electrons released by the splitting of water molecules directly replace the electrons lost from:

  • (1) Reaction centre P700 of Photosystem I
  • (2) Reaction centre P680 of Photosystem II
  • (3) Primary electron acceptor of Photosystem II
  • (4) Ferredoxin and \(\mathrm{NADP}\) reductase complex
Correct Answer: (2)
Worked Solution: Splitting of water (\(2\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow 4\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{O_2} + 4\mathrm{e^-}\)) is associated with PS II. Practice Question 6 (Single-Choice MCQ — Calvin Cycle Energetics)

  • (2) Reduction: 6 ATP, 6 NADPH; Regeneration: 3 ATP
  • (3) Reduction: 6 ATP, 3 NADPH; Regeneration: 6 ATP
  • (4) Reduction: 18 ATP, 12 NADPH; Regeneration: 6 ATP
Correct Answer: (2)
Worked Solution: Per molecule of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) fixed in the Calvin cycle, the reduction step utilises \(2\,\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(2\,\mathrm{NADPH}\), and the regeneration step utilises \(1\,\mathrm{ATP}\). 10. Common NEET UG Pitfalls & Rapid Revision Checklist

High-Yield Traps to Avoid in the Exam
  • Trap 1 — Numbering vs. Sequence of Photosystems: PS I and PS II are named in the order of their discovery, NOT their operational sequence. In the Z-scheme, PS II (P680) functions first, followed by PS I (P700).
  • Trap 2 — Site of Proton Accumulation: In chloroplasts, protons accumulate in the thylakoid lumen (resulting in a decrease in lumen pH), whereas in mitochondria during respiration, protons accumulate in the intermembrane space. Also remember that \(\mathrm{CF_1}\) protrudes into the stroma.
  • Trap 3 — RuBisCO & Calvin Cycle in \(\mathrm{C_4}\) Plants: Never say \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants lack the Calvin cycle! The Calvin cycle operates in all photosynthetic plants. In \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants, it simply takes place exclusively inside the bundle sheath cells instead of mesophyll cells.
  • Trap 4 — Carbon Count of Acceptors vs. First Products:
    • \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plant: Primary acceptor = RuBP (5C); First product = PGA (3C).
    • \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plant: Primary acceptor = PEP (3C); First product = OAA (4C).
  • Trap 5 — Water as a Limiting Factor: Water stress limits photosynthesis primarily indirectly (by causing stomatal closure and leaf wilting) rather than by limiting the stoichiometric supply of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) for photolysis.

11. Concise Final Recap

  • Overall Equation: \(6\mathrm{CO_2} + 12\mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\mathrm{H_2O} + 6\mathrm{O_2}\) (\(\mathrm{O_2}\) comes entirely from \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)).
  • Pigments: Chlorophyll a (chief pigment, blue-green; absorption peaks in blue and red), chlorophyll b (yellow-green), xanthophylls (yellow), carotenoids (yellow to yellow-orange).
  • Light Reaction (Thylakoids): Non-cyclic photophosphorylation (Grana; PS II P680 \(\rightarrow\) ETS \(\rightarrow\) PS I P700 \(\rightarrow\) \(\mathrm{NADP^+}\); yields \(\mathrm{ATP}\), \(\mathrm{NADPH + H^+}\), and \(\mathrm{O_2}\)). Cyclic photophosphorylation (Stroma lamellae; PS I only; wavelengths \(> 680\,\mathrm{nm}\); yields \(\mathrm{ATP}\) only).
  • Chemiosmosis: Proton gradient builds up in thylakoid lumen (low pH) via water splitting, PQ proton pumping, and stromal \(\mathrm{H^+}\) removal by \(\mathrm{NADP}\) reductase; proton diffusion through \(\mathrm{CF_0\text{–}CF_1}\) ATP synthase drives ATP synthesis in the stroma.
  • Calvin Cycle (Stroma): Carboxylation (RuBisCO) \(\rightarrow\) Reduction (\(2\,\mathrm{ATP} + 2\,\mathrm{NADPH}\) per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) \(\rightarrow\) Regeneration (\(1\,\mathrm{ATP}\) per \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)). Total per glucose (\(6\,\mathrm{CO_2}\)): \(18\,\mathrm{ATP}\) and \(12\,\mathrm{NADPH}\).
  • \(\mathrm{C_4}\) & Photorespiration: \(\mathrm{C_4}\) plants (maize, sorghum) have Kranz anatomy, fix \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) in mesophyll via PEPcase into OAA (4C), decarboxylate \(\mathrm{C_4}\) acids in bundle sheath cells to concentrate \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) around RuBisCO, eliminate photorespiration (which in \(\mathrm{C_3}\) plants forms 3C phosphoglycerate + 2C phosphoglycolate), saturate at \(360\,\mathrm{µL\,L^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), and thrive at \(30\text{–}40\,\mathrm{°C}\).
