NEET UG • Human Physiology

Body Fluids and Circulation

Comprehensive Concept Mastery, Quantitative Mechanics, Diagnostic Interpretation, and Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Physiological Overview

Every living cell requires a continuous supply of oxygen (\(\mathrm{O_2}\)), nutrients, and regulatory molecules, alongside uninterrupted removal of carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) and metabolic wastes. Simple aquatic animals such as sponges and coelenterates circulate surrounding water through their body cavities to exchange materials directly with cells. Complex multicellular animals, however, rely on dedicated internal transport media.

In humans and other higher vertebrates, two fluids perform this role: blood (the primary high-pressure vascular fluid) and lymph (the interstitial fluid recovered by lymphatic vessels). By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Differentiate the cellular and acellular components of blood, including plasma proteins, formed elements, and serum.
  • Apply ABO and Rh antigen–antibody rules to transfusion compatibility and haemolytic disease of the newborn.
  • Trace the enzymatic cascade of blood coagulation and the role of \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) ions.
  • Compare open vs. closed circulation and 2-, 3-, and 4-chambered vertebrate hearts.
  • Map the human cardiac conduction pathway, phases of the \(0.8\,\mathrm{s}\) cardiac cycle, heart sounds, and ECG waves.
  • Calculate cardiac output, explain autonomic and hormonal regulation, and distinguish major cardiovascular disorders.

2. Blood: Plasma and Formed Elements

Blood is a specialised connective tissue composed of a fluid matrix called plasma (nearly \(55\%\) of total blood volume) and cellular components known as formed elements (nearly \(45\%\)). Unlike typical connective tissues, blood plasma contains no structural collagen or elastin fibres in its normal circulating state.

2.1 Plasma and Serum

Plasma is a straw-coloured, viscous fluid consisting of \(90\text{–}92\%\) water and \(6\text{–}8\%\) proteins, along with dissolved minerals (\(\mathrm{Na^+}\), \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\), \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\), \(\mathrm{HCO_3^-}\), \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\)), nutrients in transit (glucose, amino acids, lipids), and inactive clotting factors.

  • Fibrinogens: Essential for blood coagulation (converted into insoluble fibrin threads).
  • Globulins: Primarily involved in body defence mechanisms (antibodies/immunoglobulins).
  • Albumins: Maintain blood osmotic (oncotic) balance, preventing excessive fluid leakage into tissues.
\[\text{Serum} = \text{Plasma} - \text{Clotting Factors}\]

2.2 Formed Elements

The formed elements comprise erythrocytes, leucocytes, and platelets. Their normal adult reference values, structural traits, and primary roles are summarised below.

3. Blood Grouping and Coagulation

3.1 ABO Compatibility

The ABO system classifies blood based on the presence or absence of two surface antigens (A and B) on the RBC plasma membrane, paired with naturally occurring plasma antibodies (anti-A and anti-B). Mismatched transfusion triggers antigen–antibody agglutination (clumping and destruction of RBCs).

Blood Group RBC Surface Antigens Plasma Antibodies Safe Donor Groups (Can Receive From)
A A anti-B A, O
B B anti-A B, O
AB A and B Nil (none) AB, A, B, O (Universal Recipient)
O Nil (none) anti-A and anti-B O only (Universal Donor)

3.2 Rh System and Erythroblastosis Foetalis

Roughly \(80\%\) of humans carry the Rh antigen (similar to that in Rhesus monkeys) on their RBCs and are termed Rh-positive (\(\mathrm{Rh^+}\)); those lacking it are Rh-negative (\(\mathrm{Rh^-}\)). An \(\mathrm{Rh^-}\) individual exposed to \(\mathrm{Rh^+}\) blood develops specific anti-Rh antibodies.

Clinical Mechanism: Erythroblastosis Foetalis

When an \(\mathrm{Rh^-}\) mother carries an \(\mathrm{Rh^+}\) foetus, the placenta normally prevents mixing of maternal and foetal blood during the first pregnancy. However, during delivery of the first child, small amounts of foetal \(\mathrm{Rh^+}\) blood may enter maternal circulation, sensitising the mother to produce anti-Rh antibodies.

In subsequent pregnancies with an \(\mathrm{Rh^+}\) foetus, maternal anti-Rh antibodies cross into the foetal circulation and destroy foetal RBCs, causing severe anaemia, jaundice, or foetal death. This is prevented by administering anti-Rh antibodies to the mother immediately after the first delivery to clear any escaped foetal RBCs before sensitisation occurs.

4. Lymph (Tissue Fluid) and Blood Vessel Histology

As blood traverses tissue capillaries, water and small water-soluble solutes filter out into intercellular spaces, leaving larger plasma proteins and most formed elements inside the vessels. This interstitial (tissue) fluid shares the same mineral distribution as plasma and serves as the direct medium for nutrient and gas exchange with cells.

An extensive network of lymphatic vessels collects this fluid—now termed lymph—and drains it back into major veins. Key features of lymph include:

  • Colourless fluid lacking RBCs and large plasma proteins.
  • Rich in specialised lymphocytes responsible for immune responses.
  • Transports nutrients and hormones; notably, dietary fats are absorbed into lymph via lacteals in intestinal villi.

4.1 Structure of Arteries and Veins

Both arteries and veins possess three concentric histological layers, whereas capillaries consist solely of a single endothelial layer for rapid exchange:

  1. Tunica intima: Inner lining of squamous endothelium.
  2. Tunica media: Middle layer of smooth muscle and elastic fibres; thick in arteries (to withstand high pumping pressure) and comparatively thin in veins.
  3. Tunica externa: Outer layer of fibrous connective tissue rich in collagen fibres.

5. Circulatory Pathways & Double Circulation

Circulatory architectures fall into two broad categories: open circulatory systems (arthropods and molluscs, where blood pumped by the heart enters open body cavities called sinuses) and closed circulatory systems (annelids and chordates, where blood remains confined within vessels, enabling precise flow regulation).

Vertebrate Group Heart Chambers Circulation Type Key Physiological Feature
Fishes \(2\) (\(1\) atrium, \(1\) ventricle) Single circulation Heart pumps deoxygenated blood to gills for oxygenation, then directly to body tissues.
Amphibians & Reptiles (except crocodiles) \(3\) (\(2\) atria, \(1\) ventricle) Incomplete double circulation Oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix in the single ventricle before being pumped out.
Crocodiles, Birds & Mammals \(4\) (\(2\) atria, \(2\) ventricles) Complete double circulation Strict separation of oxygenated (left side) and deoxygenated (right side) pathways.

5.1 Human Double Circulation & Special Vascular Loops

  • Pulmonary Circulation: Right ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood via the pulmonary artery to the lungs; oxygenated blood returns via pulmonary veins to the left atrium.
  • Systemic Circulation: Left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood into the aorta, distributing it through arteries, arterioles, and capillaries to tissues; deoxygenated blood returns via venules, veins, and the vena cava to the right atrium.
  • Hepatic Portal System: A specialised vascular link between the digestive tract and liver; the hepatic portal vein carries nutrient-rich blood from the intestine to the liver before it enters systemic circulation.
  • Coronary Circulation: Dedicated vessels supplying oxygenated blood to and draining deoxygenated blood from the cardiac musculature itself.

6. Human Heart, Conduction System, Cardiac Cycle & ECG

6.1 Chambers, Valves, and Nodal Tissue

The human heart is mesodermally derived, roughly the size of a clenched fist, located in the thoracic cavity between the lungs and slightly tilted to the left. It is enclosed in a double-walled membranous pericardium containing pericardial fluid. Ventricular walls are substantially thicker than atrial walls to generate higher ejection pressures.

  • Septa: Thin muscular inter-atrial septum; thick inter-ventricular septum; thick fibrous atrio-ventricular (AV) septum.
  • Valves (ensure strictly unidirectional flow): Tricuspid valve (3 cusps) guards the right AV opening; Bicuspid (mitral) valve guards the left AV opening; Semilunar valves guard the openings of the right and left ventricles into the pulmonary artery and aorta, respectively.
  • Nodal System (Myogenic & Auto-excitable):
    • Sino-atrial node (SAN): Upper right corner of the right atrium; generates the maximum action potentials (\(70\text{–}75\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}\), average \(72\,\mathrm{beats\,min^{-1}}\)) and acts as the pacemaker.
    • Atrio-ventricular node (AVN): Lower left corner of the right atrium near the AV septum.
    • AV Bundle (Bundle of His) & Purkinje Fibres: Conducts impulses through the AV septum to the top of the inter-ventricular septum, dividing into right and left bundle branches that ramify as Purkinje fibres throughout the ventricular musculature.

6.2 Cardiac Cycle, Volumes, and Heart Sounds

At a resting rate of \(72\,\mathrm{beats\,min^{-1}}\), one complete cardiac cycle lasts:

\[\text{Duration of 1 Cardiac Cycle} = \frac{60\,\mathrm{s}}{72} \approx 0.8\,\mathrm{s}\]
  1. Joint Diastole: All four chambers are relaxed; tricuspid and bicuspid valves are open (semilunar valves closed), allowing passive blood flow from veins through atria into ventricles.
  2. Atrial Systole: SAN triggers simultaneous contraction of both atria, increasing ventricular filling by about \(30\%\).
  3. Ventricular Systole: Impulses reach ventricles via AVN, Bundle of His, and Purkinje fibres. Rising ventricular pressure closes tricuspid and bicuspid valves—producing the first heart sound ("Lub")—and forces semilunar valves open to eject blood into the pulmonary artery and aorta. Atria simultaneously enter diastole.
  4. Ventricular Diastole: Ventricular pressure drops, closing semilunar valves—producing the second heart sound ("Dub")—and soon AV valves are pushed open by atrial blood pressure, returning the heart to joint diastole.
\[\text{Cardiac Output (CO)} = \text{Stroke Volume (SV)} \times \text{Heart Rate (HR)}\] \[\text{Normal Resting Value: } 70\,\mathrm{mL} \times 72\,\mathrm{min^{-1}} \approx 5040\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}} \approx 5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\]

Exam Note: The body can modulate both stroke volume and heart rate; trained athletes typically achieve a much higher cardiac output than sedentary individuals.

6.3 Electrocardiogram (ECG)

An ECG records the electrical activity of the heart during each cycle using three standard leads (one on each wrist and one on the left ankle; chest leads are added for detailed evaluation).

  • P-wave: Electrical excitation (depolarisation) of the atria, leading to atrial contraction.
  • QRS complex: Depolarisation of the ventricles, initiating ventricular contraction (systole starts shortly after Q). Counting QRS complexes per minute gives the heart rate.
  • T-wave: Repolarisation of the ventricles (return from excited to normal state); the end of the T-wave marks the end of ventricular systole.

7. Regulation of Cardiac Activity & Circulatory Disorders

Although the heart is intrinsically auto-regulated (myogenic), a neural centre in the medulla oblongata moderates cardiac function via the Autonomic Nervous System (ANS):

  • Sympathetic signals: Increase heart rate, strength of ventricular contraction, and cardiac output.
  • Parasympathetic signals: Decrease heart rate, speed of action potential conduction, and cardiac output.
  • Hormonal control: Adrenal medullary hormones (adrenaline/noradrenaline) also elevate cardiac output.
Disorder Diagnostic Criteria & Pathophysiology
Hypertension Normal BP is \(120/80\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\) (systolic/diastolic). Repeated readings of \(140/90\,\mathrm{mm\,Hg}\) or higher indicate hypertension, damaging the heart, brain, and kidneys.
Coronary Artery Disease (CAD / Atherosclerosis) Narrowing of coronary artery lumen due to deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol, and fibrous tissue, restricting blood supply to cardiac muscle.
Angina Pectoris Acute chest pain caused by insufficient \(\mathrm{O_2}\) reaching the heart muscle due to impaired blood flow; more common in middle-aged and elderly individuals.
Heart Failure Heart fails to pump blood effectively enough to meet body demands; often called congestive heart failure due to lung congestion. Distinct from cardiac arrest (heart stops beating) and heart attack (sudden myocardial damage from inadequate blood supply).

8. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Cardiac Output & Stroke Volume Calculation (Hypothetical Assumption)

Problem: Assume a trained marathon runner has a resting heart rate of \(54\,\mathrm{beats\,min^{-1}}\) and a resting cardiac output of \(5.4\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\). Calculate the runner's resting stroke volume and the duration of one cardiac cycle.

Solution:

  • Convert cardiac output to millilitres: \(5.4\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}} = 5400\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}\).
  • Using \(\text{CO} = \text{SV} \times \text{HR}\): \[\text{SV} = \frac{5400\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}}{54\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}} = 100\,\mathrm{mL}\]
  • Duration of one cardiac cycle: \[T = \frac{60\,\mathrm{s}}{54} \approx 1.11\,\mathrm{s}\]

Takeaway: Endurance training increases resting stroke volume, allowing normal cardiac output at a lower resting heart rate.

Worked Example 2: Transfusion Compatibility & Rh Risk (Hypothetical Scenario)

Problem: Assume a patient with blood group \(\mathrm{B^-}\) requires an urgent packed-RBC transfusion. Which blood groups from a blood bank inventory (\(\mathrm{O^+}\), \(\mathrm{O^-}\), \(\mathrm{B^+}\), \(\mathrm{AB^-}\)) are safe, and why?

Solution:

  • The recipient has B antigens and anti-A antibodies, and lacks the Rh antigen (\(\mathrm{Rh^-}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{AB^-}\) carries A antigens \(\rightarrow\) attacked by recipient's anti-A antibodies (unsafe).
  • \(\mathrm{O^+}\) and \(\mathrm{B^+}\) carry Rh antigens \(\rightarrow\) sensitise the \(\mathrm{Rh^-}\) recipient to form anti-Rh antibodies (unsafe).
  • Only \(\mathrm{O^-}\) (lacks A, B, and Rh antigens) is safe from the listed options (alongside \(\mathrm{B^-}\) if available).

9. NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ
Which of the following combinations correctly matches a plasma protein with its primary physiological role?
  • (A) Albumin — Blood coagulation
  • (B) Fibrinogen — Osmotic balance
  • (C) Globulin — Defence mechanism
  • (D) Thrombin — Oxygen transport
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Globulins primarily function in body defence mechanisms (antibodies). Albumins maintain osmotic balance, and fibrinogens are required for blood clotting. Haemoglobin (inside RBCs, not a plasma protein) transports \(\mathrm{O_2}\).

Practice Question 2 • Statement-Based MCQ
Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Basophils are the least abundant granulocytes and secrete histamine, serotonin, and heparin.
Statement II: Neutrophils and monocytes are both phagocytic leucocytes that destroy foreign organisms.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Basophils constitute \(0.5\text{–}1\%\) of total WBCs (the least abundant) and release inflammatory mediators (histamine, serotonin, heparin). Neutrophils (\(60\text{–}65\%\)) and monocytes (\(6\text{–}8\%\)) are the principal phagocytic WBCs.

Practice Question 3 • Assertion–Reason
Assertion (A): Administering anti-Rh antibodies to an \(\mathrm{Rh^-}\) mother immediately after delivering her first \(\mathrm{Rh^+}\) child prevents erythroblastosis foetalis in subsequent pregnancies.
Reason (R): The administered antibodies destroy any foetal \(\mathrm{Rh^+}\) RBCs that entered maternal blood during delivery before the mother's immune system becomes sensitised.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Clearing escaped foetal \(\mathrm{Rh^+}\) erythrocytes immediately post-partum prevents maternal memory cell formation against the Rh antigen, protecting future \(\mathrm{Rh^+}\) foetuses.

Practice Question 4 • Numerical / Quantitative MCQ
Assume an individual's ECG shows \(15\) QRS complexes in \(12\,\mathrm{s}\), and each ventricle ejects \(65\,\mathrm{mL}\) of blood per beat. What is the individual's cardiac output?
  • (A) \(4.55\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(4.875\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(5.20\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(5.85\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Heart rate \(= \frac{15}{12\,\mathrm{s}} \times 60\,\mathrm{s\,min^{-1}} = 75\,\mathrm{beats\,min^{-1}}\). Cardiac output \(= 75 \times 65\,\mathrm{mL} = 4875\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}} = 4.875\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 5 • Single-Correct MCQ
Which event directly causes the first heart sound ("Lub") during a cardiac cycle?
  • (A) Closure of semilunar valves at the onset of ventricular diastole
  • (B) Opening of tricuspid and bicuspid valves during joint diastole
  • (C) Closure of tricuspid and bicuspid valves at the onset of ventricular systole
  • (D) Contraction of atria triggered by the sino-atrial node
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: As ventricular systole begins, rising ventricular pressure attempts to push blood back into the atria, snapping shut the tricuspid and bicuspid (mitral) valves to produce "Lub". Closure of semilunar valves produces "Dub".

Practice Question 7 • Single-Correct MCQ
Which set of vertebrates possesses a \(4\)-chambered heart with complete double circulation?
  • (A) Amphibians, snakes, and mammals
  • (B) Crocodiles, birds, and mammals

Practice Question 8 • Single-Correct MCQ
Which statement accurately distinguishes heart failure from cardiac arrest?
  • (A) Heart failure means the heart stops beating completely, whereas cardiac arrest is lung congestion.
  • (B) Heart failure is ineffective pumping to meet body needs, whereas cardiac arrest is complete cessation of heartbeat.
  • (C) Heart failure is caused solely by hypertension, whereas cardiac arrest is identical to angina pectoris.
  • (D) Both terms describe sudden myocardial necrosis due to coronary artery blockage.
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Heart failure (often causing pulmonary congestion) occurs when the heart pumps insufficiently to meet tissue demands. Cardiac arrest is when the heart stops beating, and a heart attack is sudden muscle damage from inadequate blood supply.

10. High-Yield Pitfalls & Revision Recap

Common NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Pulmonary Vessels Exception: The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood, while pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.
  • Crocodile Exception: Most reptiles have \(3\)-chambered hearts, but crocodiles have \(4\)-chambered hearts.
  • Serum vs. Lymph: Serum is plasma minus clotting factors; lymph is interstitial fluid inside lymphatic vessels (lacks RBCs and large proteins, rich in lymphocytes).
  • Atrial Systole Contribution: Atrial contraction adds only about \(30\%\) to ventricular filling; roughly \(70\%\) occurs passively during joint diastole.
  • ECG Timing: Ventricular systole begins shortly after Q and ends at the end of the T-wave (not at the R peak).

Concise Final Recap

  • Blood: \(55\%\) plasma (fibrinogen, globulins, albumins) + \(45\%\) formed elements (RBCs, WBCs, platelets).
  • Coagulation: Injury \(\rightarrow\) thrombokinase (\(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)) \(\rightarrow\) prothrombin to thrombin \(\rightarrow\) fibrinogen to fibrin mesh.
  • Heart & Cycle: Myogenic SAN pacemaker (\(72\,\mathrm{bpm}\)) \(\rightarrow\) \(0.8\,\mathrm{s}\) cycle \(\rightarrow\) \(\text{SV} \approx 70\,\mathrm{mL}\), \(\text{CO} \approx 5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\).
  • Regulation: Medulla oblongata via Sympathetic (\(\uparrow\) HR, \(\uparrow\) CO) and Parasympathetic (\(\downarrow\) HR, \(\downarrow\) CO) nerves.
