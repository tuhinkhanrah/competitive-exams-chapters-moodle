Organisms and Populations

Comprehensive Concept Tutorial, Mathematical Models, Ecological Interactions, and Exam-Oriented Practice

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Overview

Ecology examines how living organisms interact with one another and with their physical (abiotic) surroundings across four hierarchical levels: organisms, populations, communities, and biomes. For NEET UG, population ecology is a high-yield topic that integrates conceptual biology, demographic graphs, differential equations, and classic field-experiment examples.

What You Will Master in This Tutorial
  • Distinguish between individual-level traits and population-level attributes (birth/death rates, sex ratio, age pyramids, and population density).
  • Analyze population fluctuations using natality, mortality, immigration, and emigration.
  • Compare exponential (J-shaped) and Verhulst–Pearl logistic (S-shaped) growth models and interpret carrying capacity (\(K\)) and intrinsic rate of natural increase (\(r\)).
  • Connect life-history strategies to Darwinian fitness under different environmental constraints.
  • Classify and apply the six major interspecific interactions—mutualism, competition, predation, parasitism, commensalism, and amensalism—using established ecological examples.

Foundational Context in Indian Ecology: Ramdeo Misra (1908–1998), honoured as the Father of Ecology in India, earned his Ph.D. in Ecology in 1937 under Prof. W. H. Pearsall at Leeds University (UK) and established ecological teaching and research at Banaras Hindu University, Varanasi. His work on tropical communities, succession, and productivity led to the first postgraduate ecology curriculum in India and contributed to the formation of the National Committee for Environmental Planning and Coordination (1972) and later the Ministry of Environment and Forests (1984).

2. Populations and Their Emergent Attributes

In natural habitats, organisms rarely exist in total isolation. A population is a group of individuals belonging to the same species that occupy a defined geographical area, share or compete for similar resources, and potentially interbreed. In ecological studies, a group of organisms produced by asexual reproduction (such as bacteria in a culture plate or clonal plants in a pond) is also treated as a population.

While an individual organism must physiologically cope with environmental changes, natural selection operates at the population level to alter gene frequencies and evolve adaptive traits. Consequently, population ecology bridges ecology with population genetics and evolution.

2.1 Attributes Exclusive to a Population

An individual organism is born and dies, and has a specific age and sex, but only a population possesses statistical group attributes:

  • Per Capita Birth Rate (Natality) and Death Rate (Mortality): Expressed as the change in number of individuals per member of the population per unit time. For example, if a pond has 20 water lilies and 8 new plants arise by reproduction in one year, the per capita birth rate is \(8 / 20 = 0.4\) offspring per lily per year.
  • Sex Ratio: The proportion of males to females in a population (for instance, 60% females and 40% males).
  • Age Distribution and Age Pyramids: A population consists of individuals of different ages. Plotting the percentage of individuals across three ecological age groups—pre-reproductive, reproductive, and post-reproductive—produces an age pyramid whose geometry predicts future population growth.
  • Population Density (\(N\)): The size of a population in a given habitat. Depending on the organism, density is measured in different ways.

2.2 Measuring Population Density

Although total headcount is the most direct measure of population density (\(N\)), it is not always practical or ecologically meaningful:

Measurement Method When Used Standard Ecological Example
Absolute Number Discrete, countable organisms in a defined area Siberian cranes (<10) at Bharatpur wetlands; cormorants in a wetland
Percent Cover or Biomass Huge size disparity between species makes raw counts misleading 1 giant banyan tree with a massive canopy vs. 200 Parthenium (carrot grass) plants
Relative Density Direct counting is impossible or unnecessary Number of fish caught per trap in a lake; dense bacterial culture in a petri dish
Indirect Estimation Elusive or nocturnal animals Tiger census in national parks using pug marks and fecal pellets

3. Population Dynamics and Growth Models

Population size is never static; it fluctuates with food availability, predation pressure, and weather. Four fundamental processes govern changes in population density over a time interval:

  • Natality (\(B\)): Number of births added to the initial density. (Increases \(N\))
  • Immigration (\(I\)): Number of individuals of the same species entering the habitat from elsewhere. (Increases \(N\))
  • Mortality (\(D\)): Number of deaths during the period. (Decreases \(N\))
  • Emigration (\(E\)): Number of individuals leaving the habitat. (Decreases \(N\))
Tutorial Diagram 2: Four demographic processes controlling population density (N).
\[N_{t+1} = N_t + [(B + I) - (D + E)]\]

Population density increases when \((B + I) > (D + E)\). Under stable, established conditions, natality and mortality are the primary drivers. However, when a new habitat is being colonised, immigration (\(I\)) often contributes more significantly to initial population growth than births.

3.1 Exponential (Geometric) Growth

When food and space are unlimited, every species realises its full innate potential to multiply. If \(b\) represents per capita birth rate and \(d\) represents per capita death rate, the rate of change of population size \(N\) over time \(t\) is:

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = (b - d)N = rN \qquad \text{and in integral form:} \qquad N_t = N_0 e^{rt}\]
  • \(r = (b - d)\): The intrinsic rate of natural increase, a crucial index for evaluating how biotic or abiotic factors affect population growth. Established benchmark values include \(r = 0.015\) for the Norway rat, \(r = 0.12\) for the flour beetle, and \(r = 0.0205\) for the Indian human population in 1981.
  • \(e\): Base of natural logarithms (\(\approx 2.71828\)).
  • Plotting \(N\) against \(t\) yields a characteristic J-shaped curve. Even slow-breeding animals such as elephants could reach immense numbers under unchecked exponential growth.

3.2 Verhulst–Pearl Logistic Growth

In real ecosystems, resources are finite and eventually become limiting, triggering competition for survival and reproduction. A habitat can sustain only a maximum possible population size, known as its carrying capacity (\(K\)).

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = rN \left( \frac{K - N}{K} \right)\]

Here, \(\left(\frac{K - N}{K}\right)\) acts as environmental resistance. A population growing under limited resources passes through four stages—a lag phase, a phase of acceleration, a phase of deceleration, and finally an asymptote when \(N = K\) (so \(\frac{dN}{dt} = 0\)). Plotting \(N\) versus \(t\) yields a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve, making the logistic model far more realistic for natural populations.

3.3 Life History Variation

Populations evolve reproductive strategies that maximise Darwinian fitness (a high \(r\) value) under specific habitat pressures. Ecologists observe distinct trade-offs:

  • Breeding Frequency: Some organisms breed only once in their lifetime (e.g., Pacific salmon fish, bamboo), whereas others breed multiple times across their lifespan (e.g., most birds and mammals).
  • Offspring Number vs. Size: Some produce huge numbers of small offspring (e.g., oysters, pelagic fishes), while others invest in a small number of large offspring (e.g., birds, mammals).

4. Interspecific Population Interactions

No species can survive in complete isolation. Even autotrophic plants require soil microbes to mineralise organic matter and animal pollinators for reproduction. Interactions between populations of two different species are classified by their net outcome: beneficial (\(+\)), detrimental (\(-\)), or neutral (\(0\)).

Interaction Species A Species B Key Ecological Feature
Mutualism \(+\) \(+\) Both species gain a net benefit; often involves co-evolution.
Competition \(-\) \(-\) Fitness (\(r\)) of both species is reduced in each other's presence.
Predation \(+\) \(-\) Predator benefits; prey is consumed. Drives trophic energy transfer.
Parasitism \(+\) \(-\) Parasite obtains lodging and food; host is harmed without immediate death.
Commensalism \(+\) \(0\) One species benefits; the other is neither helped nor harmed.
Amensalism \(-\) \(0\) One species is harmed; the other remains unaffected.

4.1 Predation (\(+, -\))

Predation transfers energy fixed by autotrophs to higher trophic levels. Ecologically, herbivores (such as a seed-eating sparrow) function similarly to carnivores as predators of plants.

  • Population Regulation & Biological Control: Predators keep prey numbers in check. When an exotic species enters a region lacking its natural predators, it becomes invasive—for example, the prickly pear cactus introduced into Australia in the early 1920s spread over millions of hectares until a cactus-feeding moth from its native habitat was introduced to control it.
  • Maintaining Species Diversity: By reducing competition among prey species, predators preserve community diversity. On the rocky intertidal coast of the American Pacific, removing the starfish Pisaster caused more than 10 invertebrate species to go extinct within a year due to unchecked interspecific competition.
  • Prudent Predators & Prey Defences: Over-efficient predators would drive their prey—and consequently themselves—to extinction. Prey species evolve defences such as camouflage (cryptic colouration in insects and frogs) and chemical toxins (the Monarch butterfly is distasteful to birds because its caterpillar feeds on a poisonous weed).
  • Plant Defences Against Herbivory: Nearly 25% of all insects are phytophagous. Because plants cannot flee, they rely on morphological defences (thorns in Acacia and Cactus) and chemical defences—such as poisonous cardiac glycosides in Calotropis (avoided by cattle and goats), as well as nicotine, caffeine, quinine, strychnine, and opium.

4.2 Competition (\(-, -\))

Competition is best defined as a process in which the fitness of one species (measured as \(r\)) is significantly lowered by the presence of another species. Two common misconceptions must be avoided:

  1. Competition is not restricted to closely related species—unrelated species can compete for the same resource (e.g., visiting flamingoes and resident fishes competing for zooplankton in shallow South American lakes).
  2. Resources need not be limiting—in interference competition, the feeding efficiency of one species is reduced simply by the inhibitory presence of another, even when food and space are abundant.
  • Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle: When resources are limiting, two closely related species competing for the exact same resources cannot coexist indefinitely; the competitively superior species eventually eliminates the inferior one. Circumstantial field evidence includes the extinction of the Abingdon tortoise in the Galapagos Islands within a decade of goat introduction.
  • Competitive Release: A species restricted to a narrow zone by a superior competitor rapidly expands its range when the competitor is removed. Connell's field experiment on Scotland's rocky coast showed that the larger barnacle Balanus dominates the intertidal area and excludes the smaller Chthamalus; removing Balanus allows Chthamalus to expand.
  • Resource Partitioning: Competing species can evolve behavioural differences to coexist rather than exclude one another. MacArthur demonstrated that five closely related species of warblers living on the same tree avoided competition by foraging at different times and using different parts of the tree.

4.3 Parasitism (\(+, -\))

Parasites secure free lodging and nutrition at the expense of their host, often reducing host survival, growth, and reproduction. Many parasites are host-specific and co-evolve with their hosts.

  • Parasitic Adaptations: Loss of unnecessary sense organs, presence of adhesive organs or suckers, loss of the digestive system, and very high reproductive capacity.
  • Complex Life Cycles: Many require intermediate hosts or vectors—for example, the human liver fluke (a trematode) uses a snail and a fish as two intermediate hosts, while the malarial parasite requires a mosquito vector.
  • Ectoparasites vs. Endoparasites: Ectoparasites live on the host's exterior (lice on humans, ticks on dogs, copepods on marine fish, and the leafless, chlorophyll-lacking plant Cuscuta on hedge plants). Endoparasites inhabit internal organs like the liver, lungs, kidneys, or red blood cells and show extreme anatomical simplification.
  • Brood Parasitism: Observed in birds where the parasitic bird (such as the cuckoo/koel) lays its eggs in a host's nest (such as a crow) for incubation. Over evolution, the parasitic eggs mimic the host's eggs in size and colour to avoid detection and ejection.
NEET UG Distinction: Why a Female Mosquito Is Not a Parasite

Although a female mosquito feeds on human blood, she is not classified ecologically as a parasite because she only needs a blood meal for egg development/reproduction and does not live in continuous association with the host for lodging.

4.4 Commensalism (\(+, 0\))

One species benefits while the other is neither harmed nor benefited. Classic NEET UG examples include:

  • An orchid growing as an epiphyte on a mango branch.
  • Barnacles attached to the back of a whale.
  • Cattle egrets foraging alongside grazing cattle (cattle stir up insects from vegetation, making them easy for egrets to catch).
  • Clown fish living among the stinging tentacles of a sea anemone (the fish gains protection from predators, while the anemone gains no apparent benefit).

4.5 Mutualism (\(+, +\))

Both interacting species derive mutual benefit, frequently leading to tight co-evolution:

  • Lichens: Intimate mutualism between a fungus and photosynthesising algae or cyanobacteria.
  • Mycorrhizae: Association between fungi and roots of higher plants (fungi aid mineral/nutrient absorption; plants supply carbohydrates).
  • Fig Tree and Wasp: A strict one-to-one species relationship. The female wasp pollinates the fig inflorescence while searching for an egg-laying (oviposition) site, and in return the fig provides some developing seeds inside the fruit as food for the wasp larvae.
  • Sexual Deceit in Orchids: The Mediterranean orchid Ophrys has a petal that mimics the female of a specific bee species in size, colour, and markings. The male bee attempts 'pseudocopulation' with the petal, picks up pollen, and transfers it to the next flower—requiring tight co-evolution between orchid petal and female bee appearance.

5. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Net Population Change Using Demographic Flux

Scenario (Assumed Values): Suppose a wetland initially supports \(N_t = 320\) waterbirds at the start of a year. During the year, 45 chicks hatch (\(B = 45\)), 15 birds immigrate into the wetland (\(I = 15\)), 28 birds die (\(D = 28\)), and 12 birds emigrate (\(E = 12\)). Calculate the population size \(N_{t+1}\) at the end of the year.

Solution:

\[N_{t+1} = N_t + [(B + I) - (D + E)]\] \[N_{t+1} = 320 + [(45 + 15) - (28 + 12)] = 320 + [60 - 40] = 340\text{ waterbirds.}\]
Worked Example 2: Exponential Growth Rate and Per Capita Comparison

Scenario (Assumed Values): In a hypothetical laboratory culture of 500 yeast cells, 90 new cells are formed and 15 cells die during a 1-hour interval. Assuming unlimited resources, find the intrinsic rate of natural increase \(r\) (per hour) and the instantaneous growth rate \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) at \(N = 500\).

Solution:

First, compute per capita birth rate (\(b\)) and per capita death rate (\(d\)):

\[b = \frac{90}{500} = 0.18\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}, \qquad d = \frac{15}{500} = 0.03\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}\]

Intrinsic rate of natural increase:

\[r = b - d = 0.18 - 0.03 = 0.15\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}\]

Instantaneous growth rate at \(N = 500\):

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = rN = 0.15 \times 500 = 75\text{ cells h}^{-1}.\]
Worked Example 3: Verhulst–Pearl Logistic Growth Rate

Scenario (Assumed Values): A grassland habitat has a carrying capacity of \(K = 1000\) deer, and the deer population has an intrinsic rate of natural increase \(r = 0.2\text{ year}^{-1}\). Calculate the growth rate \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) when the current population size is \(N = 250\).

Solution:

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = rN \left( \frac{K - N}{K} \right) = 0.2 \times 250 \times \left( \frac{1000 - 250}{1000} \right)\] \[\frac{dN}{dt} = 50 \times 0.75 = 37.5\text{ deer year}^{-1}.\]

6. NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are designed to match the NEET UG Biology examination pattern (single-correct MCQs, statement-based questions, assertion–reason, and matrix matching).

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following attributes is possessed exclusively by a biological population rather than by an individual organism?

  1. (A) Birth and death
  2. (B) Sex of the organism
  3. (C) Age distribution
  4. (D) Cellular respiration
Answer: (C) Age distribution

Explanation: An individual organism experiences birth and death, has a specific sex, and performs cellular respiration. However, statistical group properties such as birth rate, death rate, sex ratio, and age distribution (age pyramid) belong only to a population.

Practice Question 2 Single-Correct MCQ

An age pyramid for a human population displays a noticeably narrower base (pre-reproductive group) compared to the reproductive age group. This population is best described as:

  1. (A) Expanding
  2. (B) Stable
  3. (C) Declining
  4. (D) Exponentially growing
Answer: (C) Declining

Explanation: When the pre-reproductive cohort is smaller than the reproductive cohort, fewer individuals will enter the reproductive phase in the future, indicating negative growth (a declining population).

Practice Question 3 Numerical MCQ

In a hypothetical lake habitat, a population of 250 fish experiences 50 births, 20 deaths, 10 immigrations, and 15 emigrations over one season. What is the per capita birth rate for that season, and what is the final population size?

  1. (A) \(0.20\) per fish; \(275\) fish
  2. (B) \(0.25\) per fish; \(275\) fish
  3. (C) \(0.20\) per fish; \(285\) fish
  4. (D) \(0.12\) per fish; \(265\) fish
Answer: (A) \(0.20\) per fish; \(275\) fish

Explanation: Per capita birth rate \(= \frac{50}{250} = 0.20\) offspring per fish per season. Final population size \(N_{t+1} = 250 + [(50 + 10) - (20 + 15)] = 250 + (60 - 35) = 275\) fish.

Practice Question 4 Single-Correct MCQ

In the Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth equation \(\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(\frac{K - N}{K}\right)\), what happens as the population density \(N\) approaches the carrying capacity \(K\)?

  1. (A) \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) reaches its maximum possible value
  2. (B) Environmental resistance approaches zero
  3. (C) \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) approaches zero and the curve reaches an asymptote
  4. (D) The intrinsic rate of natural increase \(r\) becomes negative
Answer: (C) \(\frac{dN}{dt}\) approaches zero and the curve reaches an asymptote

Explanation: As \(N \to K\), the factor \(\left(\frac{K - N}{K}\right) \to 0\), so the population growth rate \(\frac{dN}{dt} \to 0\). This produces the upper asymptote of the S-shaped logistic curve.

Practice Question 5 Single-Correct MCQ

To compare the ecological dominance of 150 small herbaceous weeds and 1 massive banyan tree in the same forest plot, which measure of population density is most appropriate?

  1. (A) Absolute headcount
  2. (B) Percent cover or biomass
  3. (C) Pug-mark count
  4. (D) Number of individuals per trap
Answer: (B) Percent cover or biomass

Explanation: When organisms differ drastically in size, comparing raw counts underestimates the ecological role of the large tree. Biomass or percent canopy cover provides a meaningful comparison.

Practice Question 6 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): Predators help maintain species diversity within a biological community.
Reason (R): Predators reduce the intensity of interspecific competition among competing prey species.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  4. (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

Explanation: By keeping dominant prey species in check, predators prevent competitive exclusion among prey, thereby preserving species diversity—as demonstrated by the removal of the starfish Pisaster on the American Pacific coast.

Practice Question 7 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Interspecific competition can occur only when resources are strictly limiting.
Statement II: Closely related species competing for the same resource may evolve resource partitioning to coexist.

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.

Explanation: Statement I is false because in interference competition, one species reduces the feeding efficiency of another even when resources are abundant. Statement II is true: MacArthur's warblers coexist on the same tree via behavioural differences in foraging (resource partitioning).

7. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

  • Pitfall 1 — Confusing \(r\) with Total Growth: \(r = (b - d)\) is a per capita rate, whereas \(\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\) is the population-level growth rate.
  • Pitfall 2 — Misclassifying Parasitism vs. Predation: Both are \((+, -)\) interactions, but predators typically kill and consume prey immediately, whereas parasites live in close, prolonged association with a living host.
  • Pitfall 3 — Over-generalising Gause's Principle: Competitive exclusion applies when resources are limiting; species can coexist via resource partitioning.
  • Pitfall 4 — Forgetting Indirect Density Measures: Tiger censuses rely on pug marks and fecal pellets, and banyan vs. Parthenium comparisons rely on biomass or percent cover.

8. Concise Final Recap

A population is the unit of natural selection, characterised by per capita birth/death rates, sex ratio, age pyramids (expanding, stable, declining), and density (\(N\)). Density changes via \(N_{t+1} = N_t + [(B + I) - (D + E)]\). Under unlimited resources, populations grow exponentially (\(\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\), J-curve); under finite resources, they follow Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth (\(\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(\frac{K - N}{K}\right)\), S-curve) limited by carrying capacity (\(K\)). In nature, species form communities through six interspecific interactions—mutualism (\(+, +\)), competition (\(-, -\)), predation (\(+, -\)), parasitism (\(+, -\)), commensalism (\(+, 0\)), and amensalism (\(-, 0\))—driving co-evolution, trophic energy flow, and community diversity.
