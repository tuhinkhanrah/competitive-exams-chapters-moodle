NEET UG Biology • Plant Reproduction

Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

A comprehensive, exam-oriented guide to angiosperm reproductive morphology, gametogenesis, pollination, double fertilisation, seed biology, and apomixis.

1. Learning Objectives & Big-Picture Overview

In angiosperms, the flower is the specialised reproductive shoot that ensures genetic recombination and continuity of the species. The dominant plant body is a diploid sporophyte (\(2n\)), which produces haploid male and female gametophytes (\(n\)) inside the floral organs. By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Trace the structural organisation of the stamen and microsporangium, and explain microsporogenesis and pollen maturation.
  • Describe the anatomy of a typical anatropous ovule and the monosporic development of the 7-celled, 8-nucleate embryo sac.
  • Classify pollination modes, abiotic/biotic adaptations, outbreeding devices, and pollen–pistil recognition events.
  • Explain double fertilisation, track ploidy levels across post-fertilisation structures, and distinguish monocot from dicot embryos.
  • Differentiate apomixis, polyembryony, and parthenocarpy, and solve NEET UG numerical and conceptual problems accurately.
Core Conceptual Arc

Angiosperm sexual reproduction unfolds in three sequential phases: Pre-fertilisation (gametogenesis, pollination, and pollen–pistil interaction), Double Fertilisation (syngamy + triple fusion, unique to angiosperms), and Post-fertilisation (endosperm nourishment, embryogeny, ovule-to-seed maturation, and ovary-to-fruit transformation).

2. Male Reproductive Unit: Stamen, Microsporangium & Pollen Grain

2.1 Stamen and Anther Organisation

The male whorl (androecium) consists of stamens. Each stamen has a slender proximal stalk called the filament (attached to the thalamus or petal) and a terminal anther. A typical angiosperm anther is bilobed and dithecous—each lobe contains two chambers (thecae) separated by a longitudinal groove. In transverse section, the anther is tetragonal and houses four microsporangia (one at each corner, two per lobe), making it tetrasporangiate. As the anther matures, these microsporangia elongate into longitudinal pollen sacs.

2.2 Microsporangium Wall Layers & Microsporogenesis

A young microsporangium is nearly circular in cross-section and is enclosed by four distinct wall layers surrounding a central mass of homogeneous, compactly arranged diploid sporogenous tissue:

  • Epidermis: Outermost single protective layer.
  • Endothecium: Fibrous layer beneath the epidermis; its hygroscopic thickenings assist in anther dehiscence.
  • Middle layers: Typically 1–3 parenchymatous layers that provide protection and degenerate at maturity.
  • Tapetum: Innermost nutritive layer. Its cells possess dense cytoplasm and are commonly multinucleate or polyploid (via endomitosis). The tapetum nourishes developing microspores and secretes precursors for sporopollenin, pollenkitt, and compatibility proteins.

Every cell of the sporogenous tissue acts as a potential pollen mother cell (PMC) or microspore mother cell (\(2n\)). Through microsporogenesis—meiotic division of a PMC—four haploid microspores (\(n\)) are formed in a cluster called a microspore tetrad. As the anther matures and dehydrates, the microspores dissociate and develop into pollen grains.

2.3 Pollen Grain (Male Gametophyte) Structure & Viability

Pollen grains are typically spherical (\(25\text{–}50\,\mathrm{µm}\) in diameter) and represent the haploid male gametophyte. Each grain has a two-layered wall (sporoderm):

  • Exine: Hard, sculpted outer layer composed of sporopollenin—one of the most resistant organic polymers known, withstanding extreme heat, strong acids, and alkalis, with no known degrading enzyme. Because of sporopollenin, pollen grains are exceptionally well preserved as fossils. Thin areas lacking sporopollenin form germ pores, through which the pollen tube emerges.
  • Intine: Thin, continuous inner wall made of cellulose and pectin.

Upon maturation, the haploid microspore undergoes an asymmetric mitotic division to produce two unequal cells:

  • Vegetative cell: Larger, with abundant food reserves and a large, irregularly shaped nucleus.
  • Generative cell: Smaller, spindle-shaped, with dense cytoplasm and a nucleus, floating inside the cytoplasm of the vegetative cell.

In over 60% of angiosperms, pollen is shed at this 2-celled stage (the generative cell later divides mitotically inside the pollen tube to form two male gametes). In the remaining species (under 40%), the generative cell divides prior to shedding, releasing pollen at the 3-celled stage (one vegetative cell + two male gametes).

Applied Biology & Viability: Pollen of several species triggers severe respiratory allergies (asthma, bronchitis), notably Parthenium (carrot grass), which entered India as a contaminant with imported wheat. Conversely, pollen is nutrient-rich and marketed as tablets/syrups. Pollen viability varies widely with temperature and humidity—lasting barely 30 minutes in cereals such as rice and wheat, but persisting for months in members of Rosaceae, Leguminosae, and Solanaceae. Pollen can be cryopreserved for years in liquid nitrogen at \(-196\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in pollen banks for crop breeding.

3. Female Reproductive Unit: Pistil, Ovule & Embryo Sac

3.1 Pistil & Anatropous Ovule Structure

The gynoecium may consist of a single pistil (monocarpellary) or multiple pistils (multicarpellary), which may be fused (syncarpous, e.g., Papaver) or free (apocarpous, e.g., Michelia). Each pistil has three parts: the receptive stigma, the slender style, and the basal bulged ovary containing one or more ovarian cavities (locules). Inside the locule lies the placenta, from which megasporangia (ovules) arise. An ovary may house a single ovule (wheat, paddy, mango) or many ovules (papaya, watermelon, orchids).

A typical angiosperm ovule (commonly inverted or anatropous) consists of:

  • Funicle: Stalk attaching the ovule to the placenta.
  • Hilum: Junction where the ovule body fuses with the funicle.
  • Integuments: One or two protective envelopes encircling the nucellus, leaving a small apical pore called the micropyle.
  • Chalaza: Basal pole of the ovule, opposite the micropylar end.
  • Nucellus: Central mass of diploid parenchymatous cells rich in reserve food, enclosing the embryo sac.

3.2 Megasporogenesis & Monosporic Embryo Sac Development

In the micropylar region of the nucellus, a single large diploid megaspore mother cell (MMC) differentiates with dense cytoplasm and a prominent nucleus. The MMC undergoes meiosis to produce a linear tetrad of four haploid megaspores (megasporogenesis). In most angiosperms, three megaspores degenerate and only one functional megaspore (typically the chalazal one) develops into the female gametophyte—a pattern termed monosporic development.

The nucleus of the functional megaspore undergoes three sequential free-nuclear mitotic divisions (nuclear division without immediate cell wall formation), progressing through 2-nucleate, 4-nucleate, and 8-nucleate stages. Subsequently, cell walls are laid down to organise the mature 7-celled, 8-nucleate embryo sac:

  • Egg apparatus (micropylar end): 3 cells—two synergids and one egg cell. Synergids bear finger-like wall thickenings at the micropylar tip called the filiform apparatus, which chemically and physically guides the pollen tube into a synergid.
  • Antipodals (chalazal end): 3 cells that generally degenerate after fertilisation.
  • Central cell: A single large central cell containing two haploid polar nuclei (which lie below the egg apparatus and often fuse just before or at fertilisation).

4. Pollination, Outbreeding Devices & Pollen–Pistil Interaction

4.1 Kinds of Pollination

Because both male and female gametes in angiosperms are non-motile, pollination—the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma—is essential. Based on the source of pollen, pollination is classified into three types:

  • Autogamy: Transfer of pollen from anther to stigma of the same flower. Requires synchrony in pollen release and stigma receptivity, and close proximity of anthers and stigma. Plants like Viola (common pansy), Oxalis, and Commelina bear both open chasmogamous flowers and closed cleistogamous flowers. Cleistogamous flowers never open, guaranteeing autogamous seed-set even without pollinators, though at the cost of reduced genetic variation.
  • Geitonogamy: Transfer of pollen from the anther of one flower to the stigma of another flower on the same plant. Functionally it resembles cross-pollination (requires a pollinating agent), but genetically it is equivalent to autogamy because both flowers share the same genome.
  • Xenogamy: Transfer of pollen from the anther of a flower on one plant to the stigma of a flower on a genetically different plant. This is the only pollination type that introduces genetically distinct pollen to the stigma.

4.3 Outbreeding Devices & Artificial Hybridisation

Continuous self-pollination leads to inbreeding depression. Angiosperms discourage self-pollination and promote cross-pollination via four major outbreeding devices:

  1. Non-synchronised pollen release and stigma receptivity (Dichogamy): Either pollen is shed before the stigma is receptive, or the stigma becomes receptive before pollen release.
  2. Spatial separation (Herkogamy): Anther and stigma are positioned at different heights/locations, preventing self-pollen contact.
  3. Self-incompatibility: A genetically controlled mechanism that inhibits self-pollen germination or pollen tube growth in the pistil (prevents both autogamy and geitonogamy).
  4. Unisexual flowers: Monoecious plants (e.g., castor, maize) bear separate male and female flowers on the same plant—preventing autogamy but not geitonogamy. Dioecious plants (e.g., papaya) have male and female flowers on separate plants—preventing both autogamy and geitonogamy.

Pollen–Pistil Interaction & Crop Breeding: The pistil chemically recognises whether landed pollen is compatible (right species/self-compatible) or incompatible. Following compatible pollination, a pollen tube emerges through a germ pore, grows through the stigma and style, enters the ovule via the micropyle, and penetrates one of the synergids guided by the filiform apparatus. In artificial hybridisation, breeders use emasculation (removal of anthers from bisexual flower buds before dehiscence; omitted if the female parent is unisexual) and bagging (covering with a butter-paper bag to prevent unwanted pollen contamination), followed by controlled pollination and rebagging.

5. Double Fertilisation & Post-Fertilisation Transformations

5.1 Syngamy & Triple Fusion

After entering a synergid, the pollen tube discharges two haploid male gametes (\(n\)) into the synergid cytoplasm, initiating double fertilisation—a hallmark feature unique to angiosperms:

\[\text{Syngamy: } \text{Male gamete }(n) + \text{Egg cell }(n) \longrightarrow \text{Zygote }(2n) \longrightarrow \text{Embryo }(2n)\] \[\text{Triple Fusion: } \text{Male gamete }(n) + \text{2 Polar nuclei }(n + n) \longrightarrow \text{Primary Endosperm Nucleus, PEN }(3n) \longrightarrow \text{Endosperm }(3n)\]

Following triple fusion, the central cell becomes the primary endosperm cell (PEC). Note that endosperm development precedes zygotic division, ensuring an assured food supply for the developing embryo.

5.3 Endosperm, Embryogeny, Seeds & Fruits

Endosperm: In the most common (free-nuclear) mode, the PEN divides repeatedly without immediate cytokinesis, forming thousands of free nuclei before cell walls appear. Tender coconut illustrates both stages simultaneously: coconut water is free-nuclear endosperm, while the surrounding white kernel is cellular endosperm. Seeds that completely consume endosperm during embryogeny are non-albuminous (ex-albuminous) (pea, groundnut, beans), whereas seeds retaining endosperm are albuminous (wheat, maize, barley, castor).

Embryogeny: Early stages are similar in dicots and monocots: zygote \(\to\) proembryo \(\to\) globular embryo \(\to\) heart-shaped embryo \(\to\) mature embryo.

  • Dicot embryo: Two cotyledons and an embryonal axis. The region above the cotyledons is the epicotyl (terminating in the plumule / shoot tip); the region below is the hypocotyl (terminating in the radicle / root tip covered by a root cap).
  • Monocot (grass) embryo: A single lateral cotyledon called the scutellum. At the lower end, the radicle and root cap are enclosed in an undifferentiated sheath, the coleorrhiza. Above the scutellum attachment, the epicotyl (shoot apex + leaf primordia) is enclosed in a hollow foliar sheath, the coleoptile.

Seed Dormancy, Viability & Fruit Types: As seeds mature, water content drops to 10–15% by mass and the embryo enters dormancy. Notable longevity records include Lupinus arcticus (germinated after ~10,000 years in Arctic Tundra) and Phoenix dactylifera (date palm, ~2,000 years old from King Herod's palace near the Dead Sea). Fruits developing solely from the ovary are true fruits (mango, guava, mustard), whereas those where the thalamus also contributes are false fruits (apple, strawberry, cashew). Fruits formed without fertilisation are parthenocarpic fruits (e.g., banana; can be induced by growth hormones and are seedless).

6. Apomixis & Polyembryony

Apomixis is a form of asexual reproduction that mimics sexual reproduction by producing seeds without fertilisation (seen in some Asteraceae and grasses). It can arise when a diploid egg cell forms without reduction division and develops directly into an embryo, or when diploid nucellar cells surrounding the embryo sac divide, protrude into the embryo sac, and form embryos.

When a single seed contains more than one embryo—commonly due to multiple nucellar embryos alongside the zygotic embryo, as in Citrus and Mango—the condition is called polyembryony. Nucellar apomictic embryos are genetically identical to the maternal plant (clones).

Agricultural Significance of Apomixis

Hybrid crop varieties offer superior yield, but farmers must purchase costly hybrid seeds every year because sowing seeds saved from hybrids causes genetic segregation in the progeny. Converting hybrids into apomicts eliminates segregation, allowing farmers to reuse saved hybrid seeds year after year.

7. Worked Examples (NEET UG Problem-Solving)

Worked Example 1: Meiotic & Mitotic Division Counts (Hypothetical Scenario)

Problem: Assume a breeder needs to produce 160 viable seeds in a hypothetical flowering plant that shows monosporic embryo sac development and sheds pollen at the 2-celled stage. How many meiotic divisions are minimally required to generate the necessary male and female gametes, and how many total mitotic divisions occur inside the functional megaspores to form those 160 embryo sacs?

Solution:

  1. Female side: 1 MMC (\(2n\)) undergoes 1 meiosis to produce 4 megaspores, of which only 1 is functional. Thus, 160 embryo sacs (160 eggs) require 160 meiotic divisions.
  2. Male side: 1 PMC (\(2n\)) undergoes 1 meiosis to produce 4 functional microspores (pollen grains). To obtain 160 pollen grains, \(\frac{160}{4} = 40\) meiotic divisions are required.
  3. Total meiotic divisions: \(160 + 40 = 200\) meiotic divisions.
  4. Mitotic divisions in functional megaspores: Each functional megaspore undergoes 3 sequential free-nuclear mitotic divisions (1 nucleus \(\to\) 2 \(\to\) 4 \(\to\) 8 nuclei; or 7 mitotic divisions if counting individual nuclear divisions: \(1 + 2 + 4 = 7\)). Across 160 functional megaspores, there are \(160 \times 3 = 480\) sequential mitotic rounds (or \(160 \times 7 = 1120\) individual nuclear mitoses).
Worked Example 2: Ploidy in an Interspecific Cross (Hypothetical Cross)

Problem: Suppose a tetraploid female angiosperm plant (\(4n\)) is crossed with a diploid male angiosperm plant (\(2n\)) in a controlled breeding experiment. Determine the ploidy of (a) the embryo, (b) the endosperm, and (c) the seed coat in the resulting seeds.

Solution:

  1. Gamete ploidies: The tetraploid female (\(4n\)) produces gametes with ploidy \(\frac{4n}{2} = 2n\) (both the egg cell and each of the two polar nuclei are \(2n\)). The diploid male (\(2n\)) produces male gametes with ploidy \(\frac{2n}{2} = n\).
  2. (a) Embryo: Formed by syngamy of egg (\(2n\)) + male gamete (\(n\)) \(\implies 2n + n = \mathbf{3n}\) (triploid).
  3. (b) Endosperm: Formed by triple fusion of 2 polar nuclei (\(2n + 2n\)) + male gamete (\(n\)) \(\implies 2n + 2n + n = \mathbf{5n}\) (pentaploid).
  4. (c) Seed coat: Develops from the maternal integuments without fertilisation, so it retains the maternal sporophytic ploidy of \(\mathbf{4n}\) (tetraploid).

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which layer of the microsporangium wall is characterised by cells having dense cytoplasm and commonly more than one nucleus, and plays a crucial role in sporopollenin precursor secretion?
  • (A) Epidermis
  • (B) Endothecium
  • (C) Middle layers
  • (D) Tapetum
Answer: (D) — The tapetum is the innermost wall layer of the microsporangium. Its cells have dense cytoplasm, are often multinucleate/polyploid, nourish developing pollen grains, and secrete precursors for the sporopollenin exine.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ): Pollen grains of wheat and rice lose their viability after release within approximately:
  • (A) 30 minutes
  • (B) 24 hours
  • (C) 1 month
  • (D) 6 months
Answer: (A) — In cereals such as rice and wheat, pollen grains remain viable for only about 30 minutes after shedding, whereas in Rosaceae, Leguminosae, and Solanaceae they can stay viable for months.
Practice Question 3 (Statement-Based): Read the following two statements carefully and choose the correct option.
Statement I: A typical mature angiosperm embryo sac is 7-celled and 8-nucleate.
Statement II: All eight nuclei in the mature embryo sac are enclosed in separate individual cells.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C) — Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because only six of the eight nuclei are enclosed in individual cells (three in the egg apparatus and three antipodals); the remaining two polar nuclei share the single large central cell.
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following aquatic plants uses water currents for pollination with its female flowers reaching the water surface on a long stalk?
  • (A) Water hyacinth
  • (B) Water lily
  • (C) Vallisneria
  • (D) Zostera
Answer: (C) — In Vallisneria, the female flower reaches the water surface via a long stalk and receives pollen released onto the surface. Water hyacinth and water lily are insect/wind-pollinated, while Zostera is a marine seagrass with submerged pollination.
Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason):
Assertion (A): Geitonogamy is functionally cross-pollination but genetically similar to autogamy.
Reason (R): In geitonogamy, pollen grains are transferred from the anther to the stigma of another flower on the same plant, involving a pollinating agent.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A) — Geitonogamy requires an external pollinating agent (making it functionally cross-pollination), yet because both flowers belong to the same individual plant, no new genetic variation is introduced.
Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ): Dioecy, as seen in papaya, prevents:
  • (A) Autogamy only
  • (B) Geitonogamy only
  • (C) Both autogamy and geitonogamy
  • (D) Xenogamy only
Answer: (C) — In dioecious plants, male and female flowers are borne on separate plants, eliminating both self-pollination within a flower (autogamy) and between flowers of the same plant (geitonogamy).
Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ): During artificial hybridisation, if the female parent bears unisexual flowers, which step is unnecessary?
  • (A) Bagging of female flower buds
  • (B) Emasculation
  • (C) Dusting desired pollen on receptive stigma
  • (D) Rebagging after pollination
Answer: (B) — Emasculation (removal of anthers) is required only when the female parent bears bisexual flowers. Unisexual female flowers lack anthers, so only bagging, controlled pollination, and rebagging are performed.

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ): In tender coconut, the liquid coconut water and the surrounding white edible kernel represent, respectively:

Answer: (B) — Coconut water is free-nuclear endosperm (containing thousands of free nuclei), while the surrounding white kernel is cellular endosperm formed after cell wall deposition.
Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ): Nucellar polyembryony is characteristically observed in:

Answer: (C) — In many varieties of Citrus and Mango, diploid nucellar cells surrounding the embryo sac divide and protrude into the embryo sac, forming multiple clonal embryos (polyembryony).

9. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

Common NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Confusing monoecy with dioecy: Monoecious plants (castor, maize) prevent autogamy but still allow geitonogamy; only dioecious plants (papaya) prevent both.
  • Misidentifying aquatic pollinators: Not all aquatic plants are hydrophilous—water hyacinth and water lily are pollinated by insects or wind above water.
  • Mixing up coleoptile and coleorrhiza: Coleoptile = foliar sheath around shoot apex/plumule; Coleorrhiza = undifferentiated sheath around radicle/root cap.
  • Confusing perisperm and pericarp: Perisperm = persistent nucellus inside the seed (\(2n\)); Pericarp = fruit wall derived from the ovary wall (\(2n\)).
  • Confusing apomixis with parthenocarpy: Apomixis produces seeds without fertilisation (e.g., grasses, Asteraceae); parthenocarpy produces seedless fruits without fertilisation (e.g., banana).

High-Yield Revision Checklist

  • Typical anther: bilobed, dithecous, tetrasporangiate; 4 wall layers (epidermis, endothecium, middle layers, tapetum).
  • Pollen exine: sporopollenin (absent at germ pores); intine: cellulose + pectin; shed at 2-celled stage in >60% angiosperms.
  • Mature embryo sac: monosporic, 7-celled, 8-nucleate (egg apparatus with filiform apparatus + 3 antipodals + central cell with 2 polar nuclei).
  • Double fertilisation: syngamy (\(n + n \to 2n\) zygote) + triple fusion (\(n + 2n \to 3n\) PEN).
  • Oldest viable seed records: Lupinus arcticus (~10,000 years) and Phoenix dactylifera (~2,000 years).
Concise Final Recap

Angiosperm sexual reproduction integrates protected gametophyte development within floral organs, diverse pollination strategies with strict pollen–pistil recognition, and double fertilisation to coordinate diploid embryo formation with triploid endosperm nourishment. Together with seed dormancy, fruit dispersal adaptations, and specialised variants like apomixis and polyembryony, these mechanisms account for the ecological dominance of flowering plants.
