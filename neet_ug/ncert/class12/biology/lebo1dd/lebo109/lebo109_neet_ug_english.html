2. Foundations and Core Principles

2.1 Definition of Biotechnology

Biotechnology deals with techniques of using live organisms or enzymes from organisms to produce products and processes useful to humans. Traditional processes like making curd, bread, or wine are microbially mediated, but the term is applied today in a restricted sense: processes that utilize genetically modified organisms (GMOs) to achieve large-scale production. Modern biotechnology encompasses techniques such as in vitro fertilisation (test-tube baby programme), chemical gene synthesis, DNA vaccine synthesis, and gene therapy.

EFB Definition of Biotechnology

The European Federation of Biotechnology (EFB) formulated an integrated definition encompassing both traditional views and modern molecular biotechnology:
"The integration of natural science and organisms, cells, parts thereof, and molecular analogues for products and services."

2.2 The Two Pillars of Modern Biotechnology

  1. Genetic Engineering: Techniques to alter the chemistry of genetic material (DNA and RNA), introduce this modified material into host organisms, and thereby change the phenotype of the recipient host.
  2. Bioprocess Engineering: Maintenance of a sterile (contamination-free) ambience in chemical engineering processes to allow the selective growth of only the desired microbe or eukaryotic cell in large quantities for the manufacture of antibiotics, vaccines, enzymes, and therapeutics.

2.3 Sexual vs. Asexual Reproduction & The Power of Genetic Engineering

Asexual reproduction preserves genetic information strictly without change, whereas sexual reproduction permits variations and novel genetic combinations. Traditional hybridisation programmes in plant and animal breeding invariably result in the co-inheritance and multiplication of undesirable genes alongside desirable ones.

Recombinant DNA technology bypasses this bottleneck via three fundamental steps:

  1. Identification of DNA with desirable genes.
  2. Introduction of the identified foreign DNA into a suitable host.
  3. Maintenance of introduced DNA in the host and faithful transfer to its progeny.

2.4 Conceptual Breakthrough: The Cohen & Boyer Experiment (1972)

An alien piece of DNA introduced into an organism cannot replicate autonomously unless it becomes an integrated part of a chromosome having a specific DNA sequence called the origin of replication (ori). The ori initiates replication and determines the copy number of the propagating DNA.

In 1972, Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer synthesized the first artificial recombinant DNA (rDNA) molecule:

  • Boyer had observed that specific restriction enzymes isolated from Escherichia coli cleave DNA leaving single-stranded overhanging ends ("sticky ends").
  • Cohen had pioneered techniques to isolate autonomous, circular extrachromosomal DNA rings called plasmids and reintroduce them into bacterial cells.
  • They isolated an antibiotic resistance gene by cutting a native plasmid of Salmonella typhimurium using restriction endonucleases.
  • This gene was spliced into a plasmid vector using DNA ligase, creating an autonomous circular rDNA molecule in vitro.
  • The recombinant plasmid was transferred into Escherichia coli, where it replicated using host DNA polymerase, cloning the antibiotic resistance gene.

3. Tools of Recombinant DNA Technology

3.1 Restriction Endonucleases ("Molecular Scissors")

In 1963, scientists isolated two enzymes from E. coli that restricted bacteriophage propagation:

  • One added methyl groups (\(-\mathrm{CH_3}\)) to bacterial DNA to protect host DNA from self-cleavage.
  • The other cleaved foreign viral DNA and was termed restriction endonuclease.

The first sequence-specific restriction endonuclease characterized was Hind II (isolated five years later from Haemophilus influenzae Rd). It consistently cleaves DNA double helices by recognizing a specific sequence of 6 base pairs. Today, over 900 restriction enzymes have been isolated from more than 230 bacterial strains, each recognizing distinct sequences.

Nomenclature Rules:

  • First letter: Genus of the prokaryote (capitalized; e.g., E for Escherichia).
  • Next two letters: Species of the prokaryote (lowercase; e.g., co for coli).
  • Fourth letter: Strain designation (e.g., R for strain RY 13).
  • Roman numeral: Order in which the enzyme was isolated from that particular strain (e.g., I for first isolated).

Exonucleases vs. Endonucleases:

Feature Exonucleases Restriction Endonucleases
Site of Cleavage Remove nucleotides sequentially from terminal ends (5' or 3') of DNA. Make internal cuts at precise, specific phosphodiester positions within the DNA.
Sequence Specificity Generally non-specific; degrades terminal ends. Strictly sequence-specific; recognizes distinct palindromic motifs.
Role in Genetic Engineering Limited; used for trimming DNA fragments. Central tool; creates defined fragments and cohesive sticky ends.

Palindromes and Staggered Cleavage

A DNA palindrome is a sequence of base pairs that reads identical on both anti-parallel strands when reading in the same orientation (either \(5' \rightarrow 3'\) or \(3' \rightarrow 5'\)).

For example, the recognition sequence for EcoRI:

\[ \begin{aligned} 5'\text{---G}\;{\downarrow}\;\text{A---A---T---T---C---}3' \\ 3'\text{---C---T---T---A---A}\;{\uparrow}\;\text{G---}5' \end{aligned} \]

EcoRI binds the hexanucleotide palindrome and cuts both strands between the same two bases (G and A) slightly away from the center of symmetry. This staggered cut leaves short, single-stranded unpaired extensions at each terminus called sticky ends. These overhanging stretches readily form hydrogen bonds with complementary counterparts cut by the identical restriction enzyme. The stickiness greatly accelerates the final covalent sealing of the sugar-phosphate backbone by DNA ligase.

3.2 Separation and Isolation of DNA Fragments: Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

Restriction digestion produces a heterogeneous mixture of DNA fragments. These fragments are separated using agarose gel electrophoresis based on the following physical principles:

  • Charge Property: DNA fragments possess a constant negative charge across their length due to negatively charged phosphate groups (\(\mathrm{PO_4^{3-}}\)). Hence, under an applied electrical potential gradient, DNA fragments migrate toward the anode (positive electrode) away from the cathode (wells).
  • Matrix: Agarose is a natural polysaccharide polymer extracted from seaweeds. It polymerizes into a cross-linked network that provides a sieving effect.
  • Resolution by Size: Smaller DNA fragments navigate through the matrix pores with less friction and migrate faster and farther from the loading wells, whereas larger fragments migrate shorter distances. Migration distance is inversely proportional to the log of fragment molecular weight.
  • Visualisation: Pure DNA fragments cannot be seen under visible white light. The gel must be stained with ethidium bromide (EtBr) and exposed to ultraviolet (UV) radiation, revealing vibrant bright orange-colored bands.
  • Elution: The isolated band containing the desired gene is physically cut out from the agarose gel slab, and the DNA is extracted from the gel slice. This crucial purification step is termed elution.
Figure 2: Agarose gel electrophoresis showing resolution of negatively charged DNA fragments from cathode to anode according to molecular size through sieving action.

3.3 Cloning Vectors

Vectors are DNA vehicles used to deliver foreign target DNA into a host cell for autonomous replication. Commonly utilized vectors include bacterial plasmids (circular, extrachromosomal dsDNA; typically 1–100 copies per cell) and bacteriophages (viruses infecting bacteria; high copy number per cell).

Essential Characteristics of an Engineered Cloning Vector:

  1. Origin of Replication (ori): A specific nucleotide sequence where replication commences. Any alien DNA linked to ori replicates in synchrony with the vector. It also strictly controls the copy number of the cloned DNA.
  2. Selectable Marker: A gene that permits the identification and selection of transformants while selectively eliminating non-transformants. Transformation is the procedure by which exogenous DNA is introduced into a recipient bacterium. Genes conferring resistance to antibiotics like ampicillin (\(\mathrm{amp^R}\)), tetracycline (\(\mathrm{tet^R}\)), chloramphenicol, or kanamycin serve as standard selectable markers for E. coli, as normal wild-type E. coli strains are sensitive to all these antibiotics.
  3. Cloning (Restriction) Sites: To ligate alien DNA, the vector requires very few, preferably a single (unique) recognition site for commonly used restriction enzymes. If more than one recognition site exists for a given enzyme within the vector, digestion will cut the vector into multiple fragments, severely complicating gene cloning.
  4. Small Size / Low Molecular Weight: Facilitates easy isolation, manipulation, and high-efficiency transformation into host cells.

3.4 Anatomy of Plasmid pBR322 and Insertional Inactivation

The artificial cloning vector pBR322 constructed in E. coli exhibits a precisely engineered distribution of restriction sites, marker genes, and regulatory regions:

  • ampR Gene (Ampicillin resistance): Possesses unique recognition sites for Pst I and Pvu I.
  • tetR Gene (Tetracycline resistance): Contains unique recognition sites for BamH I and Sal I.
  • Additional Unique Restriction Sites: EcoR I, Cla I, and Hind III (located close together outside the antibiotic genes).
  • ori: Origin of replication sequence controlling replication initiation and copy number.
  • rop: Codes for proteins involved in the replication of the plasmid; contains a unique cleavage site for Pvu II.
Figure 3: Restriction map of plasmid vector pBR322 indicating selectable markers (ampR and tetR), rop, ori, and unique restriction cleavage sites.

Selection via Dual Antibiotic Plating (Replica Plating):

When a foreign gene is ligated into the BamH I site of pBR322, it disrupts the coding frame of the tetracycline resistance gene. This loss of phenotypic function caused by foreign DNA insertion is called insertional inactivation.

  • Non-transformants: Cells that fail to take up any plasmid remain sensitive to both ampicillin and tetracycline and die on ampicillin-containing medium.
  • Transformants: Cells that absorb a plasmid grow on ampicillin plates.
  • Recombinants vs. Non-recombinants: Transformant colonies from the ampicillin master plate are replicated onto tetracycline agar plates:
    • Recombinants: Plasmids carry the insert in \(tet^R\). Hence, they grow on ampicillin medium but fail to grow on tetracycline medium.
    • Non-recombinants (self-ligated vector): Carry intact \(tet^R\). They grow on both ampicillin and tetracycline media.

3.5 Alternative Selectable Marker: Chromogenic Selection (Blue-White Screening)

Selection by dual-antibiotic replica plating is labor-intensive and slow because it mandates simultaneous plating across two sets of agar plates. To overcome this, modern vectors utilize chromogenic selection based on the insertional inactivation of the enzyme \(\beta\)-galactosidase:

  • The cloning site resides within the coding sequence of the \(\beta\)-galactosidase gene (e.g., lacZ).
  • Non-recombinant Plasmids: The uninterrupted \(\beta\)-galactosidase gene produces functional active enzyme. In the presence of a chromogenic substrate (e.g., X-gal), the colonies turn blue.
  • Recombinant Plasmids: Insertion of the foreign DNA fragment causes insertional inactivation of the \(\beta\)-galactosidase gene. No active enzyme is produced, and the recombinant colonies remain white / colorless.

3.6 Vectors for Cloning in Plants and Animals

  1. Plant Vectors (Agrobacterium tumefaciens): A soil bacterium pathogenic to many dicotyledonous plants. It naturally transfers a defined DNA segment called T-DNA (transfer DNA) from its large Tumor-inducing (Ti) plasmid into host plant chromosomes, transforming normal plant cells into crown gall tumors that synthesize specialized chemicals required by the pathogen. In biotechnology, the Ti plasmid has been disarmed by deleting oncogenic tumor-forming genes while retaining its natural transfer machinery, making it an efficient vehicle for delivering genes of interest into crops.
  2. Animal Vectors (Retroviruses): Retroviruses naturally transform normal animal cells into cancerous cells. By disarming retroviruses (stripping their oncogenes and pathogenicity), they are converted into vectors that stably transduce desired therapeutic genes into mammalian host genomes.

3.7 Competent Host and Methods of Gene Transfer

Because DNA is a negatively charged, highly hydrophilic molecule, it cannot passively traverse the hydrophobic core of phospholipid cell membranes. Specific physical and chemical treatments make host cells competent to take up exogenous DNA:

Gene Transfer Method Principle & Mechanism Target Host System
Divalent Cation + Heat Shock Cells are treated with a divalent cation (e.g., \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\)), which neutralizes repulsive negative charges and increases cell wall pore permeability. The cells and rDNA are incubated on ice \((0^\circ\mathrm{C})\), exposed briefly to \(42^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (heat shock), and immediately returned to ice. Bacterial hosts (e.g., E. coli).
Micro-injection Recombinant DNA is directly injected into the nucleus of a target cell using an ultra-fine glass micropipette under a high-resolution inverted microscope. Animal cells (oocytes/zygotes).
Biolistics / Gene Gun Microscopic heavy-metal particles of gold or tungsten are coated with recombinant DNA and accelerated at high velocities into target cells using helium pressure. Plant cells (penetrates thick plant cell walls).
Disarmed Pathogen Vectors Genetically engineered, non-pathogenic bacteria or viruses infect host cells naturally and transfer rDNA into the host genome. Plants (Agrobacterium) and animals (retroviruses).

4. Processes of Recombinant DNA Technology

Constructing and expressing recombinant DNA follows an orderly sequence of distinct molecular and biochemical phases:

4.1 Step 1: Isolation of Genetic Material (DNA)

Nucleic acids are enclosed within cellular membranes associated with proteins, lipids, and polysaccharides. Purified DNA free of macromolecules is isolated through sequential steps:

  1. Enzymatic Cell Lysis: Digesting cell envelopes using specific enzymes:
    • Bacterial cells: Lysozyme (hydrolyzes peptidoglycan cell walls).
    • Plant cells: Cellulase and pectinase (degrades cellulose).
    • Fungal cells: Chitinase (digests chitinous walls).
  2. Macromolecular Removal: Cellular RNA is degraded using ribonucleases (RNase), while proteins (including chromatin histones) are digested using proteases.
  3. Precipitation with Chilled Ethanol: DNA is insoluble in cold alcohol. Adding chilled ethanol forces pure DNA to precipitate out of aqueous solution, appearing as a suspension of fine, white, thread-like fibers.
  4. Spooling: The precipitated filamentous DNA threads are collected onto a glass rod in a physical extraction process called spooling.

4.2 Step 2: Cutting of DNA at Specific Locations

Purified target genomic DNA and chosen vector DNA are separately incubated with the selected restriction endonuclease under optimal temperature, salt, and pH conditions. Digestion progress is monitored by running reaction aliquots on an agarose gel. The excised target gene and opened vector are then mixed together with DNA ligase to produce circular recombinant DNA.

4.3 Step 3: Amplification of Gene of Interest by PCR

The Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR), devised by Kary Mullis, is an in vitro enzymatic amplification technique used to synthesize billions of copies of a target DNA sequence from trace templates.

Essential Reaction Components:

  • DNA Template: Double-stranded DNA segment containing the region to be amplified.
  • Primers: Two sets of chemically synthesized, short oligonucleotide sequences (~18–25 nucleotides) complementary to opposite ends of the target region.
  • Deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs): dATP, dCTP, dGTP, dTTP as building blocks.
  • Thermostable DNA Polymerase: Taq polymerase, isolated from the thermophilic bacterium Thermus aquaticus. It withstands high temperatures without denaturing.
  • Divalent Cations: \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\) acting as an essential cofactor for polymerase activity.

The Three Cyclic Steps of PCR:

  1. Denaturation (\(\approx 94^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(96^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): Thermal heating breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the antiparallel strands together, separating double-stranded DNA into single strands.
  2. Primer Annealing (\(\approx 50^\circ\mathrm{C}\) to \(60^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): The temperature is reduced to permit the two oligonucleotide primers to hybridize to their complementary sequences at the 3' ends of the single-stranded DNA templates.
  3. Extension (\(\approx 72^\circ\mathrm{C}\)): At the optimal temperature for Taq polymerase, the enzyme extends the primers by synthesizing new complementary strands in the \(5' \rightarrow 3'\) direction using the free dNTPs in the reaction mixture.
Amplification Formula & 30-Cycle Yield

For \(n\) cycles of PCR, theoretical amplification follows an exponential factor of \(2^n\).
After 30 cycles of PCR: \[ \text{Amplified copies} = 2^{30} = 1{,}073{,}741{,}824 \approx 10^9 \text{ copies (1 billion-fold amplification)} \]

Figure 4: Schematic of one PCR amplification cycle showing thermal denaturation, primer hybridization, and Taq polymerase extension.

4.4 Step 4: Insertion of Recombinant DNA into Host

The ligated plasmid is transferred into chemically competent recipient cells (e.g., E. coli). When the plasmid carries a selectable marker such as \(\mathrm{amp^R}\), spreading the cell suspension on ampicillin-containing agar isolates transformants: only cells that acquired the resistance gene survive, while untransformed host cells are eliminated.

4.5 Step 5: Large-Scale Production and Bioreactors

When a protein-encoding gene is expressed in a heterologous host, the resulting product is known as a recombinant protein. Small-scale laboratory shake flasks cannot yield commercially viable quantities. Industrial mass manufacturing requires large closed vessels called bioreactors (processing working volumes of 100 to 1,000 liters).

Continuous Culture System:

In a continuous culture system, depleted used medium is drained from one side while fresh nutrient medium is continuously supplied from the other. This dynamic steady-state maintains growing cells in their physiologically most active logarithmic (exponential) phase, yielding a significantly higher biomass and maximum recombinant protein output compared to stationary batch cultures.

Bioreactor Designs:

A bioreactor provides optimum biological growth conditions by precisely regulating temperature, pH, substrate levels, oxygen, vitamins, and salts.

  • Simple Stirred-Tank Bioreactor: Typically features a cylindrical vessel with a curved base to eliminate dead zones and ensure thorough mixing. It is equipped with:
    • An agitator system with flat-bladed impellers for continuous mixing.
    • An oxygen delivery system with a sparger for air aeration.
    • A foam-breaker system to control surface froth.
    • Temperature and pH control sensors with acid/base addition ports.
    • Steam injection ports for in situ thermal sterilization.
    • Sampling ports to periodically withdraw small aliquots for testing.
  • Sparged Stirred-Tank Bioreactor: Sterile air bubbles are actively sparged (forced under pressure) through the liquid broth. These millions of tiny bubbles dramatically increase the surface area available for oxygen mass transfer into the culture medium.
Figure 5: Structural comparison between a simple stirred-tank bioreactor and a sparged bioreactor showing gas entrainment and mass aeration mechanisms.

4.6 Step 6: Downstream Processing

Once the biosynthetic phase inside the bioreactor concludes, the target product must undergo a series of post-fermentation recovery steps collectively termed downstream processing:

  • Separation: Filtering cell debris, harvesting cells, or precipitating crude broth proteins.
  • Purification: Fractional purification through chromatography (ion-exchange, affinity, gel-filtration).
  • Formulation: Stabilizing the product with suitable non-toxic preservatives.
  • Clinical Trials: Compulsory for therapeutic biopharmaceuticals to establish human safety, efficacy, and dosage pharmacokinetics.
  • Quality Control (QC) Testing: Strict batch-to-batch analytical assays to evaluate product purity, specific activity, and stability.
NEET Trap Alert

Downstream processing encompasses only separation, purification, formulation, and quality control. It does NOT include upstream operations such as gene cloning, transformation, expression induction, or biomass growth inside the bioreactor.

5. NEET UG Conceptual Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 Calculations & PCR Kinetics

A molecular biologist begins a polymerase chain reaction with 6 double-stranded DNA template molecules. How many double-stranded DNA molecules will be present in the reaction tube following the completion of 6 cycles of PCR?

Step-by-Step Solution

Each cycle of PCR duplicates the number of template molecules present at the start of that cycle. The general equation for DNA amplification after \(n\) cycles is:

\[ N = N_0 \times 2^n \]

Where:
\(N_0 = 6\) (initial double-stranded DNA templates)
\(n = 6\) (number of cycles completed)

\[ N = 6 \times 2^6 = 6 \times 64 = 384 \text{ dsDNA molecules} \]

Answer: 384 double-stranded DNA molecules are generated.

Worked Example 2 Enzyme Digestion & Gel Band Count

A linear double-stranded DNA molecule contains 3 recognition sites for the restriction endonuclease EcoRI. A circular plasmid DNA molecule also contains 3 recognition sites for EcoRI. If both molecules are completely digested with EcoRI, how many fragments will each produce?

Step-by-Step Solution

1. Linear DNA Molecule:
A linear molecule with \(k\) recognition sites cut completely by an endonuclease yields \((k + 1)\) fragments.
Number of fragments \(= 3 + 1 = 4\).

2. Circular Plasmid DNA Molecule:
A closed circular molecule with \(k\) recognition sites cut completely yields exactly \(k\) fragments because each cleavage opens a segment of the continuous ring.
Number of fragments \(= 3\).

Answer: The linear DNA produces 4 fragments; the circular plasmid produces 3 fragments.

Question 4 Assertion - Reason

Assertion (A): In agarose gel electrophoresis, smaller DNA fragments migrate farther toward the anode than larger fragments.
Reason (R): The agarose matrix provides a sieving effect where movement is inversely proportional to fragment size, while all DNA fragments carry a uniform negative charge.

(1) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
(2) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
(3) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
(4) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (1)

Explanation: DNA fragments have a uniform net negative charge across their length due to the phosphate backbone; under an electric field, they are driven toward the positive electrode (anode). The agarose gel acts as a molecular sieve, allowing smaller fragments to travel through its pores with greater ease and distance than larger ones.

7. Common Pitfalls and High-Yield Revision Checklist

NEET Trap Warnings & High-Frequency Distinctions
  • First Restriction Endonuclease Discovered vs. Characterized: The enzymes restricting bacteriophage growth were discovered in 1963 in E. coli. However, the first sequence-specific endonuclease characterized was Hind II (six base-pair specificity), isolated five years later.
  • Agarose Gel Loading & Direction: DNA is loaded into wells positioned near the negative cathode and migrates toward the positive anode. Smaller fragments migrate farther; the largest fragments remain closest to the wells.
  • pBR322 Restriction Sites: Memorize the locations!
    • \(amp^R\) contains: Pst I and Pvu I.
    • \(tet^R\) contains: BamH I and Sal I.
    • \(rop\) contains: Pvu II.
    • Outside markers: EcoR I, Cla I, Hind III.
  • Downstream Processing Limits: Downstream processing consists strictly of separation, purification, preservative formulation, and quality control. Gene cloning, transformation, and biomass fermentation belong to upstream processing.

Mastery Checklist

✓ EFB Definition: Integration of natural science and organisms, cells, parts thereof, and molecular analogues.
✓ 1972 Cohen & Boyer: First recombinant DNA constructed using Salmonella typhimurium plasmid and expressed in E. coli.
✓ EcoRI Palindrome: \(5'\text{---GAATTC---}3'\) cut between G and A, leaving \(5'\) overhanging sticky ends.
✓ Gel Visualization: Ethidium bromide + UV radiation yields bright orange bands; bands are recovered via elution.
✓ pBR322: Insertional inactivation at BamHI produces resistance to ampicillin but sensitivity to tetracycline.
✓ PCR Steps: Denaturation (\(94^\circ\mathrm{C}\)) \(\rightarrow\) Annealing (\(50\)–\(60^\circ\mathrm{C}\)) \(\rightarrow\) Extension (\(72^\circ\mathrm{C}\)) with Taq polymerase (\(2^{30} \approx 10^9\) copies).
✓ Gene Delivery: Agrobacterium tumefaciens (Ti plasmid / T-DNA) for plants, disarmed retroviruses for animals, gene gun (gold/tungsten) for plant bombardment.
✓ Cell Wall Lysis: Lysozyme (bacteria), Cellulase (plants), Chitinase (fungi); precipitation achieved with chilled ethanol.

8. Concise Chapter Summary

Modern biotechnology rests upon two foundational disciplines: genetic engineering (altering nucleic acid sequences to generate novel host phenotypes) and bioprocess engineering (maintaining sterile conditions for industrial cultivation).

Constructing recombinant DNA requires key molecular tools: restriction endonucleases that cleave at specific palindromic sequences to yield sticky ends, DNA ligase to covalently seal phosphodiester bonds, vectors (such as pBR322, Ti plasmids, or retroviruses) equipped with an ori, selectable markers, and unique cloning sites, and a competent host prepared by divalent cation treatment, heat shock, micro-injection, or biolistics.

The complete recombinant workflow begins with cell lysis (using lysozyme, cellulase, or chitinase) and chilled ethanol spooling, followed by sequence-specific restriction digestion, gel electrophoretic resolution and elution, enzymatic amplification of the target gene via PCR, ligation into a cloning vector, host transformation, high-density cultivation in stirred-tank bioreactors, and final recovery via downstream processing.
