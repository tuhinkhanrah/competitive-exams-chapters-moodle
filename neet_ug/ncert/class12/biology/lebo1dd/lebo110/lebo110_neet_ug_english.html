NEET UG Biology • Applied Biotechnology

Biotechnology and Its Applications

Exam-Oriented Conceptual Tutorial, Mechanisms, Worked Examples & Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Modern biotechnology harnesses living systems—genetically modified microbes, fungi, plants, and animals—to manufacture biopharmaceuticals, diagnostics, resilient crops, and industrial biologicals at commercial scale. Its applications span therapeutics, early disease detection, sustainable agriculture, processed foods, bioremediation, waste management, and bio-energy.

Core Learning Objectives for NEET UG
  • Identify the three foundational research pillars of industrial biotechnology.
  • Explain plant tissue culture techniques—totipotency, micro-propagation, meristem culture, and somatic hybridisation—and their agricultural roles.
  • Analyse the molecular mechanisms of pest-resistant genetically modified (GM) crops, specifically Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) toxins and RNA interference (RNAi).
  • Describe medical applications: recombinant human insulin assembly, ex vivo gene therapy for ADA deficiency, and molecular diagnostics (PCR, radioactive probes, ELISA).
  • Evaluate the five major purposes of transgenic animals and the ethical/regulatory frameworks (GEAC, biopiracy, and patent laws) governing bio-resources.

Three Critical Research Pillars of Biotechnology

  1. Optimising the Catalyst: Developing a superior biological catalyst, typically an engineered microorganism or a purified enzyme.
  2. Engineering Optimal Conditions: Designing bioreactor environments (temperature, pH, aeration, nutrients) that allow the catalyst to perform at peak efficiency.
  3. Downstream Processing: Purifying, separating, and formulating the desired protein or organic compound for safe clinical or commercial use.

2. Biotechnological Applications in Agriculture

Meeting global food demand relies on three broad agricultural strategies: (i) agro-chemical-based farming, (ii) organic agriculture, and (iii) genetically engineered crop-based agriculture. Although the Green Revolution tripled food output through improved crop varieties, better management, and agrochemicals (fertilisers and pesticides), chemical inputs remain costly for farmers in developing nations, and conventional breeding has reached yield plateaus.

2.1 Plant Tissue Culture & Cellular Totipotency

Developed in the 1950s, plant tissue culture exploits totipotency—the innate capacity of any living plant cell or tissue fragment (called an explant) to regenerate into a complete plant when cultured under sterile conditions in a specialised nutrient medium.

  • Essential Nutrient Medium Components: A carbon source (typically sucrose), inorganic salts, vitamins, amino acids, and plant growth regulators (such as auxins and cytokinins).
  • Micro-propagation & Somaclones: Producing thousands of plants rapidly in vitro. Because all regenerated plants arise from somatic tissue of a single parent without sexual recombination, they are genetically identical to the source plant and are termed somaclones. Commercial examples include tomato, banana, and apple.
  • Meristem Culture for Virus-Free Plants: Even when a plant is systemically infected with a virus, its apical and axillary meristems remain virus-free due to rapid cell division and absence of differentiated vascular connections. Culturing these meristems recovers healthy plants in crops such as banana, sugarcane, and potato.
  • Somatic Hybridisation: Cell walls of single plant cells from two distinct varieties are enzymatically digested to isolate naked protoplasts (bounded only by plasma membranes). Fusing protoplasts carrying complementary desirable traits yields a hybrid protoplast that grows into a somatic hybrid. For example, fusing tomato and potato protoplasts produced pomato, though it lacked the full combination of traits needed for commercial cultivation.

2.2 Genetically Modified (GM) Crops

Plants, bacteria, fungi, and animals whose genomes have been deliberately altered by genetic manipulation are termed Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs). In agriculture, GM crops provide five major advantages:

  1. Increased tolerance to abiotic stresses (cold, drought, salinity, and heat).
  2. Reduced dependence on chemical pesticides via built-in pest resistance.
  3. Decreased post-harvest losses.
  4. Enhanced efficiency of mineral uptake by roots, preventing premature soil fertility exhaustion.
  5. Improved nutritional quality—for instance, Golden Rice, engineered to be enriched with Vitamin A (beta-carotene precursor).

Beyond food security, GM plants also serve as bio-factories supplying industrial starches, biofuels, and pharmaceuticals.

2.3 Insect-Resistant Crops: The Bt Toxin Mechanism

Certain strains of the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) synthesise crystalline insecticidal proteins during a specific growth phase. These toxins target specific insect orders: Lepidopterans (tobacco budworm, armyworm), Coleopterans (beetles), and Dipterans (flies, mosquitoes).

Why the bacterium is unharmed: Inside B. thuringiensis, the toxin is stored as an inactive protoxin. When a susceptible insect ingests the plant tissue expressing the cloned Bt gene, the alkaline pH of the insect's midgut solubilises the protein crystals and converts the protoxin into its active toxic form. The activated toxin binds to specific receptors on the surface of midgut epithelial cells, creating membrane pores that cause osmotic swelling, cell lysis, and death of the insect.

2.4 Nematode-Resistant Crops via RNA Interference (RNAi)

The root-knot nematode Meloidogyne incognita parasitises tobacco roots, severely reducing crop yield. To counter this without chemical nematicides, scientists engineered resistance using RNA interference (RNAi), a natural cellular defence mechanism present in all eukaryotic organisms.

  • Mechanism of RNAi: A specific messenger RNA (mRNA) is silenced when a complementary double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) molecule binds to it and blocks its translation. In nature, the complementary RNA originates from RNA virus infections or mobile genetic elements (transposons) that replicate via an RNA intermediate.
  • Transgenic Implementation: Using Agrobacterium tumefaciens vectors, nematode-specific genes were inserted into tobacco plants such that the host transcribes both sense and antisense RNA strands. Because these two strands are complementary, they hybridise to form dsRNA, triggering RNAi. When the nematode feeds on the transgenic roots, its essential target mRNA is silenced, preventing parasite survival.

3. Biotechnological Applications in Medicine

Recombinant DNA (rDNA) technology has transformed healthcare by enabling mass production of safe, identical human therapeutic proteins. Unlike drugs extracted from animal tissues, recombinant therapeutics do not trigger unwanted immunological or allergic reactions and eliminate the risk of transmitting animal-borne pathogens. Globally, approximately 30 recombinant therapeutics are approved for human use, with 12 currently marketed in India.

3.1 Genetically Engineered Human Insulin

Adult-onset diabetes requires regular insulin administration. Historically, insulin was extracted from the pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs, which caused allergic reactions in some patients due to minor structural differences from human insulin. Note that insulin is a peptide hormone and cannot be administered orally because proteolytic enzymes in the stomach and intestine would digest it before absorption.

  • Structure of Mature Insulin: Composed of two short polypeptide chains—Chain A and Chain B—covalently linked by disulphide bridges.
  • Pro-hormone Processing: In mammals (including humans), insulin is initially synthesised as an inactive pro-insulin containing an extra polypeptide stretch called the C-peptide. During maturation, the C-peptide is enzymatically cleaved off, leaving mature A and B chains.
  • Recombinant Production (Eli Lilly, 1983): Because bacteria like Escherichia coli lack the eukaryotic machinery to excise the C-peptide, scientists prepared two separate DNA sequences coding for human A and B chains, inserted them into E. coli plasmids, produced the chains independently, extracted them, and joined them chemically via disulphide bonds to yield functional human insulin.

3.2 Gene Therapy: Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) Deficiency

Gene therapy encompasses techniques that correct a diagnosed hereditary gene defect by delivering a normal, functional gene into a patient's cells or embryo to compensate for the non-functional allele.

  • First Clinical Trial (1990): Administered to a 4-year-old girl suffering from adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency, caused by deletion of the gene encoding ADA—an enzyme vital for immune system function.
  • Conventional Options (Non-Curative): Bone marrow transplantation (effective in some children) and Enzyme Replacement Therapy (ERT, periodic injections of functional ADA) do not offer a complete permanent cure.
  • Ex Vivo Lymphocyte Gene Therapy: Patient blood lymphocytes are cultured outside the body, transduced with a functional ADA cDNA using a retroviral vector, and re-infused into the patient. Because differentiated lymphocytes have a finite lifespan (are not immortal), the patient requires periodic infusions.
  • Potential Permanent Cure: Introducing the functional ADA gene isolated from bone marrow cells into cells at early embryonic stages could provide a lifelong cure.

3.3 Molecular Diagnosis

Conventional serum and urine analyses only detect a pathogen once its concentration is high enough to produce clinical symptoms. Molecular diagnostics enable early detection and pathophysiological insight:

Diagnostic Technique Core Principle Key Clinical Applications
PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) Exponential enzymatic amplification of trace pathogen or mutant nucleic acids. Early HIV detection (pre-symptomatic), cancer gene mutation screening, genetic disorders.
Radioactive Probe & Autoradiography Single-stranded radioactive DNA/RNA probe hybridises with complementary DNA in cell clones. Mutated gene clones lack complementarity, fail to bind the probe, and do not appear on photographic film.
ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay) Specific antigen–antibody interaction coupled to an enzyme-linked colour signal. Detects pathogen antigens (proteins/glycoproteins) or host antibodies against the pathogen.

4. Transgenic Animals

Transgenic animals are organisms whose DNA has been manipulated to possess and express an extra (foreign) gene. While transgenic rats, rabbits, pigs, sheep, cows, and fish have been created, over 95% of all existing transgenic animals are mice.

Application Area Purpose & Mechanism NEET UG High-Yield Examples
1. Normal Physiology & Development Study how genes are regulated and how they govern normal body functions and growth. Investigating complex growth factors such as insulin-like growth factor by introducing foreign genes that alter its synthesis.
2. Study of Human Diseases Serve as living models to understand genetic contributions to disease and test new therapies. Transgenic models for cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and Alzheimer's disease.
3. Biological Products Produce expensive human therapeutic proteins in animal milk or tissues. Human protein α-1-antitrypsin (treats emphysema); efforts for PKU and cystic fibrosis; Rosie (1997), the first transgenic cow, produced human protein-enriched milk (2.4 g/L) containing human α-lactalbumin.
4. Vaccine Safety Testing Evaluate vaccine batch safety in transgenic mice before human administration. Testing polio vaccine safety in transgenic mice as a reliable alternative to monkeys.
5. Chemical Safety (Toxicity) Testing Engineer animals carrying genes that increase sensitivity to toxic compounds, yielding faster results. Rapid drug and chemical toxicity screening compared with non-transgenic controls.

5. Ethical Issues, Biosafety & Biopiracy

Unregulated genetic manipulation raises moral concerns and ecological risks, as introducing GMOs into ecosystems can produce unpredictable outcomes.

  • GEAC (Genetic Engineering Approval Committee): Established by the Indian Government to evaluate the validity of GM research and the biosafety of introducing GMOs for public services.
  • Biopiracy: The unauthorised use of bio-resources and traditional knowledge by multinational companies or organisations without proper authorisation from the concerned country/indigenous people and without compensatory payment.
  • The Biodiversity vs. Capital Divide: Industrialised nations are financially wealthy but poor in biodiversity and traditional knowledge, whereas developing and underdeveloped nations are rich in biodiversity and traditional knowledge that can accelerate commercialisation.
  • Basmati Rice & Traditional Medicine Patents: India hosts an estimated 200,000 rice varieties, including 27 documented varieties of aromatic Basmati. In 1997, a US company obtained a patent from the US Patent and Trademark Office on a "new" Basmati variety derived by crossing Indian Basmati with semi-dwarf varieties, claiming novelty and functional equivalence. Similar patent attempts targeted Indian traditional remedies such as turmeric and neem.
  • Legislative Safeguard: The Indian Parliament cleared the second amendment of the Indian Patents Bill to address biopiracy, incorporating provisions on patent terms, emergency measures, and R&D initiatives.

6. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Transgenic Milk Protein Yield Calculation

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): Suppose a transgenic cow engineered similarly to Rosie produces milk containing human α-lactalbumin at a concentration of \(2.4\,\mathrm{g/L}\). Assume this cow yields \(15\,\mathrm{L}\) of milk per day over a \(20\)-day collection window. Calculate the total mass of human α-lactalbumin harvested (in grams and kilograms).

Solution:
Daily milk volume \(= 15\,\mathrm{L/day}\).
Total milk volume over \(20\) days:

\[ V_{\text{total}} = 15\,\mathrm{L/day} \times 20\,\mathrm{days} = 300\,\mathrm{L} \]

Total mass of human α-lactalbumin:

\[ m = 300\,\mathrm{L} \times 2.4\,\mathrm{g/L} = 720\,\mathrm{g} = 0.72\,\mathrm{kg} \]

Conclusion: Under the assumed yield, \(720\,\mathrm{g}\) (\(0.72\,\mathrm{kg}\)) of human α-lactalbumin is obtained.

Worked Example 2: Early Molecular Diagnosis via PCR Amplification

Scenario (Hypothetical Assumption): A patient in the pre-symptomatic window of a viral infection has only \(N_0 = 5\) copies of viral DNA in a blood sample. If a diagnostic PCR assay runs for \(n = 20\) cycles with \(100\%\) amplification efficiency per cycle, how many copies of viral DNA are generated? Why would ELISA or conventional serum analysis be less effective at this stage?

Solution:
Each PCR cycle doubles the target DNA copy number:

\[ N = N_0 \times 2^n = 5 \times 2^{20} = 5 \times 1,048,576 = 5,242,880\,\text{copies} \approx 5.24 \times 10^6\,\text{copies} \]

Diagnostic Insight: Conventional serum/urine tests require high pathogen loads and visible symptoms. PCR amplifies trace nucleic acids to millions of copies, enabling detection long before symptoms appear.

Worked Example 3: Selecting the Appropriate Pest-Control Strategy

Scenario: An agricultural institute wants to protect (a) maize crops against corn borer, (b) cotton crops against cotton bollworms, and (c) tobacco roots against Meloidogyne incognita. Match each crop with the exact molecular tool required.

Solution:

  • Maize vs. Corn borer: Introduce the cryIAb gene from Bacillus thuringiensis.
  • Cotton vs. Cotton bollworms: Introduce cryIAc and cryIIAb genes from B. thuringiensis.
  • Tobacco vs. Meloidogyne incognita: Use Agrobacterium-mediated RNA interference (RNAi) to express sense and antisense nematode-specific RNA, forming dsRNA that silences essential nematode mRNA.

7. NEET UG Practice Questions

The following original practice questions are modelled on NEET UG patterns—including single-correct MCQs, Assertion–Reason, Statement I/II, and Match-the-Following formats.

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ (Tissue Culture)
A farmer's banana plantation is heavily infected with a systemic viral disease, yet a biotechnology lab successfully recovers completely virus-free banana plantlets from the infected stock. Which tissue was used as the explant, and what is the primary reason it remained virus-free?
  • (A) Leaf mesophyll; because chloroplasts inhibit viral replication
  • (B) Apical or axillary meristem; because rapid cell division outpaces viral spread
  • (C) Root cortex; because soil microbes neutralise the virus
  • (D) Isolated protoplasts; because enzymatic cell wall digestion destroys viral particles
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Explanation: Even in a virus-infected plant, the apical and axillary meristems remain free of the virus because meristematic cells divide rapidly and lack fully differentiated vascular connections through which viruses normally translocate. Culturing meristems in vitro yields virus-free plants (e.g., banana, sugarcane, potato).

Practice Question 2 • Single-Correct MCQ (Bt Toxin Mechanism)
Bacillus thuringiensis synthesises insecticidal Cry protein crystals without dying from its own toxin because:
  • (A) The bacterial cell wall is impermeable to active Cry toxin
  • (B) The toxin is stored inside a specialised membrane-bound vacuole
  • (C) The toxin exists as an inactive protoxin that requires alkaline midgut pH for activation
  • (D) Bacteria possess specific antitoxin enzymes that continuously degrade the protein
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Explanation: Inside B. thuringiensis, the Cry protein exists as an inactive protoxin. Only after ingestion by a susceptible insect does the alkaline pH of the insect midgut solubilise the crystals and convert the protoxin into its active, pore-forming toxin.

Practice Question 3 • Match-the-Following (NEET UG Pattern)
Match Column I with Column II and select the correct option:
Column I Column II
(a) cryIAb (i) Treats emphysema
(b) Meloidogyne incognita (ii) Controls corn borer
(c) α-1-antitrypsin (iii) Silenced via host-generated dsRNA
(d) Golden Rice (iv) Enriched with Vitamin A
  • (A) (a)-(ii), (b)-(iii), (c)-(i), (d)-(iv)
  • (B) (a)-(iii), (b)-(ii), (c)-(i), (d)-(iv)
  • (C) (a)-(ii), (b)-(i), (c)-(iii), (d)-(iv)
  • (D) (a)-(iv), (b)-(iii), (c)-(i), (d)-(ii)
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Explanation: cryIAb specifically targets corn borer (whereas cryIAc and cryIIAb target cotton bollworms). Meloidogyne incognita infects tobacco roots and is controlled via RNAi (dsRNA). Human α-1-antitrypsin produced in transgenic animals treats emphysema, and Golden Rice is biofortified with Vitamin A.

Practice Question 4 • Assertion–Reason
Assertion (A): Recombinant human insulin produced by Eli Lilly in E. coli required separate synthesis of A and B chains followed by chemical disulphide bond formation.
Reason (R): E. coli lacks the post-translational machinery needed to excise the C-peptide from mammalian pro-insulin.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false
  • (D) Both (A) and (R) are false
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Explanation: Native mammalian insulin is synthesised as pro-insulin containing an extra C-peptide that must be cleaved to yield mature insulin. Because prokaryotes like E. coli cannot process pro-insulin to remove C-peptide, Eli Lilly synthesised the A and B chains separately in E. coli and joined them via disulphide bridges in vitro.

Practice Question 5 • Statement I & Statement II
Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Periodic infusion of genetically engineered lymphocytes provides a permanent, lifelong cure for ADA deficiency.
Statement II: Introducing the functional ADA gene isolated from bone marrow cells into early embryonic cells could serve as a permanent cure.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct
Correct Answer: (D)

Worked Explanation: Statement I is incorrect because lymphocytes are not immortal; they have a limited lifespan, so patients require periodic infusions of engineered lymphocytes. Statement II is correct because introducing the marrow-isolated ADA gene at early embryonic stages ensures all subsequent immune cells carry the functional gene permanently.

Practice Question 6 • Single-Correct MCQ (Molecular Diagnosis)
In a molecular screening test, a single-stranded radioactive DNA probe is allowed to hybridise with DNA from several cell clones, followed by autoradiography. A clone carrying a mutated target gene will:
  • (A) Glow more intensely on the photographic film than normal clones
  • (B) Not appear on the photographic film because the probe lacks complementarity
  • (C) Bind to the probe twice as fast due to structural instability
  • (D) Be detected only if an antibody-linked enzyme is added
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Explanation: A radioactive probe hybridises only with its complementary DNA sequence. Because a mutated gene lacks exact complementarity to the normal probe, the probe fails to bind, and consequently that clone does not appear on the autoradiography film.

Practice Question 7 • Multi-Statement Analysis
Consider the following statements regarding transgenic animals:
(i) Over 95% of all existing transgenic animals are mice.
(ii) Transgenic mice are being tested to replace monkeys for checking polio vaccine safety.
(iii) The transgenic cow Rosie produced milk enriched with human α-1-antitrypsin at 2.4 g/L.
(iv) Transgenic animals used for chemical toxicity testing are engineered to be more sensitive to toxic substances.
How many of the above statements are correct?
  • (A) One
  • (B) Two
  • (C) Three
  • (D) Four
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Explanation: Statements (i), (ii), and (iv) are correct. Statement (iii) is incorrect because Rosie (1997) produced milk enriched with human α-lactalbumin (2.4 g/L), not α-1-antitrypsin (which is used to treat emphysema). Thus, three statements are correct.

Practice Question 8 • Single-Correct MCQ (RNA Interference)
In eukaryotic organisms, RNA interference (RNAi) silences a target mRNA by blocking its translation. Which molecule directly initiates this silencing in transgenic tobacco roots resistant to Meloidogyne incognita?
  • (A) Single-stranded viral DNA
  • (B) Complementary double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)
  • (C) Bacterial restriction endonuclease
  • (D) Alkaline midgut protease
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Explanation: Agrobacterium-mediated transfer introduces nematode-specific DNA that transcribes both sense and antisense RNA in host plant cells. These complementary strands pair to form dsRNA, which initiates RNAi and silences the nematode's target mRNA.

Practice Question 9 • Single-Correct MCQ (Bioethics & Biopiracy)
Which organisation in India is responsible for evaluating the validity of genetically modified (GM) research and the safety of introducing GMOs for public services?
  • (A) Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR)
  • (B) Genetic Engineering Approval Committee (GEAC)
  • (C) Central Drug Research Institute (CDRI)
  • (D) National Biodiversity Authority (NBA)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Explanation: The Indian Government established the Genetic Engineering Approval Committee (GEAC) to make regulatory decisions regarding the validity of GM research and the biosafety of releasing GMOs for public use.

Practice Question 10 • Single-Correct MCQ (Somatic Hybridisation)
During somatic hybridisation between two distinct plant varieties, what is the immediate step required before fusing cells from the two parents?
  • (A) Treating the cells with retroviral vectors
  • (B) Digesting the cell walls to isolate naked protoplasts
  • (C) Exposing the explants to alkaline pH
  • (D) Amplifying genomic DNA using PCR
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Explanation: Plant cells are surrounded by rigid cell walls that prevent direct plasma membrane fusion. Therefore, cell walls are digested enzymatically to isolate naked protoplasts, which are then fused to form hybrid protoplasts that grow into somatic hybrids (such as pomato).

8. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Pro-insulin vs. Mature Insulin: Mature insulin contains only Chain A and Chain B linked by disulphide bridges; the C-peptide is absent in mature insulin.
  • Bt Gene Specificity: Do not confuse cryIAc and cryIIAb (cotton bollworms) with cryIAb (corn borer).
  • Midgut pH: Bt protoxin activation requires alkaline pH, not acidic pH.
  • Autoradiography Film Interpretation: Mutated clones do not appear on the photographic film because the radioactive probe cannot bind without complementarity.
  • Rosie's Milk Protein: Rosie produced human α-lactalbumin (\(2.4\,\mathrm{g/L}\)), whereas α-1-antitrypsin is a separate transgenic product used for emphysema.
  • Oral Insulin Myth: Insulin cannot be given orally because digestive proteases hydrolyse peptide bonds.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Totipotency, explant, somaclones, meristem culture (virus-free), and somatic hybridisation (protoplast fusion).
  • Five benefits of GM plants + Golden Rice (Vitamin A).
  • Bt toxin mechanism + RNAi (Agrobacterium vector, sense + antisense → dsRNA).
  • Eli Lilly (1983) insulin + ADA gene therapy (1990, 4-year-old girl, retroviral vector, periodic infusions vs. embryonic cure).
  • PCR (early low-concentration detection), radioactive probe, ELISA (antigen–antibody).
  • Transgenic animals (>95% mice) and their 5 applications.
  • GEAC, biopiracy, 27 documented Indian Basmati varieties (1997 US patent), turmeric/neem, Indian Patents Bill second amendment.

9. Concise Final Recap

Biotechnology translates molecular genetics into practical solutions across agriculture, medicine, and industry. In agriculture, tissue culture (micro-propagation, meristem culture, somatic hybridisation) and GM crops (Bt cotton/corn, RNAi-protected tobacco, Vitamin A-rich Golden Rice) boost yield while cutting chemical pesticide use. In medicine, rDNA therapeutics like human insulin avoid animal-source allergies, ex vivo gene therapy addresses hereditary defects such as ADA deficiency, and PCR, radioactive probes, and ELISA enable pre-symptomatic diagnosis. Transgenic animals—predominantly mice—model human diseases, produce pharmaceuticals like α-1-antitrypsin and α-lactalbumin, and streamline vaccine and toxicity testing. Finally, bodies like GEAC and updated patent laws safeguard ecological biosafety and protect developing nations' biodiversity against biopiracy.
