Principles of Inheritance and Variation

Comprehensive Self-Contained Study Module, Visual Diagrams, Worked Examples, and NEET UG Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Historical Context

Genetics is the branch of biology that deals scientifically with both the inheritance and the variation of characters from parents to offspring:

  • Inheritance: The process by which characters are passed on from parent to progeny; it forms the fundamental basis of heredity.
  • Variation: The degree by which progeny differ from their parents.

As early as 8000–1000 B.C., humans recognised that one of the primary causes of variation was hidden in sexual reproduction. They exploited natural variations present in wild populations of plants and animals to selectively breed and domesticate organisms with desirable traits. A classic Indian example is the artificial selection and domestication of ancestral wild cows to produce well-known breeds such as Sahiwal cows in Punjab. However, early breeders lacked a scientific explanation for the underlying mechanisms of inheritance.

Historical Pioneers of Genetics & Molecular Biology

While Gregor Mendel's work revealed patterns of inheritance via discrete "factors", the physical and chemical nature of genetic material became the focus of biology over the subsequent century, giving rise to molecular biology through contributions from Watson, Crick, Nirenberg, Khorana, Kornbergs (father and son), Benzer, Monod, and Brenner:

  • James Dewey Watson (born 6 April 1928, Chicago): Received his B.Sc. in Zoology (1947) and his Ph.D. (1950) from Indiana University, Bloomington, studying the effect of hard X-rays on bacteriophage multiplication.
  • Francis Harry Compton Crick (born 8 June 1916, Northampton, England): Studied physics at University College, London (B.Sc., 1937) and completed his Ph.D. in 1954 on the thesis "X-ray Diffraction: Polypeptides and Proteins". He was elected Fellow of the Royal Society (F.R.S.) in 1959.
  • The 1953 Discovery & Joint Honours: Meeting when Watson was 23, their second serious collaborative effort—built on experimental evidence and nucleic acid literature—led in early March 1953 to the proposal of the complementary double-helical configuration of DNA and its replication scheme. Together they received the John Collins Warren Prize (1959), the Lasker Award (1960), the Research Corporation Prize (1962), and the Nobel Prize (1962).

2. Mendel's Hybridisation Experiments on Garden Pea

During the mid-nineteenth century, Gregor Mendel conducted hybridisation experiments on garden peas (Pisum sativum) for seven years (1856–1863) and proposed the laws of inheritance in living organisms.

Why Mendel's Approach Succeeded

  1. Mathematical & Statistical Rigour: For the first time in biology, statistical analysis and mathematical logic were applied to inheritance problems.
  2. Large Sampling Size: His large sample counts gave high credibility to the data collected.
  3. Successive Generations & True-Breeding Lines: He confirmed his inferences across successive generations (\(F_1, F_2, F_3, F_4\)), proving his findings were general rules rather than unsubstantiated ideas.
  4. Opposing Contrasting Traits: Mendel selected 14 true-breeding pea plant varieties arranged into 7 pairs, where varieties in a pair were identical except for one character showing contrasting traits. A true-breeding line is one that, having undergone continuous self-pollination, shows stable trait inheritance and expression for several generations.

Experimental Procedure for Artificial Cross-Pollination

Because pea flowers are naturally self-pollinating, Mendel performed controlled artificial cross-pollination in three steps:

  1. Emasculation: Removal of anthers from the flower bud of the female parent before pollen maturity using forceps/scissors to prevent self-pollination.
  2. Pollination (Transfer of Pollen): Dusting mature pollen grains collected from the male parent flower onto the stigma of the emasculated female flower.
  3. Seed Collection & Germination: Collecting the resulting seeds from the matured pod and growing them to produce the First Filial (\(F_1\)) hybrid generation.

3. Inheritance of One Gene (Monohybrid Cross)

To study the inheritance of a single gene, Mendel crossed true-breeding tall (\(\mathrm{TT}\)) and true-breeding dwarf (\(\mathrm{tt}\)) pea plants:

  • First Filial Generation (\(F_1\)): All \(F_1\) progeny plants were tall (\(\mathrm{Tt}\)), resembling only one parent. None were dwarf. Across all seven characters studied, the \(F_1\) always resembled one parent completely, while the other parental trait was not seen.
  • Second Filial Generation (\(F_2\)): When Mendel self-pollinated the tall \(F_1\) plants, the unexpressed dwarf trait reappeared in the \(F_2\) generation without any blending (no intermediate-height plants were formed). Exactly \(\frac{3}{4}\) of the \(F_2\) plants were tall and \(\frac{1}{4}\) were dwarf, giving a \(3:1\) phenotypic ratio.
  • \(F_3\) and \(F_4\) Generations: Self-pollinating dwarf \(F_2\) plants yielded exclusively dwarf progeny in \(F_3\) and \(F_4\), proving their genotype was homozygous recessive (\(\mathrm{tt}\)). Self-pollinating tall \(F_2\) plants showed that \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the tall plants (\(\frac{1}{4}\) of total \(F_2\)) bred true for tallness (\(\mathrm{TT}\)), whereas \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the tall plants (\(\frac{1}{2}\) of total \(F_2\)) segregated into tall and dwarf in a \(3:1\) ratio (\(\mathrm{Tt}\)).

Core Genetic Terminology

  • Factors / Genes: Discrete units of inheritance passed unchanged from parent to offspring through gametes, containing information required to express a trait.
  • Alleles: Genes that code for a pair of contrasting traits (slightly different molecular forms of the same gene occupying the same locus on homologous chromosomes).
  • Alphabetical Convention: Capital letter denotes the trait expressed in \(F_1\) (e.g., \(\mathrm{T}\) for tall), and the corresponding lowercase letter denotes the recessive trait (e.g., \(\mathrm{t}\) for dwarf). Using different letters like \(\mathrm{T}\) and \(\mathrm{d}\) is avoided so that alleles of the same gene are clearly recognised.
  • Genotype vs. Phenotype: The genetic constitution (e.g., \(\mathrm{TT}, \mathrm{Tt}, \mathrm{tt}\)) is the genotype, while the observable physical description (tall or dwarf) is the phenotype.
  • Homozygous vs. Heterozygous: Identical allelic pairs (\(\mathrm{TT}\) or \(\mathrm{tt}\)) are homozygous; dissimilar allelic pairs (\(\mathrm{Tt}\)) are heterozygous (a monohybrid for one character).

Punnett Square & Binomial Expansion

Developed by British geneticist Reginald C. Punnett, the Punnett Square is a graphical representation used to calculate the probability of all possible genotypes of offspring in a genetic cross. Test Cross

Because \(\mathrm{TT}\) and \(\mathrm{Tt}\) have the exact same dominant phenotype, external inspection cannot determine the genotype of a dominant plant. To find the unknown genotype of a dominant organism, Mendel performed a test cross: crossing the organism showing the dominant phenotype with the homozygous recessive parent instead of self-crossing.

Mendel's Two Principles from Monohybrid Crosses

1. First Law — Law of Dominance:

  • (i) Characters are controlled by discrete units called factors.
  • (ii) Factors occur in pairs.
  • (iii) In a dissimilar pair of factors, one member of the pair dominates (dominant) the other (recessive).

Significance: Explains why only one parental character is expressed in the \(F_1\) generation and why both are expressed in a \(3:1\) proportion in the \(F_2\) generation.

2. Second Law — Law of Segregation:

Alleles do not show any blending, and both characters are recovered as such in the \(F_2\) generation even though one is not seen in \(F_1\). During gamete formation, the alleles of a pair segregate (separate) from each other such that a gamete receives only one of the two factors. Homozygous parents produce one type of gamete; heterozygous parents produce two kinds of gametes in equal proportion (\(1:1\)).

4. Deviations from Mendelian Dominance & Gene Action

4.1 Incomplete Dominance

In some organisms, the \(F_1\) hybrid phenotype does not resemble either of the two parents and is intermediate between them. A classic example is flower colour in the dog flower (snapdragon or Antirrhinum sp.):

  • Parental Cross: True-breeding red-flowered (\(\mathrm{RR}\)) \(\times\) true-breeding white-flowered (\(\mathrm{rr}\)).
  • \(F_1\) Progeny: All plants are pink-flowered (\(\mathrm{Rr}\)) because allele \(\mathrm{R}\) is not completely dominant over allele \(\mathrm{r}\).
  • \(F_2\) Progeny (on selfing \(F_1\)): Both the phenotypic ratio and genotypic ratio are identical at \(1:2:1\), i.e., \(1\text{ Red }(\mathrm{RR}) : 2\text{ Pink }(\mathrm{Rr}) : 1\text{ White }(\mathrm{rr})\).

4.2 Molecular Explanation of Dominance

Why are some alleles dominant and others recessive? A diploid organism has two copies (alleles) of each gene. In a heterozygote, one allele may have undergone a modification that alters its information. Consider a gene coding for an enzyme required to transform a substrate \(S\):

  • Normal (Unmodified) Allele: Produces the normal functional enzyme that converts substrate \(S\) into product.
  • Modified Allele: Theoretically, the modified allele could be responsible for producing:
    1. The normal or a less efficient enzyme: The modified allele is equivalent to the unmodified allele and produces the same phenotype (transforms substrate \(S\)). Such equivalent allele pairs are very common.
    2. A non-functional enzyme, OR
    3. No enzyme at all: In cases (2) and (3), the phenotype depends solely on the functioning of the unmodified allele. Thus, the unmodified (functioning) allele representing the original phenotype is the dominant allele, while the modified allele is generally the recessive allele. The recessive trait is expressed only when homozygous because no functional enzyme is produced.

4.3 Co-dominance & Multiple Alleles (Human ABO Blood Grouping)

In co-dominance, the \(F_1\) generation resembles both parents simultaneously. Human ABO blood grouping is controlled by gene \(\mathrm{I}\), which determines the sugar polymers protruding from the plasma membrane surface of red blood cells (RBCs):

  • Alleles \(\mathrm{I^A}\) and \(\mathrm{I^B}\) produce slightly different forms of sugar polymers (A-type and B-type sugars), whereas allele \(\mathrm{i}\) produces no sugar at all.
  • \(\mathrm{I^A}\) and \(\mathrm{I^B}\) are each completely dominant over \(\mathrm{i}\) (\(\mathrm{I^A i}\) gives blood group A; \(\mathrm{I^B i}\) gives blood group B).
  • When \(\mathrm{I^A}\) and \(\mathrm{I^B}\) are present together (\(\mathrm{I^A I^B}\)), both express their own types of sugars simultaneously on the RBC surface due to co-dominance, resulting in blood group AB.
  • Multiple Allelism: Because there are more than two alleles (\(\mathrm{I^A}, \mathrm{I^B}, \mathrm{i}\)) governing the same character, ABO blood grouping also exemplifies multiple alleles. Since a diploid individual possesses only two alleles, multiple alleles can only be detected in population studies.

4.4 Single Gene Product with Multiple Effects: Starch Synthesis in Pea Seeds

Dominance is not an autonomous feature of a gene or its product; it depends as much on the gene product as on the specific phenotype we choose to examine. In pea seeds, starch synthesis is controlled by one gene with two alleles (\(\mathrm{B}\) and \(\mathrm{b}\)):

  • \(\mathrm{BB}\) homozygotes: Synthesise starch effectively \(\rightarrow\) produce large starch grains \(\rightarrow\) seeds remain round upon maturation.
  • \(\mathrm{bb}\) homozygotes: Have lesser efficiency in starch synthesis \(\rightarrow\) produce smaller starch grains \(\rightarrow\) seeds become wrinkled upon maturation.
  • \(\mathrm{Bb}\) heterozygotes:
    • If seed shape is examined: \(\mathrm{Bb}\) seeds are round, so allele \(\mathrm{B}\) shows complete dominance over \(\mathrm{b}\) (\(3:1\) phenotypic ratio in \(F_2\)).
    • If starch grain size is examined: \(\mathrm{Bb}\) seeds produce starch grains of intermediate size, so the alleles show incomplete dominance (\(1:2:1\) phenotypic ratio in \(F_2\)).

5. Inheritance of Two Genes (Dihybrid Cross)

Mendel crossed pea plants differing in two pairs of contrasting traits: a parent with round and yellow seeds (\(\mathrm{RRYY}\)) and a parent with wrinkled and green seeds (\(\mathrm{rryy}\)):

  • Parental Gametes: \(\mathrm{RY}\) and \(\mathrm{ry}\).
  • \(F_1\) Hybrid: All \(F_1\) seeds were round and yellow (\(\mathrm{RrYy}\)), showing that round seed shape (\(\mathrm{R}\)) is dominant over wrinkled (\(\mathrm{r}\)), and yellow seed colour (\(\mathrm{Y}\)) is dominant over green (\(\mathrm{y}\)).
  • Independent Gamete Formation in \(F_1\): During meiosis in \(\mathrm{RrYy}\), segregation of \(50\%\,\mathrm{R}\) and \(50\%\,\mathrm{r}\) is completely independent of the segregation of \(50\%\,\mathrm{Y}\) and \(50\%\,\mathrm{y}\). Consequently, four types of gametes are formed in equal frequency (\(25\%\) or \(\frac{1}{4}\) each): \(\mathrm{RY}, \mathrm{Ry}, \mathrm{rY}, \mathrm{ry}\).

Third Law — Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment

"When two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, segregation of one pair of characters is independent of the other pair of characters."

6. Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance, Linkage & Recombination

6.1 Why Mendel's Work Remained Unrecognised (1865–1900)

Mendel published his work in 1865, but it remained unrecognised until 1900 for four key reasons:

  1. Communication was not easy in those days, so his work could not be widely publicised.
  2. His concept of genes (factors) as stable, discrete units that did not "blend" was not accepted by contemporaries as an explanation for the apparently continuous variation seen in nature.
  3. His approach of using mathematics and statistics to explain biological phenomena was totally new and unacceptable to many biologists of his time.
  4. He could not provide any physical proof for the existence of factors or identify what they were made of.

6.2 Rediscovery & Sutton-Boveri Chromosomal Theory

In 1900, three scientists—Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns, and Erich von Tschermak—independently rediscovered Mendel's results. By 1902, advances in microscopy enabled scientists to observe chromosome movement during meiosis. Walter Sutton and Theodore Boveri noted that the behaviour of chromosomes was parallel to the behaviour of Mendelian genes, and Sutton united chromosomal segregation with Mendelian principles to formulate the Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance.

Tutorial Table 5: Parallelism Between the Behaviour of Chromosomes and Mendelian Genes
Column A (Chromosomes) Column B ( Mendelian Genes / Factors)
Occur in homologous pairs in diploid cells. Occur in allelic pairs in diploid organisms.
Segregate at the time of gamete formation such that only one chromosome of each homologous pair is transmitted to a gamete. Segregate at gamete formation such that only one factor of each pair is transmitted to a gamete.
Independent pairs of chromosomes segregate independently of each other during Meiosis I anaphase. One pair of genes segregates independently of another pair (if located on different chromosomes).

Key Distinction Note: In the comparison above, "Independent pairs segregate independently of each other" specifically identifies chromosomes, because genes located on the same chromosome (linked genes) do not assort independently, whereas non-homologous chromosome pairs always assort independently during Anaphase I of meiosis.

6.3 Experimental Verification by T. H. Morgan

Thomas Hunt Morgan and his colleagues experimentally verified the chromosomal theory of inheritance using the tiny fruit fly, Drosophila melanogaster, which is ideal for genetic studies because:

  1. They can be grown on a simple synthetic medium in the laboratory.
  2. They complete their life cycle in about two weeks.
  3. A single mating produces a large number of progeny flies.
  4. There is clear differentiation of sexes—male and female flies are easily distinguishable (females are larger than males).
  5. It has many types of hereditary variations observable with low-power microscopes.

6.4 Linkage, Recombination & Genetic Mapping

Morgan carried out dihybrid crosses in Drosophila to study sex-linked genes located on the X chromosome. He observed that when two genes in a dihybrid cross were situated on the same chromosome, the \(F_2\) ratio deviated significantly from Mendel's \(9:3:3:1\) ratio, and the proportion of parental gene combinations was much higher than non-parental types:

  • Linkage: Coined by Morgan to describe the physical association of genes on a chromosome.
  • Recombination: Coined by Morgan to describe the generation of non-parental gene combinations (due to crossing over between homologous chromosomes).

8. Mechanisms of Sex Determination

In 1891, Henking traced a specific nuclear structure all through spermatogenesis in a few insects and observed that \(50\%\) of the sperm received this structure while the other \(50\%\) did not. He named it the "X body". Later scientists proved that Henking's X body was a chromosome, naming it the X-chromosome (sex chromosome), while the remaining chromosomes were named autosomes.

9. Mutation, Pedigree Analysis & Human Genetic Disorders

9.1 Mutation

Mutation is a phenomenon that results in the alteration of DNA sequences and consequently changes the genotype and phenotype of an organism. Together with recombination, mutation generates genetic variation:

  • Chromosomal Aberrations: Because one continuous DNA helix runs from end to end in each chromatid in a supercoiled form, loss (deletion) or gain (insertion/duplication) of a DNA segment alters chromosomes. Chromosomal aberrations are commonly observed in cancer cells.
  • Point Mutation: Mutation arising due to a change in a single base pair of DNA (classic example: sickle-cell anaemia).
  • Frame-shift Mutation: Caused by deletions and insertions of base pairs of DNA.
  • Mutagens: Chemical and physical factors that induce mutations (e.g., UV radiations).

9.2 Pedigree Analysis

Because controlled crosses cannot be performed in humans, geneticists analyse the inheritance of traits across several generations of a family tree—a method called pedigree analysis.

9.3 Mendelian Disorders (Single-Gene Mutations)

Mendelian disorders are determined by alteration or mutation in a single gene and follow Mendelian inheritance patterns (autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or sex-linked recessive). Prevalent examples include Myotonic dystrophy (autosomal dominant), Cystic fibrosis, Haemophilia, Colour blindness, Sickle-cell anaemia, Phenylketonuria, and Thalassemia.

1. Colour Blindness (X-Linked Recessive)

  • Cause: Defect in either the red or green cone of the eye due to mutation in certain genes on the X chromosome, resulting in failure to discriminate between red and green colour.
  • Prevalence: Occurs in about \(8\%\) of males and only about \(0.4\%\) of females because males have only one X chromosome (\(\mathrm{X^c Y}\)) whereas females have two (\(\mathrm{X^c X^c}\) required for expression).
  • Inheritance: A heterozygous carrier woman (\(\mathrm{X^C X^c}\)) has normal vision (suppressed by dominant normal gene), but her sons have a \(50\%\) chance of being colour blind. A daughter is colour blind only if her mother is at least a carrier and her father is colour blind.

2. Haemophilia (X-Linked Recessive)

  • Cause: A single protein that is part of the cascade of proteins involved in blood clotting is affected. A simple cut in an affected individual results in non-stop bleeding.
  • Inheritance: Transmitted from unaffected heterozygous carrier females (\(\mathrm{X^H X^h}\)) to some male progeny (\(\mathrm{X^h Y}\)). The possibility of a female becoming haemophilic (\(\mathrm{X^h X^h}\)) is extremely rare because her mother must be at least a carrier and her father must be haemophilic (which is unviable in the later stage of life).
  • Historical Example: The family pedigree of Queen Victoria shows a number of haemophilic descendants as she was a carrier of the disease.

3. Sickle-Cell Anaemia (Autosomal Recessive — Qualitative Globin Defect)

  • Genetic Control: Controlled by a single pair of alleles, \(\mathrm{Hb^A}\) (normal) and \(\mathrm{Hb^S}\) (mutant). Only homozygous \(\mathrm{Hb^S Hb^S}\) individuals show the diseased phenotype. Heterozygous \(\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^S}\) individuals are apparently unaffected carriers (exhibiting sickle-cell trait) with a \(50\%\) probability of transmitting the mutant gene.
  • Molecular Defect (Point Mutation): At the sixth codon of the \(\beta\)-globin gene, a single base substitution changes DNA template \(\mathrm{CTC \rightarrow CAC}\) (coding strand \(\mathrm{GAG \rightarrow GTG}\)), so the mRNA codon changes from \(\mathrm{GAG}\) to \(\mathrm{GUG}\).
  • Amino Acid Substitution: Causes substitution of Glutamic acid (Glu) by Valine (Val) at the 6th position of the \(\beta\)-globin chain of haemoglobin.
  • Pathophysiology: Under low oxygen tension, the mutant haemoglobin (\(\mathrm{Hb^S}\)) undergoes polymerisation, changing the RBC shape from a biconcave disc to an elongated sickle-like structure.
Tutorial Diagram 6: Point mutation in the β-globin gene (codon 6: GAG to GUG) substitutes Glutamic acid (Glu) with Valine (Val), causing RBC sickling.

4. Phenylketonuria (PKU — Autosomal Recessive Inborn Error of Metabolism)

  • Enzyme Defect: Affected individuals lack the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase required to convert the amino acid phenylalanine into tyrosine.
  • Pathophysiology & Symptoms: Phenylalanine accumulates and is converted into phenylpyruvic acid and other derivatives. Accumulation in the brain causes mental retardation; these compounds are also excreted in urine due to poor absorption by the kidney (and cause reduced hair and skin pigmentation).

5. Thalassemia (Autosomal Recessive — Quantitative Globin Defect)

  • Mechanism: Transmitted from unaffected heterozygous carrier parents when mutation or deletion reduces the rate of synthesis of one of the globin chains (\(\alpha\) or \(\beta\)) of haemoglobin, causing abnormal haemoglobin formation and characteristic anaemia.
  • \(\alpha\)-Thalassemia: Production of \(\alpha\)-globin chain is affected. Controlled by two closely linked genes HBA1 and HBA2 on chromosome 16 of each parent (mutation/deletion of 1 to 4 genes; more genes affected \(\rightarrow\) fewer \(\alpha\)-globin molecules produced).
  • \(\beta\)-Thalassemia: Production of \(\beta\)-globin chain is affected. Controlled by a single gene HBB on chromosome 11 of each parent (mutation of one or both alleles).
  • Crucial Exam Distinction: Thalassemia is a quantitative problem of synthesising too few globin molecules, whereas sickle-cell anaemia is a qualitative problem of synthesising an incorrectly functioning globin.

9.4 Chromosomal Disorders (Aneuploidy & Polyploidy)

  • Aneuploidy: Failure of segregation of chromatids during cell division cycle results in the gain or loss of one or more chromosomes. Includes trisomy (\(2n + 1\), an extra chromosome copy) and monosomy (\(2n - 1\), lacking one chromosome of a pair).
  • Polyploidy: Failure of cytokinesis after telophase stage of cell division results in an increase in a whole set of chromosomes (often seen in plants).

11. Worked Examples (Chapter Application Problems)

Worked Example 1: Gamete Calculation for Multiple Loci

Problem: A diploid organism is heterozygous for 4 loci (e.g., \(\mathrm{AaBbCcDd}\)). How many types of gametes can be produced assuming independent assortment?

Solution:

The number of different gamete types produced by a diploid organism heterozygous for \(n\) independently assorting loci is given by \(2^n\). Here, \(n = 4\):

\[\text{Number of types of gametes} = 2^4 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 16\text{ types of gametes.}\]
Worked Example 2: Dihybrid Cross Phenotypic Proportions

Problem: When a cross is made between a tall plant with yellow seeds (\(\mathrm{TtYy}\)) and a tall plant with green seeds (\(\mathrm{Ttyy}\)), what proportions of phenotype in the offspring could be expected to be: (a) tall and green, and (b) dwarf and green?

Solution:

Resolve the dihybrid cross (\(\mathrm{TtYy} \times \mathrm{Ttyy}\)) into two independent monohybrid crosses:

  • Height cross (\(\mathrm{Tt} \times \mathrm{Tt}\)): Progeny genotypes are \(\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{TT}, \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tt}, \frac{1}{4}\mathrm{tt}\). Thus, \(P(\text{Tall}) = \frac{3}{4}\) and \(P(\text{Dwarf}) = \frac{1}{4}\).
  • Seed colour cross (\(\mathrm{Yy} \times \mathrm{yy}\)): Progeny genotypes are \(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Yy}\text{ (Yellow)}\) and \(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{yy}\text{ (Green)}\). Thus, \(P(\text{Yellow}) = \frac{1}{2}\) and \(P(\text{Green}) = \frac{1}{2}\).

Multiplying the independent probabilities:

  • (a) Tall and green (\(\mathrm{T\_yy}\)): \(\displaystyle P(\text{Tall}) \times P(\text{Green}) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{8}\) (or \(37.5\%\)).
  • (b) Dwarf and green (\(\mathrm{ttyy}\)): \(\displaystyle P(\text{Dwarf}) \times P(\text{Green}) = \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{8}\) (or \(12.5\%\)).
Worked Example 3: ABO Blood Group Paternity & Sibling Genotypes

Problem: A child has blood group O. If the father has blood group A and the mother has blood group B, work out the genotypes of the parents and the possible genotypes and phenotypes of the other offsprings.

Solution:

  • A child with blood group O must be homozygous recessive with genotype \(\mathrm{ii}\), receiving one \(\mathrm{i}\) allele from the father and one \(\mathrm{i}\) allele from the mother.
  • Therefore, the father (blood group A) must be heterozygous \(\mathrm{I^A i}\), and the mother (blood group B) must be heterozygous \(\mathrm{I^B i}\).
  • Crossing \(\mathrm{I^A i} \times \mathrm{I^B i}\) yields four equally probable offspring genotypes (\(\frac{1}{4}\) or \(25\%\) each):
    • \(\mathrm{I^A I^B} \rightarrow\) Blood group AB
    • \(\mathrm{I^A i} \rightarrow\) Blood group A
    • \(\mathrm{I^B i} \rightarrow\) Blood group B
    • \(\mathrm{ii} \rightarrow\) Blood group O
Worked Example 4: Cross Between Linked Heterozygous Loci

Problem: Two heterozygous parents are crossed. If the two loci are linked, what would be the distribution of phenotypic features in the progeny compared to an unlinked dihybrid cross?

Solution:

If two genes are situated on the same chromosome (linked), they do not assort independently during meiosis. Consequently, the phenotypic distribution deviates significantly from the Mendelian \(9:3:3:1\) (or \(1:1:1:1\) test cross) ratio: parental phenotypic combinations appear in a much higher proportion than non-parental (recombinant) combinations. If linkage is complete (zero crossing over), only parental combinations are formed; if linkage is incomplete, recombinant phenotypes appear at a lower frequency proportional to the physical distance between the two loci on the chromosome.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Choice MCQ)

Which of the following pairs of contrasting traits in the garden pea plant studied by Gregor Mendel is incorrectly matched with its dominant and recessive expressions?

  • (A) Pod colour: Green (Dominant) / Yellow (Recessive)
  • (B) Seed colour: Green (Dominant) / Yellow (Recessive)
  • (C) Flower position: Axial (Dominant) / Terminal (Recessive)
  • (D) Pod shape: Inflated (Dominant) / Constricted (Recessive)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: In garden pea (Pisum sativum), Yellow seed colour is dominant over green seed colour, whereas Green pod colour is dominant over yellow pod colour. Students frequently confuse the dominance direction of seed colour with pod colour. Options (A), (C), and (D) are all correctly matched.

Practice Question 2 (Single-Choice MCQ)

In pea seeds, starch synthesis is controlled by a single gene with two alleles (\(\mathrm{B}\) and \(\mathrm{b}\)). If a heterozygous pea plant (\(\mathrm{Bb}\)) is self-pollinated, what are the expected phenotypic ratios in the \(F_2\) generation with respect to (i) seed shape and (ii) starch grain size, respectively?

  • (A) \(1:2:1\) and \(3:1\)
  • (B) \(3:1\) and \(3:1\)
  • (C) \(3:1\) and \(1:2:1\)
  • (D) \(1:2:1\) and \(1:2:1\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Homozygous \(\mathrm{BB}\) seeds produce large starch grains and are round; homozygous \(\mathrm{bb}\) seeds produce small starch grains and are wrinkled. Heterozygous \(\mathrm{Bb}\) seeds are round (showing complete dominance for seed shape, giving a \(3\text{ Round} : 1\text{ Wrinkled}\) ratio in \(F_2\)), but produce starch grains of intermediate size (showing incomplete dominance for starch grain size, giving a \(1\text{ Large} : 2\text{ Intermediate} : 1\text{ Small}\) ratio in \(F_2\)).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Choice MCQ — Numerical Probability)

In a Mendelian dihybrid cross between two heterozygous round yellow seeded pea plants (\(\mathrm{RrYy} \times \mathrm{RrYy}\)), if \(320\) seeds are collected in the \(F_2\) generation, how many seeds are expected to have the genotype \(\mathrm{RrYY}\)?

  • (A) \(20\)
  • (B) \(40\)
  • (C) \(80\)
  • (D) \(180\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: In the cross \(\mathrm{RrYy} \times \mathrm{RrYy}\):

  • Probability of getting \(\mathrm{Rr}\) from \(\mathrm{Rr} \times \mathrm{Rr}\) is \(\frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}\).
  • Probability of getting \(\mathrm{YY}\) from \(\mathrm{Yy} \times \mathrm{Yy}\) is \(\frac{1}{4}\).

Combined probability of \(\mathrm{RrYY} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{2}{16} = \frac{1}{8}\). Out of \(320\) seeds, the expected number of \(\mathrm{RrYY}\) seeds is \(\frac{1}{8} \times 320 = 40\).

Practice Question 4 (Statement-Based Question)

Read the following two statements carefully and select the correct option:

Statement I: In a theoretically modified allele of an enzyme-coding gene, if the modified allele produces no enzyme at all, it generally behaves as a recessive allele relative to the unmodified functioning allele.

Statement II: In Antirrhinum sp. (snapdragon), a cross between a pink-flowered plant (\(\mathrm{Rr}\)) and a white-flowered plant (\(\mathrm{rr}\)) produces red, pink, and white-flowered progeny in a \(1:2:1\) ratio.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Statement I is correct because the unmodified allele produces the functional enzyme required to transform the substrate and produce the dominant phenotype, whereas an allele producing a non-functional enzyme or no enzyme is recessive. Statement II is incorrect because crossing a pink plant (\(\mathrm{Rr}\)) with a white plant (\(\mathrm{rr}\)) is a test cross (\(\mathrm{Rr} \times \mathrm{rr}\)), which yields \(\frac{1}{2}\text{ Pink }(\mathrm{Rr}) : \frac{1}{2}\text{ White }(\mathrm{rr})\) in a \(1:1\) ratio (no red flowers are produced).

  • (B) Cross A: \(98.7\%\) parental, \(1.3\%\) recombinant; Cross B: \(62.8\%\) parental, \(37.2\%\) recombinant
  • (C) Cross A: \(50.0\%\) parental, \(50.0\%\) recombinant; Cross B: \(98.7\%\) parental, \(1.3\%\) recombinant
  • (D) Cross A: \(98.7\%\) recombinant, \(1.3\%\) parental; Cross B: \(62.8\%\) recombinant, \(37.2\%\) parental

Practice Question 6 (Assertion–Reason Question)

Assertion (A): In honey bees, male drones do not have a father and cannot have sons, but they have a grandfather and can have grandsons.

Reason (R): Male drones (\(n = 16\)) develop from unfertilised eggs of a diploid female (\(2n = 32\)) by parthenogenesis, and they produce haploid sperms by mitosis that fertilise eggs to form only females.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Under the haplodiploid sex-determination system of honey bees, an unfertilised haploid egg (\(n = 16\)) develops parthenogenetically into a male (drone), so a drone has only a mother (and a maternal grandfather) but no father. When a drone produces sperm by mitosis and fertilises an egg, the diploid zygote (\(2n = 32\)) always develops into a female (daughter), never a son; that daughter can later lay unfertilised eggs that become grandsons. Thus both (A) and (R) are true and (R) directly explains (A).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Choice MCQ)

Human skin colour is a classic example of polygenic inheritance controlled by three genes \(\mathrm{A, B}\), and \(\mathrm{C}\). Which of the following genotypes will produce the exact same intermediate skin darkness as an individual with genotype \(\mathrm{AaBbCc}\)?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{AABbCc}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{AAbbCc}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{AaBbcc}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{aabbCC}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: In polygenic inheritance, the effect of each allele is additive, so the darkness of skin depends on the total number of dominant alleles present in the genotype. Genotype \(\mathrm{AaBbCc}\) has exactly 3 dominant alleles and 3 recessive alleles. Examining the options: (A) \(\mathrm{AABbCc}\) has 4 dominant alleles; (B) \(\mathrm{AAbbCc}\) has 3 dominant alleles (\(\mathrm{A, A, C}\)); (C) \(\mathrm{AaBbcc}\) has 2 dominant alleles; (D) \(\mathrm{aabbCC}\) has 2 dominant alleles. Therefore, \(\mathrm{AAbbCc}\) produces the same intermediate skin colour as \(\mathrm{AaBbCc}\).

Practice Question 9 (Single-Choice MCQ)

A woman whose father was colour blind marries a man with normal colour vision. Assuming the woman's mother was homozygous normal, what is the probability that their first son will be colour blind, and what is the probability that their first daughter will be colour blind?

  • (A) Son: \(50\%\); Daughter: \(50\%\)
  • (B) Son: \(100\%\); Daughter: \(0\%\)
  • (C) Son: \(50\%\); Daughter: \(0\%\)
  • (D) Son: \(25\%\); Daughter: \(25\%\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Because the woman's father was colour blind (\(\mathrm{X^c Y}\)) and her mother was homozygous normal (\(\mathrm{X^C X^C}\)), the woman inherited \(\mathrm{X^c}\) from her father and \(\mathrm{X^C}\) from her mother, making her a carrier (\(\mathrm{X^C X^c}\)). She marries a normal man (\(\mathrm{X^C Y}\)):

  • Sons receive \(\mathrm{Y}\) from the father and either \(\mathrm{X^C}\) (\(50\%\)) or \(\mathrm{X^c}\) (\(50\%\)) from the mother. Thus, a son has a \(50\%\) chance of being colour blind (\(\mathrm{X^c Y}\)).
  • Daughters always receive the dominant normal \(\mathrm{X^C}\) from their father (\(\mathrm{X^C X^C}\) or \(\mathrm{X^C X^c}\)), so the probability of a daughter being colour blind is \(0\%\).

13. Common Pitfalls & Final Revision Checklist

High-Yield NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid

  1. Seed Colour vs. Pod Colour in Pea: Do not mix them up! In seeds, Yellow is dominant over Green; in pods, Green is dominant over Yellow.
  2. Sickle-Cell Position & Amino Acid Direction: In sickle-cell anaemia, Glutamic acid (Glu) is replaced by Valine (Val) at the 6th position of the \(\beta\)-globin chain (mRNA codon \(\mathrm{GAG \rightarrow GUG}\)), never the reverse.
  3. Thalassemia Chromosomes & Quantitative Nature: Remember that \(\alpha\)-Thalassemia involves two genes (HBA1, HBA2) on chromosome 16, while \(\beta\)-Thalassemia involves one gene (HBB) on chromosome 11. Thalassemia is quantitative; sickle-cell anaemia is qualitative.
  4. Aneuploidy vs. Polyploidy Mechanism: Aneuploidy results from failure of segregation of chromatids during cell division; polyploidy results from failure of cytokinesis after telophase.
  5. Dominant vs. Recessive Pedigrees: In an autosomal recessive pedigree (e.g., Sickle-cell anaemia), unaffected carrier parents produce affected children. In an autosomal dominant pedigree (e.g., Myotonic dystrophy), affected children always have at least one affected parent.

Concise Final Recap

  • Mendel's Laws: Law of Dominance (explains \(F_1\) uniformity and \(3:1\) \(F_2\) ratio), Law of Segregation (universally applicable; no blending of alleles), and Law of Independent Assortment (\(9:3:3:1\) dihybrid ratio; applies only to genes on different chromosomes or distantly located on the same chromosome).
  • Non-Mendelian Ratios: Incomplete dominance (Snapdragon flower colour; pea starch grain size) gives \(1:2:1\) phenotypic and genotypic ratios. Co-dominance and multiple alleles (\(\mathrm{I^A, I^B, i}\)) produce 6 genotypes and 4 phenotypes in human ABO blood groups.
  • Chromosomes & Linkage: Rediscovered in 1900 (de Vries, Correns, von Tschermak); Chromosomal Theory proposed by Sutton and Boveri (1902); verified in Drosophila by T. H. Morgan; genetic mapping developed by Alfred Sturtevant using recombination frequencies (\(\mathrm{y-w} = 1.3\%\); \(\mathrm{w-m} = 37.2\%\)).
  • Disorders Summary:
    • X-linked Recessive: Haemophilia, Red-green Colour Blindness.
    • Autosomal Recessive: Sickle-cell Anaemia, Phenylketonuria (PKU), Thalassemia, Cystic Fibrosis.
    • Autosomal Dominant: Myotonic Dystrophy.
    • Chromosomal (Aneuploidy): Down's Syndrome (Trisomy 21; 47 chromosomes), Klinefelter's Syndrome (47, XXY; sterile male with gynaecomastia), Turner's Syndrome (45, XO; sterile female with rudimentary ovaries).
