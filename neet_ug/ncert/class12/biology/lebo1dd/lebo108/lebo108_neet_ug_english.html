Microbes in Human Welfare

Applied Microbiology: Household Fermentation, Industrial Bioproducts, Wastewater Treatment, Biogas, Biocontrol & Biofertilisers

1. Learning Objectives & Syllabus Context

Although pathogenic microorganisms cause serious diseases in humans, livestock, and crops, a vast majority of microbes perform indispensable beneficial roles. For NEET UG, this chapter is a high-scoring factual and conceptual unit testing organism–product pairings, biological sewage treatment mechanics, anaerobic methanogenesis, and eco-friendly agricultural strategies.

By completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Classify the major microbial groups and identify extreme habitats where microbes thrive.
  • Explain the biochemical roles of microbes in household foods such as curd, fermented batters, bread, toddy, and cheeses.
  • Match industrial organic acids, enzymes, antibiotics, and bioactive molecules (cyclosporin A, statins, streptokinase) to their exact microbial sources and clinical/commercial uses.
  • Trace the sequential physical and biological stages of a Sewage Treatment Plant (STP), including flocs, Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD), activated sludge, and anaerobic digestion.
  • Describe methanogenesis in biogas plants and cattle rumen, and distinguish chemical pest/nutrient management from microbial biocontrol agents and biofertilisers.

2. Microbial Ubiquity & Structural Diversity

Microorganisms form a foundational component of Earth's biosphere alongside macroscopic plants and animals. They inhabit soil, air, water, and the internal surfaces of living hosts. Crucially, extremophilic microbes survive in environments hostile to all other life forms, including:

  • Deep-sea or terrestrial thermal vents (geysers) at temperatures reaching 100°C.
  • Deep subterranean soil strata and beneath thick layers of snow several metres deep.
  • Highly acidic habitats.

Microbes encompass diverse biological entities: bacteria, fungi, protozoa, microscopic viruses (animal, plant, and bacterial viruses/bacteriophages), viroids (infectious RNA), and prions (proteinaceous infectious agents). Bacteria and many fungi can be cultured on nutritive agar media in petri dishes, where single cells multiply into macroscopic, visible colonies used for laboratory identification.

3. Microbes in Household Food Production

Daily culinary traditions rely extensively on controlled microbial fermentation, where specific bacteria or fungi alter texture, digestibility, flavour, and nutritional value.

Enhances nutritional quality by increasing Vitamin B₁₂. In the human stomach, LAB also help check disease-causing microbes.

Toddy (traditional South Indian drink) Natural fermenting microbes Produced by fermenting sap tapped from palm trees.
Fermented traditional foods Diverse food-grade microbes Used to ferment fish, soyabean, and bamboo shoots to improve preservation and taste.

Roquefort cheese Specific ripening fungi Growth of specific fungi on the cheese imparts its distinct tangy flavour and texture.

4. Microbes in Industrial Products

Industrial microbiology scales up microbial metabolism inside massive, controlled vessels called fermentors to manufacture beverages, life-saving antibiotics, organic acids, enzymes, and clinical bioactive molecules.

4.1 Fermented Beverages

Saccharomyces cerevisiae—referred to as brewer's yeast in beverage production—ferments malted cereals and fruit juices to yield ethanol. The final alcoholic drink depends on both the raw substrate and whether distillation is performed:

  • Produced without distillation: Wine and beer.
  • Produced by distillation of the fermented broth: Whisky, brandy, and rum (higher alcohol concentration).

4.2 Antibiotics

The term antibiotic derives from Greek roots: anti ("against") and bio ("life"). Relative to disease-causing pathogens they are "against life," whereas relative to human patients they are "pro-life." Antibiotics are chemical substances secreted by certain microbes that kill or inhibit the growth of other pathogenic microbes.

Discovery & Clinical Impact of Penicillin

Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin by chance while culturing Staphylococci bacteria. On an unwashed plate, he noticed a contaminating mould—Penicillium notatum—around which Staphylococci failed to grow, and named the inhibitory chemical penicillin. Later, Ernest Chain and Howard Florey established its full therapeutic potency; it was widely used to treat wounded American soldiers in World War II. Fleming, Chain, and Florey shared the 1945 Nobel Prize. Subsequent antibiotics transformed medicine by controlling deadly bacterial diseases such as plague, whooping cough (kali khansi), diphtheria (gal ghotu), and leprosy (kusht rog).

5. Microbes in Sewage Treatment

Municipal wastewater (sewage) generated in cities and towns contains large amounts of human excreta, organic matter, and pathogenic microbes. Discharging untreated sewage directly into rivers causes severe pollution and water-borne diseases. Therefore, sewage is treated in Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) using naturally occurring heterotrophic microbes before release.

Tutorial Diagram 2: Sequential workflow of a Sewage Treatment Plant (STP), separating physical solid removal (primary) from microbial oxidation and anaerobic digestion (secondary).

5.1 Primary vs. Secondary Treatment & Key Terms

  • Primary Treatment (Physical): Removes large and small particles through sequential filtration (removes floating debris) followed by sedimentation (removes grit such as soil and small pebbles). The settled solids form primary sludge, while the liquid supernatant forms the primary effluent.
  • Secondary Treatment (Biological): The primary effluent enters large aeration tanks where mechanical agitation and pumped air promote vigorous growth of aerobic microbes into flocs (mesh-like masses of bacteria associated with fungal filaments). These microbes oxidise organic matter, sharply lowering the BOD.
  • Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD): Defined as the amount of oxygen that would be consumed if all organic matter in 1 litre of water were oxidised by bacteria. The BOD test measures the rate of oxygen uptake by microorganisms in a water sample and serves as an indirect measure of organic matter present. Higher BOD = greater polluting potential.
  • Activated Sludge & Anaerobic Digestion: Once BOD drops significantly, the effluent moves to a settling tank where bacterial flocs sediment as activated sludge. A small fraction is pumped back to the aeration tank as inoculum; the major fraction enters anaerobic sludge digesters, where anaerobic bacteria digest the bacteria and fungi, generating inflammable biogas (a mixture of methane, hydrogen sulphide, and carbon dioxide).

To curb river pollution from untreated municipal sewage, the Ministry of Environment and Forests initiated the Ganga Action Plan and Yamuna Action Plan to expand STP infrastructure.

6. Microbes in Biogas Production

Biogas is an inflammable microbial gas mixture—predominantly methane (CH₄), along with CO₂ and H₂—used as fuel for cooking and lighting, especially in rural areas. While dough, cheese, and alcoholic fermentations release CO₂, anaerobic bacteria growing on cellulosic substrates produce large volumes of methane.

  • Methanogens: Collectively refers to anaerobic methane-producing bacteria, such as Methanobacterium.
  • Natural Habitats: Found in anaerobic sludge during sewage treatment and in the rumen (a compartment of the stomach) of cattle, where they aid in breaking down plant cellulose. Because humans lack these cellulolytic microbes, we cannot digest dietary cellulose.
  • Gobar Gas: Cattle dung (gobar) is rich in methanogens and undigested cellulose, making it an ideal feedstock for biogas plants.
  • Indian Technology Agencies: Biogas technology in India was developed largely through the efforts of the Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI) and the Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC).

7. Sustainable Agriculture: Biocontrol Agents & Biofertilisers

7.1 Microbes and Predators as Biocontrol Agents

Biocontrol uses biological methods to manage plant diseases and pests, reducing reliance on toxic chemical insecticides, pesticides, and weedicides that pollute soil, groundwater, and crops. Organic farming operates on the principle that biodiversity furthers ecosystem health: pests are not completely eradicated (since beneficial predatory and parasitic insects depend on them for food or hosts), but are kept at manageable levels through natural checks and balances.

Biocontrol Agent Biological Type Target Pest / Pathogen & Mechanism
Ladybird beetle (red & black markings) Predatory insect Controls aphids through natural predation.
Dragonflies Predatory insect Controls mosquitoes.
Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) Bacterium Controls butterfly caterpillars. Dried spores mixed with water are sprayed on brassicas and fruit trees; when ingested, toxin is released in the larval gut, killing caterpillars while sparing other insects. Transgenic Bt-cotton carries the Bt toxin gene.
Trichoderma species Free-living root fungus Common in root ecosystems; effective against several plant pathogens.
Baculoviruses (genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus) Arthropod-specific virus Species-specific, narrow-spectrum insecticidal control. Shows no negative impact on plants, mammals, birds, fish, or non-target insects—ideal for Integrated Pest Management (IPM) and ecologically sensitive areas.

7.2 Microbes as Biofertilisers

Overuse of chemical fertilisers drives environmental pollution, prompting a shift toward organic farming using biofertilisers—living organisms that enrich soil nutrient quality. The three principal sources are bacteria, fungi, and cyanobacteria:

  • Nitrogen-fixing Bacteria:
    • Symbiotic: Rhizobium forms root nodules in leguminous plants, fixing atmospheric nitrogen into organic forms usable by the host plant.
    • Free-living: Azospirillum and Azotobacter fix atmospheric nitrogen independently in soil, enriching soil nitrogen content.
  • Mycorrhizal Fungi: Many members of the genus Glomus form symbiotic associations (mycorrhiza) with plant roots. The fungal partner absorbs phosphorus from soil and transfers it to the plant, while also conferring resistance to root-borne pathogens, tolerance to salinity and drought, and enhanced overall plant growth.
  • Cyanobacteria (Blue-Green Algae): Autotrophic microbes such as Anabaena, Nostoc, and Oscillatoria fix atmospheric nitrogen and add organic matter to soil, serving as vital biofertilisers in paddy fields.

8. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Evaluating Water Pollution Using Hypothetical BOD Data

Scenario (Assumed Values): Four unlabelled water samples—\(S_1, S_2, S_3, S_4\)—are collected from different stages of a municipal water cycle and tested for Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) at 20°C. The measured values are:

  • \(S_1 = 350\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)
  • \(S_2 = 6\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)
  • \(S_3 = 22\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)
  • \(S_4 = 140\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)

Identify which sample represents (a) untreated raw sewage, (b) primary effluent entering the aeration tank, (c) treated secondary effluent, and (d) relatively clean river water.

Solution:

BOD measures the oxygen required by bacteria to oxidise all organic matter in 1 litre of water; thus, \(\text{BOD} \propto \text{Organic Matter Content} \propto \text{Polluting Potential}\).

Ordering the samples from highest to lowest BOD:

\[ S_1\,(350\,\mathrm{mg/L}) > S_4\,(140\,\mathrm{mg/L}) > S_3\,(22\,\mathrm{mg/L}) > S_2\,(6\,\mathrm{mg/L}) \]
  • Untreated raw sewage: \(S_1\) (\(350\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)) has the maximum organic load and highest polluting potential.
  • Primary effluent: \(S_4\) (\(140\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)) has had large solids and grit removed physically, slightly reducing organic load, but dissolved organic matter remains high before biological aeration.
  • Treated secondary effluent: \(S_3\) (\(22\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)) reflects drastic BOD reduction by aerobic flocs in the aeration tank.
  • Relatively clean river water: \(S_2\) (\(6\,\mathrm{mg/L}\)) has the lowest organic matter content.
Worked Example 2: Designing a Microbial IPM & Biofertiliser Plan

Scenario (Assumed Farm): An organic farmer cultivates paddy in low-lying fields and brassica vegetables in an adjacent ecologically sensitive wetland buffer. The farmer faces three issues: (i) low soil nitrogen in paddy plots, (ii) root-borne fungal wilt and poor phosphorus uptake in vegetable seedlings, and (iii) caterpillar infestation on brassicas without harming local honeybees or wetland fish. Recommend one specific microbial agent for each issue.

Solution:

  1. Paddy nitrogen enrichment: Apply nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria such as Anabaena, Nostoc, or Oscillatoria. They thrive in aquatic paddy fields, fix atmospheric \(\mathrm{N_2}\), and add organic matter.
  2. Phosphorus uptake & root-pathogen protection: Inoculate seedlings with mycorrhizal fungi of the genus Glomus (enhances phosphorus absorption and root-pathogen resistance) alongside free-living root-ecosystem fungus Trichoderma for direct biocontrol of root pathogens.
  3. Target-specific caterpillar control: Spray Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) spore suspension or apply a Nucleopolyhedrovirus (baculovirus) preparation. Both act specifically on target insect pests without harming non-target pollinators (honeybees), fish, birds, or mammals.

9. NEET UG Practice Questions

These original practice questions are modeled on NEET UG single-correct MCQ, statement-based, assertion–reason, and matching formats.

Practice Question 1 Single-Correct MCQ

During the conversion of fresh milk into curd by Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB), which of the following changes occurs?

  • (A) Complete hydrolysis of lactose into ethanol and carbon dioxide, decreasing Vitamin C
  • (B) Coagulation and partial digestion of milk proteins by acids, with an increase in Vitamin B₁₂
  • (C) Alkaline precipitation of casein proteins, with an increase in Vitamin D
  • (D) Oxidation of milk fats by fungal lipases, releasing methane gas

Answer: (B)

Explanation: LAB (such as Lactobacillus) produce lactic acid during growth, which coagulates and partially digests milk proteins while improving nutritional quality by increasing Vitamin B₁₂.

Practice Question 2 Match the Following

Match the bioactive molecule or enzyme in Column I with its microbial source in Column II and select the correct option:

Column I Column II
1. Cyclosporin A (i) Monascus purpureus
2. Statins (ii) Streptococcus (modified)
3. Streptokinase (iii) Trichoderma polysporum
4. Citric acid (iv) Aspergillus niger
  • (A) 1-(iii), 2-(i), 3-(ii), 4-(iv)
  • (B) 1-(i), 2-(iii), 3-(ii), 4-(iv)
  • (C) 1-(iii), 2-(ii), 3-(i), 4-(iv)
  • (D) 1-(iv), 2-(i), 3-(ii), 4-(iii)

Answer: (A)

Explanation: Cyclosporin A (immunosuppressant) is obtained from the fungus Trichoderma polysporum; statins (cholesterol-lowering) from the yeast Monascus purpureus; streptokinase (clot buster) from genetically modified Streptococcus; and citric acid from the fungus Aspergillus niger.

Practice Question 3 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: Wine and beer are produced without distillation, whereas whisky, brandy, and rum are produced by distillation of the fermented broth.
Statement II: The large holes in Swiss cheese are caused by the production of methane gas by the fungus Propionibacterium shermanii.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect for two reasons: Propionibacterium shermanii is a bacterium (not a fungus), and the large holes in Swiss cheese are caused by CO₂ gas (not methane).

Practice Question 4 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): Statins are used clinically as blood-cholesterol lowering agents.
Reason (R): Statins act by competitively inhibiting the enzyme responsible for cholesterol synthesis.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true

Answer: (A)

Explanation: Statins produced by the yeast Monascus purpureus lower blood cholesterol specifically because they competitively inhibit the key enzyme of the cholesterol biosynthetic pathway.

Practice Question 5 Single-Correct MCQ

In a Sewage Treatment Plant (STP), what is the correct composition and role of "flocs" formed in the aeration tank?

  • (A) Anaerobic archaea that convert primary sludge directly into methane
  • (B) Masses of aerobic bacteria associated with fungal filaments forming mesh-like structures that consume organic matter
  • (C) Inorganic grit and pebbles removed during primary sedimentation
  • (D) Pure algal colonies that release oxygen to kill pathogenic viruses

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Vigorous aeration and mechanical agitation in the aeration tank promote the growth of aerobic bacteria associated with fungal filaments into mesh-like flocs, which oxidise organic matter and reduce BOD.

Practice Question 6 Single-Correct MCQ

After BOD is significantly reduced in the aeration tank, the effluent is passed into a settling tank to form activated sludge. What happens to the major portion of this activated sludge?

  • (A) It is discharged directly into rivers and streams
  • (B) It is pumped back into the aeration tank as inoculum
  • (C) It is pumped into anaerobic sludge digesters to produce biogas
  • (D) It is filtered to remove floating plastic debris

Answer: (C)

Explanation: Only a small portion of activated sludge is recycled to the aeration tank as inoculum. The major portion is transferred to anaerobic sludge digesters where anaerobic bacteria digest the flocs and produce biogas (CH₄, H₂S, CO₂).

Practice Question 7 Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following combinations correctly identifies the gases produced by methanogens growing anaerobically on cellulosic material?

  • (A) Predominantly CO₂ with small amounts of O₂ and N₂
  • (B) Predominantly CH₄ along with CO₂ and H₂
  • (C) Predominantly H₂S along with N₂O and CO
  • (D) Pure O₂ and CH₄ in equal molar proportions

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Methanogens such as Methanobacterium grow anaerobically on cellulosic substrates to produce large amounts of methane (CH₄) along with carbon dioxide (CO₂) and hydrogen (H₂).

Practice Question 8 Incorrect Statement MCQ

Which of the following statements regarding biological control of pests is incorrect?

  • (A) Organic farmers aim to keep pests at manageable levels rather than completely eradicating them
  • (B) Ladybird beetles help control aphids, while dragonflies help control mosquitoes
  • (C) Bacillus thuringiensis spores kill butterfly caterpillars by releasing a toxin in the larval gut
  • (D) Nucleopolyhedrovirus is a broad-spectrum insecticide that harms non-target beneficial insects and fish

Answer: (D)

Explanation: Baculoviruses in the genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus are species-specific, narrow-spectrum biocontrol agents with no negative impacts on plants, mammals, birds, fish, or non-target insects.

Practice Question 9 Single-Correct MCQ

A leguminous crop shows stunted growth due to phosphorus deficiency and root-borne fungal infection, even though nitrogen levels are adequate. Which microbial symbiont would most directly resolve both issues?

  • (A) Azotobacter
  • (B) Glomus
  • (C) Oscillatoria
  • (D) Acetobacter aceti

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Fungi of the genus Glomus form mycorrhiza with plant roots, absorbing phosphorus from the soil for the host plant and providing resistance against root-borne pathogens, salinity, and drought.

Practice Question 10 Single-Correct MCQ

Which pair of microorganisms consists exclusively of free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria found in soil?

  • (A) Rhizobium and Anabaena
  • (B) Azospirillum and Azotobacter
  • (C) Glomus and Trichoderma
  • (D) Methanobacterium and Lactobacillus

Answer: (B)

Explanation: Azospirillum and Azotobacter are free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria in soil. Rhizobium is symbiotic in legume root nodules, Anabaena is a cyanobacterium, Glomus and Trichoderma are fungi, and Methanobacterium/Lactobacillus are not free-living soil N₂-fixers.

10. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

High-Frequency Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Yeast vs. Bacterium in Cheese: Propionibacterium shermanii (Swiss cheese) is a bacterium, whereas Roquefort cheese uses a fungus.
  • Acid-Producing Fungus vs. Bacteria: Among the four classic organic acid producers, only Aspergillus niger (citric acid) is a fungus; Acetobacter aceti, Clostridium butylicum, and Lactobacillus are all bacteria.
  • Cyclosporin A vs. Statins Source: Cyclosporin A comes from a fungus (Trichoderma polysporum), whereas statins come from a yeast (Monascus purpureus).
  • Primary vs. Secondary Sludge: Primary sludge forms by physical sedimentation of grit/solids; activated sludge forms by biological sedimentation of aerobic bacterial–fungal flocs after aeration.
  • Trichoderma Dual Role: Trichoderma polysporum produces cyclosporin A (clinical immunosuppressant), while free-living root Trichoderma species act as agricultural biocontrol agents against plant pathogens.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • LAB converts milk to curd, partially digests proteins, and boosts Vitamin B₁₂.
  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae is both baker's yeast (bread) and brewer's yeast (ethanol).
  • Penicillin: discovered by Fleming (Penicillium notatum); therapeutic role established by Chain & Florey (1945 Nobel Prize).
  • Lipases (detergents), pectinases & proteases (clear bottled juices), streptokinase (clot buster from Streptococcus).
  • Higher BOD = higher organic matter = higher polluting potential.
  • Biogas: CH₄ + CO₂ + H₂ produced by Methanobacterium in anaerobic digesters and cattle rumen (IARI & KVIC).
  • Biocontrol: Ladybird (aphids), Dragonflies (mosquitoes), Bt (caterpillars), Trichoderma (plant pathogens), Nucleopolyhedrovirus (narrow-spectrum IPM).
  • Biofertilisers: Rhizobium (symbiotic), Azospirillum & Azotobacter (free-living), Glomus (mycorrhiza, phosphorus), Anabaena/Nostoc/Oscillatoria (paddy cyanobacteria).

11. Concise Final Recap

Microorganisms underpin human welfare across domestic food fermentation, industrial bioprocessing, environmental remediation, renewable energy, and sustainable agriculture. Mastering the exact organism–product–mechanism associations—especially the distinction between physical and biological sewage treatment, the anaerobic role of methanogens, and the target-specificity of microbial biocontrol and biofertilisers—ensures full accuracy on NEET UG questions from this chapter.
