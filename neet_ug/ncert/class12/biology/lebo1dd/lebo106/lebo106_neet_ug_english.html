Evolutionary Biology: Origin of Life, Mechanisms, and Human Ancestry

A self-contained, exam-oriented tutorial covering cosmic beginnings, evolutionary evidence, population genetics, geological history, and hominid evolution.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Scope

Evolutionary biology investigates the history of life forms on Earth and the mechanisms that generate biodiversity over geological time. For NEET UG, questions frequently test exact timelines, experimental setups, examples of homology versus analogy, Hardy–Weinberg calculations, modes of natural selection, and cranial capacities in human evolution.

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Trace the timeline from the Big Bang and Earth's early reducing atmosphere to chemical and biological evolution.
  • Distinguish between competing theories of life's origin, including spontaneous generation, biogenesis, and the Oparin–Haldane hypothesis.
  • Classify evolutionary evidence across paleontology, embryology, comparative anatomy (homology vs. analogy), biochemistry, and anthropogenic selection.
  • Compare Lamarckism, Darwinian natural selection, and Hugo de Vries's mutation (saltation) theory.
  • Apply the Hardy–Weinberg principle mathematically to determine allele and genotype frequencies and identify factors that disrupt genetic equilibrium.
  • Outline the major geological milestones of plant, vertebrate, and hominid evolution.

2. Cosmic Context & the Origin of Life

2.1 Formation of the Universe and Early Earth

Observing distant stars is equivalent to looking back in time because stellar distances are measured in light-years—the light reaching Earth today began its journey millions of years ago. The Big Bang theory proposes that the universe originated roughly 13.8 billion years ago from an unimaginable singular explosion. As the universe expanded, temperatures dropped, allowing hydrogen and helium to form and condense under gravitation into galaxies.

Within the Milky Way galaxy, Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. Early Earth lacked a free-oxygen atmosphere; instead, molten masses released water vapour, methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)), carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)), and ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)). Solar ultraviolet (UV) rays split water into hydrogen and oxygen. The lighter \(\mathrm{H_2}\) escaped into space, while oxygen reacted with \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_4}\) to form water, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), and other compounds, eventually leading to the ozone layer. As the planet cooled, water vapour condensed as rain, filling surface depressions to create oceans. Life appeared roughly 500 million years after Earth's formation—nearly 4 billion years ago.

2.2 Early Hypotheses: Panspermia vs. Spontaneous Generation

  • Panspermia: Early Greek thinkers suggested that units of life called spores were transferred to Earth and other planets from outer space.
  • Theory of Spontaneous Generation: This older belief held that living organisms arose directly from decaying, non-living matter such as straw or mud.
  • Disproof by Louis Pasteur: Through controlled experiments using pre-sterilised flasks, Pasteur showed that killed yeast did not generate new life in sealed flasks, whereas flasks open to air developed new microorganisms. This established biogenesis (life arises only from pre-existing life), though it did not explain how the very first life form originated.

2.3 Chemical Evolution & Miller's Simulation

A.I. Oparin (Russia) and J.B.S. Haldane (England) proposed that the first life forms arose from pre-existing non-living organic molecules (such as RNA and proteins), preceded by chemical evolution—the synthesis of diverse organic molecules from inorganic precursors under high temperatures, volcanic storms, and a reducing atmosphere (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), etc.).

In 1953, American scientist S.L. Miller tested this in the laboratory. He circulated a mixture of \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), and water vapour at 800 °C through a closed apparatus equipped with electrodes for electric spark discharge. After cooling the vapours in a condenser, he detected amino acids in the condensed liquid. Subsequent similar experiments yielded sugars, nitrogenous bases, pigments, and fats, and meteorite analyses confirmed similar organic compounds in space.

The first non-cellular forms of life likely originated about 3 billion years ago as self-replicating giant macromolecules (RNA, proteins, polysaccharides). The first cellular forms—single-celled organisms restricted to aquatic habitats—did not appear until about 2,000 million years ago (mya).

3. Evidences Supporting Organic Evolution

Multiple independent lines of scientific inquiry confirm that existing life forms share common ancestry and have changed over geological time.

3.1 Paleontological, Embryological, and Biochemical Evidence

  • Paleontology (Fossils): Fossils are preserved hard remains of ancient organisms embedded in sedimentary rock layers. Different strata represent distinct geological periods, showing that specific life forms existed only during certain time spans and that new forms arose progressively. Fossil ages are determined using radioactive dating.
  • Embryological Evidence: Ernst Haeckel proposed that vertebrate embryos exhibit shared features absent in adults (for instance, vestigial gill slits behind the head in all vertebrate embryos, functional only in adult fish). However, Karl Ernst von Baer disproved Haeckel's biogenetic law by demonstrating that embryos never pass through the adult stages of other animals.
  • Biochemical & Molecular Similarities: Similarities in proteins and genes performing identical functions across diverse taxa point to shared ancestry, complementing anatomical evidence.
  • Artificial Selection: Selective breeding by humans in agriculture, horticulture, and animal husbandry (e.g., generating diverse dog breeds from a common stock within centuries) illustrates how selection can reshape populations over much longer natural timescales.

3.3 Natural Selection in Action & Anthropogenic Evolution

  • Industrial Melanism (England): Before industrialisation (1850s), white-winged moths outnumbered dark-winged (melanised) moths because thick white lichens covered tree trunks, camouflaging white moths from predators. After industrialisation (1920), soot darkened tree trunks and eliminated lichens (which serve as industrial pollution indicators). Dark-winged moths gained camouflage and survived, reversing the population ratio. Crucially, rural unpolluted areas retained low melanic counts, and no variant was completely wiped out.
  • Anthropogenic Selection: Heavy use of herbicides, pesticides, and antibiotics selects for pre-existing resistant variants within months or years rather than centuries. This demonstrates that evolution is a stochastic (chance-based) process driven by random mutations and environmental shifts, not a predetermined, directed process.

4. Theories of Evolution & Adaptive Radiation

4.1 Special Creation vs. Darwinism, Lamarckism, and Mutation Theory

The traditional Theory of Special Creation held three claims: all species were created as seen today, biodiversity has remained unchanged, and Earth is about 4,000 years old. Observations by Charles Darwin aboard H.M.S. Beagle and independently by Alfred Wallace (in the Malay Archipelago) overturned these claims, showing that Earth is billions of years old and life evolves gradually through extinctions and new radiations.

Theory / Proponent Core Mechanism Key Characteristics & Exam Notes
Lamarckism
(Jean-Baptiste Lamarck)		 Use and disuse of organs; inheritance of acquired characters. Proposed that giraffes elongated their necks by stretching for tall foliage and passed this trait to offspring. Rejected by modern genetics.
Darwinism
(Charles Darwin)		 Branching descent and natural selection (influenced by Thomas Malthus's population essays). Variations are small and directional; evolution is gradual. Fitness refers strictly to reproductive fitness. Rate of appearance of new forms depends on life span (fast in microbes, slow in vertebrates).
Mutation Theory
(Hugo de Vries)		 Large, sudden heritable changes (worked on evening primrose). Mutations are random and directionless; single-step large mutation causing speciation is called saltation.

4.2 Adaptive Radiation & Parallel Convergent Evolution

Adaptive radiation is the evolution of diverse species in a given geographical area starting from a single ancestral point and radiating into different habitats:

  • Darwin's Finches (Galapagos Islands): From an original seed-eating finch stock, multiple species evolved on the same islands with specialised beaks suited for insectivorous, vegetarian, or seed-eating diets.
  • Australian Marsupials: Numerous distinct marsupials (e.g., kangaroo, koala, wombat, bandicoot, sugar glider, Tasmanian wolf) evolved from a common ancestral stock within Australia.
  • Convergent Evolution in Isolated Habitats: When more than one adaptive radiation occurs in an isolated geographical area, unrelated groups evolve strikingly similar forms. In Australia, placental mammals and marsupials evolved matching pairs: Placental wolf & Tasmanian wolf, Anteater & Numbat (banded anteater), Mouse & Marsupial mouse, Lemur & Spotted cuscus, Flying squirrel & Flying phalanger (sugar glider), Bobcat & Tasmanian tiger cat, and Mole & Marsupial mole.

5. Population Genetics & Hardy–Weinberg Principle

The Hardy–Weinberg principle states that in a large, randomly mating population, allele frequencies remain stable and constant from generation to generation—a state known as genetic equilibrium (constant gene pool). For a diploid locus with two alleles \(A\) (frequency \(p\)) and \(a\) (frequency \(q\)):

\[ p + q = 1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1 \]

where \(p^2\) = frequency of \(AA\), \(2pq\) = frequency of \(Aa\), and \(q^2\) = frequency of \(aa\).

Any measurable deviation from expected Hardy–Weinberg values indicates evolutionary change. Five factors disturb genetic equilibrium:

  1. Gene Migration / Gene Flow: Movement of individuals adds alleles to the new population and removes them from the original population; repeated migration constitutes gene flow.
  2. Genetic Drift: Random, chance-driven changes in allele frequencies, especially pronounced in small populations. When a small drifted sample colonises a new area and diverges into a new species, the original drifted group acts as founders—called the founder effect.
  3. Mutation: Sudden heritable alterations creating new alleles.
  4. Genetic Recombination: Reshuffling of alleles during gametogenesis (crossing over and independent assortment).
  5. Natural Selection: Differential survival and reproduction of heritable variants.

Worked Example 1 (Hypothetical Population): Calculating Genotype Frequencies

Assumption: Consider a hypothetical, randomly mating population of 1,000 beetles in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium where black shell (\(B\)) is completely dominant over brown shell (\(b\)). Suppose 160 beetles have brown shells.

Solution:

  1. Recessive phenotype (\(bb\)) frequency: \( q^2 = \dfrac{160}{1000} = 0.16 \).
  2. Recessive allele (\(b\)) frequency: \( q = \sqrt{0.16} = 0.4 \).
  3. Dominant allele (\(B\)) frequency: \( p = 1 - q = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6 \).
  4. Heterozygous (\(Bb\)) frequency: \( 2pq = 2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.48 \) (480 beetles).
  5. Homozygous dominant (\(BB\)) frequency: \( p^2 = (0.6)^2 = 0.36 \) (360 beetles).
Worked Example 2 (Hypothetical Population): Finding Carrier Frequency

Assumption: In a hypothetical isolated human population in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, the frequency of an autosomal recessive allele \(a\) is \(0.05\). Calculate the percentage of heterozygous carriers (\(Aa\)) and homozygous dominant individuals (\(AA\)).

Solution:

  1. Given \(q = 0.05\), we obtain \(p = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95\).
  2. Carrier frequency (\(Aa\)): \( 2pq = 2 \times 0.95 \times 0.05 = 0.095 \), or 9.5%.
  3. Homozygous dominant frequency (\(AA\)): \( p^2 = (0.95)^2 = 0.9025 \), or 90.25%.

6. Brief Account of Plant, Vertebrate & Human Evolution

6.1 Major Geological Milestones

  • 2,000 mya: First cellular forms appeared; some evolved pigments capable of splitting water to release \(\mathrm{O_2}\) (similar to the light reaction of photosynthesis).
  • 500 mya: Invertebrates were active.
  • 350 mya: Jawless fishes evolved; lobefins (stout-finned fishes capable of moving onto land and returning to water; e.g., Coelacanth, rediscovered in South Africa in 1938) evolved into the first amphibians.
  • 320 mya: Seaweeds and early plants existed. Plants were the first organisms to invade land, preceding animals.
  • 200 mya: Reptiles dominated; some returned to water to become fish-like reptiles (e.g., Ichthyosaurus). Giant ferns (pteridophytes) formed extensive forests that slowly turned into coal deposits.
  • 65 mya: Dinosaurs (including the 20-foot-tall Tyrannosaurus rex) suddenly disappeared, likely due to climatic changes or evolution into birds, allowing small, viviparous, shrew-like mammals to diversify.
Key Lineages & Continental Drift

Early extinct reptiles split into two major branches: Sauropsids (giving rise to turtles, lizards, snakes, tuataras, crocodiles, dinosaurs, and birds) and Synapsids (pelycosaurs → therapsids → mammals). In plants, chlorophyte and tracheophyte ancestors led to Rhynia-type plants and Psilophyton, radiating into bryophytes, lycopods, sphenopsids, ferns, gymnosperms (cycads, conifers, ginkgos, gnetales), and angiosperms. Due to continental drift, when South America joined North America, native South American mammals were overridden by North American fauna, whereas Australian pouched mammals survived due to geographic isolation.

6.2 Origin and Evolution of Man

Dryopithecus & Ramapithecus ~15 mya — Hairy, walked like gorillas and chimpanzees. Dryopithecus was more ape-like; Ramapithecus was more man-like.

7. NEET UG Practice Questions & Worked Solutions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In S.L. Miller's 1953 spark-discharge experiment simulating primitive Earth conditions, which combination of gases was circulated in the closed flask at 800 °C?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), and water vapour
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), and water vapour
  • (C) \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\), and water vapour
  • (D) \(\mathrm{CH_4}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_3}\), and \(\mathrm{NH_3}\)
Answer: (B) Miller simulated the early reducing atmosphere (free of molecular oxygen \(\mathrm{O_2}\)) using methane (\(\mathrm{CH_4}\)), hydrogen (\(\mathrm{H_2}\)), ammonia (\(\mathrm{NH_3}\)), and water vapour at 800 °C, yielding amino acids.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following pairs represents analogous structures resulting from convergent evolution?

  • (A) Thorns of Bougainvillea and tendrils of Cucurbita
  • (B) Forelimbs of a whale and a bat
  • (C) Eye of an octopus and eye of a mammal
  • (D) Hearts of a bird and a human
Answer: (C) The eyes of an octopus and a mammal have different embryonic origins and anatomical designs yet perform the same function (vision), making them analogous. Options (A), (B), and (D) are homologous structures showing divergent evolution.

Practice Question 4 (Assertion–Reason MCQ)

Assertion (A): Evolution of antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains within a few months of antibiotic usage demonstrates that evolution is a stochastic process.
Reason (R): Anthropogenic actions select pre-existing advantageous variants that arise through chance mutations rather than directed adaptation.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) Both (A) and (R) are false.
Answer: (A) Rapid emergence of antibiotic resistance occurs because antibiotics select pre-existing random mutants; thus evolution is stochastic (chance-based) rather than deterministic.
Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

According to Hugo de Vries, speciation is driven by saltation, which specifically refers to:

  • (A) Gradual accumulation of small, directional variations
  • (B) Single-step large, random, directionless mutation

  • (D) Non-random mating in a small isolated population
Answer: (B) De Vries proposed that mutations are random and directionless, and a single-step large mutation (saltation) causes speciation, contrasting with Darwin's small, directional variations.

Practice Question 7 (Numerical MCQ — Hardy–Weinberg)

In a hypothetical plant population in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, 9% of individuals show the recessive white-flower phenotype (\(rr\)). What percentage of the population is expected to be heterozygous (\(Rr\))?

  • (A) 21%
  • (B) 42%
  • (C) 49%
  • (D) 51%
Answer: (B) Given \(q^2 = 0.09\), we get \(q = \sqrt{0.09} = 0.3\). Since \(p + q = 1\), \(p = 0.7\). Heterozygote frequency is \(2pq = 2 \times 0.7 \times 0.3 = 0.42\), or 42%.
Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When natural selection favours individuals with peripheral character values at both ends of a phenotypic distribution curve, resulting in two peaks, the selection is termed:

  • (A) Stabilising selection
  • (B) Directional selection
  • (C) Disruptive selection
  • (D) Balancing selection
Answer: (C) Disruptive selection favours both extremes over the intermediate mean, splitting the distribution into two distinct peaks.
Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which statement accurately describes the founder effect?

  • (A) Repeated gene migration between two large populations
  • (B) Extreme genetic drift where a small drifted subgroup establishes a new population with markedly altered allele frequencies
  • (C) Selection of the mean phenotype in a stable environment
  • (D) Crossing over during meiosis I in a large population
Answer: (B) When a small sample of a population drifts away and its allele frequency differs so sharply that it forms a new species, the original drifted group acts as founders (founder effect).

8. Common Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Darwin vs. de Vries: Darwinian variations are small and directional; de Vries's mutations are large, random, and directionless (saltation).
  • Sweet Potato vs. Potato: Sweet potato is a modified root; potato is a modified stem. Because they have different origins but the same storage function, they are analogous (not homologous).
  • Bougainvillea Thorn vs. Cucurbita Tendril: Both arise from axillary buds (stem modifications) for different functions (protection vs. climbing), making them homologous.
  • Hardy–Weinberg \(q\) vs. \(q^2\): Always check whether a question gives the allele frequency (\(q\)) or the recessive phenotype/genotype frequency (\(q^2\)) before taking square roots.
  • Haeckel vs. von Baer: Haeckel proposed embryonic recapitulation of adult stages; von Baer disproved it.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • Universe: ~13.8 bya | Earth: ~4.5 bya | First life: ~4 bya | First non-cellular life: ~3 bya | First cellular life: ~2,000 mya.
  • Invertebrates: ~500 mya | Jawless fish & Lobefins: ~350 mya | Seaweeds/early plants: ~320 mya | Ichthyosaurus: ~200 mya | Dinosaur extinction: ~65 mya.
  • Dryopithecus (ape-like) & Ramapithecus (man-like): ~15 mya | Homo habilis: 650–800 cc | Homo erectus: 900 cc | Neanderthal: 1,400 cc | Cave art: ~18,000 yrs ago | Agriculture: ~10,000 yrs ago.

9. Concise Final Recap

Evolutionary biology unifies the physical history of Earth with the diversification of life. Chemical evolution in a reducing atmosphere preceded the origin of cellular life, which then diversified through branching descent and natural selection. Comparative anatomy, fossils, biochemistry, and modern examples like industrial melanism and antibiotic resistance confirm that populations evolve whenever genetic equilibrium—described by \(p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1\)—is perturbed by mutation, recombination, gene flow, genetic drift, or natural selection.
