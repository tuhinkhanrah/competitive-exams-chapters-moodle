2. Chemical Architecture of Nucleic Acids & DNA Packaging

2.1 Building Blocks: Nucleosides and Nucleotides

Living cells contain two types of nucleic acids: deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA). Both are long polymers of nucleotide monomers.

  • Three Components of a Nucleotide: (1) a nitrogenous base, (2) a pentose sugar (ribose in RNA; 2′-deoxyribose in DNA), and (3) a phosphate group.
  • Nitrogenous Bases:
    • Purines (double-ring): Adenine (A) and Guanine (G) — found in both DNA and RNA.
    • Pyrimidines (single-ring): Cytosine (C) is common to both; Thymine (T, chemically 5-methyl uracil) occurs in DNA, whereas Uracil (U) replaces thymine in RNA.
  • Linkages: A nitrogenous base attaches to the 1′ carbon of the pentose sugar via an N-glycosidic linkage to form a nucleoside (e.g., adenosine, deoxyadenosine, guanosine, cytidine, uridine, deoxythymidine). Adding a phosphate group to the 5′-OH of a nucleoside via a phosphoester linkage yields a nucleotide. Adjacent nucleotides join through 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkages, creating a sugar–phosphate backbone with a free 5′-phosphate at one terminus and a free 3′-OH at the other.

2.2 The DNA Double Helix & Chargaff's Rules

DNA was first isolated in 1869 as an acidic nuclear substance named nuclein by Friedrich Meischer. In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick integrated X-ray diffraction data from Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin with Erwin Chargaff's base-composition rules to propose the double-helix model.

Structural Feature Key Quantitative / Molecular Detail
Strand Polarity Two polynucleotide chains run antiparallel: one in 5′→3′ direction, the other in 3′→5′ direction.
Base Pairing & H-Bonds Purine always pairs with pyrimidine: A = T (2 hydrogen bonds) and G ≡ C (3 hydrogen bonds), maintaining uniform helix diameter.
Chargaff's Equivalence In dsDNA, \([\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{T}]\), \([\mathrm{G}] = [\mathrm{C}]\), and \(\frac{\mathrm{A}+\mathrm{G}}{\mathrm{T}+\mathrm{C}} = 1\).
Helical Geometry Right-handed coil; pitch = \(3.4\,\mathrm{nm}\) (\(34\,\mathrm{Å}\)); ~\(10\,\mathrm{bp}\) per turn; distance between adjacent bp = \(0.34\,\mathrm{nm}\) (\(3.4\,\mathrm{Å}\)).
Stabilizing Forces Hydrogen bonds between complementary bases plus hydrophobic base-stacking interactions between adjacent base-pair planes.
Characteristic Genome Sizes (High-Yield NEET Facts)
  • Bacteriophage \(\phi \times 174\): \(5386\) nucleotides (single-stranded DNA)
  • Bacteriophage \(\lambda\): \(48{,}502\,\mathrm{bp}\)
  • Escherichia coli: \(4.6 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{bp}\) (approximate physical length \(1.36\,\mathrm{mm}\))
  • Human haploid genome: \(3.3 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{bp}\) (diploid: \(6.6 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{bp}\), physical length \(\approx 2.2\,\mathrm{m}\))

2.3 Packaging of DNA into Chromatin

A diploid human cell contains roughly \(2.2\,\mathrm{m}\) of DNA packed inside a nucleus of diameter \(\approx 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{µm}\)).

  • Prokaryotes (e.g., E. coli): Lack a membrane-bound nucleus. Negatively charged DNA is organized into large loops held by positively charged proteins in a central region called the nucleoid.
  • Eukaryotes: Negatively charged DNA wraps around a positively charged histone octamer (eight histone molecules rich in basic amino acids lysine and arginine) to form a nucleosome. Each typical nucleosome encloses ~\(200\,\mathrm{bp}\) of DNA helix, stabilized by linker histone H1. Under an electron microscope, repeating nucleosomes appear as a "beads-on-string" chromatin fiber.
  • Higher-Order Condensation: Coiling chromatin fibers into metaphase chromosomes requires additional Non-Histone Chromosomal (NHC) proteins.
  • Euchromatin vs. Heterochromatin: Loosely packed, lightly staining, transcriptionally active chromatin is euchromatin; densely packed, darkly staining, transcriptionally inactive chromatin is heterochromatin.

3. Proving DNA is the Genetic Material & The RNA World

3.1 Landmark Experiments

Experiment & Scientists Model System Key Observation & Conclusion
Transforming Principle
Frederick Griffith (1928)		 Streptococcus pneumoniae (S-strain: smooth, mucous coat, virulent; R-strain: rough, non-virulent) & mice Heat-killed S + live R killed mice; live S bacteria were recovered. A chemical transforming principle passed from dead S to live R, though its biochemical identity remained unknown.

Alfred Hershey & Martha Chase (1952)		 T2 Bacteriophage & E. coli; radioactive \(^{32}\mathrm{P}\) (labels DNA) and \(^{35}\mathrm{S}\) (labels protein coat) After infection, blending, and centrifugation: \(^{32}\mathrm{P}\) appeared inside bacterial cells (pellet), while \(^{35}\mathrm{S}\) remained in the supernatant. Proved DNA, not protein, enters the host.

3.2 Properties of Genetic Material: DNA vs. RNA

Any molecule serving as genetic material must: (1) replicate accurately, (2) remain chemically and structurally stable, (3) permit slow mutations for evolution, and (4) express itself as Mendelian traits.

  • Why DNA is Superior for Storage: RNA carries a reactive 2′-OH group on every ribose sugar, making it labile and catalytic (ribozyme activity). DNA lacks 2′-OH and uses thymine instead of uracil, conferring greater chemical stability and enabling error repair.
  • Why RNA Mutates Faster: Because RNA is less stable, RNA-genome viruses (such as Tobacco Mosaic Virus and Q\(\beta\) bacteriophage) mutate and evolve rapidly.
  • The RNA World Hypothesis: RNA was the first genetic material, acting both as information carrier and catalyst for primitive metabolism, splicing, and translation. DNA evolved later from RNA as a more stable storage archive.
Central Dogma of Molecular Biology (Francis Crick)

Genetic information normally flows unidirectionally: DNA \(\xrightarrow{\text{transcription}}\) mRNA \(\xrightarrow{\text{translation}}\) Protein, with DNA also templating its own replication. In retroviruses (RNA viruses), information flows in reverse (RNA \(\rightarrow\) DNA) via reverse transcription.

4. Semiconservative DNA Replication & Enzymatic Machinery

4.1 Experimental Proof: Meselson & Stahl (1958)

Watson and Crick predicted that each parental strand serves as a template for a new complementary strand, producing daughter duplexes with one old and one new strand (semiconservative replication). Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl proved this in E. coli (generation time = \(20\,\mathrm{min}\)):

  1. Cells were grown for many generations in heavy nitrogen medium (\(^{15}\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\)), yielding heavy DNA (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{15}\mathrm{N}\)). Note: \(^{15}\mathrm{N}\) is a heavy (non-radioactive) isotope separated from \(^{14}\mathrm{N}\) by cesium chloride (CsCl) equilibrium density gradient centrifugation.
  2. Cells were transferred to normal \(^{14}\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}\) medium and sampled across generations:
    • Generation I (\(20\,\mathrm{min}\)): 100% hybrid/intermediate density DNA (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)).
    • Generation II (\(40\,\mathrm{min}\)): 50% hybrid DNA (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)) and 50% light DNA (\(^{14}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)).
    • Generation \(n\): Out of \(2^n\) DNA duplexes, exactly \(2\) remain hybrid and \(2^n - 2\) are light.

In the same year (1958), Taylor and colleagues used radioactive thymidine in Vicia faba (faba beans) to demonstrate semiconservative replication in eukaryotic chromosomes.

4.2 Enzymology & The Replication Fork

  • Origin of Replication (ori): Replication initiates at a specific DNA sequence, not randomly. Recombinant DNA plasmids require an ori to replicate inside host cells.
  • Dual Role of dNTPs: Deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dATP, dGTP, dCTP, dTTP) act both as substrates and as energy donors (via cleavage of their two terminal high-energy phosphate bonds).
  • DNA-dependent DNA Polymerase: Catalyzes nucleotide polymerization strictly in the 5′→3′ direction. In E. coli, it polymerizes ~\(2000\,\mathrm{bp/s}\), duplicating \(4.6 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{bp}\) in ~\(18\,\mathrm{min}\) with high proofreading fidelity.
  • Leading vs. Lagging Strand at the Replication Fork: Because unwinding the entire helix at once is energetically prohibitive, replication proceeds at a local Y-shaped replication fork:
    • On the 3′→5′ template strand, synthesis is continuous (leading strand, 5′→3′).
    • On the 5′→3′ template strand, synthesis is discontinuous (lagging strand, 5′→3′ fragments), and the fragments are later sealed by DNA ligase.
  • Cell-Cycle Coordination: In eukaryotes, DNA replication occurs during the S-phase. DNA replication without subsequent cell division leads to polyploidy.

5. Transcription & Eukaryotic RNA Processing

5.1 Why Only One DNA Strand is Transcribed

Transcription copies a specific DNA segment into RNA using DNA-dependent RNA polymerase (which polymerizes in the 5′→3′ direction, pairing A with U). Both strands are never copied simultaneously from the same region because: (1) they would produce two RNA molecules with different sequences coding for two different proteins, complicating regulation; and (2) the two complementary RNAs would hybridize into double-stranded RNA, blocking translation.

5.2 The Transcription Unit & Strand Naming

  • Template Strand (3′→5′): Read by RNA polymerase to synthesize complementary RNA.
  • Coding Strand (5′→3′): Not transcribed, yet its base sequence matches the newly made RNA (except T replaces U). All positional references (upstream/downstream) use the coding strand's polarity.
  • Promoter: Located at the 5′ (upstream) end of the coding strand; binds RNA polymerase and defines which strand acts as template.
  • Terminator: Located at the 3′ (downstream) end of the coding strand; signals transcription termination.

5.3 Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Transcription

• Pol I: rRNAs (28S, 18S, 5.8S)
• Pol II: hnRNA (pre-mRNA)
• Pol III: tRNA, 5S rRNA, snRNAs

6. The Genetic Code, Mutations & Translation

6.1 Deciphering & Features of the Genetic Code

George Gamow deduced that with 4 RNA bases coding for 20 amino acids, each codon must be a triplet (\(4^3 = 64\) codons). Har Gobind Khorana synthesized defined RNA homopolymers and copolymers; Marshall Nirenberg used a cell-free translation system to decode them; and Severo Ochoa's enzyme (polynucleotide phosphorylase) polymerized RNA in a template-independent manner.

  • Triplet & Unambiguous: 61 sense codons specify amino acids; 3 stop/nonsense codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) terminate translation. Each codon specifies only one amino acid.
  • Degenerate: Most amino acids are encoded by more than one codon (exceptions: Met = AUG, Trp = UGG).
  • Contiguous (Commaless): Read continuously without punctuation.
  • Nearly Universal: Identical from bacteria to humans (e.g., UUU = Phe), with minor exceptions in mitochondrial codons and certain protozoans.
  • Dual Role of AUG: Codes for Methionine (Met) and acts as the initiator codon.

6.2 Point Mutations vs. Frameshift Mutations

  • Point Mutation (Single-Base Substitution): Example — in sickle-cell anemia, a single base-pair change in the \(\beta\)-globin gene converts a glutamate codon to a valine codon.
  • Frameshift Insertion/Deletion: Inserting or deleting 1 or 2 bases shifts the entire downstream triplet reading frame. Inserting or deleting 3 bases (or a multiple of 3) adds or removes one or more complete amino acids while preserving the downstream reading frame.

6.3 tRNA (The Adapter Molecule) & Translation

Francis Crick proposed an adapter molecule to link codons to amino acids. tRNA (earlier called soluble RNA or sRNA) has a 2D cloverleaf secondary structure and a compact 3D inverted-L shape. Its anticodon loop pairs with the mRNA codon, while its 3′ acceptor end binds a specific amino acid. There are no tRNAs for stop codons.

  1. Charging (Aminoacylation) of tRNA: Amino acids are activated using ATP and linked to their cognate tRNAs.
  2. Ribosome Binding & Initiation: The ribosome (structural rRNAs + ~80 proteins) binds mRNA at the start codon (AUG), recognized by initiator tRNA. Flanking Untranslated Regions (5′ UTR and 3′ UTR) enhance translational efficiency.
  3. Elongation & Ribozyme Catalysis: Charged tRNAs enter the large subunit's sites sequentially. In bacteria, 23S rRNA acts as a ribozyme (peptidyl transferase) catalyzing peptide bond formation.
  4. Termination: When a stop codon enters, a release factor binds and releases the completed polypeptide.

7. Regulation of Gene Expression: The Lac Operon

In eukaryotes, gene expression is regulated at four levels: (1) transcriptional, (2) processing/splicing, (3) mRNA transport from nucleus to cytoplasm, and (4) translational. In prokaryotes, control of transcriptional initiation predominates via operons—clusters of polycistronic structural genes sharing a common promoter and operator.

7.1 Components & Logic of the Lac Operon (Jacob & Monod)

  • Regulatory gene (i): Derived from inhibitor (not inducer); constitutively expresses the lac repressor protein.
  • Promoter (p) & Operator (o): RNA polymerase binds p; the repressor binds o to block transcription.
  • Structural genes:
    • lac z \(\rightarrow\) \(\beta\)-galactosidase (hydrolyzes lactose into galactose + glucose).
    • lac y \(\rightarrow\) Permease (increases cell permeability to \(\beta\)-galactosides; a basal low-level expression is always present so initial lactose can enter).
    • lac a \(\rightarrow\) Transacetylase.
  • Inducer (Lactose / Allolactose): In the absence of lactose, active repressor binds o and halts transcription (negative regulation). In the presence of lactose, the inducer binds and inactivates the repressor, allowing RNA polymerase to transcribe z, y, a. Once lactose is consumed, free repressor re-binds o and shuts the operon off. Note: glucose or galactose cannot act as inducers.

8. Human Genome Project (HGP) & DNA Fingerprinting

8.1 Human Genome Project (1990–2003)

Launched in 1990 and completed in 2003 (with Chromosome 1 finished in May 2006), HGP was a 13-year international mega-project (~US $9 billion estimated cost; spawned Bioinformatics and addressed ELSI—Ethical, Legal, and Social Issues). Non-human models sequenced include bacteria, yeast, Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila, rice, and Arabidopsis.

  • Two Methodologies: (1) Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs)—identifying all genes expressed as RNA; (2) Sequence Annotation—sequencing the entire coding and non-coding genome, then assigning functions.
  • Cloning & Sequencing: Random DNA fragments were cloned in bacteria/yeast using BAC (Bacterial Artificial Chromosomes) and YAC (Yeast Artificial Chromosomes), sequenced by automated Sanger sequencers, and aligned computationally using overlapping regions.
  • Salient Features of the Human Genome:
    • Contains \(3164.7\,\mathrm{million\ bp}\); ~\(30{,}000\) total genes.
    • Average gene size = \(3000\) bases; largest known human gene = dystrophin (\(2.4\,\mathrm{million\ bases}\)).
    • \(99.9\%\) of nucleotide bases are identical across all humans; \(< 2\%\) of the genome codes for proteins.
    • Functions remain unknown for \(> 50\%\) of discovered genes.
    • Chromosome 1 has the most genes (\(2968\)); the Y chromosome has the fewest (\(231\)).
    • ~\(1.4\,\mathrm{million}\) SNPs (Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms) identified.

8.2 DNA Fingerprinting (Alec Jeffreys)

DNA fingerprinting exploits the \(0.1\%\) sequence variation among humans by analyzing repetitive DNA (separated from bulk DNA as satellite DNA peaks during density gradient centrifugation). DNA polymorphism refers to heritable mutations occurring at a locus with a population frequency \(> 0.01\) (more frequent in non-coding regions).

  • VNTR (Variable Number of Tandem Repeats): A class of mini-satellite DNA (\(0.1\) to \(20\,\mathrm{kb}\)) showing high copy-number polymorphism between paternal/maternal chromosomes and among individuals (identical only in monozygotic twins).
  • Six Steps of Classical Southern Blotting: (1) DNA isolation \(\rightarrow\) (2) Digestion with restriction endonucleases \(\rightarrow\) (3) Gel electrophoresis \(\rightarrow\) (4) Blotting onto nitrocellulose/nylon membrane \(\rightarrow\) (5) Hybridization with radiolabeled VNTR probe \(\rightarrow\) (6) Detection by autoradiography.
  • Sensitivity & Uses: PCR amplification allows profiling from a single cell. Used in forensics (identical across blood, hair follicle, skin, saliva, sperm), paternity testing, and biodiversity studies.

9. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Chargaff's Rule Calculation (Hypothetical Assumption)

Problem: Assume a hypothetical double-stranded DNA molecule extracted from a newly cultured bacterium contains \(18\%\) Guanine. Calculate the percentage of Adenine and total Pyrimidines in this DNA.

Solution: By Chargaff's equivalence in dsDNA, \([\mathrm{G}] = [\mathrm{C}]\) and \([\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{T}]\).

\[ [\mathrm{G}] = 18\% \implies [\mathrm{C}] = 18\% \] \[ [\mathrm{A}] + [\mathrm{T}] = 100\% - (18\% + 18\%) = 64\% \] \[ [\mathrm{A}] = \frac{64\%}{2} = 32\% \]

Total Pyrimidines \(= [\mathrm{C}] + [\mathrm{T}] = 18\% + 32\% = 50\%\). (In any dsDNA, purines \(= 50\%\) and pyrimidines \(= 50\%\).)

Worked Example 2: DNA Length & Nucleosome Count (Hypothetical Assumption)

Problem: Assume a hypothetical eukaryotic chromosome fragment has a DNA length of \(6.8 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}\). Using standard constants (\(0.34\,\mathrm{nm/bp}\) and \(200\,\mathrm{bp/nucleosome}\)), estimate the number of base pairs and nucleosomes in this fragment.

Solution:

\[ \text{Number of bp} = \frac{6.8 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}}{0.34 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m/bp}} = 2.0 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{bp} \] \[ \text{Number of nucleosomes} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{bp}}{200\,\mathrm{bp/nucleosome}} = 1.0 \times 10^{4}\text{ nucleosomes} \]
Worked Example 3: Meselson–Stahl Generation Ratio (Hypothetical Assumption)

Problem: Suppose heavy (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{15}\mathrm{N}\)) E. coli cells are transferred to \(^{14}\mathrm{N}\) medium and allowed to divide for \(80\,\mathrm{min}\) (assuming a \(20\,\mathrm{min}\) generation time). What percentage of DNA molecules will be hybrid (\(^{15}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\)) and what percentage will be light (\(^{14}\mathrm{N}/^{14}\mathrm{N}\))?

Solution: Number of generations \(n = \frac{80}{20} = 4\). Total DNA duplexes formed from one parental duplex \(= 2^4 = 16\).

Because replication is semiconservative, the two original \(^{15}\mathrm{N}\) strands remain intact in exactly \(2\) hybrid duplexes; the remaining \(16 - 2 = 14\) duplexes are completely light.

\[ \text{Hybrid} = \frac{2}{16} \times 100\% = 12.5\%, \qquad \text{Light} = \frac{14}{16} \times 100\% = 87.5\% \]
Worked Example 4: Predicting mRNA from a Transcription Unit (Hypothetical Assumption)

Problem: Assume a hypothetical transcription unit has the template strand 3′-TACCGTAGCAAT-5′. Write the sequence and polarity of the coding strand and the transcribed mRNA.

Solution:

  • Coding strand (antiparallel and complementary to template): 5′-ATGGCATCGTTA-3′
  • Transcribed mRNA (identical to coding strand, with U replacing T): 5′-AUGGCAUCGUUA-3′

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following statements correctly distinguishes a nucleoside from a nucleotide?

  • (1) A nucleoside contains a pentose sugar and phosphate, whereas a nucleotide lacks phosphate.
  • (2) A nucleoside has a nitrogenous base linked to 1′C of pentose sugar via N-glycosidic linkage, while a nucleotide additionally has a phosphate at 5′C via phosphoester linkage.
  • (3) A nucleoside is joined to the next nucleoside by a 3′–5′ phosphodiester bond.
  • (4) Uracil is a nucleoside, whereas uridine is a nitrogenous base.
Correct Answer: (2)

Explanation: A nucleoside = Nitrogenous base + Pentose sugar (N-glycosidic bond at 1′C). Adding a phosphate group at 5′C via a phosphoester linkage forms a nucleotide. Uracil is a base; uridine is its nucleoside.

Practice Question 2 (Numerical MCQ)

In a double-stranded DNA sample, if Cytosine constitutes \(28\%\) of the total bases, what is the percentage of Thymine in this DNA?

  • (1) \(28\%\)
  • (2) \(44\%\)
  • (3) \(22\%\)
  • (4) \(56\%\)
Correct Answer: (3)

Explanation: By Chargaff's rule, \([\mathrm{C}] = [\mathrm{G}] = 28\%\). Thus, \([\mathrm{C}] + [\mathrm{G}] = 56\%\). The remaining \(100\% - 56\% = 44\%\) is shared equally between Adenine and Thymine, giving \([\mathrm{T}] = 22\%\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty experiment, which enzyme treatment prevented the transformation of live R-strain Streptococcus pneumoniae into virulent S-strain?

  • (1) Protease
  • (2) RNase
  • (3) Lipase
  • (4) DNase
Correct Answer: (4)

Explanation: Proteases and RNases did not affect transformation, ruling out protein and RNA. Digestion with DNase destroyed DNA and inhibited transformation, proving DNA is the transforming principle.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Why is DNA preferred over RNA for long-term storage of genetic information in most organisms?

  • (1) DNA has a reactive 2′-OH group that speeds up catalysis.
  • (2) DNA lacks a 2′-OH group and has thymine instead of uracil, making it chemically more stable.
  • (3) RNA cannot replicate by complementary base pairing.
  • (4) DNA can directly translate into proteins without any intermediate.
Correct Answer: (2)

Explanation: The 2′-OH group in RNA makes it labile and reactive. DNA's deoxyribose sugar and thymine (5-methyl uracil) provide superior chemical stability and repair capability.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

During DNA replication at a replication fork, which statement is accurate?

  • (1) DNA polymerase synthesizes the leading strand in 3′→5′ direction.
  • (2) Both strands are synthesized continuously by DNA ligase.
  • (3) Synthesis is continuous on the 3′→5′ template and discontinuous on the 5′→3′ template.
  • (4) dNTPs provide substrates only, while ATP provides all polymerization energy.
Correct Answer: (3)

Explanation: DNA-dependent DNA polymerase always polymerizes in the 5′→3′ direction. Therefore, it moves continuously along the 3′→5′ template and discontinuously along the 5′→3′ template (fragments joined by DNA ligase). dNTPs provide both substrate and energy.

Practice Question 9 (Assertion–Reason)

Explanation: In bacteria, the 23S rRNA component of the large ribosomal subunit acts as a catalytic RNA (ribozyme) for peptide bond formation.

In the lac operon of E. coli, if a mutation inactivates the i gene so it cannot produce functional repressor protein, what will happen in the absence of lactose?

  • (1) The structural genes z, y, and a will remain permanently switched off.
  • (2) The structural genes z, y, and a will be constitutively transcribed.
  • (3) Only lac z will be transcribed, while y and a remain off.
  • (4) Glucose will act as an inducer to turn off the operon.
Correct Answer: (2)

Explanation: Without a functional repressor to bind the operator (o), RNA polymerase freely accesses the promoter and continuously transcribes z, y, and a even without lactose.

Practice Question 13 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In DNA fingerprinting, VNTRs are used as probes because they:

  • (1) Code for essential housekeeping enzymes in every tissue.
  • (2) Show high degree of copy-number polymorphism and vary from individual to individual (except monozygotic twins).
  • (3) Are identical in all members of a human population.
  • (4) Cannot be amplified by PCR.
Correct Answer: (2)

Explanation: Variable Number of Tandem Repeats (VNTRs) are non-coding mini-satellite sequences (\(0.1\text{–}20\,\mathrm{kb}\)) with high heritable polymorphism, generating unique banding patterns for every individual except identical twins.

11. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

Common NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid
  • \(^{15}\mathrm{N}\) is NOT radioactive: Meselson and Stahl used density gradient centrifugation (CsCl), whereas Hershey and Chase used radioactive \(^{32}\mathrm{P}\) and \(^{35}\mathrm{S}\).
  • Coding strand does NOT code for RNA: RNA polymerase reads the 3′→5′ template strand; the 5′→3′ coding strand is merely the reference strand whose sequence matches the RNA (with T in place of U).
  • lac i stands for inhibitor, not inducer: The inducer is lactose (or allolactose), never glucose or galactose.
  • Stop codons have no tRNAs: UAA, UAG, and UGA are recognized by protein release factors, not tRNA anticodons.

High-Yield Revision Checklist

  • DNA pitch = \(3.4\,\mathrm{nm}\), rise/bp = \(0.34\,\mathrm{nm}\), ~\(10\,\mathrm{bp}\)/turn; nucleosome = \(200\,\mathrm{bp}\) + histone octamer (Lys, Arg rich).
  • A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds); Chargaff: \([\mathrm{A}]=[\mathrm{T}]\), \([\mathrm{G}]=[\mathrm{C}]\).
  • Replication: \(5′\rightarrow 3′\) by DNA Pol; leading = continuous, lagging = discontinuous + DNA ligase.
  • Prokaryotic RNA Pol: \(\sigma\) initiates, \(\rho\) terminates; Eukaryotic: Pol I (rRNA), Pol II (hnRNA), Pol III (tRNA, 5S rRNA, snRNA).
  • Genetic code: 61 sense + 3 stop (UAA, UAG, UGA); AUG = Met + start; 23S rRNA = bacterial ribozyme.
  • HGP: \(3164.7\,\mathrm{Mbp}\), ~\(30{,}000\) genes, \(<2\%\) coding, \(99.9\%\) identical, \(1.4\,\mathrm{M}\) SNPs, Chr 1 (2968), Chr Y (231).

Concise Final Recap

DNA stores hereditary information as a stable, antiparallel, complementary double helix packaged around basic histone octamers into chromatin. Semiconservative replication copies the genome with high fidelity using 5′→3′ DNA polymerases, while transcription selectively copies one strand into RNA (requiring capping, splicing, and tailing in eukaryotes). The contiguous triplet genetic code is decoded by tRNA adapters on ribosomes to synthesize polypeptides, tightly governed by regulatory switches like the inducible lac operon. Modern genomics (HGP) and VNTR-based DNA fingerprinting harness this sequence architecture for medicine, forensics, and evolutionary biology.
