NEET UG Standard Biology — Human Physiology & Reproduction

Human Reproduction

Syllabus Context: Complete exam-aligned tutorial covering the Male and Female Reproductive Systems, Gametogenesis (Spermatogenesis and Oogenesis), Menstrual Cycle, Fertilisation and Implantation, Pregnancy and Embryonic Development, and Parturition and Lactation.

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Key Learning Objectives for NEET UG
  • Understand the anatomical organisation and histological structure of human male and female reproductive organs.
  • Trace the exact sequence of male and female accessory ducts and mammary gland ducts.
  • Compare the cytological stages, ploidy levels, chromosome numbers, and hormonal regulation of spermatogenesis and oogenesis.
  • Correlate pituitary gonadotropins (LH and FSH) and ovarian hormones (estrogens and progesterone) with ovarian and uterine events across the menstrual cycle.
  • Explain the mechanism of fertilisation, block to polyspermy, sex determination, cleavage, blastocyst formation, and implantation.
  • Identify placental functions, pregnancy-specific hormones, trimester-wise organogenesis milestones, the neuroendocrine foetal ejection reflex during parturition, and lactation.

Overview of Reproductive Events

Humans are sexually reproducing and viviparous (giving birth to live young). All major reproductive events occur after puberty in a strict chronological sequence:

Gametogenesis \(\rightarrow\) Insemination \(\rightarrow\) Fertilisation \(\rightarrow\) Zygote Formation \(\rightarrow\) Cleavage & Blastocyst Development \(\rightarrow\) Implantation \(\rightarrow\) Gestation (Embryonic Development) \(\rightarrow\) Parturition
  • Gametogenesis: Formation of haploid gametes—sperms in males and ovum in females.
  • Insemination: Transfer of sperms into the female genital tract during coitus.
  • Fertilisation: Fusion of male and female gametes in the ampullary region of the fallopian tube, leading to the formation of a diploid zygote.
  • Implantation: Formation and development of the blastocyst and its attachment/embedding into the uterine wall (endometrium).
  • Gestation: Embryonic development inside the uterus (lasting about 9 months in humans).
  • Parturition: Delivery of the baby.

Remarkable Difference Between Male and Female Reproductive Timing: Sperm formation (spermatogenesis) begins at puberty and continues even in old men, whereas the formation of the ovum (oogenesis) is initiated during the embryonic development stage in the female foetus and ceases in women around the age of 50 years (menopause).

2. The Male Reproductive System

The male reproductive system is located in the pelvis region. It consists of four major components:

  1. A pair of testes (primary sex organs)
  2. Male sex accessory ducts
  3. Male accessory glands
  4. External genitalia (penis)

2.1 Testes and Scrotum

  • Location and Thermoregulation: The testes are situated outside the abdominal cavity within a pouch called the scrotum. The scrotum maintains the low temperature of the testes—about 2–2.5 °C lower than the normal internal body temperature—which is essential for spermatogenesis.
  • Dimensions and Shape: In adults, each testis is oval in shape, with a length of about 4 to 5 cm and a width of about 2 to 3 cm.
  • Internal Compartments: Each testis is covered by a dense covering and contains about 250 compartments called testicular lobules.
  • Seminiferous Tubules: Each testicular lobule contains 1 to 3 highly coiled seminiferous tubules where sperms are produced.

2.2 Histology of the Seminiferous Tubule and Interstitial Space

Each seminiferous tubule is lined on its inside by two types of cells:

  • Male germ cells (Spermatogonia): Undergo mitotic multiplication followed by meiotic divisions, finally leading to sperm formation.
  • Sertoli cells: Provide nutrition to the developing germ cells.

The regions outside the seminiferous tubules are called interstitial spaces. They contain small blood vessels, interstitial cells (Leydig cells), and other immunologically competent cells. Leydig cells synthesise and secrete testicular hormones called androgens.

2.3 Male Sex Accessory Ducts

The accessory ducts store and transport sperms from the testis to the outside through the urethra. They include the rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis, and vas deferens.

Seminiferous Tubules \(\rightarrow\) Rete Testis \(\rightarrow\) Vasa Efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Epididymis \(\rightarrow\) Vas Deferens \(\rightarrow\) Ejaculatory Duct \(\rightarrow\) Urethra \(\rightarrow\) Urethral Meatus
  • The seminiferous tubules open into the vasa efferentia through the rete testis.
  • The vasa efferentia leave the testis and open into the epididymis, which is located along the posterior surface of each testis.
  • The epididymis leads to the vas deferens, which ascends to the abdomen and loops over the urinary bladder.
  • The vas deferens receives a duct from the seminal vesicle and opens into the urethra as the ejaculatory duct.
  • The urethra originates from the urinary bladder and extends through the penis to its external opening called the urethral meatus.

2.4 External Genitalia and Accessory Glands

  • Penis: The male external genitalia, made up of special tissue that helps in erection to facilitate insemination. The enlarged end of the penis is called the glans penis, which is covered by a loose fold of skin called the foreskin.
  • Male Accessory Glands: Include paired seminal vesicles, a single prostate gland, and paired bulbourethral glands.
  • Seminal Plasma: Secretions of these accessory glands constitute the seminal plasma, which is rich in fructose, calcium, and certain enzymes.
  • Lubrication: The secretions of the bulbourethral glands also help in the lubrication of the penis.

3. The Female Reproductive System

The female reproductive system is located in the pelvic region and consists of:

  1. A pair of ovaries (primary sex organs)
  2. Female accessory ducts: a pair of oviducts (fallopian tubes), a single uterus, cervix, and vagina
  3. External genitalia
  4. A pair of mammary glands (integrated structurally and functionally to support ovulation, fertilisation, pregnancy, birth, and child care)

3.1 Ovaries

  • Functions: Produce the female gamete (ovum) and several steroid hormones (ovarian hormones: estrogens and progesterone).
  • Location & Size: Located one on each side of the lower abdomen. Each ovary is about 2 to 4 cm in length and is connected to the pelvic wall and uterus by ligaments.
  • Histology: Covered by a thin epithelium which encloses the ovarian stroma. The stroma is divided into two zones: a peripheral cortex and an inner medulla.

3.2 Oviducts (Fallopian Tubes), Uterus, and Vagina

  • Fallopian Tubes (10–12 cm long): Extend from the periphery of each ovary to the uterus. Each tube has three anatomical regions:
    • Infundibulum: Funnel-shaped part closer to the ovary. Its edges possess finger-like projections called fimbriae, which help in the collection of the ovum after ovulation.
    • Ampulla: A wider part of the oviduct leading from the infundibulum (site of fertilisation).
    • Isthmus: The last part of the oviduct having a narrow lumen; it joins the uterus.
  • Uterus (Womb): Single, shaped like an inverted pear, supported by ligaments attached to the pelvic wall. It opens into the vagina through a narrow cervix. The cavity of the cervix is called the cervical canal.
  • Birth Canal: The cervical canal along with the vagina forms the birth canal.
  • Three Tissue Layers of the Uterine Wall:
    1. Perimetrium: External thin membranous layer.
    2. Myometrium: Middle thick layer of smooth muscle; exhibits strong contractions during delivery of the baby (parturition).
    3. Endometrium: Inner glandular layer that lines the uterine cavity; undergoes cyclical changes during the menstrual cycle and is the site of blastocyst implantation.

3.3 Female External Genitalia

  • Mons pubis: A cushion of fatty tissue covered by skin and pubic hair.
  • Labia majora: Fleshy folds of tissue that extend down from the mons pubis and surround the vaginal opening.
  • Labia minora: Paired folds of tissue located under the labia majora.
  • Hymen: A membrane that often partially covers the opening of the vagina.
  • Clitoris: A tiny finger-like structure that lies at the upper junction of the two labia minora, above the urethral opening.
Important NCERT Concept Note on the Hymen

The hymen is often torn during the first coitus (intercourse). However, it can also be broken by a sudden fall or jolt, insertion of a vaginal tampon, or active participation in sports like horseback riding and cycling. In some women, the hymen persists even after coitus. Therefore, the presence or absence of the hymen is not a reliable indicator of virginity or sexual experience.

3.4 Mammary Glands

A functional mammary gland is characteristic of all female mammals. They are paired structures (breasts) containing glandular tissue and a variable amount of fat.

  • The glandular tissue of each breast is divided into 15–20 mammary lobes.
  • Each lobe contains clusters of cells called alveoli.
  • The cells of alveoli secrete milk, which is stored in the cavities (lumens) of the alveoli.
Mammary Alveoli (lumen stores milk) \(\rightarrow\) Mammary Tubules \(\rightarrow\) Mammary Duct \(\rightarrow\) Mammary Ampulla \(\rightarrow\) Lactiferous Duct \(\rightarrow\) Nipple (milk expressed out)

4. Gametogenesis: Spermatogenesis & Oogenesis

Gametogenesis is the process by which the primary sex organs—the testes in males and the ovaries in females—produce gametes (sperms and ovum, respectively).

4.1 Spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis begins at puberty in the seminiferous tubules:

  1. Multiplication & Growth: Immature male germ cells called spermatogonia (singular: spermatogonium, diploid \(2n = 46\) chromosomes) present on the inside wall of seminiferous tubules multiply by mitotic division. Some spermatogonia grow and differentiate into primary spermatocytes (\(2n = 46\)).
  2. First Meiotic Division (Reduction Division): A primary spermatocyte completes Meiosis I to form two equal, haploid cells called secondary spermatocytes (\(n = 23\) chromosomes each).
  3. Second Meiotic Division: Each secondary spermatocyte undergoes Meiosis II to produce four equal, haploid spermatids (\(n = 23\) chromosomes each).
  4. Spermiogenesis: The transformation of non-motile haploid spermatids into spermatozoa (sperms).
  5. Spermiation: After spermiogenesis, sperm heads become embedded in the Sertoli cells and are finally released from the seminiferous tubules into the lumen by the process called spermiation.

4.2 Hormonal Control of Male Reproductive Functions

  • At puberty, there is a significant increase in the secretion of Gonadotropin Releasing Hormone (GnRH), a hypothalamic hormone.
  • GnRH acts on the anterior pituitary gland and stimulates the secretion of two gonadotropins:
    • Luteinising Hormone (LH): Acts on the Leydig cells and stimulates synthesis and secretion of androgens, which in turn stimulate the process of spermatogenesis and maintain the functions of male accessory ducts and glands.
    • Follicle Stimulating Hormone (FSH): Acts on the Sertoli cells and stimulates the secretion of some factors that help in the process of spermiogenesis.

4.3 Structure of a Human Sperm and Semen Parameters

A mature sperm is a microscopic structure enveloped as a whole by a plasma membrane. It consists of four parts: head, neck, middle piece, and tail.

  • Head: Contains an elongated haploid nucleus. The anterior portion of the nucleus is covered by a cap-like structure called the acrosome, which is filled with enzymes that help in fertilisation of the ovum.
  • Neck: Connects the head and the middle piece.
  • Middle Piece: Possesses numerous mitochondria, which produce energy for the movement of the tail, facilitating sperm motility essential for fertilisation.
  • Tail: Facilitates vigorous swimming motility.
Quantitative Fertility Parameters (High-Yield NEET Data)
  • During a single coitus, a human male ejaculates about 200 to 300 million sperms.
  • For normal fertility, at least 60% of sperms must have normal shape and size.
  • At least 40% of them (i.e., 40% of the total ejaculated sperms, or two-thirds of the normal-shaped ones) must show vigorous motility.
  • Secretions of the epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicle, and prostate are essential for maturation and motility of sperms. Seminal plasma + sperms = semen.

4.4 Oogenesis and Follicular Development

The process of formation of a mature female gamete is called oogenesis. Unlike spermatogenesis, it begins during embryonic life:

  • Foetal Life (Initiation): A couple of million gamete mother cells (oogonia, \(2n = 46\)) are formed within each foetal ovary during embryonic development. No more oogonia are formed and added after birth.
  • Primary Oocytes: Oogonia start division and enter into Prophase-I of the meiotic division and get temporarily arrested at that stage as primary oocytes (\(2n = 46\)).
  • Primary Follicle & Follicular Atresia: Each primary oocyte gets surrounded by a single layer of granulosa cells and is called a primary follicle. A large number of these follicles degenerate from birth to puberty; hence, at puberty only 60,000 to 80,000 primary follicles are left in each ovary.
  • Secondary Follicle: Primary follicles get surrounded by more layers of granulosa cells and a new theca, forming secondary follicles (no fluid-filled cavity yet).
  • Tertiary Follicle & Completion of Meiosis I: The secondary follicle transforms into a tertiary follicle, which is characterised by a fluid-filled cavity called the antrum. The theca layer organises into an inner theca interna and an outer theca externa. At this tertiary follicle stage, the primary oocyte grows in size and completes its first meiotic division.
    • Meiosis I is an unequal division resulting in a large haploid secondary oocyte (\(n = 23\)) and a tiny first polar body (\(n = 23\)).
    • The secondary oocyte retains the bulk of the nutrient-rich cytoplasm of the primary oocyte to nourish the early embryo after fertilisation.
  • Graafian Follicle & Zona Pellucida: The tertiary follicle matures into the Graafian follicle. The secondary oocyte forms a new non-cellular membrane called the zona pellucida surrounding it.
  • Ovulation: The Graafian follicle ruptures to release the secondary oocyte from the ovary.
  • Completion of Meiosis II: Triggered only when a sperm enters the secondary oocyte during fertilisation, producing a second polar body and a haploid ovum (ootid).

5. The Menstrual Cycle

The reproductive cycle in female primates (e.g., monkeys, apes, and human beings) is called the menstrual cycle.

  • Menarche: The first menstruation, which begins at puberty.
  • Menopause: Cessation of menstrual cycles in human beings around 50 years of age. Cyclic menstruation between menarche and menopause is an indicator of a normal reproductive phase.
  • Cycle Duration: Repeated at an average interval of about 28/29 days. One ovum is released (ovulation) during the middle of each menstrual cycle.

5.1 Four Phases of the Menstrual Cycle

  1. Menstrual Phase (Days 1 to 3–5):
    • Menstrual flow occurs and lasts for 3–5 days.
    • Results from the breakdown of the endometrial lining of the uterus and its blood vessels, forming liquid that comes out through the vagina.
    • Occurs only if the released ovum is not fertilised. Lack of menstruation may indicate pregnancy, or may be caused by stress, poor health, etc.
  2. Follicular Phase / Proliferative Phase (Days 6 to 13):
    • Primary follicles in the ovary grow to become a fully mature Graafian follicle.
    • Simultaneously, the endometrium of the uterus regenerates through proliferation.
    • Secretion of pituitary gonadotropins (LH and FSH) increases gradually, stimulating follicular development and the secretion of estrogens by the growing follicles.
  3. Ovulatory Phase (Middle of Cycle — about Day 14):
    • Both LH and FSH attain a peak level in the middle of the cycle (about the 14th day).
    • Rapid secretion of LH leading to its maximum level during mid-cycle is called the LH surge.
    • The LH surge induces rupture of the Graafian follicle and thereby the release of the ovum (secondary oocyte)—a process called ovulation.
  4. Luteal Phase / Secretory Phase (Days 15 to 28/29):
    • The remaining parts of the ruptured Graafian follicle transform into the corpus luteum.
    • The corpus luteum secretes large amounts of progesterone, which is essential for the maintenance of the endometrium (for implantation of the fertilised ovum and other events of pregnancy).
    • During pregnancy, all events of the menstrual cycle stop and there is no menstruation.
    • In the absence of fertilisation, the corpus luteum degenerates, causing disintegration of the endometrium and leading to menstruation, marking a new cycle.

6. Fertilisation, Sex Determination, Cleavage & Implantation

6.1 Site and Mechanism of Fertilisation

  • During copulation (coitus), semen is released by the penis into the vagina (insemination).
  • Motile sperms swim rapidly through the cervix, enter the uterus, and finally reach the ampullary region of the fallopian tube.
  • The ovum released by the ovary is also transported to the ampullary region, where fertilisation takes place.
  • Why not all copulations lead to fertilisation and pregnancy: Fertilisation can occur only if the ovum and sperms are transported simultaneously to the ampullary region.
  • Block to Polyspermy: During fertilisation, a sperm comes in contact with the zona pellucida layer of the ovum (which is surrounded externally by cells of the corona radiata and separated from the oocyte plasma membrane by the perivitelline space) and induces changes in the membrane that block the entry of additional sperms. This ensures that only one sperm can fertilise an ovum.
  • Acrosomal Action & Meiosis II Completion: Secretions of the acrosome help the sperm enter into the cytoplasm of the ovum through the zona pellucida and the plasma membrane. This induces the completion of the second meiotic division of the secondary oocyte (an unequal division), forming a second polar body and a haploid ovum (ootid).
  • Karyogamy: The haploid nucleus of the sperm (\(n = 23\)) and that of the ovum (\(n = 23\)) fuse together to form a diploid zygote (\(2n = 46\) chromosomes).

6.2 Chromosomal Sex Determination in Humans

  • The chromosome pattern in the human female is XX (homogametic: all haploid ova carry only the X sex chromosome).
  • The chromosome pattern in the human male is XY (heterogametic: 50% of sperms carry the X chromosome and the other 50% carry the Y chromosome).
  • After fusion of gametes, the zygote carries either XX (develops into a female baby) or XY (develops into a male baby) depending on whether a sperm carrying X or Y fertilised the ovum.
  • Therefore, scientifically, the sex of the baby is determined by the father and not by the mother.

6.3 Cleavage, Morula, Blastocyst, and Implantation

Zygote (1 cell, 2n) \(\xrightarrow{\text{Cleavage}}\) 2 \(\rightarrow\) 4 \(\rightarrow\) 8–16 Blastomeres (Morula) \(\rightarrow\) Blastocyst (Trophoblast + Inner Cell Mass) \(\rightarrow\) Implantation in Endometrium
  • Cleavage: Mitotic division starts as the zygote moves through the isthmus of the oviduct towards the uterus, forming 2, 4, 8, 16 daughter cells called blastomeres.
  • Morula: The embryo with 8 to 16 blastomeres is called a morula.
  • Blastocyst: The morula continues to divide and transforms into a blastocyst as it moves further into the uterus. The blastomeres in the blastocyst are arranged into:
    • Trophoblast (outer layer): Gets attached to the endometrium.
    • Inner cell mass (inner group of cells attached to trophoblast): Gets differentiated as the embryo.
  • Implantation: After attachment of the trophoblast, the uterine cells divide rapidly and cover the blastocyst. As a result, the blastocyst becomes embedded in the endometrium of the uterus. This is called implantation and it leads to pregnancy.

7. Pregnancy, Embryonic Development, Parturition & Lactation

7.1 Placenta Formation and Functions

  • Chorionic Villi & Placenta: After implantation, finger-like projections called chorionic villi appear on the trophoblast, surrounded by uterine tissue and maternal blood. The chorionic villi and uterine tissue become interdigitated with each other and jointly form a structural and functional unit between the developing embryo (foetus) and maternal body called the placenta.
  • Transport & Umbilical Cord: The placenta facilitates the supply of oxygen and nutrients to the embryo and the removal of carbon dioxide and excretory/waste materials produced by the embryo. The placenta is connected to the embryo through an umbilical cord, which helps in transporting substances to and from the embryo.
  • Endocrine Functions of the Placenta: Produces several hormones:
    1. Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG)
    2. Human Placental Lactogen (hPL)
    3. Estrogens
    4. Progestogens
  • Relaxin: In the later phase of pregnancy, a hormone called relaxin is secreted by the ovary.
High-Yield Endocrine Distinction in Pregnancy
  • Hormones produced in women ONLY during pregnancy: hCG, hPL, and relaxin.
  • Hormones whose levels increase several-fold in maternal blood during pregnancy: Estrogens, progestogens, cortisol, prolactin, thyroxine, etc. (Essential for supporting foetal growth, metabolic changes in the mother, and maintenance of pregnancy).

7.2 Germ Layers, Stem Cells, and Month-by-Month Foetal Milestones

  • Immediately after implantation, the inner cell mass (embryo) differentiates into an outer layer called ectoderm and an inner layer called endoderm. A mesoderm soon appears between the ectoderm and the endoderm. These three germ layers give rise to all tissues (organs) in adults.
  • The inner cell mass contains stem cells, which have the potency to give rise to all the tissues and organs.
Stage of Human Pregnancy (Gestation = 9 Months) Major Embryonic / Foetal Development Milestones
After 1 Month The embryo's heart is formed. (First sign of a growing foetus may be noticed by listening to the heart sound carefully through the stethoscope).
By the End of 2nd Month The foetus develops limbs and digits.
By the End of 12 Weeks (First Trimester) Most of the major organ systems are formed; for example, the limbs and external genital organs are well-developed.
During the 5th Month The first movements of the foetus and appearance of hair on the head are usually observed.
By the End of about 24 Weeks (End of Second Trimester) The body is covered with fine hair, eyelids separate, and eyelashes are formed.
By the End of 9 Months The foetus is fully developed and is ready for delivery.

7.3 Parturition (Childbirth)

  • The average duration of human pregnancy is about 9 months, called the gestation period.
  • Vigorous contraction of the uterus at the end of pregnancy causes expulsion/delivery of the foetus, called parturition.
  • Neuroendocrine Mechanism:
    • Signals for parturition originate from the fully developed foetus and the placenta, which induce mild uterine contractions called the foetal ejection reflex.
    • This reflex triggers the release of oxytocin from the maternal pituitary.
    • Oxytocin acts on the uterine muscle (myometrium) and causes stronger uterine contractions, which in turn stimulates further secretion of oxytocin.
    • This positive stimulatory reflex between uterine contraction and oxytocin secretion continues, resulting in stronger and stronger contractions that expel the baby out of the uterus through the birth canal, followed soon after by the expulsion of the placenta. (Hormones involved in parturition include cortisol, estrogens, and oxytocin; doctors inject oxytocin to induce delivery).

7.4 Lactation and Colostrum

  • The mammary glands of the female undergo differentiation during pregnancy and start producing milk towards the end of pregnancy by the process called lactation.
  • The milk produced during the initial few days of lactation is called colostrum, which contains several antibodies (predominantly IgA) absolutely essential to develop resistance for newborn babies.

8. Comprehensive Worked Examples & Conceptual Applications

Worked Example 1: Chromosome Numbers & Ploidy Across Reproductive Stages

Problem: State the ploidy level and exact chromosome number in the following human cells: (a) Spermatogonium, (b) Primary spermatocyte, (c) Secondary spermatocyte, (d) Spermatid, (e) Primary oocyte, (f) Secondary oocyte, (g) First polar body, and (h) Zygote.

Solution:

  • Diploid cells (\(2n = 46\) chromosomes): Spermatogonium (46), Primary spermatocyte (46), Primary oocyte (46), and Zygote (46).
  • Haploid cells (\(n = 23\) chromosomes): Secondary spermatocyte (23), Spermatid (23), Secondary oocyte (23), and First polar body (23).
Worked Example 2: Ovulation Counts in Twins and Multiparity

Problem: (a) How many eggs are released by a human ovary in a month? (b) How many eggs would have been released if the mother gave birth to identical twins? (c) Would your answer change if the twins born were fraternal? (d) How many eggs were released by the ovary of a female dog which gave birth to 6 puppies?

Solution:

  1. Normally, 1 egg (secondary oocyte) is released per month (during the middle of each menstrual cycle) by one of the human ovaries.
  2. For identical (monozygotic) twins: 1 egg was released and fertilised by 1 sperm to form a single zygote, which subsequently split into two embryos during early cleavage.
  3. For fraternal (dizygotic) twins: Yes, the answer changes—2 eggs were released simultaneously and fertilised by two separate sperms, forming two genetically distinct zygotes.
  4. For a female dog giving birth to 6 puppies: Dogs are multiparous mammals; therefore, at least 6 eggs were released and fertilised to produce 6 puppies.
Worked Example 3: Verification of Key Statements (True / False Analysis)

Problem: Evaluate whether each statement below is True or False, and provide the corrected scientific statement where False:

  1. Androgens are produced by Sertoli cells. \(\rightarrow\) False. Androgens are synthesised and secreted by Leydig cells (interstitial cells). Sertoli cells provide nutrition to the germ cells.
  2. Spermatozoa get nutrition from Sertoli cells. \(\rightarrow\) True.
  3. Leydig cells are found in the ovary. \(\rightarrow\) False. Leydig cells are found in the interstitial spaces of the testes (outside the seminiferous tubules).
  4. Leydig cells synthesise androgens. \(\rightarrow\) True.
  5. Oogenesis takes place in the corpus luteum. \(\rightarrow\) False. Oogenesis takes place in the ovary (within ovarian follicles); the corpus luteum is formed from the ruptured Graafian follicle after ovulation and secretes progesterone.
  6. Menstrual cycle ceases during pregnancy. \(\rightarrow\) True.
  7. Presence or absence of hymen is not a reliable indicator of virginity or sexual experience. \(\rightarrow\) True.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following represents the correct sequence of ducts involved in the transport of sperms from the site of their production to the exterior in human males?

  • (A) Seminiferous tubules \(\rightarrow\) Vasa efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Rete testis \(\rightarrow\) Epididymis \(\rightarrow\) Vas deferens \(\rightarrow\) Ejaculatory duct \(\rightarrow\) Urethra
  • (B) Seminiferous tubules \(\rightarrow\) Rete testis \(\rightarrow\) Vasa efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Epididymis \(\rightarrow\) Vas deferens \(\rightarrow\) Ejaculatory duct \(\rightarrow\) Urethra
  • (C) Seminiferous tubules \(\rightarrow\) Rete testis \(\rightarrow\) Epididymis \(\rightarrow\) Vasa efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Vas deferens \(\rightarrow\) Urethra \(\rightarrow\) Ejaculatory duct
  • (D) Testicular lobules \(\rightarrow\) Vasa efferentia \(\rightarrow\) Rete testis \(\rightarrow\) Vas deferens \(\rightarrow\) Epididymis \(\rightarrow\) Ejaculatory duct \(\rightarrow\) Urethra
Correct Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: Sperms are produced in the seminiferous tubules. The seminiferous tubules open into the vasa efferentia through the rete testis. The vasa efferentia leave the testis and open into the epididymis, which leads to the vas deferens. The vas deferens receives a duct from the seminal vesicle and opens into the urethra as the ejaculatory duct, finally exiting via the urethral meatus.

During a fertility evaluation, a human male ejaculates \(250\text{ million}\) sperms in a single coitus. For normal fertility, what is the minimum number of sperms that must have normal shape and size, and what is the minimum number of sperms that must show vigorous motility, respectively?

  • (A) \(150\text{ million}\) and \(100\text{ million}\)
  • (B) \(150\text{ million}\) and \(60\text{ million}\)
  • (C) \(100\text{ million}\) and \(150\text{ million}\)
  • (D) \(200\text{ million}\) and \(120\text{ million}\)
Correct Answer: (A)
Worked Solution: For normal fertility, at least \(60\%\) of the ejaculated sperms must have normal shape and size, and at least \(40\%\) of them must show vigorous motility. Given total sperms \(= 250\text{ million}\):
\(\text{Normal shape and size} = 60\%\text{ of } 250\text{ million} = 0.60 \times 250 = 150\text{ million}\).
\(\text{Vigorous motility} = 40\%\text{ of } 250\text{ million} = 0.40 \times 250 = 100\text{ million}\).

Practice Question 11 (Numerical / Ploidy Ratio MCQ)

How many secondary spermatocytes and how manyprimary oocytes are required to produce \(400\) spermatozoa and \(400\) functional ova in humans, respectively?

  • (A) \(100\) secondary spermatocytes and \(400\) primary oocytes
  • (B) \(200\) secondary spermatocytes and \(400\) primary oocytes
  • (C) \(200\) secondary spermatocytes and \(100\) primary oocytes
  • (D) \(400\) secondary spermatocytes and \(400\) primary oocytes
Correct Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: Each primary spermatocyte produces \(2\) secondary spermatocytes, and each secondary spermatocyte undergoes Meiosis II to form \(2\) spermatids (which transform into \(2\) spermatozoa). Thus, to produce \(400\) spermatozoa, \(\frac{400}{2} = 200\text{ secondary spermatocytes}\) are required (or \(100\) primary spermatocytes). In contrast, each primary oocyte undergoes unequal meiotic divisions to produce only \(1\) functional ovum (plus polar bodies). Therefore, \(400\) primary oocytes are required to yield \(400\) ova.

10. Common NEET Pitfalls & Quick Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Pitfall 1 — Primary vs. Secondary vs. Tertiary Follicle: The fluid-filled cavity (antrum) and differentiated theca layers (theca interna and theca externa) are characteristic of the tertiary follicle, not the secondary follicle. Also, the primary oocyte completes Meiosis I inside the tertiary follicle prior to ovulation.
  • Pitfall 2 — Follicle Count at Puberty: 60,000–80,000 primary follicles remain in each ovary at puberty—watch out for questions asking for the total count in both ovaries (120,000–160,000).
  • Pitfall 3 — Paired vs. Single Male Accessory Glands: Seminal vesicles (paired) and bulbourethral glands (paired) are in pairs, whereas the prostate gland is single.
  • Pitfall 4 — Site of Fertilisation: Fertilisation occurs specifically in the ampullary region of the fallopian tube. Cleavage begins as the zygote moves through the isthmus towards the uterus.
  • Pitfall 5 — Source of Relaxin: While hCG and hPL are secreted by the placenta, relaxin is secreted by the ovary in the later phase of pregnancy.
