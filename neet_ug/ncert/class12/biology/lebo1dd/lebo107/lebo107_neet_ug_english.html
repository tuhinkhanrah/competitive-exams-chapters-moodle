  • Contrast normal cellular regulation (contact inhibition) with oncogenic transformation, classifying tumor types, carcinogens, diagnostic modalities, and therapeutics.

1. Concept of Health and Disease

Historically, health was viewed as a state of body and mind characterized by an equilibrium of bodily humors. Early Greeks (such as Hippocrates) and the traditional Indian Ayurvedic system postulated that an individual possessing an excess of "blackbile" belonged to a hot temperament and experienced frequent fevers.

Experimental Refutation of the "Good Humor" Hypothesis

William Harvey, through his demonstration of blood circulation via experimental methodology and accurate recording of normal body temperature in individuals possessing blackbile using a clinical thermometer, completely disproved the reflective "good humor" hypothesis.

Modern biology demonstrates that the mind influences the immune system via the neural and endocrine systems, thereby directly affecting somatic health. Human health is governed by three major determinants:

  1. Genetic Disorders: Inherent defects and deficiencies inherited from parents from birth.
  2. Infections: Invasion and damage induced by pathogenic organisms.
  3. Lifestyle: Nutritional intake, hydration, rest, physical exercise, and behavioural habits.
Definition of Health (WHO Standard)

Health does not merely represent the absence of disease or physical fitness; it is defined as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being. Maintained health enhances worker productivity, longevity, and lowers maternal and infant mortality rates.

2. Common Infectious Pathogens in Humans

Any disease-causing biological agent is termed a pathogen. Pathogens have evolved specialized physiological adaptations to survive host internal environments, such as tolerating the acidic pH of the stomach and resisting luminal digestive enzymes.

Disease Causative Organism Class Primary Site / Target Key Symptoms & Diagnostic Confirmation
Typhoid Salmonella typhi Bacterium Small intestine; migrates to blood and organs Sustained high fever (39° to 40°C), stomach pain, constipation, intestinal perforation in severe cases. Confirmed by Widal test. Famous historical carrier: Mary Mallon ("Typhoid Mary").
Pneumonia Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae Bacterium Alveoli of lungs (fluid accumulation in air sacs) Chills, fever, cough, headache; severe stages show gray-to-bluish discoloration of lips and fingernails. Transmitted via inhaled aerosols/droplets or shared utensils.

Life Cycle of Malarial Parasite (Plasmodium)

Malaria is caused by the protozoan Plasmodium. Species include P. vivax, P. malariae, and Plasmodium falciparum. The latter is responsible for malignant malaria, which is frequently fatal. Plasmodium is a digenetic endoparasite requiring two hosts:

  • Human Host: Secondary/intermediate host supporting asexual reproduction (schizogony).
  • Female Anopheles Mosquito: Primary/definitive host supporting sexual reproduction (syngamy and sporogony); acts simultaneously as the biological vector.
Schematic Flow: Diphasic Life Cycle of Plasmodium across human and mosquito vectors.

Vector Control Interventions

  • Eradication of larval breeding grounds by eliminating water stagnation in residential areas and coolers.
  • Biological biocontrol: Introducing larvivorous fish such as Gambusia into ponds.
  • Chemical vector management: Systematic application of insecticides in ditches and marshy terrains.
  • Protective physical barriers: Wire-meshed fenestrations and bed nets to impede mosquito vectors (Anopheles, Culex, and Aedes—the vector for dengue and chikungunya).

3. Immunity and the Lymphoid System

Immunity represents the overall capability of the host organism to defend itself against and eradicate foreign antigenic agents. It is divided into two arms: Innate Immunity and Acquired Immunity.

3.1 Innate Immunity

Innate immunity is a non-specific defense mechanism present from birth, operating via four distinct barrier systems:

  • Physical Barriers: Intact skin (epidermal stratum corneum) prevents entry. Mucus coating lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and urogenital tracts entraps microbes.
  • Physiological Barriers: Hydrochloric acid in gastric juice (\(\mathrm{pH} \approx 1\text{--}2\)), saliva in the oral cavity, and lysozyme-rich tears prevent microbial proliferation.
  • Cellular Barriers: Phagocytic leukocytes in blood including Polymorphonuclear Leukocytes (PMNL-neutrophils), monocytes, Natural Killer (NK) lymphocytes, and tissue macrophages.
  • Cytokine Barriers: Virally infected cells synthesize and secrete glycoprotein factors called interferons, which protect neighbouring uninfected cells against viral replication.

3.2 Acquired Immunity

Acquired immunity is pathogen-specific and uniquely defined by immunological memory. The initial encounter with an antigen produces a primary response of low intensity. Re-exposure to the identical antigen elicits a rapid, amplified secondary or anamnestic response due to long-lived memory clones.

Humoral Immune Response (HIR)

Mediated by B-lymphocytes. B-cells differentiate into plasma cells that secrete circulating soluble glycoproteins termed antibodies into body fluids (blood and lymph). T-cells assist B-cells in antibody production but do not secrete antibodies directly.

Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI)

Mediated by T-lymphocytes. CMI is responsible for eliminating intracellular pathogens, contact hypersensitivity, and critically, the rejection of allografts (tissue/organ transplants). The immune system discriminates between "self" and "non-self". Immunosuppressive therapy is required lifelong after transplantation.

Antibody Structure (\(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{L}_2\))

Each antibody monomer comprises four polypeptide chains interconnected by covalent interchain disulfide bonds (\(-\mathrm{S}-\mathrm{S}-\)):

  • Two identical Light (\(\mathrm{L}\)) chains of shorter molecular length.
  • Two identical Heavy (\(\mathrm{H}\)) chains of longer length.
  • The N-terminal variable domains of both the heavy and light chains cooperatively form the antigen-binding site (paratope). Each monomer possesses two identical paratopes.
  • Major immunoglobulin classes in human serum: \(\mathrm{IgG}\), \(\mathrm{IgA}\), \(\mathrm{IgM}\), \(\mathrm{IgE}\), and \(\mathrm{IgD}\).
Structure of an Antibody Molecule (\(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{L}_2\)) showing variable antigen-binding paratopes and disulfide bridges.

3.3 Classification: Active vs. Passive Immunity

Parameter Active Immunity Passive Immunity
Mechanism Host's own cells synthesize antibodies in response to live or dead antigens/pathogens. Preformed, ready-made antibodies are directly administered into the recipient.
Kinetics Slow onset; takes considerable time to generate peak effective antibody titers. Provides immediate, instantaneous immunological protection.
Memory Induces formation of long-lived memory B and T lymphocytes. No immunological memory is generated; temporary protection.
Examples Natural infection; active vaccination (e.g., attenuated vaccines). Colostrum (yellowish mammary fluid rich in \(\mathrm{IgA}\)); placental transfer of maternal \(\mathrm{IgG}\) to the fetus; anti-tetanus serum (ATS); anti-venom in snakebites.

3.4 Hypersensitivity (Allergies) and Autoimmunity

Allergy: An exaggerated inflammatory immune response against otherwise harmless environmental antigens (allergens: pollen, dust mites, animal dander). Mediated by \(\mathrm{IgE}\) antibodies and the degranulation of mast cells, releasing vasoactive amines: histamine and serotonin. Symptoms include wheezing, dyspnea, sneezing, and conjunctivitis. Antidotes/symptomatic relief drugs: antihistamines, adrenaline, and corticosteroids.

Autoimmunity: Breakdown of immunological tolerance or self-tolerance mechanisms wherein the immune system attacks host self-tissues. Cause: Genetic factors and idiopathic triggers. Prototypic example: Rheumatoid arthritis.

3.5 Human Lymphoid Organs

  • Primary Lymphoid Organs: Bone marrow and Thymus. Sites of lymphocytic origin, proliferation, and phenotypic maturation into antigen-sensitive immunocompetent cells. The thymus is prominent at birth but undergoes gradual involution post-puberty. Both bone marrow and thymus generate the microenvironment for T-cell differentiation.
  • Secondary Lymphoid Organs: Spleen, lymph nodes, tonsils, Peyer's patches of the small intestine, and appendix. Sites where mature lymphocytes migrate, trap antigens, interact with specific epitopes, and clonally expand into effector and memory cells.
    • Spleen: Large bean-shaped organ containing lymphocytes and phagocytes; acts as a hemolymphatic filter to trap blood-borne pathogens and serves as a major reservoir for erythrocytes.
    • Lymph Nodes: Trap particulate antigens from lymph and tissue fluid, presenting them to resident immune cells.
    • Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue (MALT): Infiltrates the mucosal linings of major tracts (digestive, respiratory, urogenital), constituting roughly 50% of total lymphoid tissue in the human body.

4. Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS)

AIDS was first documented in 1981. It is caused by the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), an enveloped single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) retrovirus carrying the reverse transcriptase enzyme. The incubation period from initial infection to full clinical manifestation ranges from 5 to 10 years.

Modes of HIV Transmission

  • Direct sexual contact with an infected partner.
  • Transfusion of contaminated blood or blood-derived products.
  • Parenteral route via contaminated needles (prevalent in intravenous drug users).
  • Perinatal transmission from an infected mother to child via the placenta.

Crucial Note: HIV is not transmitted through non-sexual touch, fomites, or casual social contact; it requires direct transmission of infected body fluids.

Molecular Pathogenesis and HIV Life Cycle

  1. Cell Entry: The viral envelope glycoproteins bind to target cells expressing CD4 surface receptors, notably macrophages and helper T-lymphocytes (\(\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{H}\)).
  2. Reverse Transcription: Viral RNA is released into the cytoplasm and transcribed into complementary viral double-stranded DNA by reverse transcriptase.
  3. Genomic Integration: The synthesized viral DNA translocates into the nucleus and integrates into the host chromosomal DNA via viral integrase.
  4. HIV Factory: The infected cell directs the synthesis of viral genomic RNA and polyproteins. Macrophages actively shed new viral particles without immediate lysis, serving as a continuous "HIV factory".
  5. Helper T-cell Depletion: Simultaneously, HIV enters, replicates within, and lyses helper T-lymphocytes (\(\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{H}\) cells), resulting in a progressive and drastic decline in systemic \(\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{H}\) cell counts.
  6. Opportunistic Invasions: Severe immunocompromise renders the patient vulnerable to life-threatening infections by opportunistic pathogens: Mycobacterium (tuberculosis), Toxoplasma (protozoan), systemic fungi, and viruses.
Diagnostics, Treatment, and Prevention

Standard diagnostic screening test: ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay). Pharmacotherapy utilizes anti-retroviral drugs (reverse transcriptase and protease inhibitors), which are only partially effective—they prolong patient lifespan but cannot eradicate the provirus or prevent eventual fatality. Educational and regulatory bodies include NACO (National AIDS Control Organisation) and the WHO.

5. Cancer: Oncogenic Transformation and Therapeutics

Normal cell division and tissue homeostasis are tightly governed by regulatory mechanisms. Normal cells demonstrate contact inhibition—a phenomenon where physical contact with adjoining cells inhibits proliferation. Cancerous neoplastic cells lose contact inhibition, yielding uncontrolled, continuous mitoses that form cellular aggregates termed tumors.

Benign Tumors

Remain strictly circumscribed to their tissue of origin without invading adjacent parenchyma; encapsulated, slow-growing, and produce minimal local damage.

Malignant Tumors

Composed of rapidly dividing neoplastic cells that invade surrounding tissues, monopolize vital nutrients, and disrupt organs. Display metastasis: malignant cells detach, enter lymphatic/blood vessels, and form secondary neoplasms at distant anatomical sites. Metastasis represents the most lethal characteristic of malignant cancers.

Etiology of Cancer (Carcinogens)

  • Physical Carcinogens: Ionizing radiations (X-rays, \(\gamma\)-rays) and non-ionizing radiation (UV rays) induce double-strand DNA breaks and cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers, yielding neoplastic mutations.
  • Chemical Carcinogens: Chemical mutagens, notably polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in tobacco smoke, are established causative agents of bronchogenic carcinoma (lung cancer).
  • Biological Agents: Oncogenic viruses possess viral oncogenes (\(\mathrm{v}\text{-}\mathrm{onc}\)). Normal cellular genomes contain cellular oncogenes (\(\mathrm{c}\text{-}\mathrm{onc}\)) or proto-oncogenes, which upon aberrant transcriptional activation or genetic mutation trigger neoplastic transformation.

Diagnostic Methods

  • Histopathology & Biopsy: Suspected tissue is excised, microtome-sliced, stained, and examined microscopically for atypical mitotic figures and loss of cellular differentiation.
  • Blood and Bone Marrow Examination: Evaluates elevated abnormal leukocytic proliferations in leukemias.
  • Radiography (X-rays) & Computed Tomography (CT): CT utilizes rotating X-ray sources and detectors to generate 3D volumetric images of internal organs.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Utilizes powerful magnetic fields and non-ionizing radiofrequency pulses to accurately detect pathological and physiological tissue alterations without radiation toxicity.
  • Immunological & Molecular Techniques: Monoclonal antibodies directed against cancer-specific antigens; DNA probes to detect inherited germline genetic susceptibility to familial cancers.

Modalities of Cancer Therapy

Clinical management typically uses a combination regimen: surgical excision of localized tumor mass, radiotherapy (focused lethal irradiation of malignant cells sparing adjacent healthy parenchyma), and chemotherapy (cytotoxic drugs with associated adverse side effects such as alopecia/hair loss and severe bone marrow suppression/anemia). To overcome tumor evasion of immunosurveillance, patients are administered biological response modifiers, primarily \(\alpha\)-interferon, which stimulates the host immune system to destroy tumor cells.

6. Drugs and Alcohol Abuse

Substance abuse refers to the consumption of psychoactive agents for non-medical reasons or in concentrations and frequencies that impair physiological, cognitive, and physical capabilities.

Drug Category Botanical / Chemical Source Receptors & Mechanism Physiological Effects & Clinical Features
Opioids Extracted from latex of opium poppy, Papaver somniferum. Heroin ("smack") is diacetylmorphine, synthesized via acetylation of morphine. Bind to specific opioid receptors in the Central Nervous System (CNS) and Gastrointestinal Tract (GIT). Heroin is a white, odorless, bitter crystalline compound. Taken via snorting or intravenous injection. Acts as a potent depressant, downregulating systemic body functions. Morphine is an effective clinical analgesic/sedative post-surgery.
Cannabinoids Obtained from the inflorescences, leaves, and resins of Cannabis sativa (Hemp). Forms: marijuana, hashish, charas, ganja. Interact with specific cannabinoid receptors localized principally in the brain. Inhaled or ingested. Prominently alters the cardiovascular system (tachycardia, altered vascular tone). Frequently abused by some athletes to alter performance.
Coca Alkaloids (Cocaine) Extracted from leaves of Erythroxylum coca (native to South America). Commonly called "coke" or "crack". Blocks the reuptake transporter of neurotransmitter dopamine at neurological synapses. Potent CNS stimulant. Produces euphoria and increased energy; high doses induce hallucinations. Other hallucinogenic plants: Atropa belladonna and Datura.
Tobacco & Nicotine Leaves of Nicotiana tabacum. Nicotine stimulates receptors on the adrenal medulla. Induces systemic release of adrenaline and nor-adrenaline, elevating blood pressure and heart rate. Combustion produces carbon monoxide (\(\mathrm{CO}\)), binding hemoglobin to form carboxyhemoglobin and reducing oxygen transport capacity. Linked to emphysema, cardiovascular disease, and oral/lung/bladder cancers.

Addiction, Dependence, and Withdrawal Syndrome

  • Addiction: A psychological attachment to the euphoric effects and transient feelings of well-being induced by chemical agents. Continuous intake elevates cellular receptor tolerance thresholds, demanding higher doses to achieve identical subjective responses.
  • Dependence: Physiological adaptation where the body functions normally only in the presence of the drug. Abrupt cessation produces withdrawal syndrome, characterized by acute anxiety, tremors, profuse diaphoresis (sweating), nausea, and tachycardia, which subside upon drug resumption.

Systemic Repercussions of Anabolic Steroid Abuse

Athletes misuse anabolic androgenic steroids to increase muscle bulk, strength, and aggressive drive. However, prolonged administration causes severe systemic side effects:

  • In Females: Masculinization, heightened aggressiveness, affective mood lability, depression, irreversible oligomenorrhea/amenorrhea (menstrual irregularities), hirsutism (excessive facial and somatic hair), clitoromegaly (enlargement of clitoris), and vocal cord thickening (deepening of voice).
  • In Males: Severe cystic acne, mood swings, testicular atrophy, severe oligospermia (reduced sperm count), hepatic and nephric dysfunction, gynecomastia (breast tissue enlargement), premature pattern baldness, and prostatic hypertrophy.
  • In Adolescents of Both Sexes: Severe acne and premature epiphyseal closure of long bones, producing permanent growth stunting.

7. NEET-UG Pattern Practice Assessment

The following original practice questions match the format, rigor, and distribution of the NEET-UG examination.

Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Identify the statement that accurately differentiates Humoral Immune Response (HIR) from Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI):

A. HIR is primarily mediated by cytotoxic T-lymphocytes, while CMI relies on plasma cell secretions.
B. CMI is directly responsible for graft rejection following allogeneic kidney transplantation, mediated by T-lymphocytes.
C. Antibodies involved in HIR recognize only processed peptide fragments presented on MHC molecules.
D. CMI acts solely through the synthesis and circulation of \(\mathrm{IgG}\) and \(\mathrm{IgM}\) pentamers in body fluids.
Correct Answer: B
Detailed Explanation: Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI) is governed by T-lymphocytes, which possess the cellular mechanism to differentiate self from non-self antigens. Following organ transplantation (allograft), host T-cells recognize foreign histocompatibility antigens, triggering allograft rejection. Consequently, transplant recipients require lifelong immunosuppressants. Humoral immunity is mediated by B-cell-derived antibodies circulating in humors (fluids).
Question 2 (Assertion-Reasoning)

Given below are two statements labeled as Assertion (A) and Reason (R):
Assertion (A): Malignant tumors exhibit metastasis, which is considered the most feared characteristic of neoplastic disease.
Reason (R): Cancerous cells lack the property of contact inhibition, allowing them to slough off, invade blood vessels, and lodge at distant anatomical locations.

A. Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
B. Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
C. (A) is true, but (R) is false.
D. (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: A
Detailed Explanation: Normal cells exhibit contact inhibition, halting mitoses when contacting neighbouring cells. Neoplastic cells lose this control, forming malignant tumors whose invasive cells detach (slough off), enter the circulation, and establish secondary metastatic foci in distant organs.

Match the infectious human diseases in Column I with their respective clinical features or diagnostic points in Column II:

C. (a)–(iv), (b)–(i), (c)–(ii), (d)–(iii)

Detailed Explanation:
  • Typhoid fever is confirmed by the Widal test and causes high sustained fever (39°–40°C).
  • Pneumonia infects lung alveoli, filling them with fluid and potentially turning fingernails and lips blue-gray.
  • Filariasis (Wuchereria) obstructs lymphatic drainage of limbs and genitalia, causing chronic elephantiasis.
  • Malaria involves erythrocyte lysis and the release of crystalline haemozoin, inducing paroxysms of fever.
Question 4 (Multiple-Statement Type)

Evaluate the correctness of the following statements regarding retroviral replication and AIDS pathogenesis:

  1. Upon cellular entry, viral genomic RNA undergoes replication directly via host DNA polymerase.
  2. Macrophages infected with HIV continue shedding new viral particles without immediate lysis, acting as an HIV factory.
  3. Depletion of CD4\({}^+\) helper T-lymphocytes (\(\mathrm{T}_\mathrm{H}\)) predisposes patients to opportunistic pathogens like Mycobacterium and Toxoplasma.
  4. HIV transmission occurs primarily through casual physical contact, airborne respiratory droplets, and fomites.
A. Statements 1, 2, and 4 are correct.
B. Statements 2 and 3 are correct.
C. Statements 1, 3, and 4 are correct.
D. Statements 2, 3, and 4 are correct.
Correct Answer: B
Detailed Explanation: Statement 1 is incorrect because viral RNA requires the viral enzyme reverse transcriptase to form complementary DNA. Statement 4 is incorrect because HIV is transmitted through parenteral contact, contaminated blood, sexual intercourse, and maternal-fetal transfer—not through casual contact, droplets, or fomites. A. Heroin • Cannabis sativa • Stimulates dopamine transport in the prefrontal cortex
B. Cocaine • Erythroxylum coca • Interferes with dopamine transport, producing CNS stimulation

Question 6 (Diagram-Based Structural Analysis)

In a standard immunoglobulin monomer (\(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{L}_2\)), the specific region responsible for direct antigen recognition and binding consists of:

A. The constant carboxy-terminal (\(\mathrm{C}\)-terminus) domains of the heavy chains only.
B. The variable amino-terminal (\(\mathrm{N}\)-terminus) domains of both the heavy and light chains.
C. The interchain disulfide bonds connecting the two heavy chains in the Fc fragment.
D. The carboxy-terminal domains of the light chains exclusively.
Correct Answer: B
Detailed Explanation: The antigen-binding paratope is formed by the hypervariable, amino-terminal (\(\mathrm{N}\)-terminal) domains of both the light (\(\mathrm{L}\)) and heavy (\(\mathrm{H}\)) chains at the tips of the Y-shaped molecule.
Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A non-specific defense mechanism present from birth that uses proteins secreted by virus-infected cells to protect surrounding uninfected cells is classified as:

A. Physical barrier of innate immunity
B. Physiological barrier of acquired immunity
C. Cellular barrier of innate immunity
D. Cytokine barrier of innate immunity
Correct Answer: D
Detailed Explanation: Cytokine barriers consist of interferons—glycoproteins released by cells infected with viruses. Interferons bind to uninfected neighbouring cells, stimulating the synthesis of antiviral proteins that inhibit subsequent viral replication.
Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Correct Answer: C
Detailed Explanation: Mucosa-Associated Lymphoid Tissue (MALT) is located within the mucosal linings of major physiological tracts and comprises approximately 50 percent of the total lymphoid tissue in the human body.

8. Common Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

Essential NEET Distinctions to Memorize:
  • Common Cold vs. Pneumonia: Rhino viruses infect the nose and upper respiratory tract but never the lungs. Bacterial pneumonia (Streptococcus pneumoniae) directly infects the alveoli, causing fluid buildup.
  • Vector Clarifications: Anopheles transmits malaria; Culex transmits filariasis; Aedes transmits viral pathogens (dengue and chikungunya); houseflies are mechanical carriers for Entamoeba histolytica.
  • Immunoglobulin Profiles: \(\mathrm{IgA}\) is enriched in secretory fluids like colostrum; \(\mathrm{IgE}\) triggers mast cell degranulation in allergies; \(\mathrm{IgG}\) crosses the placenta to confer passive immunity.
  • Morphine vs. Heroin: Morphine is extracted directly from the latex of Papaver somniferum. Diacetylmorphine (heroin/smack) is chemically synthesized via acetylation of morphine.
  • Tobacco/Smoking Hemodynamics: Nicotine stimulates the adrenal medulla to release adrenaline and noradrenaline, raising blood pressure and heart rate. Inhaled carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin, displacing oxygen and causing systemic hypoxemia.

9. Chapter Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Health: A state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being maintained through balanced nutrition, hygiene, immunization, and lifestyle choices. William Harvey disproved the ancient "good humor" hypothesis using clinical thermometry and the discovery of blood circulation.
  • Pathogenic Invasions: Organisms across bacterial, viral, fungal, protozoan, and helminthic domains breach barriers and induce disease. Plasmodium cycles between humans (asexual merozoites/haemozoin release) and mosquitoes (sexual fertilization/sporozoite storage).
  • Immune Organization: Divided into non-specific innate mechanisms (skin, secretions, PMNLs, interferons) and specific acquired defenses (humoral B-cells/\(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{L}_2\) antibodies vs. cell-mediated T-cells). Organ rejection is mediated by CMI.
  • Pathologies of Excess and Deficit: Allergies involve \(\mathrm{IgE}\)-mediated hypersensitivity; autoimmune disorders (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis) arise when tolerance fails. AIDS stems from retroviral destruction of CD4\({}^+\) helper T-cells, opening the door to lethal opportunistic infections.
  • Malignancies: Result from loss of contact inhibition, clonal expansion, and metastatic invasion. Managed through multimodality regimens (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, \(\alpha\)-interferon).
  • Substance Abuse: Opioids, cannabinoids, and cocaine target central receptors, altering physiological homeostasis and carrying high potential for psychological dependence and withdrawal symptoms.
