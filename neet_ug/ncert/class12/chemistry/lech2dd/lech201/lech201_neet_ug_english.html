NEET UG Chemistry • Organic Chemistry

Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

A complete, exam-oriented study guide covering structure, nomenclature, reaction mechanisms, stereochemistry, and NEET UG practice assessments.

1. Learning Objectives and Overview

Organohalogen compounds are formed when one or more hydrogen atoms in an aliphatic or aromatic hydrocarbon are replaced by halogen atoms (F, Cl, Br, or I). In haloalkanes (alkyl halides), the halogen is bonded to an \(sp^3\)-hybridised carbon atom of an alkyl framework, whereas in haloarenes (aryl halides), the halogen is bonded directly to an \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbon atom of an aromatic ring.

Target Learning Outcomes
  • Classify organohalogen molecules by halogen count and carbon hybridisation (\(sp^3\) versus \(sp^2\)), and assign accurate IUPAC and common names.
  • Relate carbon–halogen (\(C\text{–}X\)) bond polarity, length, and enthalpy to physical properties and chemical reactivity.
  • Master synthetic routes to haloalkanes and haloarenes, including halogen exchange and diazonium chemistry.
  • Distinguish between \(S_N1\), \(S_N2\), and \(\beta\)-elimination mechanisms using kinetics, substrate structure, solvent effects, and stereochemistry.
  • Explain why haloarenes resist nucleophilic substitution, how electron-withdrawing substituents activate them, and why halogens are ortho/para-directing yet deactivating in electrophilic aromatic substitution.
  • Identify the industrial uses, biological significance, and environmental hazards of key polyhalogen compounds.

Biological and Clinical Context: Organohalogen compounds occur both naturally and synthetically. The soil-derived antibiotic chloramphenicol treats typhoid fever, and the endogenous iodine-containing hormone thyroxine regulates metabolism (its deficiency causes goitre). Clinically important synthetic halides include chloroquine (antimalarial) and halothane (surgical anaesthetic), while perfluorinated hydrocarbons have been investigated as temporary blood substitutes.

2. Classification and IUPAC Nomenclature

2.1 Classification by Halogen Count

Compounds are grouped as monohalo, dihalo, or polyhalo (tri-, tetra-, etc.) depending on whether they contain one, two, or more halogen atoms per molecule. For example, \(\mathrm{C_2H_5Br}\) is a monohaloalkane, \(\mathrm{ClCH_2CH_2Cl}\) is a dihaloalkane, and \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) is a trihaloalkane. Aromatic rings follow the same logic (monohaloarene, dihaloarene, trihaloarene).

2.2 Classification by Hybridisation of the Carbon Bearing Halogen

Allylic Halides \(sp^3\) Halogen is attached to an \(sp^3\) carbon directly adjacent to a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (the allylic position), e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH\text{–}CH_2Cl}\) or 3-bromocyclohexene.
Benzylic Halides \(sp^3\) Halogen is attached to an \(sp^3\) carbon bonded directly to an aromatic ring. Can be \(1^\circ\) (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2X}\)), \(2^\circ\) (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH(R)X}\)), or \(3^\circ\) (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5C(R)(R')X}\)).
Vinylic Halides \(sp^2\) Halogen is bonded directly to one of the \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbons of a \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond, e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH\text{–}Cl}\).
Aryl Halides (\(\mathrm{Ar\text{–}X}\)) \(sp^2\) Halogen is bonded directly to an \(sp^2\) carbon of an aromatic ring, e.g., chlorobenzene or p-bromotoluene.

2.3 Geminal vs. Vicinal Dihalides & Naming Rules

In IUPAC nomenclature, haloalkanes are named as halosubstituted hydrocarbons (prefixes: fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-). Common names list the alkyl group followed by the halide name. For disubstituted benzenes, common names use ortho- (o-), meta- (m-), and para- (p-), whereas IUPAC names use locants 1,2-, 1,3-, and 1,4-.

  • Geminal dihalides (gem-dihalides): Both halogen atoms are attached to the same carbon atom. Common names use the suffix alkylidene halide (e.g., ethylidene chloride, \(\mathrm{CH_3CHCl_2}\), IUPAC: 1,1-dichloroethane).
  • Vicinal dihalides (vic-dihalides): The two halogen atoms are attached to adjacent carbon atoms. Common names use alkylene dihalide (e.g., ethylene dichloride, \(\mathrm{ClCH_2CH_2Cl}\), IUPAC: 1,2-dichloroethane).

3. Nature of the C–X Bond and Physical Properties

3.1 Polarity, Bond Length, Bond Enthalpy, and Dipole Moment

Because halogens are more electronegative than carbon, the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}X}\) bond is polarised: carbon carries a partial positive charge (\(\delta^+\)) and the halogen carries a partial negative charge (\(\delta^-\)). As atomic size increases down Group 17 (\(\mathrm{F < Cl < Br < I}\)), the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}X}\) bond length increases and the bond dissociation enthalpy decreases.

Bond Bond Length (pm) Bond Enthalpy (\(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)) Dipole Moment (D)
\(\mathrm{CH_3\text{–}F}\) 139 452 1.847
\(\mathrm{CH_3\text{–}Cl}\) 178 351 1.860
\(\mathrm{CH_3\text{–}Br}\) 193 293 1.830
\(\mathrm{CH_3\text{–}I}\) 214 234 1.636
High-Yield NEET Concept — Dipole Moment Anomaly: Although fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine, \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\) (\(1.860\,\mathrm{D}\)) has a slightly higher dipole moment than \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\) (\(1.847\,\mathrm{D}\)). Since dipole moment \(\mu = q \times d\), the significantly longer \(\mathrm{C\text{–}Cl}\) bond distance (\(178\,\mathrm{pm}\) vs. \(139\,\mathrm{pm}\)) outweighs the slightly smaller partial charge. Overall order: \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl > CH_3F > CH_3Br > CH_3I}\).

3.2 Boiling Points, Melting Points, Density, and Solubility

  • Boiling Points: Haloalkanes have substantially higher boiling points than alkanes of comparable molecular mass because of stronger dipole–dipole and van der Waals attractions.
    • Effect of halogen: For the same alkyl group, boiling point follows \(\mathrm{R\text{–}I > R\text{–}Br > R\text{–}Cl > R\text{–}F}\) due to increasing molecular mass and polarisability (stronger van der Waals forces).
    • Effect of branching: Among isomeric haloalkanes, boiling point decreases with increased branching because the molecule becomes more compact and spherical, reducing surface area and van der Waals contact (e.g., 1-bromobutane \(375\,\mathrm{K}\) > 2-bromobutane \(364\,\mathrm{K}\) > 2-bromo-2-methylpropane \(346\,\mathrm{K}\)).
  • Melting Points of Dihalobenzenes: Isomeric o-, m-, and p-dichlorobenzenes have nearly identical boiling points (\(453\,\mathrm{K}, 446\,\mathrm{K}, 448\,\mathrm{K}\)), but the para-isomer has a much higher melting point (\(323\,\mathrm{K}\) vs. \(256\,\mathrm{K}\) for ortho and \(249\,\mathrm{K}\) for meta) because its high symmetry allows tight packing in the crystal lattice.
  • Density: Bromo, iodo, and polychloro derivatives are denser than water. Density increases with the number of carbons, number of halogens, and atomic mass of the halogen.
  • Solubility: Haloalkanes are only slightly soluble in water. Breaking strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds in water requires more energy than is released when weaker dipole–dipole attractions form between haloalkane and water molecules. They dissolve readily in non-polar or weakly polar organic solvents.

4. Methods of Preparation

4.1 Preparation of Haloalkanes

A. From Alcohols (\(\mathrm{R\text{–}OH \rightarrow R\text{–}X}\))

The hydroxyl group of an alcohol is replaced by a halogen using halogen acids, phosphorus halides, or thionyl chloride. Alcohol reactivity toward \(\mathrm{HX}\) follows the order \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\).

  • With \(\mathrm{HCl}\): \(1^\circ\) and \(2^\circ\) alcohols require anhydrous \(\mathrm{ZnCl_2}\) catalyst (Groove's process), whereas \(3^\circ\) alcohols react rapidly upon shaking with concentrated \(\mathrm{HCl}\) at room temperature.
  • With \(\mathrm{HBr}\) / \(\mathrm{HI}\): Alkyl bromides are prepared using constant-boiling \(48\%\) \(\mathrm{HBr}\) or \(\mathrm{NaBr/H_2SO_4}\). Alkyl iodides are prepared by heating alcohols with \(\mathrm{NaI}\) or \(\mathrm{KI}\) in \(95\%\) orthophosphoric acid (\(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)). Note: \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) cannot be used with \(\mathrm{KI}\) because it oxidises \(\mathrm{HI}\) to \(\mathrm{I_2}\).
  • With Phosphorus Halides: \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) gives \(\mathrm{RCl + POCl_3 + HCl}\); \(\mathrm{PX_3}\) (\(\mathrm{X = Cl, Br}\)) gives \(3\mathrm{RX + H_3PO_3}\). \(\mathrm{PBr_3}\) and \(\mathrm{PI_3}\) are usually generated in situ from red phosphorus and \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) or \(\mathrm{I_2}\).
  • With Thionyl Chloride (\(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\)): Preferred laboratory method for \(\mathrm{R\text{–}Cl}\) because the by-products \(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\) are gases that escape, leaving pure alkyl chloride: \[\mathrm{R\text{–}OH + SOCl_2 \longrightarrow R\text{–}Cl + SO_2\uparrow + HCl\uparrow}\]

Limitation: None of these alcohol methods convert phenols to aryl halides because the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}O}\) bond in phenol has partial double-bond character due to resonance and is much stronger than a \(\mathrm{C\text{–}O}\) single bond.

B. From Hydrocarbons

  • Free Radical Halogenation of Alkanes: Chlorination or bromination under UV light or heat yields a complex mixture of isomeric mono- and polyhaloalkanes, resulting in low yields of any single isomer. Only highly symmetrical alkanes (such as neopentane or cyclopentane) give a single monochloro product.
  • Addition of \(\mathrm{HX}\) to Alkenes: Follows Markovnikov's rule (in the absence of peroxides): the proton adds to the alkene carbon with more hydrogens, forming the more stable carbocation intermediate. Thus, propene + \(\mathrm{HI}\) gives 2-iodopropane as the major product.
  • Addition of \(\mathrm{Br_2/CCl_4}\) to Alkenes: Discharges the reddish-brown colour of bromine to form a colourless vic-dibromide; used as a qualitative test for unsaturation.

C. Halogen Exchange Reactions

Finkelstein Reaction (Alkyl Iodides): \(\mathrm{R\text{–}X + NaI \xrightarrow{\text{dry acetone}} R\text{–}I + NaX\downarrow} \quad (\mathrm{X = Cl, Br})\)

In dry acetone, \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) and \(\mathrm{NaBr}\) precipitate out, driving the equilibrium forward according to Le Chatelier's principle.

Swarts Reaction (Alkyl Fluorides): \(\mathrm{H_3C\text{–}Br + AgF \longrightarrow H_3C\text{–}F + AgBr\downarrow}\)

Metallic fluorides such as \(\mathrm{AgF}\), \(\mathrm{Hg_2F_2}\), \(\mathrm{CoF_2}\), or \(\mathrm{SbF_3}\) are heated with alkyl chlorides or bromides to synthesise alkyl fluorides.

4.2 Preparation of Haloarenes

  • Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution: Arenes react with \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in the dark in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst (\(\mathrm{Fe}\) or anhydrous \(\mathrm{FeCl_3}\)) to give ortho- and para-haloarenes, separable by fractional crystallisation/distillation due to large melting-point differences. Direct iodination is reversible and requires an oxidising agent (\(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) or \(\mathrm{HIO_4}\)) to oxidise \(\mathrm{HI}\) back to \(\mathrm{I_2}\). Direct fluorination is too violent to control.
  • Sandmeyer's Reaction (from Primary Aromatic Amines): Aniline is diazotised with \(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + HX}\) at \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\) to form a benzenediazonium halide. Treating this freshly prepared salt with \(\mathrm{Cu_2Cl_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Cu_2Br_2}\) replaces the diazonium group with \(\mathrm{\text{–}Cl}\) or \(\mathrm{\text{–}Br}\) with evolution of \(\mathrm{N_2}\). For aryl iodides, \(\mathrm{Cu_2X_2}\) is unnecessary—simply shaking the diazonium salt with aqueous \(\mathrm{KI}\) yields iodobenzene.

5. Chemical Reactions of Haloalkanes

Haloalkanes undergo three major reaction classes: nucleophilic substitution, \(\beta\)-elimination, and reactions with metals.

5.1 Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions & Ambident Nucleophiles

An electron-rich nucleophile (\(\mathrm{Nu^-}\)) attacks the electron-deficient \(\delta^+\) carbon, displacing the halide leaving group (\(\mathrm{X^-}\)).

\(\mathrm{NH_3}\) (excess) \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) \(\mathrm{R\text{–}NH_2}\) Primary amine

Ambident Nucleophiles (\(\mathrm{CN^-}\) and \(\mathrm{NO_2^-}\)): Species with two nucleophilic centres are called ambident nucleophiles.
  • Cyanides: \(\mathrm{KCN}\) is predominantly ionic, supplying free \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) ions; attack occurs mainly through carbon because a \(\mathrm{C\text{–}C}\) bond is stronger than a \(\mathrm{C\text{–}N}\) bond, yielding nitriles (\(\mathrm{RCN}\)). \(\mathrm{AgCN}\) is covalent, so only the nitrogen lone pair is free to attack, yielding isocyanides (\(\mathrm{RNC}\)).
  • Nitrites: \(\mathrm{KNO_2}\) is ionic (\(\mathrm{O^-}\) attacks) giving alkyl nitrites (\(\mathrm{R\text{–}O\text{–}N=O}\)); \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\) is covalent (N lone pair attacks) giving nitroalkanes (\(\mathrm{R\text{–}NO_2}\)).

5.2 \(S_N2\) vs. \(S_N1\) Mechanisms

Feature \(S_N2\) (Bimolecular) \(S_N1\) (Unimolecular)
Kinetics & Rate Law 2nd order: \(\mathrm{Rate} = k[\mathrm{R\text{–}X}][\mathrm{Nu^-}]\) 1st order: \(\mathrm{Rate} = k[\mathrm{R\text{–}X}]\) (independent of \([\mathrm{Nu^-}]\))
Steps & Intermediate Single concerted step via a pentacoordinate transition state (no intermediate). Two steps: slow, reversible \(\mathrm{C\text{–}X}\) cleavage to form a planar \(sp^2\) carbocation, followed by fast nucleophilic attack.
Governing Factor Steric hindrance around the \(\alpha\)-carbon. Stability of the carbocation intermediate.
Alkyl Halide Order \(\mathrm{CH_3X > 1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ}\) \(\mathrm{3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > CH_3X}\) (allylic & benzylic are also very fast due to resonance).
Stereochemistry 100% Inversion of configuration (backside attack, umbrella flip). Racemisation (planar carbocation attacked equally from both faces, giving a 50:50 \(d/l\) mixture).
Favoured Solvent Polar aprotic solvents (acetone, DMSO, DMF). Polar protic solvents (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\), alcohols, \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\)) to solvate ions.

Leaving Group Effect: In both \(S_N1\) and \(S_N2\) mechanisms, reactivity for a given alkyl group follows \(\mathrm{R\text{–}I > R\text{–}Br > R\text{–}Cl \gg R\text{–}F}\) because \(\mathrm{I^-}\) is the largest, weakest base and best leaving group.

5.3 Stereochemical Foundations

  • Optical Activity: Compounds that rotate the plane of plane-polarised light (measured via a polarimeter) are optically active. Clockwise rotation is dextrorotatory (\(d\) or \(+\)); anticlockwise rotation is laevorotatory (\(l\) or \(-\)).
  • Chirality & Enantiomers: A carbon atom bonded to four different groups is an asymmetric carbon (stereocentre). Molecules that are non-superimposable on their mirror images are chiral (optically active), while superimposable ones (like propan-2-ol) are achiral (optically inactive). Non-superimposable mirror-image stereoisomers are enantiomers; they share identical melting points, boiling points, and refractive indices, differing only in the sign of optical rotation.
  • Racemic Mixture: An equimolar (\(50:50\)) mixture of \(d\)- and \(l\)-enantiomers, denoted \(dl\) or \((\pm)\), has zero net optical rotation because the rotations cancel out.
  • Retention vs. Inversion: If no bond to the stereocentre is broken during a reaction, configuration is retained (note: the sign of rotation, \(+\) or \(-\), may still change because different compounds with the same configuration can rotate light in opposite directions).

5.4 Elimination Reactions (\(\beta\)-Elimination) & Competition

When a haloalkane containing a \(\beta\)-hydrogen is heated with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\), a \(\beta\)-hydrogen and the \(\alpha\)-halogen are eliminated to form an alkene (dehydrohalogenation). When multiple alkenes are possible, Zaitsev's (Saytzeff's) rule predicts that the major product is the more substituted (more stable) alkene. For example, 2-bromopentane with alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) yields pent-2-ene (\(81\%\)) over pent-1-ene (\(19\%\)).

Substitution vs. Elimination: Bulky bases/nucleophiles (e.g., tert-butoxide) favour elimination over substitution by abstracting a \(\beta\)-proton rather than approaching a crowded carbon. Aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\) favours substitution (alcohols), whereas hot alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) favours elimination (alkenes).

5.5 Reactions with Metals

  • Grignard Reagents (\(\mathrm{R\text{–}MgX}\)): Formed by reacting haloalkanes with magnesium in dry ether. The \(\mathrm{C^{\delta-}\text{–}Mg^{\delta+}}\) bond is covalent yet highly polar, making the alkyl group strongly basic and nucleophilic. Grignard reagents react violently with any proton source (\(\mathrm{H_2O}\), alcohols, amines) to yield alkanes: \[\mathrm{R\text{–}MgX + H_2O \longrightarrow R\text{–}H + Mg(OH)X}\] Hence, strictly anhydrous conditions are mandatory.
  • Wurtz Reaction: Alkyl halides react with sodium in dry ether to give symmetrical alkanes with double the number of carbons: \[2\mathrm{R\text{–}X} + 2\mathrm{Na} \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} \mathrm{R\text{–}R} + 2\mathrm{NaX}\]

6. Reactions of Haloarenes

6.1 Why Haloarenes Resist Nucleophilic Substitution

Aryl halides are far less reactive toward nucleophilic substitution than alkyl halides due to four factors:

  1. Resonance Effect: Lone pairs on the halogen delocalise into the aromatic \(\pi\)-system, giving the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}X}\) bond partial double-bond character that is harder to cleave.
  2. Hybridisation Difference: The halogen in haloarenes is attached to an \(sp^2\) carbon (\(33.3\%\) \(s\)-character, more electronegative), holding the bonding electrons more tightly than an \(sp^3\) carbon (\(25\%\) \(s\)-character). Thus, the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}Cl}\) bond in chlorobenzene (\(169\,\mathrm{pm}\)) is shorter and stronger than in chloroethane (\(177\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  3. Instability of Phenyl Cation: Self-ionisation would produce a phenyl cation that cannot be stabilised by resonance, ruling out the \(S_N1\) pathway.
  4. Electronic Repulsion: The electron-rich \(\pi\)-cloud of the benzene ring repels approaching nucleophiles.

6.2 Replacement by Hydroxyl Group & Nitro-Group Activation

Chlorobenzene converts to phenol only under drastic conditions: heating with aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at \(623\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(300\,\mathrm{atm}\) pressure, followed by acidification. Introducing electron-withdrawing \(\mathrm{\text{–}NO_2}\) groups at the ortho and para positions dramatically lowers the required temperature because they stabilise the negative charge of the intermediate carbanion by resonance:

  • p-Nitrochlorobenzene: \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at \(443\,\mathrm{K}\) \(\rightarrow\) p-nitrophenol.
  • 2,4-Dinitrochlorobenzene: \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at \(368\,\mathrm{K}\) \(\rightarrow\) 2,4-dinitrophenol.
  • 2,4,6-Trinitrochlorobenzene: warm \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) alone \(\rightarrow\) 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid).

Crucial Exception: A \(\mathrm{\text{–}NO_2}\) group at the meta position does not enhance nucleophilic substitution because none of the carbanion resonance structures place the negative charge on the meta carbon bearing the \(\mathrm{\text{–}NO_2}\) group.

6.3 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution & Metal Reactions

In electrophilic substitution (halogenation, nitration with \(\mathrm{HNO_3/H_2SO_4}\), sulphonation with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), and Friedel–Crafts alkylation/acylation with anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\)), halogens are weakly deactivating yet ortho, para-directing (the para-isomer is always major due to lower steric hindrance).

  • Reactivity vs. Orientation: The strong \(-I\) (inductive) effect of the halogen withdraws electron density, destabilising the intermediate carbocation and making the ring less reactive than benzene. However, the \(+M\) (resonance) effect preferentially stabilises the carbocation for attack at the ortho and para positions. Thus, inductive effect controls reactivity, while resonance effect controls orientation.
  • Wurtz–Fittig Reaction: An aryl halide + alkyl halide + \(2\mathrm{Na}\) in dry ether yields an alkylarene (\(\mathrm{Ar\text{–}R}\)).
  • Fittig Reaction: Two equivalents of aryl halide + \(2\mathrm{Na}\) in dry ether couple to form biphenyl (diphenyl, \(\mathrm{Ar\text{–}Ar}\)).

8. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Classification and IUPAC Naming

Problem: Assign the IUPAC name and classify the halogen-bearing carbon (\(sp^3\) or \(sp^2\), and specific subtype) for the compound \(\mathrm{CH_3\text{–}CH=CH\text{–}C(Br)(CH_3)_2}\).

Solution:

  1. Identify the longest carbon chain containing the double bond: a 5-carbon pentene chain.
  2. Number from the end giving the double bond the lower locant: \(\mathrm{C5(CH_3)\text{–}C4H=C3H\text{–}C2(Br)(CH_3)\text{–}C1H_3}\) is incorrect; numbering from the right gives the bromo and methyl substituents at C-2 and the double bond at C-3 (\(\text{pent-3-ene}\)), whereas numbering from the left gives the double bond at C-2 (\(\text{pent-2-ene}\)) and substituents at C-4. Double bonds take priority over halo/alkyl prefixes.
  3. Thus, the IUPAC name is 4-bromo-4-methylpent-2-ene.
  4. The bromine atom is attached to an \(sp^3\)-hybridised carbon (C-4) that is directly adjacent to the \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond (C-3) and bonded to three carbon groups. Therefore, it is a tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) allylic halide.
Worked Example 2: Comparing \(S_N1\) and \(S_N2\) Reactivity

Problem: Arrange the following three compounds in order of increasing reactivity toward (a) \(S_N1\) hydrolysis in aqueous ethanol, and (b) \(S_N2\) displacement with \(\mathrm{NaCN}\) in DMSO:
I: 1-bromo-2-methylbutane  |  II: 2-bromo-2-methylbutane  |  III: 1-bromopentane

Solution:

  • (a) \(S_N1\) Order: Rate depends on carbocation stability. Compound II is a \(3^\circ\) bromide forming a stable \(3^\circ\) carbocation. Both I and III are \(1^\circ\) bromides, but I has a \(\beta\)-methyl branch that provides a slightly stronger \(+I\) inductive stabilisation (and relieves steric crowding upon ionisation) compared to the straight-chain III. Increasing \(S_N1\) order: III < I < II.
  • (b) \(S_N2\) Order: Rate depends on steric accessibility of the \(\alpha\)-carbon. Straight-chain \(1^\circ\) bromide III is least hindered; I is \(1^\circ\) but has a \(\beta\)-branch slowing backside attack; II is a \(3^\circ\) bromide and is virtually unreactive toward \(S_N2\). Increasing \(S_N2\) order: II < I < III.
Worked Example 3: Ambident Nucleophiles & Elimination

Problem: Suppose 1-bromobutane is split into three portions and treated separately with: (i) \(\mathrm{KCN}\) in aqueous ethanol, (ii) \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\) in ethanol, and (iii) alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) with heating. Predict the major organic product in each case.

Solution:

  • (i) With \(\mathrm{KCN}\): \(\mathrm{KCN}\) is ionic; carbon attacks to form a stronger \(\mathrm{C\text{–}C}\) bond, giving pentanenitrile (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2CN}\)).
  • (ii) With \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\): \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\) is covalent; nitrogen's lone pair attacks to give 1-nitrobutane (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2NO_2}\)).
  • (iii) With hot alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\): \(\beta\)-elimination (dehydrohalogenation) occurs to yield but-1-ene (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH=CH_2}\)).
Worked Example 4: Grignard Sequence & Wurtz Coupling

Problem: An alkyl bromide \(\mathrm{R\text{–}Br}\) reacts with \(\mathrm{Mg}\) in dry ether to form compound P. Treating P with \(\mathrm{D_2O}\) yields \(\mathrm{CH_3\text{–}CD(CH_3)_2}\) (2-deuterio-2-methylpropane). What is the structure of \(\mathrm{R\text{–}Br}\), and what alkane forms if \(\mathrm{R\text{–}Br}\) is heated with \(\mathrm{Na}\) in dry ether?

Solution:

Deuterolysis of a Grignard reagent replaces the \(\mathrm{\text{–}MgBr}\) group with \(\mathrm{\text{–}D}\) at the exact carbon originally bonded to bromine. Since \(\mathrm{D}\) is at C-2 of 2-methylpropane, \(\mathrm{R\text{–}Br}\) must be 2-bromo-2-methylpropane (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C\text{–}Br}\)), and P is \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C\text{–}MgBr}\).
Note on Wurtz reaction: Treating a \(3^\circ\) halide like \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C\text{–}Br}\) with \(\mathrm{Na}\) in dry ether primarily undergoes disproportionation/elimination in practice rather than clean coupling, whereas for \(1^\circ\) halides Wurtz coupling gives \(\mathrm{R\text{–}R}\). (For a hypothetical idealised Wurtz coupling of \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C\text{–}Br}\), the coupled product would be 2,2,3,3-tetramethylbutane.)

Practice Question 1 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following compounds has the highest dipole moment?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{CH_3Br}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) is tetrahedral and symmetrical, so its net dipole moment is zero. Among halomethanes, \(\mu = q \times d\). Because the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}Cl}\) bond length (\(178\,\mathrm{pm}\)) is substantially greater than the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}F}\) bond length (\(139\,\mathrm{pm}\)), \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\) (\(1.860\,\mathrm{D}\)) has a slightly higher dipole moment than \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\) (\(1.847\,\mathrm{D}\)) and \(\mathrm{CH_3Br}\) (\(1.830\,\mathrm{D}\)).

Practice Question 2 Statement-Based MCQ

Read the two statements carefully and choose the correct option:
Statement I: Sulphuric acid (\(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)) is not used alongside \(\mathrm{KI}\) when converting an alcohol into an alkyl iodide.
Statement II: \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) oxidises the \(\mathrm{HI}\) formed in the reaction mixture to molecular iodine (\(\mathrm{I_2}\)).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
  • (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false.
  • (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: Concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) converts \(\mathrm{KI}\) into \(\mathrm{HI}\), which is a strong reducing agent and is immediately oxidised by \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) to \(\mathrm{I_2}\), preventing substitution of the alcohol. Therefore, non-oxidising \(95\%\) \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\) is used instead. Both statements are true.

Practice Question 3 Reaction Mechanism MCQ

When optically active \((+)\)-2-bromobutane is hydrolysed with aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\) via an \(S_N1\) mechanism, the resulting butan-2-ol is optically inactive because:

  • (A) The reaction proceeds with 100% inversion of configuration.
  • (B) A planar \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbocation intermediate is formed, allowing equal attack from both sides.
  • (C) Elimination competes completely to form achiral but-2-ene.
  • (D) The \(\mathrm{\text{–}OH}\) group has zero optical rotatory power.
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: In an \(S_N1\) reaction, slow ionisation generates a planar, achiral \(sp^2\)-hybridised carbocation. The nucleophile (\(\mathrm{H_2O/OH^-}\)) attacks both faces of the plane with equal probability, yielding a \(50:50\) racemic mixture of \((+)\)- and \((-)\)-butan-2-ol, which is optically inactive.

Practice Question 4 Named Reactions MCQ

Which of the following reagents converts ethyl bromide into ethyl fluoride?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{NaF}\) in dry acetone
  • (B) \(\mathrm{F_2}\) in dark with \(\mathrm{Fe}\)

  • (D) \(\mathrm{HF}\) in \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Alkyl fluorides are best synthesised by the Swarts reaction, in which an alkyl chloride or bromide is heated with a heavy-metal fluoride such as \(\mathrm{AgF}\), \(\mathrm{Hg_2F_2}\), \(\mathrm{CoF_2}\), or \(\mathrm{SbF_3}\). (\(\mathrm{NaI}\) in dry acetone is Finkelstein for iodides.)

Practice Question 5 Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): 1-Chloro-3-nitrobenzene (m-nitrochlorobenzene) undergoes nucleophilic substitution with aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at nearly the same sluggish rate as chlorobenzene.
Reason (R): A \(\mathrm{\text{–}NO_2}\) group at the meta position cannot stabilise the negative charge of the intermediate carbanion by resonance.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Worked Solution: During nucleophilic attack on a halobenzene, the incoming negative charge is delocalised only onto the ortho and para carbons relative to the leaving group. Thus, only o- and p-\(\mathrm{NO_2}\) groups can accept and stabilise this negative charge by resonance; a meta-\(\mathrm{NO_2}\) group provides no resonance stabilisation.

Practice Question 7 Physical Properties MCQ

Practice Question 8 Elimination & Zaitsev Rule MCQ

Dehydrohalogenation of 2-chloro-2-methylbutane with sodium ethoxide in ethanol gives which alkene as the major product?

  • (A) 2-Methylbut-1-ene
  • (B) 2-Methylbut-2-ene
  • (C) 3-Methylbut-1-ene

Worked Solution: Elimination can remove a \(\beta\)-H from C-1 (giving 2-methylbut-1-ene, disubstituted) or from C-3 (giving 2-methylbut-2-ene, trisubstituted). By Zaitsev's rule, the more substituted alkene—2-methylbut-2-ene—is the major product.

Practice Question 9 Isomerism & Halogenation MCQ

Practice Question 10 Electrophilic Substitution MCQ

In the electrophilic aromatic substitution of chlorobenzene, the chlorine atom is:

  • (A) Activating and ortho/para-directing
  • (B) Deactivating and meta-directing
  • (C) Deactivating and ortho/para-directing
  • (D) Activating and meta-directing
Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Chlorine has a stronger electron-withdrawing inductive effect (\(-I\)) than its electron-donating resonance effect (\(+M\)), causing net deactivation of the benzene ring. However, resonance increases electron density at the ortho and para positions, making it ortho/para-directing.

Practice Question 11 Reactivity Order MCQ

Which of the following halides will react fastest in an \(S_N2\) reaction with \(\mathrm{OH^-}\)?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CH_3I}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CBr}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl}\)
Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: \(S_N2\) favours unhindered methyl halides over \(3^\circ\) halides (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CBr}\)) and aryl halides (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl}\), unreactive). Between \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\) and \(\mathrm{CH_3I}\), \(\mathrm{I^-}\) is a much better leaving group than \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) due to its larger size and weaker \(\mathrm{C\text{–}I}\) bond, so \(\mathrm{CH_3I}\) reacts fastest.

Practice Question 12 Environmental & Polyhalogen MCQ

Why is chloroform (\(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\)) stored in dark-coloured bottles filled completely to the brim?

10. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

Common NEET UG Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Dipole Moment Trap: Do not assume \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\) has a higher dipole moment than \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\); \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\) is slightly higher due to greater bond length.
  • Retention vs. Optical Sign: Retention of configuration does not guarantee the same \((+)\) or \((-)\) sign of optical rotation in the product.
  • Wurtz Reaction Limitation: Wurtz coupling works cleanly only for symmetrical alkanes from \(1^\circ\) alkyl halides; mixing two different halides gives a mixture of three alkanes.
  • Melting vs. Boiling Point of Dihalobenzenes: Only the melting point of p-dichlorobenzene is anomalously high (lattice symmetry); its boiling point is similar to o- and m-isomers.
  • \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\) vs. Phenol: \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\) converts aliphatic alcohols to \(\mathrm{RCl}\) cleanly, but it does not convert phenol to chlorobenzene.
Rapid Revision Checklist
  • \(S_N2\): 1-step, backside attack, inversion, \(\mathrm{CH_3X > 1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ}\), polar aprotic solvent.
  • \(S_N1\): 2-step, planar carbocation, racemisation, \(\mathrm{3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ > CH_3X}\), benzylic/allylic very fast, polar protic solvent.
  • Leaving group order (both \(S_N1\) and \(S_N2\)): \(\mathrm{R\text{–}I > R\text{–}Br > R\text{–}Cl \gg R\text{–}F}\).
  • Ambident pairs: \(\mathrm{KCN \rightarrow RCN}\) vs. \(\mathrm{AgCN \rightarrow RNC}\); \(\mathrm{KNO_2 \rightarrow R\text{–}ONO}\) vs. \(\mathrm{AgNO_2 \rightarrow R\text{–}NO_2}\).
  • Haloarenes: Resist \(S_N\) due to resonance + \(sp^2\) C; activated by o/p-\(\mathrm{NO_2}\) (not m-\(\mathrm{NO_2}\)); o/p-directing and deactivating in EAS.

Concise Final Recap

The chemistry of haloalkanes and haloarenes hinges on the polarity and strength of the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}X}\) bond. In \(sp^3\) alkyl halides, nucleophilic substitution (\(S_N1\) vs. \(S_N2\)) and \(\beta\)-elimination (Zaitsev's rule) compete based on substrate branching, nucleophile/base strength, and solvent polarity, while reactions with \(\mathrm{Mg}\) or \(\mathrm{Na}\) forge versatile \(\mathrm{C\text{–}Mg}\) and \(\mathrm{C\text{–}C}\) bonds. In \(sp^2\) aryl halides, resonance and higher \(s\)-character toughen the \(\mathrm{C\text{–}X}\) bond against nucleophilic attack unless stabilised by ortho/para electron-withdrawing groups, while directing electrophilic substitution to the ortho and para positions.
