1. Introduction and Clinical Significance

The replacement of one or more hydrogen atoms in aliphatic or aromatic hydrocarbons by halogen atoms (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine) produces haloalkanes (alkyl halides) and haloarenes (aryl halides), respectively. In haloalkanes, the halogen is bonded to an \(sp^3\)-hybridised tetrahedral carbon atom, whereas in haloarenes, the halogen is attached directly to an \(sp^2\)-hybridised planar aromatic carbon atom.

These organic halides serve as non-polar solvents, versatile synthetic intermediates, and biologically active pharmaceuticals. Several specific compounds highlighted in medical biochemistry and pharmacology frequently appear in NEET UG questions:

  • Chloramphenicol: A chlorine-containing natural antibiotic synthesized by soil microorganisms; highly effective in the clinical treatment of typhoid fever.
  • Thyroxine: An iodine-containing hormone secreted by the thyroid gland; deficiency of thyroxine leads to enlargement of the thyroid gland, known as goiter.
  • Chloroquine: A synthetic halogenatedquinoline derivative used worldwide in the prophylaxis and therapy of malaria.
  • Halothane (\(\mathrm{CF_3-CHBrCl}\)): A volatile synthetic halogenated hydrocarbon employed widely as a safe, non-explosive general inhalation anaesthetic during surgical procedures.
  • Fluorinated Hydrocarbons: Several fully fluorinated synthetic compounds are biocompatible with high oxygen-dissolving capacities and are clinically investigated as potential blood substitutes in surgery.
Environmental Persistence Note
Due to the great strength and stability of carbon-halogen bonds, synthetic halogenated compounds resist metabolic degradation by soil bacteria, leading to environmental bioaccumulation and ecological hazards.

2. Classification of Halogen Derivatives

Halogen compounds are classified on two major systematic criteria:

2.1 Classification Based on Number of Halogen Atoms

Depending on whether molecules contain one, two, three, or more halogen atoms, they are classified as monohalo, dihalo, trihalo, or polyhalo compounds:

  • Monohaloalkane / Monohaloarene: e.g., Ethyl bromide (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5Br}\)), Chlorobenzene (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl}\)).
  • Dihaloalkane:
    • Geminal dihalides (gem-dihalides or alkylidene halides): Both halogen atoms reside on the same carbon atom, e.g., Ethylidene chloride (\(\mathrm{CH_3-CHCl_2}\), 1,1-dichloroethane).
    • Vicinal dihalides (vic-dihalides or alkylene dihalides): Halogen atoms reside on two adjacent carbon atoms, e.g., Ethylene dichloride (\(\mathrm{Cl-CH_2-CH_2-Cl}\), 1,2-dichloroethane).
  • Dihaloarene / Trihaloarene: e.g., 1,2-dichlorobenzene, 1,3,5-tribromobenzene.

2.2 Classification Based on Hybridisation of the Halogen-Bearing Carbon

Halogen compounds are divided based on whether the carbon bonded to halogen is \(sp^3\)- or \(sp^2\)-hybridised:

4. Nature of \(\mathrm{C-X}\) Bond and Physical Properties

Halogens are distinctly more electronegative than carbon. Consequently, the shared pair of electrons in the \(\mathrm{C-X}\) \(\sigma\)-bond is displaced towards the halogen atom, imparting a partial positive charge (\(\delta^+\)) to carbon and a partial negative charge (\(\delta^-\)) to the halogen atom:

\[ \mathrm{-\overset{\delta^+}{C}-\overset{\delta^-}{X}} \]

Down Group 17 (\(\mathrm{F \rightarrow Cl \rightarrow Br \rightarrow I}\)), atomic size increases progressively while electronegativity decreases. Consequently, the \(\mathrm{C-X}\) bond length increases, and \(\mathrm{C-X}\) bond enthalpy decreases.

Halomethane Molecule Bond Length (pm) Bond Enthalpy (\(\mathrm{kJ\cdot mol^{-1}}\)) Dipole Moment (Debye, D)
\(\mathrm{CH_3-F}\) 139 452 1.847
\(\mathrm{CH_3-Cl}\) 178 351 1.860 (Maximum)
\(\mathrm{CH_3-Br}\) 193 293 1.830
\(\mathrm{CH_3-I}\) 214 234 1.636
High-Yield NEET Fact: Dipole Moment Anomaly
Dipole moment is the product of charge separation (\(q\)) and bond distance (\(d\)): \(\mu = q \times d\). Although fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine (yielding higher \(q\)), the \(\mathrm{C-Cl}\) bond distance is significantly longer (178 pm vs 139 pm). This greater distance overcomes the slightly smaller charge separation, making the dipole moment of \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\) (\(1.860\,\mathrm{D}\)) strictly greater than that of \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\) (\(1.847\,\mathrm{D}\)).
Trend: \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl > CH_3F > CH_3Br > CH_3I}\).

4.1 Boiling Points

  • Physical state at room temperature: Methyl chloride (\(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\)), methyl bromide (\(\mathrm{CH_3Br}\)), ethyl chloride (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2Cl}\)), and chlorofluoromethanes are gases. Higher members are colourless liquids or solids.
  • Comparison with parent hydrocarbons: Alkyl halides possess significantly higher boiling points than hydrocarbons of comparable molecular mass because of polar \(\mathrm{C-X}\) bonds generating strong dipole-dipole interactions in addition to higher van der Waals dispersion forces.
  • Effect of halogen size: For a given alkyl group, boiling points decrease in the order: \[ \mathrm{R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F} \] This occurs because atomic size and polarisable electron clouds increase from \(\mathrm{F}\) to \(\mathrm{I}\), strengthening intermolecular van der Waals forces.
  • Effect of chain branching: Among isomeric haloalkanes, boiling point decreases systematically with increasing branchiness: \[ \mathrm{\underset{(375\,K)}{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Br} > \underset{(364\,K)}{CH_3CH_2CH(Br)CH_3} > \underset{(346\,K)}{(CH_3)_3CBr}} \] Branching causes the molecule to adopt a more spherical shape, decreasing total surface area and reducing attractive van der Waals contact area.

4.2 Melting Points and Symmetry of Isomeric Dihalobenzenes

For isomeric dihalobenzenes (e.g., dichlorobenzenes), the boiling points are remarkably close: ortho (\(453\,\mathrm{K}\)), meta (\(446\,\mathrm{K}\)), and para (\(448\,\mathrm{K}\)). However, the melting point of the para-isomer is dramatically higher:

\[ \text{Melting Points: } p\text{-Dichlorobenzene (}323\,\mathrm{K}\text{)} \gg o\text{-Dichlorobenzene (}256\,\mathrm{K}\text{)} > m\text{-Dichlorobenzene (}249\,\mathrm{K}\text{)} \]

Explanation: The symmetrical linear structure of the para-isomer allows its molecules to pack tightly and uniformly into the crystal lattice, maximising intermolecular attractions and requiring greater thermal energy to disrupt the crystal solid.

4.3 Density and Solubility

  • Density: Alkyl fluorides and monochloroalkanes are generally lighter than water. In contrast, alkyl bromides, alkyl iodides, and polyhaloalkanes are denser than water. Density increases with increasing carbon count, number of halogen atoms, and atomic mass of the halogen.
  • Solubility in water: Haloalkanes are only very slightly soluble (virtually immiscible) in water. Even though they are polar, haloalkanes cannot form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. The energy released when new dipole-dipole attractions form between haloalkane and water molecules is insufficient to overcome both the existing halogen-halogen van der Waals attractions and the strong hydrogen-bonding network between water molecules.
  • Solubility in organic solvents: Readily soluble in non-polar organic solvents (benzene, ether, alcohol, chloroform) because solute-solvent interactions are similar in strength to the intermolecular forces being broken.

5. Methods of Preparation of Haloalkanes

5.1 From Alcohols

The replacement of the alcoholic hydroxyl (\(-\mathrm{OH}\)) group by a halogen atom is the most common laboratory approach:

1. Halogen acids (\(\mathrm{HX}\)):

\[ \mathrm{R-OH} + \mathrm{HCl} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{anhydrous\ ZnCl_2}} \mathrm{R-Cl} + \mathrm{H_2O} \quad (\text{Lucas Test}) \] \[ \mathrm{R-OH} + \mathrm{NaBr} + \mathrm{H_2SO_4} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R-Br} + \mathrm{NaHSO_4} + \mathrm{H_2O} \]

The reactivity of alcohols with a given haloacid follows the order: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\). Concentrated \(\mathrm{HCl}\) and anhydrous \(\mathrm{ZnCl_2}\) (Lucas reagent) reacts with \(3^\circ\) alcohols immediately at room temperature (producing instant cloudiness/turbidity), \(2^\circ\) alcohols react within 5 minutes, while \(1^\circ\) alcohols require heating.

2. Phosphorus halides (\(\mathrm{PX_3}\) and \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\)):

\[ 3\mathrm{R-OH} + \mathrm{PX_3} \longrightarrow 3\mathrm{R-X} + \mathrm{H_3PO_3} \quad (\mathrm{X = Cl, Br;}\ \mathrm{PBr_3/PI_3}\text{ formed } in\ situ\text{ from red P + } \mathrm{X_2}) \] \[ \mathrm{R-OH} + \mathrm{PCl_5} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R-Cl} + \mathrm{POCl_3} + \mathrm{HCl\uparrow} \]

3. Thionyl chloride (\(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\) - Darzens Procedure):

\[ \mathrm{R-OH} + \mathrm{SOCl_2} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R-Cl} + \mathrm{SO_2\uparrow} + \mathrm{HCl\uparrow} \]
Why is \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\) the Preferred Reagent?
Thionyl chloride (\(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\)) is preferred over phosphorus halides because both co-products, sulfur dioxide (\(\mathrm{SO_2}\)) and hydrogen chloride (\(\mathrm{HCl}\)), are escapable gases, leaving the pure alkyl chloride without requiring tedious purification.
Why \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) is NEVER used during preparation of Alkyl Iodide from \(\mathrm{ROH + KI}\)
Sulfuric acid is a powerful oxidizing agent. It oxidizes potassium iodide (\(\mathrm{KI}\)) to hydrogen iodide (\(\mathrm{HI}\)), and immediately oxidizes the formed \(\mathrm{HI}\) further into molecular iodine (\(\mathrm{I_2}\)): \[ 2\mathrm{HI} + \mathrm{H_2SO_4} \longrightarrow \mathrm{I_2} + \mathrm{SO_2} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} \] Thus, \(\mathrm{HI}\) is destroyed and cannot react with alcohol. Instead, a non-oxidizing acid like 95% orthophosphoric acid (\(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)) must be used: \[ \mathrm{R-OH} + \mathrm{KI} + \mathrm{H_3PO_4} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{R-I} + \mathrm{KH_2PO_4} + \mathrm{H_2O} \]
Aryl Halides Cannot Be Prepared from Phenols
Phenols do not react with \(\mathrm{HX}\), \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\), or \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\) to yield aryl halides. In phenols, the unshared electron pair on oxygen is delocalised into the \(\pi\)-system of the aromatic ring, imparting a partial double-bond character to the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond, making it extremely strong and resistant to cleavage.

5.2 From Hydrocarbons

  1. Free-radical halogenation of alkanes: Chlorination or bromination of alkanes in ultraviolet light or heat yields complex mixtures of isomeric mono- and polyhaloalkanes that have close boiling points and are difficult to separate pure. \[ \mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3} \xrightarrow[h\nu]{\mathrm{Cl_2}} \mathrm{\underset{(1\text{-chlorobutane})}{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Cl}} + \mathrm{\underset{(2\text{-chlorobutane})}{CH_3CH_2CH(Cl)CH_3}} \]
  2. Electrophilic addition of \(\mathrm{HX}\) to alkenes: Follows Markovnikov's rule (electrophile \(\mathrm{H^+}\) adds to the carbon possessing more hydrogen atoms to yield the more stable carbocation intermediate): \[ \mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH_2} + \mathrm{HI} \longrightarrow \mathrm{\underset{\text{Major (2-iodopropane)}}{CH_3-CH(I)-CH_3}} + \mathrm{\underset{\text{Minor (1-iodopropane)}}{CH_3-CH_2-CH_2I}} \] In the presence of organic peroxides, addition of \(\mathrm{HBr}\) (only \(\mathrm{HBr}\), not \(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{HI}\)) occurs via anti-Markovnikov free-radical addition (the Kharasch effect).
  3. Addition of halogens (Test for Unsaturation): Addition of reddish-brown bromine dissolved in \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) to alkenes forms a colourless vicinal dibromide. The discharge of the reddish-brown colour serves as a standard diagnostic laboratory test for unsaturation. \[ \mathrm{CH_2=CH_2} + \mathrm{Br_2} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{CCl_4}} \mathrm{\underset{\text{vic-dibromide (colourless)}}{Br-CH_2-CH_2-Br}} \]

5.3 Halogen Exchange Reactions

1. Finkelstein Reaction (Synthesis of Alkyl Iodides):

\[ \mathrm{R-X} + \mathrm{NaI} \xrightarrow{\text{dry acetone}} \mathrm{R-I} + \mathrm{NaX}\downarrow \quad (\mathrm{X = Cl, Br}) \]

Driving Force: Sodium chloride (\(\mathrm{NaCl}\)) and sodium bromide (\(\mathrm{NaBr}\)) are insoluble in dry acetone and precipitate out, shifting the equilibrium in the forward direction according to Le Chatelier’s principle.

2. Swarts Reaction (Synthesis of Alkyl Fluorides):

\[ \mathrm{CH_3-Br} + \mathrm{AgF} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{CH_3-F} + \mathrm{AgBr}\downarrow \]

Heating alkyl chlorides or bromides with heavy metal fluorides such as \(\mathrm{AgF}\), \(\mathrm{Hg_2F_2}\), \(\mathrm{CoF_2}\), or \(\mathrm{SbF_3}\) cleanly produces alkyl fluorides.

6. Methods of Preparation of Haloarenes

6.1 Nuclear Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution of Arenes

Direct chlorination and bromination of benzene or alkylbenzenes occurs smoothly in the presence of Lewis acid catalysts (anhydrous \(\mathrm{FeCl_3}\), \(\mathrm{FeBr_3}\), or \(\mathrm{Fe}\) metal) in the dark:

\[ \mathrm{C_6H_5CH_3} + \mathrm{Cl_2} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Fe,\ dark}} \mathrm{\underset{o\text{-chlorotoluene}}{o\text{-Cl-C}_6H_4CH_3}} + \mathrm{\underset{p\text{-chlorotoluene (Major)}}{p\text{-Cl-C}_6H_4CH_3}} \]

The ortho and para isomers can be easily separated by fractional crystallization because of the substantial difference in their melting points.

  • Iodination: Aromatic iodination with \(\mathrm{I_2}\) is reversible. To drive the reaction forward, it must be performed in the presence of strong oxidising agents like \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) or \(\mathrm{HIO_4}\), which oxidize the byproduct \(\mathrm{HI}\) to \(\mathrm{I_2}\).
  • Fluorination: Fluoroarenes cannot be prepared by this method due to the violent, explosive reactivity of fluorine gas.

6.2 From Diazonium Salts (Sandmeyer Reaction)

When a primary aromatic amine (aniline) dissolved in cold aqueous mineral acid (\(\mathrm{HCl}\) or \(\mathrm{HBr}\)) is treated with sodium nitrite (\(\mathrm{NaNO_2}\)) at \(273\text{--}278\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(0\text{--}5^\circ\mathrm{C}\)), a diazonium salt is formed:

\[ \mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2} + \mathrm{NaNO_2} + 2\mathrm{HX} \xrightarrow{273\text{--}278\,\mathrm{K}} \mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+ X^-} + \mathrm{NaX} + 2\mathrm{H_2O} \]

Mixing the freshly prepared diazonium salt with cuprous chloride (\(\mathrm{Cu_2Cl_2}\)) or cuprous bromide (\(\mathrm{Cu_2Br_2}\)) causes replacement of the diazonium group by \(-\mathrm{Cl}\) or \(-\mathrm{Br}\), liberating nitrogen gas:

\[ \mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+ Cl^-} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Cu_2Cl_2 / HCl}} \mathrm{C_6H_5Cl} + \mathrm{N_2\uparrow} \quad (\text{Sandmeyer Reaction}) \] \[ \mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+ Br^-} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Cu_2Br_2 / HBr}} \mathrm{C_6H_5Br} + \mathrm{N_2\uparrow} \]
Synthesis of Iodobenzene Does Not Require Cuprous Halide!
Preparation of iodobenzene does not require cuprous iodide (\(\mathrm{Cu_2I_2}\)). Shaking the diazonium salt directly with aqueous potassium iodide (\(\mathrm{KI}\)) smoothly produces iodobenzene in high yield: \[ \mathrm{C_6H_5N_2^+ Cl^-} + \mathrm{KI} \longrightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_5I} + \mathrm{KCl} + \mathrm{N_2\uparrow} \]

7. Chemical Reactions of Haloalkanes

Haloalkanes participate in three principal categories of chemical reactions:

  1. Nucleophilic substitution reactions
  2. Elimination reactions (\(\beta\)-elimination)
  3. Reactions with metals (formation of organometallics)

7.1 Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions (\(\mathrm{S_N}\))

Ambident Nucleophiles in Detail

Ambident nucleophiles are nucleophiles possessing two distinct nucleophilic centres through either of which they can coordinate:

  • Cyanide ion (\(\mathrm{CN^-}\)): It is a resonance hybrid: \(\mathrm{^-\!:\!C\equiv N: \longleftrightarrow :\!C=\ddot{N}:^-}\). With \(\mathrm{KCN}\), which is predominantly ionic, cyanide ions are free in solution. Although both \(\mathrm{C}\) and \(\mathrm{N}\) can donate electron pairs, attack occurs preferentially through the carbon atom because the resulting \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond is thermodynamically much stronger and more stable than a \(\mathrm{C-N}\) bond. In contrast, \(\mathrm{AgCN}\) is predominantly covalent; silver is bonded to carbon, leaving the lone pair on nitrogen free to initiate nucleophilic attack, yielding alkyl isocyanide (\(\mathrm{R-NC}\)) as the primary product.
  • Nitrite ion (\(\mathrm{NO_2^-}\)): With ionic \(\mathrm{KNO_2}\), the negative charge resides predominantly on oxygen; attack through oxygen generates alkyl nitrites (\(\mathrm{R-O-N=O}\)). With covalent \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\), the \(\mathrm{Ag-O}\) bond is covalent, so the lone pair on nitrogen attacks the alkyl group, producing nitroalkanes (\(\mathrm{R-NO_2}\)).

7.3 Stereochemical Principles of Nucleophilic Substitution

  • Plane Polarised Light and Optical Activity: Ordinary light vibrates in all planes perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Passing it through a Nicol prism produces plane-polarised light vibrating in only one plane. Compounds that rotate this plane are optically active. Clockwise rotation is termed dextrorotatory (designated by \(+\) or \(d\)), while anticlockwise rotation is termed laevorotatory (designated by \(-\) or \(l\)).
  • Chirality and Stereocentres: A tetrahedral carbon bonded to four distinct atoms or groups is called an asymmetric carbon or stereocentre. A molecule that is non-superimposable on its mirror image lacks reflection symmetry and is termed chiral. Chirality is the essential condition for optical activity.
  • Enantiomers: Stereoisomers related to each other as non-superimposable mirror images. They possess identical physical properties (boiling point, melting point, density, refractive index) but rotate plane-polarised light by equal magnitudes in opposite directions.
  • Racemic Modification (Racemate): An equimolar (50:50) mixture of two enantiomers has zero net optical rotation due to external compensation (the rotation caused by molecules of one isomer is exactly cancelled by the equal and opposite rotation of the other isomer). Represented by the prefix \((\pm)\) or \(dl\), e.g., \((\pm)\)-butan-2-ol. The process of converting an enantiomer into a racemic mixture is termed racemisation.
  • Retention vs. Inversion: If no bond to the stereocentre is broken during a reaction, the relative spatial arrangement is preserved (retention of configuration). In \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) reactions, attack from the side strictly opposite to the leaving group results in 100% inversion of configuration (e.g., \((-)\)-2-bromooctane reacts with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to produce \((+)\)-octan-2-ol). In \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) reactions, nucleophilic attack on the flat carbocation from both faces yields racemisation.

7.4 Elimination Reactions (\(\beta\)-Elimination and Saytzeff Rule)

When a haloalkane containing at least one \(\beta\)-hydrogen is heated with a strong base such as alcoholic potassium hydroxide (\(\mathrm{alc.\ KOH}\)), a proton is removed from the \(\beta\)-carbon and the halogen departs from the \(\alpha\)-carbon, generating an alkene:

\[ \mathrm{R-CH_2-CH_2-X} + \mathrm{KOH(alc.)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathrm{R-CH=CH_2} + \mathrm{KX} + \mathrm{H_2O} \]

Saytzeff (Zaitsev) Rule: When dehydrohalogenation can yield more than one isomeric alkene, the preferred major product is the more highly substituted alkene (i.e., the alkene carrying the greater number of alkyl substituents attached to the doubly bonded carbons):

\[ \mathrm{\underset{\text{2-Bromopentane}}{CH_3-CH_2-CH_2-CH(Br)-CH_3}} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{alc.\ KOH,\ \Delta}} \mathrm{\underset{\text{Pent-2-ene (81% - Major)}}{CH_3-CH_2-CH=CH-CH_3}} + \mathrm{\underset{\text{Pent-1-ene (19% - Minor)}}{CH_3-CH_2-CH_2-CH=CH_2}} \]
Competition: Elimination vs Substitution
Alkyl halides with \(\beta\)-hydrogens encounter competition between substitution and elimination:
  • Aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\): Water has a high dielectric constant and hydrates hydroxide ions (\(\mathrm{OH^-}\)), reducing their basicity while retaining their nucleophilicity. Hence, substitution (alcohol formation) predominates.
  • Alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\): In alcohol, potassium ethoxide (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5O^-K^+}\)) forms in equilibrium. Ethoxide is a much stronger base than hydroxide and abstracts a \(\beta\)-proton preferentially, making elimination (alkene formation) the major pathway.
  • Steric Factors: Bulky bases (e.g., potassium tert-butoxide) and sterically hindered \(3^\circ\) halides strongly favor elimination over substitution.

7.5 Reactions with Metals

1. Formation of Grignard Reagents (Organomagnesium Halides):

\[ \mathrm{R-X} + \mathrm{Mg} \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} \mathrm{R-Mg-X} \]

In a Grignard reagent, the \(\mathrm{C-Mg}\) bond is covalent but highly polar (\(\mathrm{\overset{\delta-}{R}-\overset{\delta+}{Mg}\overset{\delta-}{X}}\)), while the \(\mathrm{Mg-X}\) bond is ionic.

Absolute Requirement for Anhydrous Conditions: Grignard reagents are exceptionally reactive bases. They react rapidly with any proton source (water, alcohols, amines, carboxylic acids) to produce alkanes: \[ \mathrm{R-Mg-X} + \mathrm{H_2O} \longrightarrow \mathrm{R-H} + \mathrm{Mg(OH)X} \] Even traces of atmospheric moisture destroy the reagent. Consequently, Grignard reactions must strictly be conducted under dry ether conditions.

2. Wurtz Reaction:

\[ 2\mathrm{R-X} + 2\mathrm{Na} \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} \mathrm{R-R} + 2\mathrm{NaX} \]

Couples two alkyl radicals to produce symmetrical alkanes having twice the carbon count of the starting alkyl halide.

8. Chemical Reactions of Haloarenes

8.1 Low Reactivity of Haloarenes Towards Nucleophilic Substitution

Aryl halides are exceptionally unreactive toward nucleophilic substitution compared to haloalkanes. Four distinct electronic and steric factors account for this stability:

  1. Resonance Effect: The unshared electron pairs on the halogen atom enter into conjugation with the \(\pi\)-electrons of the benzene ring. Resonating canonical structures confer a partial double-bond character upon the carbon-halogen (\(\mathrm{C-X}\)) bond. Cleaving this bond requires significantly more energy than cleaving a pure single bond in haloalkanes.
  2. Difference in Carbon Hybridisation: In haloalkanes, the halogen is attached to an \(sp^3\)-hybridised carbon (\(25\%\) \(s\)-character), whereas in haloarenes, it is bonded to an \(sp^2\)-hybridised ring carbon (\(33.3\%\) \(s\)-character). Greater \(s\)-character makes the carbon atom more electronegative, drawing the bonding electrons more tightly toward the nucleus. Thus, the \(\mathrm{C-Cl}\) bond length in chlorobenzene is shorter (\(169\,\mathrm{pm}\)) and stronger than that in chloroethane (\(177\,\mathrm{pm}\)).
  3. Instability of Phenyl Cation: If self-ionisation were to occur, the resulting phenyl cation (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5^+}\)) would have its positive charge residing in an \(sp^2\) orbital perpendicular to the \(\pi\)-cloud, precluding any resonance stabilisation. Hence, the \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) mechanism is completely ruled out.
  4. Electrostatic Repulsion: The electron-rich incoming nucleophile experiences repulsive forces as it approaches the \(\pi\)-electron clouds of the aromatic ring.
Resonance canonical forms of chlorobenzene illustrating partial double-bond character of C—Cl and delocalisation of negative charge specifically into ortho and para positions.

8.2 Nucleophilic Displacement of Halogen: Activating Effect of \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\) Groups

Under drastic conditions (Dow Process), chlorobenzene can be hydrolysed to phenol by heating with aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) at \(623\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(300\,\mathrm{atm}\):

\[ \mathrm{C_6H_5Cl} \xrightarrow[\text{(ii) }\mathrm{H^+}]{\text{(i) } \mathrm{NaOH,\ 623\,K,\ 300\,atm}} \mathrm{C_6H_5OH} \]

However, the introduction of strong electron-withdrawing nitro (\(-\mathrm{NO_2}\)) groups at ortho- and para-positions dramatically increases the reactivity of haloarenes toward nucleophilic substitution (\(\mathrm{S_NAr}\)):

  • p-Nitrochlorobenzene: Reacts smoothly at \(443\,\mathrm{K}\) with dilute aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to give \(p\)-nitrophenol.
  • 2,4-Dinitrochlorobenzene: Reacts at \(368\,\mathrm{K}\) with aqueous alkali to yield 2,4-dinitrophenol.
  • 2,4,6-Trinitrochlorobenzene (Picryl chloride): Hydrolyses readily upon simply warming with pure water to produce 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid).
Why Does \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\) Activate Only at ortho and para Positions?

The nucleophilic aromatic substitution occurs through the formation of a resonance-stabilised carbanion intermediate (Meisenheimer complex). Examination of the resonance structures reveals that the negative charge is delocalised exclusively to the ortho and para carbons with respect to the halogen. When an electron-withdrawing \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) group is located at an ortho or para position, the negative charge is directly placed on the carbon attached to the strongly electronegative nitrogen atom, which powerfully disperses the charge into its oxygen atoms:

\[ \mathrm{>\!C^- - N^+( = O) - O^- \longleftrightarrow >\!C = N^+( - O^-) - O^-} \]

In contrast, when the \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\) group is at the meta-position, none of the resonating canonical structures ever bears the carbanionic negative charge on the carbon carrying the nitro group. Consequently, a meta-nitro group cannot directly stabilise the negative charge through resonance, exerting virtually no activating effect on nucleophilic substitution.

8.3 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution Reactions of Haloarenes

Halogen atoms attached to an aromatic ring present an interesting duality:

  • Orientation: Halogens are ortho, para-directing because their \(+R\) (resonance) electron-donating effect increases electron density specifically at the ortho and para positions relative to the meta position.
  • Reactivity: Halogens are net deactivating because their powerful \(-I\) (inductive) electron-withdrawing effect withdraws overall electron density from the ring. Consequently, electrophilic aromatic substitutions on haloarenes proceed more slowly and require more forcing conditions than those on benzene.
  • Steric Predominance: Because of steric hindrance at the ortho position, the para-isomer is overwhelmingly the major product.

8.4 Reaction of Haloarenes with Metals

1. Wurtz-Fittig Reaction:

\[ \mathrm{Ar-X} + 2\mathrm{Na} + \mathrm{R-X} \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} \mathrm{Ar-R} + 2\mathrm{NaX} \]

A mixture of an alkyl halide and an aryl halide treated with sodium in dry ether produces an alkylarene (e.g., bromobenzene + bromomethane + \(\mathrm{Na}\) gives toluene).

2. Fittig Reaction:

\[ 2\mathrm{Ar-X} + 2\mathrm{Na} \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} \mathrm{Ar-Ar} + 2\mathrm{NaX} \]

Two molecules of aryl halide couple in the presence of sodium metal in dry ether to yield a biaryl (e.g., chlorobenzene yields diphenyl / biphenyl).

9. Polyhalogen Compounds and Environmental Impact

Carbon compounds containing more than one halogen atom are designated as polyhalogen compounds. Many possess significant industrial, pharmaceutical, and agricultural utility, but several pose grave toxicological and environmental hazards.

9.1 Dichloromethane (Methylene Chloride, \(\mathrm{CH_2Cl_2}\))

  • Applications: Widely employed as an industrial solvent for paint removal, as an aerosol propellant, as a process solvent in drug manufacture, and as a metal degreaser.
  • Toxicology: Highly hazardous to the human central nervous system (CNS). Exposure to lower levels in air induces slight hearing and visual impairment; higher concentrations cause dizziness, nausea, paresthesia, and numbness in extremities. Direct ocular contact burns the cornea.

9.2 Trichloromethane (Chloroform, \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\))

  • Applications: Solvent for fats, alkaloids, and iodine. Extensively consumed in the industrial synthesis of Freon refrigerant \(\mathrm{R-22}\). Historically used as a general inhalation anaesthetic.
  • Physiological Effects: CNS depressant. Short exposure to \(900\,\mathrm{ppm}\) causes dizziness, fatigue, and headache. Chronic exposure induces serious hepatic and renal toxicity because chloroform is metabolised in the liver to phosgene.
  • Oxidation and Storage: Chloroform oxidises slowly in the presence of air and light to produce carbonyl chloride (phosgene, \(\mathrm{COCl_2}\)), an intensely poisonous gas: \[ 2\mathrm{CHCl_3} + \mathrm{O_2} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} 2\mathrm{COCl_2} + 2\mathrm{HCl} \] Safety storage requirement: Chloroform must strictly be stored in completely filled, airtight, dark amber bottles to completely exclude light and air.

9.3 Triiodomethane (Iodoform, \(\mathrm{CHI_3}\))

  • Properties & Use: Bright yellow crystalline solid with a persistent, pungent medicinal odour. Formerly utilized as an antiseptic dressing for wounds.
  • Key Fact: The antiseptic action is due entirely to the liberation of free elemental iodine upon contact with tissues, not due to the intact iodoform molecule itself. Due to its obnoxious odour, it has been replaced by modern iodophor formulations.

9.4 Tetrachloromethane (Carbon Tetrachloride, \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\))

  • Applications: Feedstock for chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), industrial solvent for degreasing, and historically used as a commercial fire extinguisher under the trade name Pyrene.
  • Health Hazards: Exposure causes liver cancer, dizziness, nausea, irreversible nerve cell damage, coma, and fatal cardiac arrhythmias.
  • Environmental Impact: Upon atmospheric release, volatile \(\mathrm{CCl_4}\) diffuses into the stratosphere, where photolytic breakdown generates reactive chlorine radicals that catalyze the catalytic destruction of the stratospheric ozone layer, leading to elevated terrestrial UV exposure.

9.5 Freons (Chlorofluorocarbons, CFCs)

  • Definition & Properties: Chlorofluorocarbon derivatives of methane and ethane. Highly stable, unreactive, non-corrosive, non-toxic, easily liquefiable gases.
  • Freon-12 (\(\mathrm{CCl_2F_2}\)): Manufactured commercially from carbon tetrachloride via the Swarts reaction: \[ \mathrm{CCl_4} + 2\mathrm{SbF_3} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{SbCl_5}} \mathrm{CCl_2F_2} + 2\mathrm{SbCl_3} \]
  • Atmospheric Depletion: Released freons diffuse unchanged through the troposphere into the stratosphere, where intense UV radiation photolyses \(\mathrm{C-Cl}\) bonds to generate chlorine free radicals (\(\mathrm{Cl^\bullet}\)), which initiate destructive catalytic ozone breakdown cycles.

9.6 \(\mathrm{p,p'}\)-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT)

  • History: First synthesized in 1873. Its powerful insecticidal efficacy was discovered in 1939 by Swiss chemist Paul Müller (who was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1948). Extensively used during and after World War II to combat malaria mosquitoes and typhus-carrying lice.
  • Structure: 1,1,1-trichloro-2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)ethane.
  • Ecological Toxicity & Ban: Highly stable chemically and exceptionally fat-soluble. Animals metabolise DDT very slowly; it undergoes biomagnification through the trophic food web, accumulating in fatty adipose tissues. In birds, DDT disrupts calcium metabolism, causing severe eggshell thinning and population collapse. Consequently, DDT was formally banned in the United States in 1973.
Chemical structure of p,p'-DDT: 2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)-1,1,1-trichloroethane.

10. In-Depth Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Rationalizing SN2 vs SN1 Reactivity Profiles

Problem: Arrange the four isomeric bromobutanes in order of increasing reactivity toward:
(a) \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) displacement
(b) \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) solvolysis

Solution:

The four constitutional isomers of \(\mathrm{C_4H_9Br}\) are:

  • \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Br}\) (1-Bromobutane, primary, unbranched at \(\beta\))
  • \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_2CHCH_2Br}\) (1-Bromo-2-methylpropane, primary, \(\beta\)-branched)
  • \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH(Br)CH_3}\) (2-Bromobutane, secondary)
  • \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CBr}\) (2-Bromo-2-methylpropane, tertiary)

(a) \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) Reactivity Order:
Controlled by steric hindrance around the electrophilic carbon. Increasing alkyl bulk dramatically blocks back-side nucleophilic approach: \[ \mathrm{(CH_3)_3CBr < CH_3CH_2CH(Br)CH_3 < (CH_3)_2CHCH_2Br < CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Br} \] Note: 1-Bromobutane reacts faster than isobutyl bromide because isobutyl bromide possesses a bulky methyl substituent on the adjacent \(\beta\)-carbon.

(b) \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) Reactivity Order:
Controlled by carbocation intermediate stability: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\): \[ \mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_2Br < (CH_3)_2CHCH_2Br < CH_3CH_2CH(Br)CH_3 < (CH_3)_3CBr} \] The tertiary carbocation \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C^+}\) has 9 hyperconjugative \(\alpha\)-hydrogens and maximum \(+I\) dispersal, forming most rapidly.

Worked Example 2: Stereochemical Trajectory of Substitution

Problem: An optically pure sample of \((-)\)-2-bromooctane is reacted with aqueous sodium hydroxide.
(a) Predict the stereochemical outcome and optical activity of the resulting octan-2-ol.
(b) Explain how the mechanism dictates this outcome.

Solution:

(a) Outcome: The product formed is pure \((+)\)-octan-2-ol. The reaction proceeds with complete inversion of configuration, and the product is optically active.

(b) Mechanism: Because 2-bromooctane is an uncrowded secondary halide and hydroxide ion (\(\mathrm{OH^-}\)) is a strong nucleophile, the reaction proceeds predominantly through the \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) mechanism. The hydroxide ion attacks the chiral carbon from the side exactly opposite (at \(180^\circ\)) to the departing bromide leaving group. During the transition state, the three remaining groups (\(-\mathrm{H}\), \(-\mathrm{CH_3}\), \(-\mathrm{C_6H_{13}}\)) pass through a planar arrangement, inverting to the opposite side like an umbrella caught in a gale. Consequently, \((-)\)-2-bromooctane yields exclusively \((+)\)-octan-2-ol.

Worked Example 3: Multi-Step Organic Transformation Scheme

Problem: Identify the missing reagents and structural intermediates \(\mathrm{A, B, C, D}\) in the following sequential synthetic series:

\[ \mathrm{Cyclohexyl\ bromide} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Mg,\ dry\ ether}} \mathbf{A} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H_2O}} \mathbf{B} \] \[ \mathbf{C} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Mg,\ dry\ ether}} \mathbf{D} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{D_2O}} \mathrm{CH_3-CH(D)-CH_3} \]
Solution:
  1. Reaction of cyclohexyl bromide with magnesium in dry ether forms the Grignard reagent: \[ \mathbf{A} = \mathrm{Cyclohexylmagnesium\ bromide\ (C_6H_{11}MgBr)} \]
  2. Hydrolysis of cyclohexylmagnesium bromide with water donates a proton to the nucleophilic cyclohexyl ring: \[ \mathbf{B} = \mathrm{Cyclohexane\ (C_6H_{12})} \]
  3. The deuterated product is 2-deuteropropane (\(\mathrm{CH_3-CH(D)-CH_3}\)). Since deuterium (\(\mathrm{D}\)) comes from \(\mathrm{D_2O}\), the organometallic intermediate \(\mathbf{D}\) must have its \(\mathrm{-MgX}\) bond at carbon-2: \[ \mathbf{D} = \mathrm{Isopropylmagnesium\ halide\ ((CH_3)_2CH-MgX)} \]
  4. Therefore, the precursor alkyl halide \(\mathbf{C}\) must be: \[ \mathbf{C} = \mathrm{2\text{-}Bromopropane\ or\ 2\text{-}Halopropane\ ((CH_3)_2CH-Br)} \]

12. Common NEET Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Critical Traps to Avoid in NEET UG
  • Dipole Moment Order: Never assume \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\) has the highest dipole moment based solely on fluorine's electronegativity. \(\mathrm{CH_3Cl}\) (\(1.860\,\mathrm{D}\)) is higher than \(\mathrm{CH_3F}\) (\(1.847\,\mathrm{D}\)).
  • Lucas Test: Lucas reagent is conc. \(\mathrm{HCl} + \text{anhydrous } \mathrm{ZnCl_2}\). Turbidity appears immediately for \(3^\circ\) alcohols, in 5 minutes for \(2^\circ\) alcohols, and only upon heating for \(1^\circ\) alcohols.
  • KCN vs AgCN: \(\mathrm{KCN}\) gives cyanides (\(\mathrm{R-CN}\)); \(\mathrm{AgCN}\) gives isocyanides (\(\mathrm{R-NC}\)).
  • KNO2 vs AgNO2: \(\mathrm{KNO_2}\) gives alkyl nitrites (\(\mathrm{R-ONO}\)); \(\mathrm{AgNO_2}\) gives nitroalkanes (\(\mathrm{R-NO_2}\)).
  • Aqueous vs Alcoholic KOH: Aqueous \(\mathrm{KOH}\) promotes nucleophilic substitution (\(\mathrm{R-OH}\)); alcoholic \(\mathrm{KOH}\) promotes \(\beta\)-elimination (alkenes).
  • Grignard Reactions: Must be carried out under strictly anhydrous ether conditions because any active hydrogen (\(\mathrm{H_2O, ROH, RNH_2}\)) immediately converts \(\mathrm{RMgX}\) to \(\mathrm{R-H}\).
  • Iodobenzene from Diazonium Salt: No \(\mathrm{Cu_2I_2}\) is required. Simply shaking with aqueous \(\mathrm{KI}\) produces iodobenzene.
  • Halogen Directing Effect vs Reactivity: Halogens are ortho, para-directing (due to \(+R\) resonance effect) but deactivating (due to strong \(-I\) inductive effect).
  • Melting Point of Dihalobenzenes: para-Dichlorobenzene melts at a much higher temperature than ortho or meta due to crystal lattice symmetry.

Rapid Revision Checklist

  • ☑ Know all IUPAC locant priority rules for haloalkenes and substituted arenes.
  • ☑ Master the mechanistic differences between \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) (carbocation, two steps, racemisation) and \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) (concerted, transition state, inversion).
  • ☑ Understand Saytzeff's rule: more substituted alkene = major product.
  • ☑ Understand the electronic basis for the inertness of aryl and vinyl halides.
  • ☑ Memorize the physiological and environmental impacts of chloroform (phosgene), carbon tetrachloride (ozone depletion), Freon-12, and DDT.
