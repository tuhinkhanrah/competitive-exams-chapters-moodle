1. NEET Exam Context & Learning Objectives

In living organisms, non-living chemical components assemble dynamically into structures capable of growth, sustainment, and genetic reproduction. This field of chemical inquiry represents Biochemistry. Biomolecules—including carbohydrates, proteins, enzymes, vitamins, nucleic acids, and hormones—constitute the fundamental molecular logic of physiological processes.

Core NEET-UG Learning Objectives:
  • Delineate the structural classification and chemical evidence establishing the open and cyclic hemiacetal pyranose forms of \(\mathrm{D}\)-(+)-glucose and the furanose forms of \(\mathrm{D}\)-(-)-fructose.
  • Identify glycosidic bonds in disaccharides (sucrose, maltose, lactose) and polysaccharides (amylose, amylopectin, cellulose, glycogen), predicting reducing and non-reducing characteristics.
  • Characterize \(\alpha\)-amino acids, their zwitterionic dipolar equilibria, isoelectric properties, and essential versus non-essential categorizations.
  • Distinguish the four structural levels of proteins (\(1^\circ, 2^\circ, 3^\circ, 4^\circ\)), differentiate fibrous from globular architectures, and interpret molecular denaturation mechanisms.
  • Quantify biocatalysis by enzymes via activation energy reduction.
  • Classify vitamins by solubility, storage, dietary sources, and deficiency pathologies.
  • Master nucleotide composition (purines, pyrimidines, pentose sugars, phosphate), \(3'\rightarrow 5'\) phosphodiester bridges, Watson-Crick double-helical complementary base pairing, RNA forms, and forensic DNA fingerprinting.
  • Categorize endocrine hormones by chemical origin (steroids, polypeptides, amino acid derivatives) and understand their homeostatic regulatory roles.

2. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are naturally occurring organic compounds predominantly synthesized by plants via photosynthesis. Historically, they were regarded as hydrates of carbon obeying the empirical formula \(C_x(H_2O)_y\). For instance, glucose (\(C_6H_{12}O_6\)) matches \(C_6(H_2O)_6\). However, this classical definition fails:

  • Acetic acid (\(CH_3COOH \equiv C_2H_4O_2 \equiv C_2(H_2O)_2\)) fits the formula but exhibits pure carboxylic acid characteristics, not carbohydrate behavior.
  • Rhamnose (\(C_6H_{12}O_5\)) and 2-deoxyribose (\(C_5H_{10}O_4\)) are verified carbohydrates that violate the empirical formula.
Modern Chemical Definition: Optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or substances that produce such units upon aqueous hydrolysis.

2.1 Classification Based on Hydrolytic Behavior

  1. Monosaccharides: Simplest monomeric carbohydrates that cannot be hydrolysed further into smaller polyhydroxy aldehyde or ketone units. About 20 exist in nature (e.g., glucose, fructose, ribose, galactose).
  2. Oligosaccharides: Carbohydrates that yield \(2\) to \(10\) monosaccharide units upon hydrolysis. Sub-classified by monomer count into disaccharides (e.g., sucrose, maltose, lactose), trisaccharides (e.g., raffinose), etc. Disaccharides are the most prevalent.
  3. Polysaccharides: High-molecular-mass polymers that yield a large number of monosaccharide units on complete hydrolysis (e.g., starch, cellulose, glycogen). Because they lack sweet taste, they are termed non-sugars.

2.2 Reducing vs. Non-Reducing Sugars

Sugars that reduce Fehling's solution (giving a red precipitate of \(Cu_2O\)) and Tollens' reagent (forming a silver mirror) are designated reducing sugars.

  • All monosaccharides—whether aldoses or ketoses—are reducing sugars. (Ketoses reduce Tollens' and Fehling's reagents because they undergo \(\alpha\)-hydroxyketone tautomeric rearrangement to aldoses via an enediol intermediate in alkaline test solutions).
  • In disaccharides, if the anomeric reducing centers (hemiacetal/hemiketal carbons) are engaged in glycosidic linkage, the sugar is non-reducing (e.g., sucrose). If at least one anomeric carbon retains a free hemiacetal group capable of mutarotating to an open aldehyde, it is a reducing sugar (e.g., maltose, lactose).

2.3 D-(+)-Glucose: Preparation and Proof of Constitution

Glucose (aldohexose, dextrose) is the monomer of starch and cellulose. It is prepared via two standard pathways:

1. Laboratory Hydrolysis of Sucrose (Cane Sugar):
\[\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow[\text{alcoholic medium}]{\mathrm{H^+}} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \;(\text{D-glucose}) + \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \;(\text{D-fructose})\]
2. Industrial Hydrolysis of Starch:
\[(\mathrm{C_6H_{10}O_5})_n + n\mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow[393\,\mathrm{K},\; 2\text{--}3\,\mathrm{atm}]{\text{dil. } \mathrm{H_2SO_4}} n\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \;(\text{D-glucose})\]

Chemical Reactions Establishing Glucose Structure

  1. Molecular Formula: Elemental analysis and cryoscopic molecular mass determination reveal \(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\).
  2. Straight 6-Carbon Chain: Prolonged heating of glucose with hydrogen iodide (\(\mathrm{HI}\)) and red phosphorus at elevated temperature yields exclusively \(n\)-hexane: \[\mathrm{CHO\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{HI},\;\Delta} \mathrm{CH_3\text{--}CH_2\text{--}CH_2\text{--}CH_2\text{--}CH_2\text{--}CH_3}\] This confirms all six carbons are connected in a continuous, unbranched sequence.
  3. Carbonyl Group Confirmation:
    • Reacts with hydroxylamine (\(\mathrm{NH_2OH}\)) to form a stable oxime: \[\mathrm{CHO\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH} + \mathrm{NH_2OH} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH=N\text{--}OH\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH} + \mathrm{H_2O}\]
    • Adds hydrogen cyanide (\(\mathrm{HCN}\)) to yield a cyanohydrin: \[\mathrm{CHO\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH} + \mathrm{HCN} \rightarrow \mathrm{CH(OH)(CN)\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH}\]
    These nucleophilic additions corroborate the presence of a carbonyl (\(>\mathrm{C=O}\)) function.
  4. Aldehyde Group Nature: Glucose reacts with a mild oxidising agent like bromine water (\(\mathrm{Br_2}/\mathrm{H_2O}\)) to form a six-carbon monocarboxylic acid, gluconic acid: \[\mathrm{CHO\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Br_2}\text{ water}} \mathrm{COOH\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH}\] Because bromine water oxidises aldehydes without attacking ketones or secondary alcohols, the carbonyl must be terminal (\(-\mathrm{CHO}\)).
  5. Presence of Five Hydroxyl Groups: Acetylation with acetic anhydride (\(\mathrm{Ac_2O}\)) in the presence of pyridine yields glucose pentaacetate: \[\mathrm{CHO\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH} + 5(\mathrm{CH_3CO})_2\mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{CHO\text{--}[CH(OCOCH_3)]_4\text{--}CH_2OCOCH_3} + 5\mathrm{CH_3COOH}\] Because geminal diols are unstable and dehydrate spontaneously to carbonyls, each of the five \(-\mathrm{OH}\) groups must reside on a different carbon atom.
  6. Presence of a Primary Alcoholic Group: Vigorous oxidation of glucose or gluconic acid with concentrated nitric acid (\(\mathrm{HNO_3}\)) yields the six-carbon dicarboxylic acid saccharic acid (glucaric acid): \[\mathrm{CHO\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{HNO_3},\;[\mathrm{O}]} \mathrm{COOH\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}COOH} \xleftarrow{\mathrm{HNO_3},\;[\mathrm{O}]} \mathrm{COOH\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH}\] Oxidation of both the terminal aldehyde and the opposite terminus into carboxyl groups proves that the sixth carbon atom bears a primary alcohol group (\(-\mathrm{CH_2OH}\)).

2.4 Fischer Projection and Relative D/L Configuration

Carbohydrate stereochemistry is standardized against the reference standard glyceraldehyde. In Fischer projections oriented with the most oxidized carbon (\(-\mathrm{CHO}\)) at the top:

  • If the hydroxyl group at the lowest asymmetric center (carbon-5 in aldohexoses) projects to the right, the molecule possesses the \(\mathrm{D}\)-configuration.
  • If this hydroxyl group projects to the left, it has the \(\mathrm{L}\)-configuration.
  • The symbols \(\mathrm{D}\) and \(\mathrm{L}\) indicate relative spatial stereochemical configuration and must not be confused with optical rotation symbols \((+)\) [dextrorotatory] or \((-)\) [laevorotatory], which describe physical polarimetric rotation. Glucose is naturally \(\mathrm{D}\)-(+)-glucose.

2.5 Cyclic Structure of Glucose and Anomerism

The open-chain Fischer aldehyde structure fails to explain three fundamental experimental observations:

Limitations of the Open-Chain Aldehyde Model:
  1. Despite bearing an aldehyde function, glucose does not restore the magenta color of Schiff's reagent and fails to form the sodium bisulphite (\(\mathrm{NaHSO_3}\)) adduct.
  2. Glucose pentaacetate fails to react with hydroxylamine (\(\mathrm{NH_2OH}\)), indicating the complete absence of an accessible free \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) group in the acetylated form.
  3. Glucose exists as two distinct, isolable crystalline diastereomers designated \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\):
    • \(\alpha\)-form: Melting point \(419\,\mathrm{K}\) (specific rotation \(+112^\circ\)), obtained by crystallization from concentrated solution at \(303\,\mathrm{K}\).
    • \(\beta\)-form: Melting point \(423\,\mathrm{K}\) (specific rotation \(+19^\circ\)), obtained by crystallization from hot, saturated aqueous solution at \(371\,\mathrm{K}\).

These observations proved that glucose forms an intramolecular cyclic hemiacetal. The nucleophilic \(-\mathrm{OH}\) group on carbon-5 adds across the carbon-1 carbonyl group, generating a stable, unstrained six-membered heterocyclic ring (termed pyranose, by analogy with pyran).

During cyclization, carbon-1—originally achiral in the open chain—becomes asymmetric. It is termed the anomeric carbon. The two resulting stereoisomers, differing solely in the configuration at \(\mathrm{C1}\), are called anomers:

  • \(\alpha\)-D-(+)-glucopyranose: The anomeric \(\mathrm{C1}\) hydroxyl group lies on the right in the Fischer ring (pointing downward below the ring plane in Haworth projection).
  • \(\beta\)-D-(+)-glucopyranose: The anomeric \(\mathrm{C1}\) hydroxyl group lies on the left in the Fischer ring (pointing upward above the ring plane in Haworth projection).

2.6 D-(-)-Fructose

Fructose is a natural ketohexose occurring in honey, sweet fruits, and vegetables. With molecular formula \(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\), it possesses a ketonic group at carbon-2 and an unbranched six-carbon chain. Fructose is laevorotatory, designated \(\mathrm{D}\)-(-)-fructose.

In solution, the \(-\mathrm{OH}\) at carbon-5 adds intramolecularly across the \(\mathrm{C2}\) ketone, forming a five-membered cyclic hemiketal called furanose (by analogy with furan). Its anomers are:

  • \(\alpha\)-D-(-)-fructofuranose: Anomeric \(\mathrm{C2}\) bears \(-\mathrm{OH}\) pointing downward in Haworth projection.
  • \(\beta\)-D-(-)-fructofuranose: Anomeric \(\mathrm{C2}\) bears \(-\mathrm{OH}\) pointing upward in Haworth projection.

2.7 Disaccharides and the Glycosidic Linkage

Disaccharides are formed when two identical or distinct monosaccharide units condense with the loss of one water molecule. The covalent oxide bridge connecting the two monosaccharides through an oxygen atom is termed a glycosidic linkage.

1. Sucrose (\(\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}}\))

  • Composed of \(\alpha\)-D-glucopyranose and \(\beta\)-D-fructofuranose.
  • Linkage: Glycosidic linkage connects \(\mathrm{C1}\) of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose to \(\mathrm{C2}\) of \(\beta\)-D-fructose.
  • Non-reducing Sugar: Because both anomeric reducing centers (\(\mathrm{C1}\) of glucose and \(\mathrm{C2}\) of fructose) are bonded in the glycosidic bridge, sucrose cannot open into a free carbonyl form. It does not reduce Tollens' or Fehling's reagents and does not undergo mutarotation.
  • Invert Sugar: Native sucrose is dextrorotatory, with a specific rotation of \(+66.5^\circ\). Upon acid-catalysed or enzymatic (invertase) hydrolysis, it produces an equimolar mixture of \(\mathrm{D}\)-(+)-glucose (\([\alpha]_\mathrm{D} = +52.5^\circ\)) and \(\mathrm{D}\)-(-)-fructose (\([\alpha]_\mathrm{D} = -92.4^\circ\)): \[\text{Net Rotation of Hydrolysate} = \frac{+52.5^\circ + (-92.4^\circ)}{2} = -19.95^\circ\] Because the intense laevorotation of fructose exceeds the dextrorotation of glucose, the optical rotation inverts from positive (\(+\)) to negative (\(-\)). The resulting hydrolysed mixture is known as invert sugar.

2. Maltose (\(\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}}\))

  • Composed of two \(\alpha\)-D-glucopyranose units.
  • Linkage: \(\alpha\)-1,4-glycosidic bond joining \(\mathrm{C1}\) of the first glucose unit to \(\mathrm{C4}\) of the second.
  • Reducing Sugar: The second glucose unit retains an intact hemiacetal group at its anomeric \(\mathrm{C1}\), which can open in aqueous solution to yield a free aldehyde. Hence, maltose reduces Fehling's and Tollens' reagents.

3. Lactose (\(\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}}\)) — Milk Sugar

  • Composed of \(\beta\)-D-galactopyranose and \(\beta\)-D-glucopyranose.
  • Linkage: \(\beta\)-1,4-glycosidic linkage connecting \(\mathrm{C1}\) of \(\beta\)-D-galactose to \(\mathrm{C4}\) of \(\beta\)-D-glucose.
  • Reducing Sugar: The \(\mathrm{C1}\) carbon of the glucose ring retains a hemiacetal group capable of generating a free aldehyde in solution.
Comparative Glycosidic Connectivity: Non-reducing Sucrose (\(\alpha\text{-}1 \leftrightarrow \beta\text{-}2\)) vs. Reducing Maltose (\(\alpha\text{-}1,4\)) and Lactose (\(\beta\text{-}1,4\))

2.9 Biological Importance of Carbohydrates

  • Primary metabolic fuel yielding ATP via cellular respiration in mammals.
  • Major storage reserves: starch in plants and glycogen in animals. When physiological glucose demand rises, enzymes hydrolyse glycogen back to free glucose.
  • Structural constituents: cellulose forms rigid bacterial and plant cell walls, timber, and cotton fibre.
  • Genetic architecture: the pentoses \(\mathrm{D}\)-ribose and \(2\)-deoxy-\(\mathrm{D}\)-ribose form the invariant structural backbone of RNA and DNA.

3. Amino Acids, Peptides, and Proteins

Proteins (Greek proteios, meaning primary or of prime importance) are the most abundant biomolecules in the living cell. They occur in every part of the organism, executing structural, transport, catalytic, and immunological functions. Proteins are linear heteropolymers of \(\alpha\)-amino acids.

3.1 Structure, Stereochemistry, and Zwitterion Character

An \(\alpha\)-amino acid possesses an amino group (\(-\mathrm{NH_2}\)) and a carboxyl group (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)) bonded to the same \(\alpha\)-carbon atom: \(\mathrm{R\text{--}CH(NH_2)\text{--}COOH}\).

  • Chirality: With the single exception of glycine (\(\mathrm{R = H}\)), the \(\alpha\)-carbon of all naturally occurring amino acids is asymmetric (chiral). Hence, 19 of the 20 standard amino acids are optically active.
  • Configuration: Naturally occurring amino acids predominantly exhibit the \(\mathrm{L}\)-configuration, which means the \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) group projects to the left when represented in Fischer projection with \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) oriented at the top.
  • Zwitterionic Dipolar Character: In aqueous solution, internal acid-base neutralization occurs: the acidic carboxyl group (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)) releases a proton, while the basic amino group (\(-\mathrm{NH_2}\)) accepts it. This forms a dipolar ion termed a zwitterion: \[\mathrm{H_2N\text{--}CH(R)\text{--}COOH} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H_3N^+\text{--}CH(R)\text{--}COO^-}\] Although electrically neutral, zwitterions possess distinct positive and negative charges. Consequently, amino acids behave as high-melting, water-soluble, crystalline salt-like solids with high dipole moments, unlike ordinary neutral amines or haloacids.
  • Amphoteric Behavior: In acidic media (\(\mathrm{pH} < \mathrm{pI}\)), the carboxylate ion accepts a proton, yielding a cation that migrates toward the cathode during electrophoresis. In basic media (\(\mathrm{pH} > \mathrm{pI}\)), the ammonium group surrenders a proton, yielding an anion that migrates toward the anode: \[\underset{\text{Cationic (in strong acid)}}{\mathrm{H_3N^+\text{--}CH(R)\text{--}COOH}} \xleftarrow[\mathrm{H^+}]{\mathrm{OH^-}} \underset{\text{Dipolar Zwitterion (neutral)}}{\mathrm{H_3N^+\text{--}CH(R)\text{--}COO^-}} \xrightarrow[\mathrm{H^+}]{\mathrm{OH^-}} \underset{\text{Anionic (in strong base)}}{\mathrm{H_2N\text{--}CH(R)\text{--}COO^-}}\]
Dipolar Zwitterionic Transformations and Amide Peptide Bond Formation via Dehydration Condensation

3.2 Classification of Amino Acids

  • Acidic Amino Acids: Contain more carboxyl groups than amino groups (e.g., Aspartic acid, Glutamic acid).
  • Basic Amino Acids: Contain more basic nitrogenous/amino centers than carboxyl groups (e.g., Lysine, Arginine, Histidine).
  • Neutral Amino Acids: Contain equal numbers of amino and carboxyl functions (e.g., Glycine, Alanine, Valine, Leucine).
  • Essential vs. Non-essential:
    • Non-essential amino acids: Ten amino acids that can be biosynthesized de novo by human tissues; dietary supplementation is not mandatory.
    • Essential amino acids: Ten amino acids that human metabolic pathways cannot synthesize; they must be ingested regularly through food. These are: Valine, Leucine, Isoleucine, Phenylalanine, Methionine, Tryptophan, Threonine, Lysine, Arginine, and Histidine.

3.3 The Peptide Linkage

Proteins are built when \(\alpha\)-amino acids link covalently through an amide bridge called a peptide bond (\(-\mathrm{CO\text{--}NH\text{--}}\)). When the \(\alpha\text{-COOH}\) of one amino acid condenses with the \(\alpha\text{-NH_2}\) of another, a molecule of water is eliminated. Two joined amino acids constitute a dipeptide (e.g., Glycylalanine, Gly-Ala). Linking three yields a tripeptide (bearing two peptide bonds). Polypeptides possess more than ten amino acid residues. By convention, a polypeptide with over \(100\) residues and a molecular mass exceeding \(10,000\,\mathrm{u}\) is categorized as a protein (though small proteins with well-defined 3D shapes, such as insulin with \(51\) amino acids, are also designated proteins).

3.5 Four Hierarchical Levels of Protein Structure

  1. Primary Structure (\(1^\circ\)): The precise linear sequence in which specific amino acids are covalently linked by peptide bonds. Every protein has a genetically specified, unique primary sequence. Altering even a single amino acid disrupts biological activity (e.g., substitution of glutamic acid by valine at position 6 of the \(\beta\)-globin chain causes sickle-cell anaemia).
  2. Secondary Structure (\(2^\circ\)): Local, regular conformational folding of the polypeptide backbone stabilized exclusively by hydrogen bonding between the carbonyl oxygen (\(>\mathrm{C=O}\)) of one peptide group and the amide hydrogen (\(-\mathrm{NH\text{--}}\)) of another:
    • \(\alpha\)-Helix: The polypeptide chain twists into a right-handed screw. Every \(\mathrm{-NH}\) group forms an intramolecular hydrogen bond with the \(>\mathrm{C=O}\) situated four residues ahead in the adjacent turn of the helix. Side-chain \(\mathrm{R}\) groups project outward.
    • \(\beta\)-Pleated Sheet: Polypeptide chains are stretched out to near-maximum extension and aligned side-by-side. Neighboring chains are cross-linked by intermolecular hydrogen bonds, creating a rippled drapery-like sheet.
  3. Tertiary Structure (\(3^\circ\)): The overall, three-dimensional spatial folding of secondary structural elements into compact globular or fibrous shapes. This structure is stabilized by four distinct non-covalent and covalent interactions:
    • Hydrogen bonds
    • Disulphide linkages (\(-\mathrm{S\text{--}S\text{--}}\) covalent bonds between cysteine residues)
    • Electrostatic ionic attractions (salt bridges between basic and acidic side chains)
    • Van der Waals forces and hydrophobic interactions
  4. Quaternary Structure (\(4^\circ\)): The spatial arrangement and assembly of two or more independent polypeptide subunits (individual tertiary folded chains) into a single functional, oligomeric protein complex. For example, haemoglobin is a tetramer composed of two identical \(\alpha\)-subunits and two identical \(\beta\)-subunits arranged cooperatively around four iron-protoporphyrin haem groups.

3.6 Denaturation of Proteins

A protein existing in its natural biological environment with a specific three-dimensional conformation and active biological function is termed a native protein. When this native protein is exposed to physical changes (e.g., thermal heating) or chemical disturbances (e.g., pH shifts, mineral acids, organic solvents):

  • Weak non-covalent hydrogen bonds, ionic salt bridges, and hydrophobic attractions are disrupted.
  • Globular domains unfold, helical coils uncoil, and the precise tertiary and secondary conformations collapse.
  • The protein precipitates or coagulates irreversibly, losing its biological activity completely.
  • Crucial NEET Point: During denaturation, secondary (\(2^\circ\)) and tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) structures are destroyed, but the primary (\(1^\circ\)) structure remains completely intact because strong covalent peptide bonds are not hydrolysed under moderate denaturing conditions.
Classic Manifestations of Denaturation:
  1. Coagulation of egg white on boiling: The soluble globular protein albumin undergoes heat-induced denaturation, forming an insoluble, cross-linked fibrous matrix that entraps surrounding water.
  2. Curdling of milk: Metabolic action of Lactobacillus bacteria produces lactic acid, dropping the \(\mathrm{pH}\). The acidic environment disrupts ionic stabilization in milk casein, causing it to coagulate.

4. Enzymes and Biocatalysis

Nearly all biological processes are coordinated by biochemical catalysts termed enzymes. Chemically, almost all enzymes are globular proteins. Enzymes exhibit exceptional catalytic power and absolute substrate- and reaction-specificity, operating under mild physiological temperatures (\(\sim 310\,\mathrm{K}\)) and neutral \(\mathrm{pH}\) (\(\sim 7.4\)).

4.1 Nomenclature of Enzymes

  • Named after the specific substrate acted upon, replacing the suffix with -ase. For example, maltase catalyses the hydrolytic cleavage of maltose into two molecules of glucose: \[\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{Maltase}} 2\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \;(\text{Glucose})\]
  • Named according to the nature of the reaction catalysed. For instance, enzymes that catalyse simultaneous oxidation of one substrate and reduction of another are termed oxidoreductases.

4.2 Mechanism: Lowering of Activation Energy

Like inorganic catalysts, enzymes accelerate reactions by providing an alternate reaction pathway characterized by a markedly lower activation energy (\(E_a\)). The substrate binds to a specific active site on the enzyme surface to form an enzyme-substrate complex, which undergoes reaction and releases products, regenerating the enzyme:

Quantitative Comparison: Hydrolysis of Sucrose
  • Activation energy for ordinary mineral acid-catalysed hydrolysis: \(E_a = 6.22\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).
  • Activation energy for biological sucrase-catalysed hydrolysis: \(E_a = 2.15\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).
By depressing the kinetic activation barrier by over \(65\%\), sucrase accelerates sucrose cleavage by millions of times under physiological conditions.

5. Vitamins

Vitamins are essential organic compounds required in small quantities in the diet to carry out vital physiological functions for the maintenance, normal growth, and health of the organism. Most vitamins cannot be synthesized endogenously by human cells (though intestinal gut microflora can generate limited quantities of certain B-complex vitamins and vitamin K). Deficiency of specific vitamins leads to well-characterized pathological deficiency syndromes.

5.1 Classification by Solubility

  1. Fat-Soluble Vitamins:
    • Vitamins A, D, E, and K.
    • Soluble in fats, oils, and non-polar organic solvents, but insoluble in water.
    • Stored endogenously in the liver and adipose tissues. Hypervitaminosis can occur if taken in excessive amounts.
  2. Water-Soluble Vitamins:
    • B-group vitamins (\(\mathrm{B_1, B_2, B_6, B_{12}}\), etc.) and Vitamin C (ascorbic acid).
    • Readily soluble in water.
    • Because they are readily excreted in urine, they cannot be stored in the body and must be supplied continuously via daily diet.
    • High-Yield NEET Exception: Vitamin \(\mathrm{B_{12}}\) (cyanocobalamin) is water-soluble, yet it is stored in significant reserves in the liver for several years.

Vitamin K (Phylloquinones) Fat Green leafy vegetables (spinach, cabbage), synthesized by intestinal microflora. Increased blood clotting time (delayed prothrombin synthesis leading to haemorrhagic tendencies).

6. Nucleic Acids and Molecular Genetics

The transmission of inherited characteristics from parent to progeny across generations is termed heredity. The nuclear structures orchestrating heredity are chromosomes, which are composed of proteins and high-molecular-weight polynucleotides called nucleic acids: Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and Ribonucleic acid (RNA).

6.1 Chemical Composition

Complete hydrolytic decomposition of any nucleic acid yields three chemical fragments:

  1. A Five-Carbon Pentose Sugar:
    • In RNA, the sugar is \(\beta\)-D-ribose (possessing an \(-\mathrm{OH}\) group at \(\mathrm{C2'}\)).
    • In DNA, the sugar is \(\beta\)-D-2-deoxyribose (lacking an oxygen atom at \(\mathrm{C2'}\), possessing two \(\mathrm{-H}\) atoms).
  2. Phosphoric Acid (\(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)): Forms ester linkages with sugar hydroxyl groups, creating the negatively charged polyanionic backbone.
  3. Nitrogenous Heterocyclic Bases:
    • Purines (Bicyclic): Adenine (A) and Guanine (G) (present in both DNA and RNA).
    • Pyrimidines (Monocyclic): Cytosine (C) and Thymine (T) occur in DNA. In RNA, Uracil (U) replaces Thymine. Thus: \[\text{DNA Bases: } \mathrm{A, G, C, T} \qquad \text{RNA Bases: } \mathrm{A, G, C, U}\]

6.2 Nucleosides vs. Nucleotides

  • Nucleoside: A unit formed by the covalent attachment of a purine or pyrimidine base to the \(\mathrm{C1'}\) position of the pentose sugar via an \(N\)-glycosidic bond: \[\text{Nucleoside} = \text{Pentose Sugar} + \text{Nitrogenous Base at } \mathrm{C1'}\] (To avoid ambiguity, sugar carbons are numbered with primes: \(1', 2', 3', 4', 5'\)).
  • Nucleotide: Formed when a nucleoside undergoes esterification with phosphoric acid at the \(\mathrm{C5'}\) hydroxyl group of the pentose sugar: \[\text{Nucleotide} = \text{Nucleoside} + \text{Phosphate at } \mathrm{C5'} = \text{Base} + \text{Sugar} + \text{Phosphate}\] Nucleotides serve as the monomeric repeat units of all polynucleotides.

6.3 The Phosphodiester Linkage

Nucleotides polymerize when the \(5'\)-phosphate group of one nucleotide condenses with the \(3'\)-hydroxyl group of the pentose sugar of an adjacent nucleotide. This bridging unit is termed a \(3'\rightarrow 5'\) phosphodiester linkage. A polynucleotide chain has a definite directional polarity: a free \(5'\)-phosphate terminus at one end and an unesterified \(3'\)-hydroxyl terminus at the opposite end.

Structural Architecture of Nucleic Acids: Pentose Differentiation and \(3'\rightarrow 5'\) Phosphodiester Backbone Continuity

6.4 Watson-Crick Double-Helical Model of DNA

In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick postulated the three-dimensional double-helix model of DNA:

  • DNA consists of two polynucleotide strands coiling helically around a central common axis in a right-handed direction, resembling a twisted ladder.
  • The two strands run in strictly antiparallel orientations: one strand is directed \(5'\rightarrow 3'\), while the complementary strand is oriented \(3'\rightarrow 5'\).
  • The hydrophilic rails (backbone) of the ladder consist of alternating units of deoxyribose sugar and phosphate groups; the hydrophobic rungs are composed of paired planar nitrogenous bases pointing inward.
  • Complementary Base Pairing: The bases from opposite strands pair through specific hydrogen bonding:
    • Adenine pairs exclusively with Thymine (\(\mathrm{A = T}\)) via two hydrogen bonds.
    • Guanine pairs exclusively with Cytosine (\(\mathrm{G \equiv C}\)) via three hydrogen bonds.
    Because of this stereochemical requirement, the base sequence of one strand automatically dictates the sequence of its partner.
Antiparallel Directionality and Watson-Crick Complementary Hydrogen-Bonding Topology: \(\mathrm{A=T}\) (2 H-Bonds) and \(\mathrm{G\equiv C}\) (3 H-Bonds)

6.5 Ribonucleic Acid (RNA) and Biological Roles

Unlike DNA, the secondary structure of RNA is predominantly single-stranded, though it can fold back upon itself into local hairpin loops. RNA molecules occur as three distinct functional types:

  • Messenger RNA (m-RNA): Carries transcribed genetic instructions from nuclear DNA to cytoplasmic ribosomes.
  • Ribosomal RNA (r-RNA): Structural and catalytic component of ribosomes, the site of protein translation.
  • Transfer RNA (t-RNA): Adapter molecules that bind specific amino acids and align them with matching codons on the m-RNA template.

6.6 DNA Fingerprinting

Conventional dermal fingerprints can be cosmetically or surgically altered. However, the sequence of nucleotide base pairs in an individual's DNA is strictly unique (except in identical twins), identical in every somatic cell, and completely refractory to alteration by any known clinical or surgical intervention. Determining this sequence pattern is known as DNA fingerprinting. Major applications include:

  • Forensic crime scene investigation to identify suspects.
  • Paternity disputes and family lineage verification.
  • Identification of severely disfigured victims in mass casualty incidents.
  • Tracing racial origin, evolutionary migrations, and phylogenetic lineage.

7. Hormones

Hormones are biochemical molecules produced by ductless endocrine glands that function as intercellular chemical messengers. They are secreted directly into the bloodstream, which transports them to distant target cells and organs to maintain biological homeostasis, regulate metabolism, mediate stress responses, and direct growth and sexual development.

7.2 Clinical Pathophysiology of Endocrine Glands

  • Blood Glucose Regulation: Insulin and glucagon act antagonistically. A rapid spike in blood glucose prompts insulin release, driving glucose conversion to glycogen. When blood sugar drops, glucagon stimulates glycogen breakdown to maintain blood glucose within narrow physiological limits (\(70\text{--}100\,\mathrm{mg/dL}\) fasting).
  • Thyroid Pathophysiology: Thyroxine is an iodinated derivative of tyrosine.
    • Hypothyroidism: Abnormally low thyroxine production, characterized by lethargy, low basal metabolic rate, and obesity. Dietary iodine deficiency causes compensatory thyroid enlargement, known as goitre. This condition is controlled by adding sodium iodide to commercial table salt ("iodised salt").
    • Hyperthyroidism: Excessive secretion of thyroxine, resulting in elevated metabolic rate, weight loss, and nervous excitability.
  • Adrenal Cortex Pathophysiology: The adrenal cortex releases glucocorticoids (regulating carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism and modulating inflammation) and mineralocorticoids (regulating renal salt and water reabsorption). Dysfunction or destruction of the adrenal cortex leads to Addison's disease, characterized by severe hypoglycemia, chronic asthenia, hyperpigmentation, and heightened susceptibility to stress. Addison's disease is fatal unless treated with replacement mineralocorticoid and glucocorticoid therapy.

8. Worked Examples: Step-by-Step NEET Problem Solving

Worked Example 1: Deducing Monosaccharide Reaction Stoichiometry

Problem: An unknown open-chain aldohexose \(X\) has the molecular formula \(\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}\). Upon prolonged heating with concentrated \(\mathrm{HI}\), it produces compound \(Y\). When treated with excess acetic anhydride in pyridine, \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(X\) reacts with \(5\,\mathrm{mol}\) of acetic anhydride to yield derivative \(Z\). Mild oxidation of \(X\) with bromine water yields monocarboxylic acid \(W\), which upon oxidation with hot concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) produces dicarboxylic acid \(U\). Identify compounds \(Y, Z, W,\) and \(U\), and explain why derivative \(Z\) fails to form an oxime upon treatment with \(\mathrm{NH_2OH}\).

Step-by-Step Solution:
  1. Heating an aldohexose with \(\mathrm{HI}\) causes complete reductive deoxygenation of the 6-carbon skeleton to produce unbranched \(n\)-hexane (\(\mathrm{CH_3\text{--}(CH_2)_4\text{--}CH_3}\)). Therefore, compound \(Y\) is \(n\)-hexane.
  2. Reaction of \(1\,\mathrm{mol}\) of hexose with \(5\,\mathrm{mol}\) of acetic anhydride demonstrates the presence of five hydroxyl groups on separate carbon atoms. Derivative \(Z\) is glucose pentaacetate.
  3. Bromine water is a selective, mild oxidising agent that oxidises only terminal aldehyde groups without attacking primary or secondary alcohols. Hence, compound \(W\) is gluconic acid (\(\mathrm{HOOC\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}CH_2OH}\)).
  4. Concentrated nitric acid oxidises both the terminal aldehyde and the primary alcohol (\(-\mathrm{CH_2OH}\)) to carboxyl groups. Hence, compound \(U\) is the dicarboxylic acid saccharic acid (\(\mathrm{HOOC\text{--}(CHOH)_4\text{--}COOH}\)).
  5. Absence of Oxime Formation: In glucose pentaacetate (\(Z\)), the anomeric oxygen at \(\mathrm{C1}\) is esterified into a stable cyclic hemiacetal acetate. Because the anomeric \(\mathrm{C1}\) is locked, it cannot open into a free, unhindered aldehyde group. Consequently, it cannot react with hydroxylamine to form an oxime.

Worked Example 2: Inversion of Cane Sugar and Optical Rotation

Problem: Native sucrose is dextrorotatory with a specific optical rotation of \(+66.5^\circ\). When subjected to hydrolytic cleavage catalysed by dilute acid or the enzyme invertase, the resulting aqueous solution turns laevorotatory (\(-19.95^\circ\)). Explain this optical inversion chemically using specific optical rotation values for the hydrolysis products.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Sucrose is a non-reducing disaccharide composed of \(\alpha\)-D-glucopyranose and \(\beta\)-D-fructofuranose joined by an \(\alpha\text{-}1 \leftrightarrow \beta\text{-}2\) glycosidic linkage.

Hydrolysis cleaves this glycosidic linkage, releasing an equimolar (\(1:1\)) mixture of \(\mathrm{D}\)-(+)-glucose and \(\mathrm{D}\)-(-)-fructose:

\[\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}} + \mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \;(\text{D-(+)-glucose}) + \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} \;(\text{D-(-)-fructose})\]
  • Specific optical rotation of \(\mathrm{D}\)-(+)-glucose: \([\alpha]_\mathrm{D} = +52.5^\circ\) (dextrorotatory).
  • Specific optical rotation of \(\mathrm{D}\)-(-)-fructose: \([\alpha]_\mathrm{D} = -92.4^\circ\) (strongly laevorotatory).

The net observed optical rotation of the equimolar product mixture is the average of these two values:

\[[\alpha]_\mathrm{mixture} = \frac{+52.5^\circ + (-92.4^\circ)}{2} = \frac{-39.9^\circ}{2} = -19.95^\circ\]

Because the magnitude of laevorotation of \(\mathrm{D}\)-(-)-fructose (\(-92.4^\circ\)) significantly exceeds the dextrorotation of \(\mathrm{D}\)-(+)-glucose (\(+52.5^\circ\)), the sign of optical rotation inverts from dextro (\(+\)) to laevo (\(-\)). The product is therefore called invert sugar.

Worked Example 3: Polynucleotide Base Pairing and Chargaff's Equivalence

Problem: Quantitative analysis of a sample of double-stranded DNA demonstrates that \(32\%\) of the total nitrogenous bases are Cytosine. Determine the percentage composition of Adenine, Thymine, and Guanine in this DNA sample. Would this quantitative relationship hold if the sample were hydrolysed single-stranded RNA?

Step-by-Step Solution:
  1. In double-stranded DNA, Watson-Crick base pairing requires that Guanine pairs exclusively with Cytosine via three hydrogen bonds (\(\mathrm{G \equiv C}\)). Therefore: \[\%\,\mathrm{Guanine} = \%\,\mathrm{Cytosine} = 32\%\]
  2. The combined percentage of \(\mathrm{G + C}\) is: \[32\% + 32\% = 64\%\]
  3. The remaining percentage belongs to the \(\mathrm{A = T}\) base pairs: \[\%\,(\mathrm{Adenine + Thymine}) = 100\% - 64\% = 36\%\]
  4. Because Adenine pairs exclusively with Thymine (\(\mathrm{A = T}\)), their molar concentrations are identical: \[\%\,\mathrm{Adenine} = \%\,\mathrm{Thymine} = \frac{36\%}{2} = 18\%\]
  5. RNA Consideration: If the sample were single-stranded RNA, no fixed molar equivalence exists between bases (\(\mathrm{A \neq U}\) and \(\mathrm{G \neq C}\)). In single-stranded RNA, nucleotides are not paired along an antiparallel complementary strand. Therefore, the base ratios vary unpredictably.

10. Common Student Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

Critical Conceptual Traps in NEET Biomolecules

  • Confusing \(\mathrm{D/L}\) Configuration with Optical Rotation \((+/-)\): The prefix \(\mathrm{D}\) or \(\mathrm{L}\) denotes spatial stereochemical configuration relative to \(\mathrm{D}\)-(+)-glyceraldehyde at the highest-numbered chiral carbon. It does not dictate whether a substance rotates plane-polarized light clockwise \((+)\) or counterclockwise \((-)\). For example, natural fructose has the \(\mathrm{D}\)-configuration but is strongly laevorotatory, written as \(\mathrm{D}\)-(-)-fructose.
  • Open-Chain vs. Cyclic Reducing Activity: Fructose is a ketohexose, yet it acts as a reducing sugar because its \(\alpha\)-hydroxyketone group tautomerizes to an aldose via an enediol intermediate in the alkaline media used for Tollens' and Fehling's tests.
  • Reducing vs. Non-reducing Disaccharides: Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons (\(\mathrm{C1}\) of glucose and \(\mathrm{C2}\) of fructose) form the glycosidic bond. In contrast, maltose and lactose leave one anomeric carbon as a free hemiacetal, retaining their reducing capacity.
  • Denaturation Scope: Denaturation breaks secondary and tertiary hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and salt bridges, but leaves primary covalent peptide bonds intact.
  • Vitamin \(\mathrm{B_{12}}\) Solubility vs. Storage: Vitamin \(\mathrm{B_{12}}\) is water-soluble, but unlike other water-soluble vitamins, it is stored in the liver for extended periods.
Carbohydrates Mastery:

Remember that glucose + \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) water yields gluconic acid (monocarboxylic), while glucose + concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) yields saccharic acid (dicarboxylic).

Amino Acid Configurations:

All natural \(\alpha\)-amino acids from proteins have the \(\mathrm{L}\)-configuration. Glycine is the only achiral amino acid.

Enzyme Energetics:

Enzymes lower the activation energy barrier (\(E_a\)) without altering the overall thermodynamic equilibrium (\(\Delta G^\circ\)).

Nucleotide Linkages:

Sugars connect to bases at \(\mathrm{C1'}\) via an \(N\)-glycosidic bond; adjacent nucleotides link from \(\mathrm{C3'}\) to \(\mathrm{C5'}\) through a phosphodiester bridge.

11. Quick High-Yield Recap

  • Glucose Reactions: Reaction with \(\mathrm{HI}\) yields \(n\)-hexane; \(\mathrm{NH_2OH}\) yields an oxime; \(\mathrm{HCN}\) yields a cyanohydrin; \(\mathrm{Br_2}/\mathrm{H_2O}\) yields gluconic acid; acetic anhydride yields a pentaacetate; concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) yields saccharic acid.
  • Cyclic Anomers: In \(\alpha\)-D-glucopyranose, the anomeric \(\mathrm{C1\text{-}OH}\) points downward in Haworth projection; in \(\beta\)-D-glucopyranose, it points upward.
  • Disaccharide Bonds: Sucrose has an \(\alpha\text{-}1 \leftrightarrow \beta\text{-}2\) link (non-reducing); maltose has an \(\alpha\text{-}1,4\) link (reducing); lactose has a \(\beta\text{-}1,4\) link (reducing).
  • Polysaccharide Bonds: Amylose has \(\alpha\text{-}1,4\) linkages; amylopectin and glycogen have \(\alpha\text{-}1,4\) linear links with \(\alpha\text{-}1,6\) branches; cellulose has \(\beta\text{-}1,4\) linkages.
  • Proteins: Primary structure = amino acid sequence; secondary = \(\alpha\)-helices and \(\beta\)-sheets (H-bonding); tertiary = overall 3D shape (fibrous/globular); quaternary = multi-subunit assembly.
  • Nucleic Acids: Purines = Adenine, Guanine; Pyrimidines = Cytosine, Thymine (DNA), Uracil (RNA). In DNA, \(\mathrm{A=T}\) (2 H-bonds) and \(\mathrm{G\equiv C}\) (3 H-bonds).
  • Hormones: Steroids (estrogens, androgens, glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids); Polypeptides (insulin, glucagon); Amino acid derivatives (epinephrine, thyroxine).
