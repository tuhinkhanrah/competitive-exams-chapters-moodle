Biomolecules: Structure, Reactivity, and Biological Roles

A standalone, exam-oriented NEET UG tutorial covering carbohydrates, proteins, enzymes, vitamins, nucleic acids, and hormones.

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Overview

Living organisms sustain growth, metabolism, and reproduction through coordinated chemical reactions driven by non-living molecules. For NEET UG, Biomolecules is a high-yield chapter bridging Organic Chemistry and Biology, frequently tested through structural identification, chemical tests, deficiency diseases, and base-pairing logic.

Core Competencies Built in This Tutorial
  • Classify carbohydrates, amino acids, proteins, vitamins, and nucleic acids based on structure, hydrolysis, and solubility.
  • Interpret experimental proofs for the open-chain and cyclic hemiacetal structures of glucose and fructose.
  • Distinguish reducing from non-reducing sugars using glycosidic linkage positions.
  • Explain protein structural hierarchy, zwitterionic behaviour, denaturation, and enzyme catalysis.
  • Compare DNA and RNA composition, nucleoside versus nucleotide architecture, and hormonal regulation.

2. Carbohydrates

2.1 Modern Definition and Exceptions

Early chemists viewed carbohydrates as "hydrates of carbon" with the general formula \(C_x(H_2O)_y\). While glucose (\(C_6H_{12}O_6 = C_6(H_2O)_6\)) fits this pattern, two classic exceptions prove the formula is unreliable:

  • Acetic acid (\(CH_3COOH\)): Fits \(C_2(H_2O)_2\), yet it is a carboxylic acid, not a carbohydrate.
  • Rhamnose (\(C_6H_{12}O_5\)): Does not fit \(C_x(H_2O)_y\), yet it is a genuine carbohydrate.

Chemical Definition: Carbohydrates (saccharides) are optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that yield such units upon hydrolysis. Sweet-tasting carbohydrates (such as sucrose in cane sugar and lactose in milk) are commonly termed sugars.

2.2 Classification by Hydrolysis and Reducing Power

Category Hydrolysis Behaviour Key Examples Reducing Nature
Monosaccharides Cannot be hydrolysed into simpler polyhydroxy aldehyde or ketone units (~20 occur naturally). Glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose All monosaccharides (both aldoses and ketoses) are reducing sugars.
Oligosaccharides Yield 2 to 10 monosaccharide units on hydrolysis; disaccharides are the most common. Sucrose, maltose, lactose Maltose & lactose are reducing; sucrose is non-reducing.
Polysaccharides Yield a very large number of monosaccharide units on hydrolysis; tasteless (non-sugars). Starch, cellulose, glycogen, gums Generally non-reducing.

Reducing Sugar Criterion: Any carbohydrate that reduces Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent is a reducing sugar. This requires a free aldehydic group (\(-CHO\)) or a hemiacetal carbon capable of opening into a free carbonyl group in aqueous solution.

2.3 Monosaccharide Nomenclature

Monosaccharides are named by combining their carbonyl functional group (aldo- for aldehyde, keto- for ketone) with the carbon chain length: 3 carbons (triose), 4 (tetrose), 5 (pentose), 6 (hexose), and 7 (heptose). For instance, glucose is an aldohexose, fructose is a ketohexose, and ribose is an aldopentose.

2.4 Glucose: Preparation and Structure Elucidation

Glucose (dextrose) occurs freely in sweet fruits, honey, and ripe grapes, and serves as the monomer of starch and cellulose.

  • From Sucrose (Laboratory): Boiling sucrose with dilute \(HCl\) or \(H_2SO_4\) in alcoholic solution yields equimolar glucose and fructose: \[C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} + H_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} C_6H_{12}O_6\text{ (Glucose)} + C_6H_{12}O_6\text{ (Fructose)}\]
  • From Starch (Commercial): Hydrolysing starch with dilute \(H_2SO_4\) at \(393\,\mathrm{K}\) under \(2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{atm}\) pressure yields glucose: \[(C_6H_{10}O_5)_n + nH_2O \xrightarrow[393\,\mathrm{K},\;2\text{–}3\,\mathrm{atm}]{H^+} nC_6H_{12}O_6\]
Experimental Observation Structural Inference
Prolonged heating with \(HI\) gives n-hexane. All six carbon atoms are linked in an unbranched straight chain.
Forms an oxime with \(NH_2OH\) and a cyanohydrin with \(HCN\). Presence of a carbonyl group (\(>C=O\)).
Mild oxidation with bromine water (\(Br_2/\mathrm{H_2O}\)) gives gluconic acid (6C monocarboxylic acid). The carbonyl group is specifically an aldehydic group (\(-CHO\)) at C1.
Acetylation with acetic anhydride forms glucose pentaacetate. Presence of five \(-OH\) groups attached to five different carbon atoms.
Strong oxidation with \(HNO_3\) of either glucose or gluconic acid yields saccharic acid (dicarboxylic acid). Presence of one primary alcoholic (\(-CH_2OH\)) group at C6.

2.5 D/L Configuration vs. Optical Rotation

The letters D and L denote relative configuration with respect to glyceraldehyde, NOT the sign of optical rotation (\((+)\) dextrorotatory or \((-)\) laevorotatory, also written as d or l). In a Fischer projection with the most oxidised carbon (\(-CHO\)) at the top, if the \(-OH\) group on the lowest asymmetric carbon (C5 in glucose) lies on the right, it belongs to the D-series (matching D-(+)-glyceraldehyde); if on the left, it belongs to the L-series. Naturally occurring glucose is D-(+)-glucose, whereas naturally occurring fructose is D-(−)-fructose.

2.6 Cyclic Structure of Glucose and Fructose

Three experimental facts contradict a purely open-chain structure for glucose:

  1. Glucose does not give Schiff's test and does not form a hydrogensulphite addition product with \(NaHSO_3\).
  2. Glucose pentaacetate fails to react with hydroxylamine (\(NH_2OH\)), proving the absence of a free \(-CHO\) group.
  3. Glucose exists in two distinct crystalline forms: \(\alpha\)-D-(+)-glucose (m.p. \(419\,\mathrm{K}\), crystallised from concentrated solution at \(303\,\mathrm{K}\)) and \(\beta\)-D-(+)-glucose (m.p. \(423\,\mathrm{K}\), crystallised from hot saturated aqueous solution at \(371\,\mathrm{K}\)).

This occurs because the \(-OH\) at C5 adds intramolecularly to the C1 aldehydic group, forming a six-membered cyclic hemiacetal called a pyranose ring (analogous to pyran). The two stereoisomers differing only in the configuration at the hemiacetal carbon (C1, the anomeric carbon) are called anomers (\(\alpha\) and \(\beta\)).

Fructose: Contains a ketonic group at C2 and six carbons in a straight chain. Intramolecular addition of the C5 \(-OH\) to C2 forms a five-membered furanose ring (analogous to furan), yielding \(\alpha\)-D-(−)-fructofuranose and \(\beta\)-D-(−)-fructofuranose.

2.7 Disaccharides and Polysaccharides

Monosaccharide units link via an oxide bridge formed by eliminating water, known as a glycosidic linkage.

Disaccharide Monomer Units Glycosidic Linkage Key Exam Facts
Sucrose \(\alpha\)-D-glucose + \(\beta\)-D-fructose C1 of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose to C2 of \(\beta\)-D-fructose Non-reducing (both anomeric carbons are bonded). Hydrolysis gives invert sugar: dextrorotatory sucrose changes to laevorotatory mixture because fructose (\(-92.4°\)) outweighs glucose (\(+52.5°\)).
Maltose Two \(\alpha\)-D-glucose units C1 of unit I to C4 of unit II Reducing (free C1 hemiacetal on unit II).
Lactose (Milk sugar) \(\beta\)-D-galactose + \(\beta\)-D-glucose C1 of \(\beta\)-D-galactose to C4 of \(\beta\)-D-glucose Reducing (free C1 hemiacetal on glucose unit).
Polysaccharide Monomer & Linkage Structure & Biological Role
Starch \(\alpha\)-D-glucose Main plant storage polysaccharide. Two components: Amylose (15–20%, water-soluble, unbranched C1–C4 chain of 200–1000 units) and Amylopectin (80–85%, water-insoluble, branched: C1–C4 chains with C1–C6 branching).
Cellulose \(\beta\)-D-glucose (C1–C4 \(\beta\)-links) Unbranched straight-chain structural polysaccharide; chief constituent of plant cell walls.
Glycogen \(\alpha\)-D-glucose (C1–C4 & C1–C6) "Animal starch" stored in liver, muscles, and brain (also in yeast and fungi); structurally similar to amylopectin but more highly branched.

3. Proteins and Amino Acids

3.1 \(\alpha\)-Amino Acids, Zwitterions, and Classification

Proteins are polymers of \(\alpha\)-amino acids, which carry both an amino (\(-NH_2\)) and a carboxyl (\(-COOH\)) group on the \(\alpha\)-carbon. Except for glycine (\(R = H\)), all naturally occurring \(\alpha\)-amino acids are optically active and possess the L-configuration (\(-NH_2\) written on the left in Fischer projection).

  • Zwitterion & Amphoteric Nature: In aqueous solution, the \(-COOH\) group donates a proton to the \(-NH_2\) group, forming a dipolar ion called a zwitterion: \(H_3N^+\text{–}CH(R)\text{–}COO^-\). This explains why amino acids are high-melting, water-soluble crystalline solids that behave like salts and react with both acids and bases.
  • Acidic, Basic, and Neutral: Equal \(-NH_2\) and \(-COOH\) groups give neutral amino acids (e.g., glycine, alanine); extra \(-COOH\) gives acidic (aspartic acid, glutamic acid); extra \(-NH_2\)/basic nitrogen gives basic (lysine, arginine, histidine).
  • Essential vs. Non-essential: Out of 20 common amino acids, 10 cannot be synthesised by the human body and must come from the diet (essential: Val, Leu, Ile, Arg, Lys, Thr, Met, Phe, Trp, His). The remaining 10 are non-essential.

3.2 Peptide Bond, Molecular Shape, and Structural Hierarchy

A peptide linkage (\(-CO\text{–}NH-\)) is an amide bond formed by eliminating water between the \(-COOH\) of one amino acid and the \(-NH_2\) of another. A dipeptide has 2 amino acids (1 peptide bond), a tripeptide has 3 amino acids (2 peptide bonds), and a polypeptide has more than 10. Proteins typically have >100 amino acid residues (molecular mass >\(10,000\,\mathrm{u}\)), though insulin (51 amino acids) is also classified as a protein due to its well-defined conformation.

  • Fibrous Proteins: Polypeptide chains run parallel and are held by hydrogen and disulphide bonds, forming insoluble fibres (e.g., keratin in hair/wool/silk, myosin in muscles).
  • Globular Proteins: Chains coil into a compact spherical shape and are generally water-soluble (e.g., insulin, albumins).
Level of Structure Definition Stabilising Forces
Primary (\(1°\)) Specific linear sequence of amino acids in each polypeptide chain. Covalent peptide bonds.
Secondary (\(2°\)) Regular local folding of the backbone: right-handed \(\alpha\)-helix or side-by-side \(\beta\)-pleated sheet. Hydrogen bonds between \(>C=O\) and \(-NH-\) of peptide backbone (intramolecular in \(\alpha\)-helix; intermolecular in \(\beta\)-sheet).
Tertiary (\(3°\)) Overall 3D folding of the polypeptide chain into fibrous or globular shapes. H-bonds, disulphide linkages (\(-S\text{–}S-\)), van der Waals forces, and electrostatic attractions.
Quaternary (\(4°\)) Spatial arrangement of two or more polypeptide subunits relative to one another (e.g., haemoglobin). Non-covalent interactions and disulphide bridges between subunits.
Denaturation of Proteins (High-Yield NEET Concept)

When a native protein experiences physical stress (heat) or chemical change (pH shift), hydrogen bonds are disrupted, globules unfold, and helices uncoil—destroying secondary and tertiary structures and biological activity. Crucially, the primary structure remains intact. Classic examples: coagulation of egg white on boiling and curdling of milk by lactic acid bacteria.

4. Enzymes and Vitamins

4.1 Enzymes as Biocatalysts

Almost all enzymes are globular proteins. They are highly specific for both substrate and reaction type, and their names typically end in -ase (e.g., maltase hydrolyses maltose to glucose; oxidoreductases catalyse coupled oxidation–reduction). Like chemical catalysts, enzymes accelerate reactions by lowering the activation energy (\(E_a\)). For example, acid hydrolysis of sucrose requires \(E_a = 6.22\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), whereas the enzyme sucrase lowers it to \(2.15\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

4.2 Vitamins: Solubility and Deficiency Disorders

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small dietary amounts for normal growth and health. They are classified by solubility:

  • Fat-Soluble (A, D, E, K): Soluble in fats/oils, insoluble in water; stored in the liver and adipose tissues.
  • Water-Soluble (B-complex and C): Readily excreted in urine and must be supplied regularly in the diet, except Vitamin \(B_{12}\), which can be stored in the body.
Vitamin Key Dietary Sources Deficiency Disease / Symptoms
Vitamin A Fish liver oil, carrots, butter, milk Xerophthalmia (hardening of cornea), night blindness
Vitamin \(B_1\) (Thiamine) Yeast, milk, green vegetables, cereals Beri-beri (loss of appetite, retarded growth)
Vitamin \(B_2\) (Riboflavin) Milk, egg white, liver, kidney Cheilosis (fissuring at corners of mouth/lips), digestive disorders, burning skin
Vitamin \(B_6\) (Pyridoxine) Yeast, milk, egg yolk, cereals, grams Convulsions
Vitamin \(B_{12}\) Meat, fish, egg, curd Pernicious anaemia (RBCs deficient in haemoglobin)
Vitamin C (Ascorbic acid) Citrus fruits, amla, green leafy vegetables Scurvy (bleeding gums)
Vitamin D Sunlight exposure, fish, egg yolk Rickets (bone deformities in children), osteomalacia (soft bones/joint pain in adults)
Vitamin E Wheat germ oil, sunflower oil Increased fragility of RBCs, muscular weakness
Vitamin K Green leafy vegetables Increased blood clotting time

5. Nucleic Acids

5.1 Chemical Composition and Nucleotide Architecture

Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polynucleotides located in the chromosomes of the cell nucleus that govern heredity and protein synthesis. Complete hydrolysis of a nucleic acid yields three components:

  1. Pentose Sugar: \(\beta\)-D-2-deoxyribose in DNA; \(\beta\)-D-ribose in RNA.
  2. Nitrogenous Heterocyclic Bases:
    • Purines: Adenine (A) and Guanine (G) — present in both DNA and RNA.
    • Pyrimidines: Cytosine (C) in both; Thymine (T) exclusively in DNA; Uracil (U) exclusively in RNA.
  3. Phosphoric Acid (\(H_3PO_4\)): Forms the negatively charged phosphate backbone.
\(\text{Nucleoside} = \text{Pentose Sugar} + \text{Base (at } C1'\text{)}\)   |   \(\text{Nucleotide} = \text{Nucleoside} + \text{Phosphate (at } C5'\text{)}\)

Adjacent nucleotides join through a \(3'\text{–}5'\) phosphodiester linkage connecting the \(3'\)-carbon of one sugar to the \(5'\)-carbon of the next.

5.2 DNA vs. RNA and DNA Fingerprinting

  • DNA Secondary Structure: Watson and Crick proposed a double-stranded helix in which two antiparallel chains are held together by specific hydrogen bonds between complementary bases: Adenine pairs with Thymine (\(A=T\)) and Cytosine pairs with Guanine (\(C \equiv G\)). Because of complementarity, DNA self-duplicates during cell division.
  • RNA Structure & Types: Single-stranded helices that may fold back on themselves. Three functional types carry out protein synthesis from DNA's coded message: mRNA (messenger), rRNA (ribosomal), and tRNA (transfer).
  • DNA Fingerprinting: Every individual has a unique DNA base sequence that is identical in every cell and cannot be altered by surgery or treatment. It is used in forensic identification, paternity determination, identifying accident victims, and tracing biological evolution.

6. Hormones

Hormones are intercellular chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands directly into the bloodstream and transported to target organs.

Chemical Class Examples Key Physiological Functions & Disorders
Polypeptides Insulin, glucagon, endorphins Insulin lowers elevated blood glucose; glucagon raises blood glucose. Together they maintain blood glucose homeostasis.
Amino Acid Derivatives Epinephrine, norepinephrine, thyroxine Epinephrine/norepinephrine mediate responses to external stimuli. Thyroxine (iodinated tyrosine from thyroid gland): low levels cause hypothyroidism (lethargy, obesity) and thyroid enlargement (prevented by iodised salt); excess causes hyperthyroidism.
Steroids Glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, testosterone, estradiol, progesterone Adrenal cortex: glucocorticoids regulate carbohydrate metabolism/stress/inflammation; mineralocorticoids control kidney salt-water excretion. Adrenal cortex failure causes Addison's disease (hypoglycaemia, weakness, stress susceptibility). Gonads: testosterone (male secondary characters), estradiol (female secondary characters, menstrual cycle), progesterone (prepares uterus for implantation).

7. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Identifying Reducing Sugars and Hydrolysis Products

Scenario (Assumed Lab Sample): A student tests two unlabelled aqueous carbohydrate solutions, Sample X and Sample Y, with Tollens' reagent and dilute \(HCl\). Sample X gives no silver mirror with Tollens' reagent, but after boiling with dilute \(HCl\), the resulting solution becomes strongly laevorotatory and reduces Tollens' reagent. Sample Y gives a silver mirror directly and yields only D-glucose upon acid hydrolysis. Identify X and Y.

Solution:

  1. Sample X does not reduce Tollens' reagent directly, meaning both anomeric carbons are locked in a glycosidic bond—this is characteristic of sucrose (a non-reducing disaccharide).
  2. Upon acid hydrolysis, sucrose yields an equimolar mixture of D-(+)-glucose (\(+52.5°\)) and D-(−)-fructose (\(-92.4°\)). Both monosaccharides are reducing sugars, and the net optical rotation is negative (invert sugar).
  3. Sample Y is a reducing sugar that hydrolyses exclusively to D-glucose; therefore, Sample Y is maltose (composed of two \(\alpha\)-D-glucose units linked C1–C4, leaving one free C1 hemiacetal).
Worked Example 2: Counting Peptide Bonds and Molecular Mass

Scenario (Assumed Polypeptide): Suppose a synthetic linear peptide is constructed from 12 identical molecules of alanine (molecular mass of each alanine residue = \(89\,\mathrm{u}\), assumed for this calculation). How many peptide linkages are present, and how many water molecules are eliminated during its synthesis?

Solution:

  1. A linear chain of \(n\) amino acids contains \(n - 1\) peptide linkages (\(-CO\text{–}NH-\)). For \(n = 12\), the number of peptide linkages is \(12 - 1 = 11\).
  2. Each peptide bond forms by eliminating one \(H_2O\) molecule, so 11 water molecules are released.
  3. Total mass of the dodecapeptide \(= (12 \times 89) - (11 \times 18) = 1068 - 198 = 870\,\mathrm{u}\).
Worked Example 3: DNA Base Composition Analysis

Scenario (Assumed Nucleic Acid Sample): In a hypothetical double-stranded DNA fragment of 200 base pairs, 30% of the total bases are adenine (A). Calculate the number of guanine (G) bases in this fragment.

Solution:

  1. Total number of bases \(= 200 \text{ base pairs} \times 2 = 400\text{ bases}\).
  2. In double-stranded DNA, A pairs with T and G pairs with C, so \(\%\mathrm{A} = \%\mathrm{T} = 30\%\).
  3. Thus, \(\%\mathrm{G} + \%\mathrm{C} = 100\% - (30\% + 30\%) = 40\%\), giving \(\%\mathrm{G} = 20\%\).
  4. Number of guanine bases \(= 20\%\text{ of }400 = 80\text{ bases}\).

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: All items below are original practice questions modelled on the NEET UG single-correct MCQ format (4 options).

Practice Question 1: Which of the following pairs consists of a compound that fits the empirical formula \(C_x(H_2O)_y\) without being a carbohydrate, and a true carbohydrate that does not fit \(C_x(H_2O)_y\)?
  • (A) Acetic acid and rhamnose
  • (B) Formaldehyde and sucrose
  • (C) Lactic acid and ribose
  • (D) Acetic acid and fructose
Correct Answer: (A)
Explanation: Acetic acid (\(CH_3COOH\)) can be written as \(C_2(H_2O)_2\), fitting \(C_x(H_2O)_y\) even though it is a carboxylic acid. Rhamnose (\(C_6H_{12}O_5\)) is a naturally occurring deoxy-sugar (carbohydrate) that does not fit \(C_x(H_2O)_y\).
Practice Question 2: Glucose reacts with bromine water to yield gluconic acid, whereas oxidation with concentrated \(HNO_3\) yields saccharic acid. These two reactions respectively confirm the presence of:
  • (A) Five \(-OH\) groups and a straight six-carbon chain
  • (B) A ketonic group and a secondary alcoholic group
  • (C) An aldehydic group and a primary alcoholic (\(-CH_2OH\)) group
  • (D) A cyclic hemiacetal ring and an anomeric carbon
Correct Answer: (C)
Explanation: Bromine water is a mild oxidising agent that oxidises only the aldehydic (\(-CHO\)) group of glucose to \(-COOH\) (gluconic acid). Strong oxidation with \(HNO_3\) oxidises both the terminal \(-CHO\) and the primary \(-CH_2OH\) group at C6 to \(-COOH\) groups, forming the dicarboxylic acid (saccharic acid).
Practice Question 3: Which statement regarding \(\alpha\)-D-(+)-glucopyranose and \(\beta\)-D-(+)-glucopyranose is INCORRECT?
  • (A) They are anomers differing in configuration at C1.
  • (B) Both exist in equilibrium with the open-chain form in aqueous solution.
  • (C) Glucose pentaacetate formed from them reacts readily with \(NH_2OH\) to give an oxime.
  • (D) They are six-membered cyclic hemiacetals formed by addition of the C5 \(-OH\) to C1.
Correct Answer: (C)
Explanation: Glucose pentaacetate has all five \(-OH\) groups (including the anomeric C1 \(-OH\)) acetylated. Because the cyclic hemiacetal ring cannot open to regenerate a free \(-CHO\) group, glucose pentaacetate does not react with \(NH_2OH\).
Practice Question 4: Identify the non-reducing disaccharide and the specific glycosidic linkage responsible for its non-reducing nature:
  • (A) Maltose; C1 of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose to C4 of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose
  • (B) Lactose; C1 of \(\beta\)-D-galactose to C4 of \(\beta\)-D-glucose
  • (C) Sucrose; C1 of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose to C2 of \(\beta\)-D-fructose
  • (D) Cellobiose; C1 of \(\beta\)-D-glucose to C4 of \(\beta\)-D-glucose
Correct Answer: (C)
Explanation: In sucrose, both the aldehydic carbon (C1) of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose and the ketonic carbon (C2) of \(\beta\)-D-fructose are tied up in the glycosidic bond. With no free hemiacetal/hemiketal group, sucrose cannot open to reduce Tollens' or Fehling's reagents.
Practice Question 5: Statement I: Amylopectin is water-insoluble and constitutes 80–85% of starch.
Statement II: Amylopectin is a branched-chain polymer of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose with C1–C4 linkages along the chain and C1–C6 linkages at branch points.
Choose the correct option:
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (A)
Explanation: Starch consists of water-soluble amylose (15–20%, unbranched C1–C4) and water-insoluble amylopectin (80–85%, branched with C1–C4 chains and C1–C6 branch points). Both statements are factually accurate.
Practice Question 6: Which of the following \(\alpha\)-amino acids is optically INACTIVE?
  • (A) Alanine
  • (B) Valine
  • (C) Glycine
  • (D) Serine
Correct Answer: (C)
Explanation: In glycine (\(H_2N\text{–}CH_2\text{–}COOH\)), the \(\alpha\)-carbon is bonded to two identical hydrogen atoms (\(R = H\)), so it lacks a chiral centre and is optically inactive. All other 19 natural \(\alpha\)-amino acids have an asymmetric \(\alpha\)-carbon.
Practice Question 7: Assertion (A): During the boiling of an egg, the soluble globular proteins coagulate into an insoluble mass.
Reason (R): Heating disrupts hydrogen bonds, destroying the primary, secondary, and tertiary structures of the protein.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (C)
Explanation: Assertion (A) is true because boiling denatures egg white proteins. However, Reason (R) is false because denaturation destroys only the secondary and tertiary structures; the covalent peptide bonds of the primary structure remain intact.
Practice Question 8: Match each vitamin with its corresponding deficiency disorder:
1. Vitamin \(B_1\)    (p) Increased blood clotting time
2. Vitamin \(B_{12}\)    (q) Beri-beri
3. Vitamin C    (r) Pernicious anaemia
4. Vitamin K    (s) Scurvy
  • (A) 1-(q), 2-(r), 3-(s), 4-(p)
  • (B) 1-(r), 2-(q), 3-(s), 4-(p)
  • (C) 1-(q), 2-(s), 3-(r), 4-(p)

Practice Question 9: Complete hydrolysis of a thymine-containing nucleotide isolated from DNA yields:
  • (A) \(\beta\)-D-ribose, thymine, and phosphoric acid
  • (B) \(\beta\)-D-2-deoxyribose, thymine, and phosphoric acid
  • (C) \(\beta\)-D-2-deoxyribose, uracil, and phosphoric acid
  • (D) \(\beta\)-D-ribose, uracil, and phosphoric acid
Correct Answer: (B)
Explanation: A DNA nucleotide contains \(\beta\)-D-2-deoxyribose as its pentose sugar, a nitrogenous base (here, thymine), and a phosphate group at the \(5'\) position.
Practice Question 10: Which hormone is an iodinated derivative of the amino acid tyrosine, whose deficiency in the body leads to lethargy and obesity?
  • (A) Insulin
  • (B) Glucagon
  • (C) Thyroxine
  • (D) Estradiol

Explanation: Thyroxine, secreted by the thyroid gland, is an iodinated derivative of tyrosine. Abnormally low thyroxine causes hypothyroidism, characterised by lethargy and obesity.

9. Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • D/L vs. (+)/(−): Never assume D-compounds are dextrorotatory. D-(−)-fructose is D-series yet laevorotatory.
  • Sucrose vs. Maltose/Lactose: Sucrose is the only common disaccharide that is non-reducing. Maltose and lactose are reducing sugars.
  • Starch vs. Cellulose: Starch is a polymer of \(\alpha\)-D-glucose; cellulose is a polymer of \(\beta\)-D-glucose.
  • Vitamin \(B_{12}\) Exception: Although water-soluble, Vitamin \(B_{12}\) is stored in the human body, unlike Vitamin C and other B-vitamins.
  • Denaturation Scope: Denaturation unfolds \(2°\) and \(3°\) structures (disrupting H-bonds), but never breaks the \(1°\) peptide sequence.
  • RNA Base Ratios: Unlike double-stranded DNA (where \(\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{T}\) and \(\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{C}\)), single-stranded RNA shows no fixed relationship among the quantities of its four bases (A, G, C, U).

Concise Final Recap

Biomolecules sustain life through precise molecular architecture: carbohydrates supply energy and structural frameworks via glycosidic bonds; proteins execute structural and enzymatic catalysis through specific \(\alpha\)-amino acid sequences folded into \(2°\), \(3°\), and \(4°\) shapes; vitamins act as essential cofactors; nucleic acids encode and translate genetic information via \(3'\text{–}5'\) phosphodiester backbones and complementary base pairing; and hormones coordinate physiological balance across organs.
