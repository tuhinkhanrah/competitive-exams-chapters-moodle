NEET UG Chemistry • Organic Functional Groups

Alcohols, Phenols, and Ethers

A complete, mechanism-driven tutorial covering structure, nomenclature, physical trends, synthetic routes, chemical reactivity, and NEET UG practice assessment.

1. Learning Objectives and Syllabus Scope

Oxygen-containing organic derivatives—alcohols (\(\mathrm{R-OH}\)), phenols (\(\mathrm{Ar-OH}\)), and ethers (\(\mathrm{R-O-R'}\))—form a central pillar of NEET UG organic chemistry. Questions frequently test your ability to compare acidities, predict regioselectivity in alkene hydration or ether cleavage, choose valid Williamson synthesis pairs, and distinguish functional group isomers.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Classify alcohols, phenols, and ethers based on hydroxyl count, carbon hybridization (\(sp^3\) vs. \(sp^2\)), and substituent symmetry, and assign unambiguous IUPAC names.
  • Explain bond-angle and bond-length variations across methanol, phenol, and methoxymethane using hybridization and resonance.
  • Predict boiling point and water-solubility trends from intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding.
  • Design syntheses of alcohols (alkene hydration, hydroboration–oxidation, carbonyl reduction, Grignard addition), phenols (Dow's process, sulphonation, diazonium hydrolysis, cumene process), and ethers (acidic dehydration, Williamson synthesis).
  • Analyze O–H cleavage (acidity, esterification), C–O cleavage (Lucas test, dehydration, oxidation), aromatic electrophilic substitution in phenols/anisole, and \(\mathrm{HX}\) cleavage of ethers.

2. Classification, Functional Group Geometry, and IUPAC Nomenclature

2.1 Structural Classification

Replacing one or more hydrogen atoms of an aliphatic hydrocarbon with a hydroxyl (\(\mathrm{-OH}\)) group yields an alcohol, whereas direct attachment of \(\mathrm{-OH}\) to an aromatic ring carbon produces a phenol. Replacing the hydroxyl hydrogen of an alcohol or phenol with an alkyl or aryl group gives an ether.

  • By number of \(\mathrm{-OH}\) groups: Compounds are termed monohydric (1 \(\mathrm{-OH}\)), dihydric (2 \(\mathrm{-OH}\), e.g., ethane-1,2-diol), trihydric (3 \(\mathrm{-OH}\), e.g., propane-1,2,3-triol), or polyhydric.
  • By carbon hybridization in monohydric alcohols:
    • \(\mathrm{C}_{sp^3}\mathrm{-OH}\) alcohols: Include primary (\(1^\circ\), \(\mathrm{RCH_2OH}\)), secondary (\(2^\circ\), \(\mathrm{R_2CHOH}\)), and tertiary (\(3^\circ\), \(\mathrm{R_3COH}\)) alkyl alcohols; allylic alcohols (\(\mathrm{-OH}\) on an \(sp^3\) carbon adjacent to \(\mathrm{C=C}\), e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-CH_2OH}\)); and benzylic alcohols (\(\mathrm{-OH}\) on an \(sp^3\) carbon directly bonded to a benzene ring, e.g., \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2OH}\)). Both allylic and benzylic alcohols can be \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), or \(3^\circ\).
    • \(\mathrm{C}_{sp^2}\mathrm{-OH}\) alcohols (Vinylic): The \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group is attached directly to an \(sp^2\)-hybridized carbon of a double bond, as in ethenol (\(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-OH}\)), which rapidly tautomerizes to ethanal.
  • Classification of Ethers: Symmetrical (simple) ethers have identical groups on oxygen (\(\mathrm{R-O-R}\), e.g., \(\mathrm{C_2H_5OC_2H_5}\)), while unsymmetrical (mixed) ethers have two different groups (\(\mathrm{R-O-R'}\) or \(\mathrm{Ar-O-R}\), e.g., \(\mathrm{CH_3OC_2H_5}\) or \(\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3}\)).

2.2 Geometry and Bond Parameters

In alcohols and ethers, oxygen is \(sp^3\)-hybridized with two bond pairs and two lone pairs. Lone-pair–lone-pair repulsion compresses the \(\mathrm{C-O-H}\) bond angle in methanol (\(108.9^\circ\)) slightly below the ideal tetrahedral angle (\(109.5^\circ\)). In methoxymethane, steric repulsion between the two bulky methyl groups widens the \(\mathrm{C-O-C}\) angle to \(111.7^\circ\). In phenol, the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond (\(136\ \mathrm{pm}\)) is noticeably shorter than in methanol (\(142\ \mathrm{pm}\)) or methoxymethane (\(141\ \mathrm{pm}\)) because (i) the oxygen lone pair conjugates with the aromatic \(\pi\)-system, imparting partial double-bond character, and (ii) the ring carbon is \(sp^2\)-hybridized (higher \(s\)-character, shorter bond).

2.3 IUPAC Nomenclature Rules

  • Alcohols: Select the longest carbon chain containing the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) carbon. Number from the end giving the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group the lowest locant. Replace the terminal -e of the parent alkane with -ol (retain -e for diols/triols, e.g., propane-1,2-diol). In cyclic alcohols, C-1 is the carbon bearing \(\mathrm{-OH}\).
  • Phenols: The parent name is phenol (C-1 bears \(\mathrm{-OH}\)). Common prefixes ortho (1,2-), meta (1,3-), and para (1,4-) are used for disubstituted derivatives; dihydroxybenzenes are named benzene-1,2-diol (catechol), benzene-1,3-diol (resorcinol), and benzene-1,4-diol (hydroquinone/quinol).
  • Ethers: Named as alkoxyalkanes (or alkoxyarenes). The larger/more complex alkyl or aryl group is chosen as the parent hydrocarbon, and the smaller \(\mathrm{-OR}\) group is treated as an alkoxy substituent.

3. Physical Properties and Hydrogen Bonding

The physical behavior of alcohols, phenols, and ethers is governed by the interplay between the polar oxygen-containing functional group and the nonpolar hydrocarbon skeleton.

  • Boiling Points: Alcohols and phenols exhibit substantially higher boiling points than alkanes, haloalkanes, and ethers of comparable molar mass because their \(\mathrm{-OH}\) groups form extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Among isomeric alcohols, boiling point decreases with branching because branching reduces surface area and weakens van der Waals forces (e.g., \(\text{butan-1-ol} > \text{butan-2-ol} > \text{2-methylpropan-2-ol}\)). Ethers lack \(\mathrm{O-H}\) bonds and cannot hydrogen-bond with themselves, so their boiling points resemble those of alkanes of similar molar mass (e.g., ethoxyethane boils at \(307.6\ \mathrm{K}\), close to n-pentane at \(309.1\ \mathrm{K}\), whereas butan-1-ol boils at \(390\ \mathrm{K}\)).
  • Solubility in Water: Lower alcohols and simple ethers (such as ethoxyethane, \(\sim 7.5\ \mathrm{g/100\ mL}\)) are appreciably soluble in water because the oxygen lone pairs accept hydrogen bonds from water molecules. Solubility drops rapidly as the hydrophobic alkyl or aryl chain grows larger.
  • Steam Volatility of Nitrophenols: o-Nitrophenol forms intramolecular hydrogen bonds (chelation), leaving molecules discrete and steam volatile. In contrast, p-nitrophenol undergoes intermolecular hydrogen bonding, causing molecular association and a much higher boiling point (non-volatile in steam). This difference enables their separation by steam distillation.

4. Synthetic Methods: Preparing Alcohols, Phenols, and Ethers

4.1 Preparation of Alcohols

  1. Acid-Catalyzed Hydration of Alkenes: Alkenes add water in the presence of dilute acid (\(\mathrm{H^+}\)) via a carbocation intermediate, following Markovnikov's rule. Carbocation rearrangement (1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shift) may occur if a more stable carbocation can form.
    \[ \mathrm{R-CH=CH_2 + H_2O \xrightleftharpoons{H^+} R-CH(OH)-CH_3} \]
  2. Hydroboration–Oxidation: Diborane (\(\mathrm{(BH_3)_2}\)) adds across a double bond in a concerted syn fashion so boron attaches to the less substituted \(sp^2\) carbon; oxidation with \(\mathrm{H_2O_2/OH^-}\) yields the anti-Markovnikov alcohol without carbocation rearrangement.
    \[ \mathrm{3\,R-CH=CH_2 \xrightarrow{(i)\ (BH_3)_2} (R-CH_2-CH_2)_3B \xrightarrow{(ii)\ 3\,H_2O_2,\ OH^-} 3\,R-CH_2-CH_2OH + B(OH)_3} \]
  3. Reduction of Carbonyls, Carboxylic Acids, and Esters:
    • Aldehydes give \(1^\circ\) alcohols and ketones give \(2^\circ\) alcohols upon catalytic hydrogenation (\(\mathrm{H_2/Pd,\ Pt,\ or\ Ni}\)) or hydride reduction using \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) or \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\).
    • Carboxylic acids and esters require the stronger reducing agent \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) (followed by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) to yield \(1^\circ\) alcohols; \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) is too mild to reduce carboxylic acids or esters. Industrially, acids are esterified and reduced catalytically with \(\mathrm{H_2}\).
  4. Grignard Reagent Addition (\(\mathrm{RMgX}\)): Nucleophilic addition of \(\mathrm{R^{\delta-}-Mg^{\delta+}X}\) to a carbonyl followed by acidic hydrolysis constructs new \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bonds:
    • Methanal (\(\mathrm{HCHO}\)) + \(\mathrm{RMgX}\) \(\rightarrow\) \(1^\circ\) alcohol (\(\mathrm{RCH_2OH}\)).
    • Other aldehydes (\(\mathrm{R'CHO}\)) + \(\mathrm{RMgX}\) \(\rightarrow\) \(2^\circ\) alcohol (\(\mathrm{R'CH(OH)R}\)).
    • Ketones (\(\mathrm{R'COR''}\)) + \(\mathrm{RMgX}\) \(\rightarrow\) \(3^\circ\) alcohol (\(\mathrm{R'R''C(OH)R}\)).

4.3 Preparation of Ethers

  1. Acid-Catalyzed Dehydration of \(1^\circ\) Alcohols: Heating primary alcohols with \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) shows temperature-dependent selectivity. For ethanol, \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) substitution gives ethoxyethane at \(413\ \mathrm{K}\), whereas elimination gives ethene at \(443\ \mathrm{K}\). Secondary and tertiary alcohols fail to give ethers cleanly because E1/E2 elimination to alkenes dominates.
    \[ \mathrm{2\,CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow{H_2SO_4,\ 413\ K} CH_3CH_2-O-CH_2CH_3 + H_2O} \]
  2. Williamson Ether Synthesis: An \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) reaction between a sodium alkoxide (or phenoxide) and an unhindered primary alkyl halide:
    \[ \mathrm{R-X\ (1^\circ) + R'-O^-Na^+ \longrightarrow R-O-R' + NaX} \]
    NEET UG High-Yield Rule for Williamson Synthesis
    Alkoxides are strong bases as well as nucleophiles. If you use a \(3^\circ\) alkyl halide with a primary alkoxide, E2 elimination occurs exclusively to give an alkene. Always place the bulky (\(2^\circ\) or \(3^\circ\)) group on the alkoxide and keep the alkyl halide primary (or methyl). Similarly, to prepare aryl alkyl ethers, react sodium phenoxide with an alkyl halide—never a haloarene with an alkoxide.

5. Chemical Reactions and Mechanisms

5.1 Reactions Involving O–H Bond Cleavage (Nucleophilic Behavior & Acidity)

  • Reaction with Active Metals and Alkali: Both alcohols and phenols react with \(\mathrm{Na}\), \(\mathrm{K}\), or \(\mathrm{Al}\) to liberate \(\mathrm{H_2}\) gas and form alkoxides/phenoxides. However, only phenols are acidic enough to dissolve in aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to form sodium phenoxide; alcohols do not react appreciably with aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\).
  • Acidity of Alcohols: Electron-releasing alkyl groups (\(+I\) effect) increase electron density on oxygen, destabilizing the alkoxide ion and decreasing O–H polarity. Thus, acid strength follows:
    \[ \mathrm{H_2O > 1^\circ\ alcohol > 2^\circ\ alcohol > 3^\circ\ alcohol} \]
    Because water is a stronger acid than alcohols, alkoxide ions (\(\mathrm{RO^-}\)) are stronger bases than hydroxide (\(\mathrm{OH^-}\)).
  • Why Phenols (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 10\)) Are Millions of Times More Acidic Than Alcohols (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 16\)):
    1. The \(\mathrm{-OH}\) oxygen is bonded to a more electronegative \(sp^2\)-hybridized ring carbon, pulling electron density away from O–H.
    2. The conjugate base (phenoxide ion) is strongly stabilized by resonance delocalization of the negative charge onto the ortho and para ring carbons, without charge separation (whereas resonance in neutral phenol involves unfavourable charge separation).
  • Substituent Effects on Phenol Acidity: Electron-withdrawing groups (\(\mathrm{-NO_2}\), \(\mathrm{-CN}\), \(\mathrm{-X}\)) stabilize the phenoxide ion and increase acidity—most strongly at ortho and para positions (e.g., p-nitrophenol \(\mathrm{p}K_a = 7.1\), o-nitrophenol \(\mathrm{p}K_a = 7.2\), m-nitrophenol \(\mathrm{p}K_a = 8.3\), phenol \(\mathrm{p}K_a = 10.0\)). Electron-releasing groups (\(\mathrm{-CH_3}\), \(\mathrm{-OCH_3}\)) destabilize phenoxide and decrease acidity (cresols, \(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 10.1\text{–}10.2\)).
  • Esterification & Acetylation: Alcohols and phenols react with carboxylic acids or acid anhydrides (catalytic \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), reversible) and with acid chlorides (in pyridine to neutralize \(\mathrm{HCl}\)) to form esters. Acetylation of salicylic acid with acetic anhydride gives aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid).

5.2 Reactions Involving C–O Bond Cleavage in Alcohols

  • Lucas Test (\(\mathrm{conc.\ HCl + anhydrous\ ZnCl_2}\)): Distinguishes \(1^\circ\), \(2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) alcohols by the rate of formation of insoluble alkyl chloride turbidity via carbocation stability:
    • \(3^\circ\) alcohol: Immediate turbidity.
    • \(2^\circ\) alcohol: Turbidity appears in ~5 minutes.
    • \(1^\circ\) alcohol: No turbidity at room temperature.
  • Acid-Catalyzed Dehydration: Alcohols lose water with protic acids (\(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\)) to yield alkenes. Ease of dehydration follows carbocation stability: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\) (e.g., ethanol requires conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) at \(443\ \mathrm{K}\), whereas 2-methylpropan-2-ol dehydrates in \(20\%\ \mathrm{H_3PO_4}\) at \(358\ \mathrm{K}\)).
  • Oxidation & Catalytic Dehydrogenation:
    • \(1^\circ\) alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{PCC\ or\ anhydrous\ CrO_3}}\) Aldehyde (stops at aldehyde; PCC preserves \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds).
    • \(1^\circ\) alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{KMnO_4\ or\ K_2Cr_2O_7/H^+}}\) Carboxylic acid.
    • \(2^\circ\) alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CrO_3}}\) Ketone.
    • \(3^\circ\) alcohol resists mild oxidation (vigorous \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)/heat cleaves \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bonds).
    • Heated Copper (\(573\ \mathrm{K}\)): \(1^\circ\) alcohol \(\rightarrow\) Aldehyde; \(2^\circ\) alcohol \(\rightarrow\) Ketone (dehydrogenation); \(3^\circ\) alcohol \(\rightarrow\) Alkene (dehydration!).

5.3 Reactions Specific to Phenols

  • Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS): The \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group strongly activates the ring and directs incoming electrophiles to ortho and para positions.
    • Nitration: Dilute \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) (\(298\ \mathrm{K}\)) gives o- and p-nitrophenol; conc. \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) gives 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid, low yield due to oxidation; better made via phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid).
    • Bromination: \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in low-polarity \(\mathrm{CS_2}\) or \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) (\(273\ \mathrm{K}\)) gives p-bromophenol (major) + o-bromophenol. \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) water gives an immediate white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol.
  • Kolbe's Reaction: Sodium phenoxide reacts with weak electrophile \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), followed by acidification, to yield 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid).
  • Reimer–Tiemann Reaction: Phenol treated with \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) and aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) introduces a \(\mathrm{-CHO}\) group at the ortho position, giving salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde) after acidification.
  • Zinc Dust & Oxidation: Heating phenol with \(\mathrm{Zn}\) dust reduces it to benzene. Oxidation with \(\mathrm{Na_2Cr_2O_7/H_2SO_4}\) yields benzoquinone.

5.4 Commercially Important Alcohols

  • Methanol (\(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\), wood spirit): Produced industrially by catalytic hydrogenation of \(\mathrm{CO}\) over \(\mathrm{ZnO-Cr_2O_3}\) at \(573\text{–}673\ \mathrm{K}\) and \(200\text{–}300\ \mathrm{atm}\). Highly toxic: oxidized in the liver to methanal and methanoic acid, causing blindness or death (treated with intravenous ethanol to competitively swamp the oxidizing enzyme).
  • Ethanol (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}\)): Produced by ethene hydration or anaerobic fermentation of sugars (sucrose \(\xrightarrow{\text{invertase}}\) glucose + fructose \(\xrightarrow{\text{zymase}}\) ethanol + \(\mathrm{CO_2}\); zymase is inhibited above \(14\%\) alcohol). Industrial ethanol is denatured with \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) (color) and pyridine (foul odor).

5.5 Chemical Reactions of Ethers

  • Cleavage by Hydrogen Halides (\(\mathrm{HI > HBr > HCl}\)):
    • Mixed \(1^\circ/2^\circ\) dialkyl ethers: Protonation gives an oxonium ion; \(\mathrm{I^-}\) attacks the less sterically hindered carbon via \(\mathrm{S_N2}\), yielding the smaller alkyl iodide and the larger alcohol. With excess hot \(\mathrm{HI}\), the alcohol also converts to alkyl iodide.
    • Ethers with a \(3^\circ\) alkyl group: Cleavage proceeds via \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) to form the stable \(3^\circ\) carbocation, yielding the \(3^\circ\) alkyl iodide and the smaller alcohol (e.g., \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-O-CH_3 + HI \rightarrow (CH_3)_3C-I + CH_3OH}\)).
    • Alkyl aryl ethers (e.g., anisole): Always cleave at the \(\mathrm{O-R}\) bond to yield phenol + alkyl iodide, because the \(\mathrm{C_{sp^2}-O}\) bond is stronger (partial double-bond character) and aryl carbon resists \(\mathrm{S_N2}\).
  • Electrophilic Substitution in Anisole: The \(\mathrm{-OCH_3}\) group activates the ring and is o/p-directing. Anisole undergoes bromination in ethanoic acid without \(\mathrm{FeBr_3}\) (\(p\)-bromoanisole \(90\%\)), Friedel–Crafts alkylation/acylation (with \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\)), and nitration (\(\mathrm{HNO_3/H_2SO_4}\)) to give predominantly the para isomer.

6. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: IUPAC Naming and Classification

Problem: Assign the IUPAC name and classify the alcohol \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CH(OH)-CH_2CH_3}\) and the ether \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2-O-CH(CH_3)CH_2CH_3}\).

Solution:

  • For \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CH(OH)-CH_2CH_3}\): The longest chain has 6 carbons. Numbering from the end closer to \(\mathrm{-OH}\) places \(\mathrm{-OH}\) at C-3 and the double bond at C-4. IUPAC name: hex-4-en-3-ol. Because \(\mathrm{-OH}\) is on an \(sp^3\) carbon bonded to two carbons and adjacent to \(\mathrm{C=C}\), it is a secondary (\(2^\circ\)) allylic alcohol.
  • For \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2-O-CH(CH_3)CH_2CH_3}\): The larger chain is butane (4 carbons), with an ethoxy substituent at C-2. IUPAC name: 2-ethoxybutane (an unsymmetrical ether).
Worked Example 2: Regioselectivity in Alkene Hydration vs. Hydroboration–Oxidation

Problem: Starting from 2-methylbut-1-ene, predict the major alcohol formed upon (a) reaction with \(\mathrm{H_2O/H^+}\) and (b) reaction with \(\mathrm{(i)\ BH_3,\ (ii)\ H_2O_2/OH^-}\).

Solution:

  • (a) Acid-catalyzed hydration: Protonation of \(\mathrm{CH_2=C(CH_3)CH_2CH_3}\) forms the more stable \(3^\circ\) carbocation at C-2, which is trapped by water to yield 2-methylbutan-2-ol (Markovnikov product).
  • (b) Hydroboration–oxidation: Boron adds to the less hindered terminal carbon (C-1), and oxidation replaces boron with \(\mathrm{-OH}\) to give 2-methylbutan-1-ol (anti-Markovnikov product).
Worked Example 3: Selecting Reactants for Williamson Synthesis

Problem: How would you synthesize 2-ethoxy-2-methylpropane cleanly using Williamson synthesis, and why does the alternative pair fail?

Solution:

  • Correct pair: React sodium 2-methylpropan-2-oxide, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-ONa}\), with primary bromoethane, \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2Br}\), to give \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-OCH_2CH_3}\) via \(\mathrm{S_N2}\).
  • Why the reverse fails: Reacting \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2ONa}\) with \(3^\circ\) \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-Br}\) causes E2 elimination instead of substitution, producing 2-methylpropene as the major product.
Worked Example 4: Ether Cleavage with HI

Problem: Predict the major products when (a) 1-methoxypropane and (b) 1-ethoxy-4-methylbenzene are heated with one equivalent of concentrated \(\mathrm{HI}\).

Solution:

  • (a) 1-Methoxypropane (\(\mathrm{CH_3-O-CH_2CH_2CH_3}\)): Both alkyl groups are primary, so \(\mathrm{I^-}\) attacks the less hindered methyl carbon via \(\mathrm{S_N2}\), yielding iodomethane (\(\mathrm{CH_3I}\)) + propan-1-ol (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2OH}\)).
  • (b) 1-Ethoxy-4-methylbenzene: The \(\mathrm{C_{sp^2}-O}\) bond does not cleave; \(\mathrm{I^-}\) attacks the ethyl group to give 4-methylphenol (p-cresol) + iodoethane (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5I}\)).

7. NEET UG Practice Questions with Worked Solutions

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which of the following compounds has the shortest \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length?

  • (A) Ethanol
  • (B) Methoxymethane
  • (C) Phenol
  • (D) 2-Methylpropan-2-ol
Answer: (C)
Worked Solution: In phenol, the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group is attached to an \(sp^2\)-hybridized aromatic carbon, and the lone pair on oxygen conjugates with the benzene ring. This gives the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond partial double-bond character (\(136\ \mathrm{pm}\)), making it shorter than the single \(\mathrm{C_{sp^3}-O}\) bonds in ethanol, methoxymethane (\(141\ \mathrm{pm}\)), and 2-methylpropan-2-ol.
Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

What is the correct decreasing order of acidic strength for the following compounds: (I) p-Nitrophenol, (II) Phenol, (III) p-Cresol, (IV) Propan-1-ol?

  • (A) I > II > III > IV
  • (B) I > III > II > IV
  • (C) II > I > III > IV
  • (D) IV > III > II > I
Answer: (A)
Worked Solution: Phenols are much more acidic than aliphatic alcohols like propan-1-ol (IV) due to resonance stabilization of the phenoxide ion. In p-nitrophenol (I), the electron-withdrawing \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\) group (\(-M, -I\)) stabilizes the phenoxide ion, increasing acidity above phenol (II). In p-cresol (III), the electron-releasing \(\mathrm{-CH_3}\) group (\(+I\), hyperconjugation) destabilizes the phenoxide ion, lowering acidity below phenol. Hence: I > II > III > IV.
Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

When 3-methylbutan-2-ol is heated with concentrated \(\mathrm{HBr}\), the major organic product obtained is:

  • (A) 2-Bromo-3-methylbutane
  • (B) 1-Bromo-3-methylbutane
  • (C) 2-Bromo-2-methylbutane
  • (D) 2-Methylbut-2-ene
Answer: (C)
Worked Solution: Protonation of 3-methylbutan-2-ol followed by loss of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) initially generates a \(2^\circ\) carbocation at C-2. A rapid 1,2-hydride shift from C-3 produces a more stable \(3^\circ\) carbocation at C-3 (re-numbered C-2), which is trapped by \(\mathrm{Br^-}\) to yield 2-bromo-2-methylbutane.
Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which reagent selectively reduces \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CHO}\) to \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH-CH_2OH}\) without affecting the carbon–carbon double bond?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{H_2 / Pd}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{KMnO_4 / OH^-}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Cu / 573\ K}\)
Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) selectively reduces the polar carbonyl group (\(\mathrm{-CHO}\)) to a primary alcohol (\(\mathrm{-CH_2OH}\)) while leaving the nonpolar \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bond intact. Catalytic hydrogenation (\(\mathrm{H_2/Pd}\)) would reduce both \(\mathrm{C=C}\) and \(\mathrm{C=O}\).
Practice Question 5 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): o-Nitrophenol is more volatile in steam than p-nitrophenol.
Reason (R): o-Nitrophenol exhibits intramolecular hydrogen bonding, whereas p-nitrophenol exhibits intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)
Worked Solution: Intramolecular H-bonding in o-nitrophenol keeps molecules discrete with weak intermolecular forces, making it steam volatile. Intermolecular H-bonding in p-nitrophenol causes molecular association, raising its boiling point and reducing steam volatility.
Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Vapours of 2-methylpropan-2-ol are passed over heated copper at \(573\ \mathrm{K}\). The major product formed is:

  • (A) 2-Methylpropanal
  • (B) Propanone
  • (C) 2-Methylpropene
  • (D) Butan-2-one
Answer: (C)
Worked Solution: While \(1^\circ\) and \(2^\circ\) alcohols undergo dehydrogenation over \(\mathrm{Cu}\) at \(573\ \mathrm{K}\) to form aldehydes and ketones respectively, \(3^\circ\) alcohols lack an \(\alpha\)-hydrogen and instead undergo dehydration to form an alkene—here, 2-methylpropene.
Practice Question 7 (Statement-Based MCQ)

Statement I: Reaction of phenol with \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) and aqueous \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) followed by acidification yields salicylaldehyde.
Statement II: Reaction of sodium phenoxide with \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) followed by acidification yields 2-hydroxybenzoic acid.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (A)
Worked Solution: Statement I describes the Reimer–Tiemann reaction (product: salicylaldehyde). Statement II describes Kolbe's reaction (product: 2-hydroxybenzoic acid / salicylic acid). Both statements are factually correct.
Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Heating tert-butyl ethyl ether with one equivalent of \(\mathrm{HI}\) yields:

  • (A) 2-Methylpropan-2-ol and iodoethane
  • (B) 2-Iodo-2-methylpropane and ethanol
  • (C) 2-Methylpropene and ethanol
  • (D) 2-Iodo-2-methylpropane and iodoethane
Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: When one alkyl group of an ether is tertiary, cleavage with \(\mathrm{HI}\) follows an \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) mechanism because departure of ethanol leaves behind the stable \(3^\circ\) tert-butyl carbocation, which combines with \(\mathrm{I^-}\) to give 2-iodo-2-methylpropane and ethanol.
Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In the cumene process for manufacturing phenol, the hydrocarbon cumene is oxidized by air and then hydrolyzed with dilute acid. The structure of cumene and the co-product formed are, respectively:

  • (A) Propylbenzene and propanal
  • (B) Isopropylbenzene and propanone
  • (C) Ethylbenzene and ethanal
  • (D) Toluene and methanal
Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: Cumene is isopropylbenzene. Air oxidation forms cumene hydroperoxide, which undergoes acid-catalyzed rearrangement and cleavage to yield phenol and propanone (acetone) as the valuable co-product.
Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Which set of reactants is most suitable for preparing 4-methoxynitrobenzene (1-methoxy-4-nitrobenzene) via Williamson synthesis?

  • (A) 1-Bromo-4-nitrobenzene + \(\mathrm{CH_3ONa}\)
  • (B) Sodium 4-nitrophenoxide + \(\mathrm{CH_3Br}\)
  • (C) 4-Nitrophenol + \(\mathrm{CH_3OH + H_2SO_4}\)
  • (D) 4-Nitroaniline + \(\mathrm{CH_3Br}\)
Answer: (B)
Worked Solution: In Williamson synthesis, the nucleophile should be the phenoxide (sodium 4-nitrophenoxide) and the electrophile should be a primary methyl halide (\(\mathrm{CH_3Br}\)), which readily undergoes \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) attack. Option (A) requires nucleophilic substitution on an \(sp^2\) aryl halide carbon, which is unreactive under mild \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) conditions.

8. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist, and Final Recap

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Williamson Synthesis Trap: Never pair a \(3^\circ\) alkyl halide with an alkoxide; E2 elimination gives an alkene instead of an ether.
  • HI Cleavage Trap: \(1^\circ/2^\circ\) mixed ethers follow \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) (smaller group forms iodide), whereas \(3^\circ\) ethers follow \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) (\(3^\circ\) group forms iodide). Alkyl aryl ethers always give phenol + alkyl iodide.
  • \(\mathrm{Cu / 573\ K}\) Trap: \(1^\circ\) and \(2^\circ\) alcohols dehydrogenate to carbonyls, but \(3^\circ\) alcohols dehydrate to alkenes.
  • Bromination of Phenol: \(\mathrm{Br_2/CS_2}\) gives monobromophenols (p-major), whereas \(\mathrm{Br_2/H_2O}\) gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate).

Quick Revision Checklist

  • \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length: Phenol (\(136\ \mathrm{pm}\)) < Methoxymethane (\(141\ \mathrm{pm}\)) < Methanol (\(142\ \mathrm{pm}\)).
  • Boiling point: Alcohols/Phenols \(\gg\) Ethers \(\approx\) Alkanes of comparable molar mass.
  • Acidity: Nitrophenols > Phenol > Cresols > \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) > \(1^\circ\) Alcohol > \(2^\circ\) Alcohol > \(3^\circ\) Alcohol.
  • Lucas test reactivity: \(3^\circ\) (instant) > \(2^\circ\) (~5 min) > \(1^\circ\) (no turbidity at room temp).
  • Alkene \(\rightarrow\) Alcohol: \(\mathrm{H_2O/H^+}\) (Markovnikov, rearrangement possible) vs. \(\mathrm{BH_3/H_2O_2, OH^-}\) (anti-Markovnikov, no rearrangement).
Concise Final Recap

Alcohols and phenols owe their high boiling points and water solubility to hydrogen bonding, while ethers behave physically like alkanes in boiling point yet dissolve in water by accepting H-bonds. Resonance stabilization of the phenoxide ion makes phenols far more acidic than alcohols and strongly activates the aromatic ring toward ortho/para electrophilic substitution. Mastering the competition between substitution (\(\mathrm{S_N1}/\mathrm{S_N2}\)) and elimination (E1/E2) governs both the synthesis and \(\mathrm{HX}\) cleavage of ethers.
