Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

Standard: National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (NEET UG) | Subject: Chemistry (Organic Chemistry)

NEET UG Strategy & Weightage Blueprint

The unit Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers is one of the most heavily weighted pillars of organic chemistry in NEET UG, historically contributing 2 to 3 questions (8 to 12 marks) every year. The assessment profile focuses sharply on:

  • Mechanistic Reagents: Acid-catalysed hydration vs. Hydroboration-Oxidation (anti-Markovnikov syn-addition), Oxymercuration-Demercuration, and Grignard additions to carbonyl compounds.
  • Acidity & Electronic Effects: Resonance stability of the phenoxide ion, substituent effects (electron-withdrawing \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\) vs. electron-donating \(\mathrm{-OCH_3}\) / \(\mathrm{-CH_3}\)), and comparative \(pK_a\) trends.
  • Named Chemical Conversions: Cumene process for phenol, Reimer-Tiemann and Kolbe reactions, Dow process, and Williamson ether synthesis (substrate elimination vs. substitution).
  • Regioselective Cleavage: Unsymmetric ether cleavage by cold vs. hot concentrated \(\mathrm{HI}\) (\(\mathrm{S_N2}\) on \(1^\circ/2^\circ\) alkyl groups vs. \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) via stable \(3^\circ\) carbocations).

By studying this comprehensive module, aspirants will achieve the mastery required to solve NEET UG single-correct MCQs, assertion-reason items, and multi-statement matching problems on:

2. Classification & IUPAC Nomenclature

2.1 Structural Classification

Organic hydroxy and alkoxy derivatives are classified according to the number of functional groups and the electronic environment of the carbon atom bearing the oxygen atom:

  • Monohydric, Dihydric, Trihydric, and Polyhydric Alcohols: Based on whether they contain one, two, three, or many hydroxyl (\(\mathrm{-OH}\)) groups per molecule. Examples: Ethanol (monohydric), Ethane-1,2-diol (glycol, dihydric), Propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol, trihydric).
  • Primary (\(1^\circ\)), Secondary (\(2^\circ\)), and Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) Alcohols: Based on the hybridization and degree of substitution of the carbon atom bound to the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group (\(-\mathrm{CH_2OH}\), \(>\mathrm{CHOH}\), or \(-\mathrm{C(OH)<}\)).
  • Allylic Alcohols: Hydroxyl group attached to an \(sp^3\)-hybridized carbon adjacent to a carbon-carbon double bond: \(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-CH_2OH}\).
  • Benzylic Alcohols: Hydroxyl group attached to an \(sp^3\)-hybridized carbon atom adjacent to an aromatic ring: \(\mathrm{C_6H_5-CH_2OH}\).
  • Vinylic Alcohols & Phenols: Hydroxyl group bound directly to an \(sp^2\)-hybridized carbon. In vinylic alcohols, it is attached to an alkene carbon (\(\mathrm{CH_2=CH-OH}\), tautomerizes to ethanal); in phenols, it is directly bound to an \(sp^2\) aromatic carbon (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH}\)).
  • Ethers (Symmetrical vs. Unsymmetrical): Classified as simple (symmetrical) if the two alkyl or aryl groups bound to oxygen are identical (\(\mathrm{R = R'}\), e.g., diethyl ether \(\mathrm{C_2H_5-O-C_2H_5}\)), or mixed (unsymmetrical) if they differ (\(\mathrm{R \neq R'}\), e.g., ethyl methyl ether \(\mathrm{CH_3-O-C_2H_5}\)).

2.2 IUPAC Rules and Systematic Names

In the IUPAC system, aliphatic alcohols are named by replacing the terminal -e of the parent alkane name with the suffix -ol. The longest carbon chain containing the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group is numbered from the end that gives the hydroxyl carbon the lowest locant. For polyhydric alcohols, the suffix -diol, -triol, etc., is appended to the full alkane name without dropping the terminal -e (e.g., ethane-1,2-diol). Ethers are named as alkoxyalkanes, where the larger alkyl group serves as the parent alkane, and the smaller alkyl group along with the oxygen is treated as an alkoxy substituent.

3. Structures of Functional Groups

The geometries around the oxygen atom in alcohols, phenols, and ethers reflect the interplay between hybridisation, non-bonding electron pair repulsions, and steric crowding:

  • Methanol (\(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\)): The central oxygen atom is \(sp^3\)-hybridized with a tetrahedral electron-pair geometry. However, because lone pair-lone pair repulsions exceed bond pair-bond pair repulsions (VSEPR theory), the \(\mathrm{C-O-H}\) bond angle is slightly compressed to \(108.9^\circ\) (less than the ideal tetrahedral angle of \(109.5^\circ\)). The \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length is \(142\,\mathrm{pm}\).
  • Phenol (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH}\)): The oxygen atom is bonded to an \(sp^2\)-hybridized ring carbon. The \(\mathrm{C-O-H}\) bond angle is \(109^\circ\). Crucially, the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length in phenol is \(136\,\mathrm{pm}\), significantly shorter than in methanol (\(142\,\mathrm{pm}\)). This bond contraction stems from two distinct factors:
    1. Partial double-bond character due to conjugation of the unshared electron pair on oxygen with the \(\pi\)-electron system of the aromatic ring.
    2. Greater electronegativity of the \(sp^2\)-hybridized aromatic carbon atom compared to the \(sp^3\)-hybridized carbon of methanol.
  • Methoxymethane (\(\mathrm{CH_3OCH_3}\)): The oxygen atom is also \(sp^3\)-hybridized with two bonding pairs and two lone pairs. However, the \(\mathrm{C-O-C}\) bond angle is widened to \(111.7^\circ\) (greater than \(109.5^\circ\)). This angle expansion is caused by strong repulsive steric interactions between the two bulky methyl groups. The \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length is \(141\,\mathrm{pm}\).

4. Synthesis of Alcohols and Phenols

4.1 Preparation of Alcohols

A. From Alkenes

  1. Acid-Catalyzed Hydration: Alkenes react with water in the presence of dilute mineral acid (\(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)) as a catalyst to yield alcohols according to Markovnikov's rule. \[\mathrm{CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} CH_3-CH(OH)-CH_3}\] NEET Mechanistic Check: The reaction proceeds via a planar carbocation intermediate. Consequently, skeletal carbocation rearrangements (1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shifts) readily occur if a more stable carbocation can form.
  2. Hydroboration-Oxidation: Diborane (\(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\)) reacts with alkenes to form trialkylboranes, which are subsequently oxidized by alkaline hydrogen peroxide (\(\mathrm{H_2O_2 / OH^-}\)) to yield alcohols. \[\mathrm{6\,CH_3-CH=CH_2 + (BH_3)_2 \longrightarrow 2\,(CH_3-CH_2-CH_2)_3B \xrightarrow{3\,H_2O_2,\,OH^-} 6\,CH_3-CH_2-CH_2-OH + 2\,B(OH)_3}\] Key Features: Yields the pure anti-Markovnikov addition product. The net addition of \(\mathrm{H}\) and \(\mathrm{OH}\) occurs via concerted syn-addition (stereospecific) with zero carbocation rearrangement.

B. Reduction of Carbonyl Compounds and Carboxylic Acids

  • Aldehydes and Ketones: Aldehydes are reduced to primary (\(1^\circ\)) alcohols, while ketones are reduced to secondary (\(2^\circ\)) alcohols using catalytic hydrogenation (\(\mathrm{H_2 / Ni, Pt, Pd}\)) or hydride transfer reagents like sodium borohydride (\(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\)) in ethanol or lithium aluminium hydride (\(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\)) in ether. \[\mathrm{R-CHO \xrightarrow{NaBH_4} R-CH_2OH}\] \[\mathrm{R-CO-R' \xrightarrow{NaBH_4} R-CH(OH)-R'}\] NEET Selectivity: \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) selectively reduces aldehydes and ketones without affecting carboxylic acids, esters, or isolated alkene double bonds.
  • Carboxylic Acids and Esters: Carboxylic acids are reduced to primary alcohols by the powerful reducing agent \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\). Because \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) is expensive, commercial synthesis converts acids to esters first, followed by catalytic hydrogenation: \[\mathrm{R-COOH \xrightarrow{R'OH,\,H^+} R-COOR' \xrightarrow{H_2\,/\,catalyst} R-CH_2OH + R'OH}\]

C. Addition of Grignard Reagents to Carbonyl Compounds

Nucleophilic addition of an alkyl/aryl magnesium halide (\(\mathrm{RMgX}\)) to carbonyl groups followed by hydrolytic workup is a versatile route to synthesize all three classes of alcohols:

  • Methanal (Formaldehyde, \(\mathrm{HCHO}\)) \(\longrightarrow\) Primary (\(1^\circ\)) alcohol. \[\mathrm{HCHO + RMgX \longrightarrow R-CH_2-OMgX \xrightarrow{H_2O} R-CH_2OH + Mg(OH)X}\]
  • All other Aldehydes (\(\mathrm{R'CHO}\)) \(\longrightarrow\) Secondary (\(2^\circ\)) alcohol. \[\mathrm{R'CHO + RMgX \longrightarrow R'-CH(R)-OMgX \xrightarrow{H_2O} R'-CH(OH)-R + Mg(OH)X}\]
  • Ketones (\(\mathrm{R'COR''}\)) \(\longrightarrow\) Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) alcohol. \[\mathrm{R'COR'' + RMgX \longrightarrow R'R''C(R)-OMgX \xrightarrow{H_2O} R'R''(R)C-OH + Mg(OH)X}\]

4.2 Preparation of Phenols

Phenols (carbolic acids) are manufactured industrially or synthesized in the laboratory via four primary pathways:

  1. From Haloarenes (Dow's Process): Chlorobenzene is fused with molten sodium hydroxide at high temperature (\(623\,\mathrm{K}\)) and elevated pressure (\(300\,\mathrm{atm}\)) to produce sodium phenoxide, which upon acidification yields phenol. \[\mathrm{C_6H_5Cl + 2\,NaOH \xrightarrow[300\,atm]{623\,K} C_6H_5ONa + NaCl + H_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} C_6H_5OH}\]
  2. From Benzenesulphonic Acid: Benzene is sulphonated with oleum to give benzenesulphonic acid. Heating with molten \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) forms sodium phenoxide, which yields phenol on acidification. \[\mathrm{C_6H_6 \xrightarrow{Oleum} C_6H_5SO_3H \xrightarrow{(i)\,NaOH,\,(ii)\,H^+} C_6H_5OH}\]
  3. From Diazonium Salts: Aniline is diazotized with nitrous acid (\(\mathrm{NaNO_2 + HCl}\)) at \(273\text{–}278\,\mathrm{K}\) to form benzenediazonium chloride. Warming with water or dilute acids liberates phenol and dinitrogen gas. \[\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2 \xrightarrow[273-278\,K]{NaNO_2 + HCl} C_6H_5N_2^+Cl^- \xrightarrow{Warm\,H_2O} C_6H_5OH + N_2 \uparrow + HCl}\]
  4. From Cumene (Isopropylbenzene) — Industrial Method: Cumene is oxidized by aerial oxygen to cumene hydroperoxide. Subsequent cleavage with dilute aqueous acid converts it quantitatively to phenol and propan-2-one (acetone) as an economically valuable byproduct. \[\mathrm{C_6H_5-CH(CH_3)_2 + O_2 \longrightarrow C_6H_5-C(CH_3)_2(OOH) \xrightarrow{H^+,\,H_2O} C_6H_5OH + CH_3COCH_3}\]

5. Physical Properties & Intermolecular Forces

5.1 Boiling Points

The physical properties of alcohols and phenols are governed by the highly polar \(\mathrm{O-H}\) bond, which enables extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding:

  • Comparison with Hydrocarbons and Ethers: Alcohols have significantly higher boiling points than alkanes, haloalkanes, and ethers of comparable molecular masses. For example, ethanol (\(\mathrm{M_r = 46}\), bp \(351\,\mathrm{K}\)) boils far higher than methoxymethane (\(\mathrm{M_r = 46}\), bp \(248\,\mathrm{K}\)) and propane (\(\mathrm{M_r = 44}\), bp \(231\,\mathrm{K}\)).
  • Homologous Trends: Within a homologous series, boiling points increase monotonically with carbon chain length due to larger surface area and stronger van der Waals dispersion forces.
  • Effect of Branching: Among isomeric alcohols, boiling points decrease with increased branching (\(1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ\)). Branching causes the molecule to adopt a more compact spherical shape, decreasing surface area and weakening dispersion interactions: \[\mathrm{n\text{-}Butyl\,alcohol\,(391\,K) > sec\text{-}Butyl\,alcohol\,(373\,K) > tert\text{-}Butyl\,alcohol\,(356\,K)}\]

5.2 Intramolecular vs. Intermolecular Hydrogen Bonding in Nitrophenols

A classic high-frequency NEET concept is the separation of ortho-nitrophenol and para-nitrophenol by steam distillation:

  • ortho-Nitrophenol: The proximity of the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) and \(\mathrm{-NO_2}\) groups allows the formation of a 6-membered chelate ring via intramolecular hydrogen bonding. This prevents the hydroxyl proton from associating with neighbouring molecules, resulting in weaker intermolecular attractions, higher volatility, lower boiling point, and steam volatility.
  • para-Nitrophenol (and meta-nitrophenol): The spatial separation of the two functional groups precludes intramolecular bonding. Instead, molecules associate strongly into polymeric chains via intermolecular hydrogen bonding, causing a higher boiling point, lower volatility, higher aqueous solubility, and complete lack of steam volatility.

5.3 Solubility

Lower molecular mass alcohols and ethers form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, making them miscible with water. However, solubility decreases sharply as the size of the non-polar, hydrophobic alkyl/aryl group increases. High-mass alcohols and diaryl ethers are practically insoluble in water.

6. Chemical Reactions of Alcohols & Phenols

Alcohols act as versatile species: they react as nucleophiles when the \(\mathrm{O-H}\) bond breaks, and as electrophiles when protonated, leading to \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond cleavage.

6.1 Reactions Involving Cleavage of the \(\mathrm{O-H}\) Bond

A. Acidity of Alcohols and Phenols

  1. Reaction with Active Metals: Alcohols and phenols react with electropositive metals like \(\mathrm{Na, K, Al}\) to liberate dihydrogen gas: \[\mathrm{2\,R-OH + 2\,Na \longrightarrow 2\,R-O^-Na^+ + H_2 \uparrow}\] \[\mathrm{6\,(CH_3)_3C-OH + 2\,Al \longrightarrow 2\,((CH_3)_3C-O)_3Al + 3\,H_2 \uparrow}\] Alcohols act as Brønsted acids by donating a proton to active metals. However, alcohols are weaker acids than water because electron-releasing alkyl groups (\(+I\) effect) intensify electron density on oxygen, destabilizing the alkoxide anion. Acidity decreases in the order: \[\mathbf{H_2O > 1^\circ\,Alcohol > 2^\circ\,Alcohol > 3^\circ\,Alcohol}\]
  2. Acidity of Phenols: Phenols are markedly more acidic than aliphatic alcohols and react with aqueous sodium hydroxide (\(\mathrm{NaOH}\)) to form sodium phenoxide: \[\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + NaOH \rightleftharpoons C_6H_5ONa + H_2O}\] Why are phenols more acidic than alcohols?
    • The \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group is attached to an \(sp^2\)-hybridized carbon with higher electronegativity than an \(sp^3\) carbon.
    • Deprotonation yields the phenoxide ion, which is resonance-stabilized by delocalization of the negative charge over the ortho and para positions of the benzene ring. Although neutral phenol also exhibits resonance, it involves unfavorable charge separation (\(\mathrm{O^+}\) and \(\mathrm{C^-}\)), making the un-ionized form less stable than the phenoxide ion. In contrast, in alkoxide ions (\(\mathrm{R-O^-}\)), negative charge is localized strictly on oxygen with no resonance stabilization.

B. Esterification

Alcohols and phenols react with carboxylic acids, acid anhydrides, and acid chlorides to form esters:

  • With Carboxylic Acids (Fischer Esterification): Catalyzed by concentrated \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\). The reaction is reversible, and water is removed concurrently to drive equilibrium. \[\mathrm{Ar/R-OH + R'-COOH \xrightleftharpoons{H^+} Ar/R-OCO-R' + H_2O}\]
  • With Acid Chlorides: Carried out in the presence of a base (such as pyridine) to neutralize the generated hydrochloric acid and shift equilibrium rightward. \[\mathrm{R-OH + R'COCl \xrightarrow{Pyridine} R-OCOR' + HCl}\]
  • Synthesis of Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic Acid): Acetylation of salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) with acetic anhydride in the presence of trace acid yields aspirin, an essential analgesic and antipyretic: \[\mathrm{2\text{-(HO)}C_6H_4COOH + (CH_3CO)_2O \xrightarrow{H^+} 2\text{-(CH_3COO)}C_6H_4COOH + CH_3COOH}\]

6.2 Reactions Involving Cleavage of the Carbon-Oxygen (\(\mathrm{C-O}\)) Bond

A. Reaction with Hydrogen Halides (Lucas Test)

Alcohols react with hydrogen halides to form alkyl halides: \(\mathrm{R-OH + HX \xrightarrow{ZnCl_2} R-X + H_2O}\). This forms the basis of the Lucas Test (equimolar concentrated \(\mathrm{HCl}\) and anhydrous \(\mathrm{ZnCl_2}\)) to distinguish \(1^\circ, 2^\circ\), and \(3^\circ\) alcohols based on the rate of appearance of insoluble alkyl chloride turbidity:

  • Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) Alcohols: React immediately at room temperature to form instantaneous cloudiness/turbidity (proceeds via stable \(3^\circ\) carbocation).
  • Secondary (\(2^\circ\)) Alcohols: Form turbidity within 5 to 10 minutes at room temperature.
  • Primary (\(1^\circ\)) Alcohols: Do not produce turbidity at room temperature; solution remains clear until heated.

B. Reaction with Phosphorus Halides and Thionyl Chloride

  • \(\mathrm{3\,R-OH + PCl_3 \longrightarrow 3\,R-Cl + H_3PO_3}\) (phosphorous acid).
  • \(\mathrm{R-OH + PCl_5 \longrightarrow R-Cl + POCl_3 + HCl \uparrow}\).
  • \(\mathrm{R-OH + SOCl_2 \xrightarrow{Pyridine} R-Cl + SO_2 \uparrow + HCl \uparrow}\). This method gives the highest purity because both coproducts (\(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\)) are gases that escape automatically.

C. Acid-Catalyzed Dehydration of Alcohols

Alcohols undergo dehydration (loss of a water molecule) upon heating with concentrated protic acids like \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) or \(\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\) to produce alkenes. The ease of dehydration follows the carbocation stability order: \(3^\circ > 2^\circ > 1^\circ\).

  • Ethanol (\(1^\circ\)): Requires harsh conditions — conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) at \(443\,\mathrm{K}\). \[\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow[443\,K]{conc.\,H_2SO_4} CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O}\]
  • Propan-2-ol (\(2^\circ\)): Dehydrates under milder conditions — \(85\%\,\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\) at \(440\,\mathrm{K}\). \[\mathrm{CH_3-CH(OH)-CH_3 \xrightarrow[440\,K]{85\%\,H_3PO_4} CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H_2O}\]
  • 2-Methylpropan-2-ol (\(3^\circ\)): Dehydrates under extremely mild conditions — \(20\%\,\mathrm{H_3PO_4}\) at \(358\,\mathrm{K}\). \[\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-OH \xrightarrow[358\,K]{20\%\,H_3PO_4} (CH_3)_2C=CH_2 + H_2O}\]

Three-Step Mechanism of Acid-Catalyzed Dehydration of Ethanol to Ethene

  1. Step 1: Formation of protonated alcohol (Oxonium ion): Rapid, reversible proton transfer from acid to the unshared electron pair on oxygen: \[\mathrm{CH_3CH_2-OH + H^+ \xrightleftharpoons{fast} CH_3CH_2-\overset{+}{O}H_2}\]
  2. Step 2: Formation of carbocation: Slowest, rate-determining step (RDS). Cleavage of the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond releases water: \[\mathrm{CH_3CH_2-\overset{+}{O}H_2 \xrightleftharpoons{slow\,(RDS)} CH_3-\overset{+}{C}H_2 + H_2O}\]
  3. Step 3: Elimination of a proton to form ethene: Fast base attack by water on the \(\beta\)-hydrogen: \[\mathrm{CH_3-\overset{+}{C}H_2 \xrightarrow{fast} CH_2=CH_2 + H^+}\]

D. Oxidation and Catalytic Dehydrogenation over Heated Copper

Oxidation of alcohols involves the cleavage of both an \(\mathrm{O-H}\) and a \(\mathrm{C-H}\) bond (dehydrogenation):

  • Primary (\(1^\circ\)) Alcohols: Oxidize to aldehydes using mild reagents such as Pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC) or Collins reagent in anhydrous \(\mathrm{CH_2Cl_2}\). Strong oxidizing agents like acidified potassium permanganate (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\)) or Jones reagent (\(\mathrm{CrO_3 / H_2SO_4}\)) oxidize primary alcohols directly to carboxylic acids: \[\mathrm{R-CH_2OH \xrightarrow{PCC} R-CHO \xrightarrow{KMnO_4,\,H^+} R-COOH}\]
  • Secondary (\(2^\circ\)) Alcohols: Oxidize smoothly to ketones with chromic anhydride (\(\mathrm{CrO_3}\)) or potassium dichromate: \[\mathrm{R-CH(OH)-R' \xrightarrow{CrO_3} R-CO-R'}\]
  • Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) Alcohols: Do not possess an \(\alpha\)-hydrogen and resist oxidation under neutral or alkaline conditions. Under vigorous acidic conditions (\(\mathrm{HNO_3, KMnO_4}\)), \(\mathrm{C-C}\) bond cleavage occurs, yielding a mixture of carboxylic acids with fewer carbon atoms.
  • Dehydrogenation over Hot Copper (\(\mathrm{Cu}\) at \(573\,\mathrm{K}\)):
    • \(1^\circ\) Alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Cu,\,573\,K}}\) Aldehyde (\(\mathrm{RCH_2OH \longrightarrow RCHO + H_2}\)).
    • \(2^\circ\) Alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Cu,\,573\,K}}\) Ketone (\(\mathrm{R_2CHOH \longrightarrow R_2CO + H_2}\)).
    • \(3^\circ\) Alcohol \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Cu,\,573\,K}}\) Alkene (Dehydration!) Tert-butyl alcohol dehydrates to 2-methylpropene instead of dehydrogenating: \[\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-OH \xrightarrow{Cu,\,573\,K} (CH_3)_2C=CH_2 + H_2O}\]

6.3 Distinctive Reactions of Phenol

Because the hydroxyl group strongly activates the aromatic ring by resonance (\(+R\) effect) toward electrophilic aromatic substitution at the ortho and para positions, phenols exhibit unique reactivity:

  1. Electrophilic Halogenation:
    • Bromine in Non-polar Solvents (\(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in \(\mathrm{CS_2}\) or \(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\) at \(273\,\mathrm{K}\)): Monobromination occurs yielding predominantly p-bromophenol along with minor o-bromophenol. (No Lewis acid catalyst like \(\mathrm{FeBr_3}\) is required because the \(\mathrm{-OH}\) group strongly activates the ring). \[\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + Br_2 \xrightarrow[273\,K]{CS_2} p\text{-}Br-C_6H_4OH\,(major) + o\text{-}Br-C_6H_4OH\,(minor)}\]
    • Bromine Water (\(\mathrm{Br_2 / H_2O}\)): In aqueous media, phenol ionizes significantly to the phenoxide ion, which activates the ring so strongly that rapid trisubstitution occurs, giving a characteristic white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol: \[\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + 3\,Br_2 \xrightarrow{H_2O} 2,4,6\text{-}Br_3C_6H_2OH \downarrow\,(white\,ppt) + 3\,HBr}\]
  2. Nitration:
    • With dilute \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\): Yields a mixture of ortho-nitrophenol and para-nitrophenol, separable by steam distillation.
    • With concentrated \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\) in conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\): Undergoes violent poly-nitration accompanied by substantial oxidative destruction to produce 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (Picric acid) in low yield. Modern commercial preparation treats phenol with conc. \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) to form phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid first, followed by warming with conc. \(\mathrm{HNO_3}\).
  3. Kolbe's Reaction: Phenoxide ion, being more nucleophilic than neutral phenol, undergoes electrophilic substitution with the weak electrophile carbon dioxide (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)) at \(400\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(4\text{–}7\,\mathrm{atm}\) pressure. Acidification produces 2-hydroxybenzoic acid (Salicylic acid) as the major product. \[\mathrm{C_6H_5ONa + CO_2 \xrightarrow[4-7\,atm]{400\,K} 2\text{-(HO)}C_6H_4COONa \xrightarrow{H^+} 2\text{-(HO)}C_6H_4COOH}\]
  4. Reimer-Tiemann Reaction: Heating phenol with chloroform (\(\mathrm{CHCl_3}\)) in aqueous sodium hydroxide at \(340\,\mathrm{K}\) introduces an aldehyde group (\(\mathrm{-CHO}\)) ortho to the hydroxyl group. Hydrolysis and acidification yield Salicylaldehyde (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde): \[\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + CHCl_3 + 3\,NaOH \longrightarrow 2\text{-(HO)}C_6H_4CHO + 3\,NaCl + 2\,H_2O}\] NEET Mechanistic Check: The reactive electrophile is the neutral, divalent, electron-deficient intermediate dichlorocarbene (\(:\mathrm{CCl_2}\)), generated by base-induced \(\alpha\)-elimination of \(\mathrm{HCl}\) from chloroform. If carbon tetrachloride (\(\mathrm{CCl_4}\)) is substituted for chloroform, salicylic acid is formed instead.
  5. Reaction with Zinc Dust: Heating phenol with zinc dust strips the oxygen atom, reducing phenol to benzene: \[\mathrm{C_6H_5OH + Zn \xrightarrow{\Delta} C_6H_6 + ZnO}\]
  6. Oxidation with Chromic Acid: Oxidation of phenol with acidified sodium dichromate (\(\mathrm{Na_2Cr_2O_7 / H_2SO_4}\)) forms a conjugated cyclic diketone, benzo-1,4-quinone (p-benzoquinone): \[\mathrm{C_6H_5OH \xrightarrow{Na_2Cr_2O_7 / H_2SO_4} O=C_6H_4=O\,(p\text{-}benzoquinone)}\] Phenols slowly oxidize in air to form complex quinone mixtures, turning commercial phenol samples pinkish-red over time.

7. Chemistry of Ethers

7.1 Preparation of Ethers

A. Industrial Dehydration of Alcohols

Ethanol undergoes intermolecular dehydration in the presence of concentrated sulfuric acid at \(413\,\mathrm{K}\) to yield ethoxyethane via a bimolecular nucleophilic substitution (\(\mathrm{S_N2}\)) mechanism:

\[\mathrm{2\,CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow[413\,K]{conc.\,H_2SO_4} CH_3CH_2-O-CH_2CH_3 + H_2O}\]

NEET Critical Temperature Warning: \(413\,\mathrm{K}\) vs. \(443\,\mathrm{K}\)

  • At \(413\,\mathrm{K}\): Intermolecular nucleophilic attack of unprotonated alcohol on protonated alcohol dominates, producing ether (\(\mathrm{S_N2}\)).
  • At \(443\,\mathrm{K}\): Intramolecular \(\beta\)-elimination dominates, producing ethene (\(\mathrm{E1}\)).
  • This synthetic method is strictly applicable to primary (\(1^\circ\)) unhindered alcohols. Secondary (\(2^\circ\)) and tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) alcohols yield predominantly alkenes due to steric hindrance and competitive elimination.

B. Williamson Ether Synthesis

Williamson synthesis is the most reliable laboratory method to prepare symmetrical and unsymmetrical ethers. An alkyl halide reacts with a sodium alkoxide or aryloxide:

\[\mathrm{R-X + R'-O^-Na^+ \longrightarrow R'-O-R + NaX}\] Key NEET Reaction Rules:
  • The reaction proceeds via an \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) substitution pathway. Therefore, the alkyl halide (\(\mathrm{R-X}\)) must be primary (\(1^\circ\)) and sterically unhindered.
  • Sodium alkoxides are strong nucleophiles and powerful Brønsted bases. If a secondary (\(2^\circ\)) or tertiary (\(3^\circ\)) alkyl halide is used, elimination dominates completely over substitution, producing an alkene as the exclusive major product (E2 mechanism).
  • To prepare tert-butyl methyl ether:
    • Correct Path: Sodium tert-butoxide + Chloromethane/Bromomethane \[\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-O^-Na^+ + CH_3-Br \xrightarrow{S_N2} (CH_3)_3C-O-CH_3 + NaBr}\]
    • Incorrect Path: Sodium methoxide + tert-Butyl bromide \[\mathrm{CH_3O^-Na^+ + (CH_3)_3C-Br \xrightarrow{E2} CH_2=C(CH_3)_2\,(2\text{-methylpropene}) + CH_3OH + NaBr}\]
  • Aryl halides and vinyl halides cannot be used as the halide component because partial double-bond character makes the \(\mathrm{C-X}\) bond unreactive toward nucleophilic substitution. Phenolic ethers (e.g., anisole) are prepared by reacting sodium phenoxide with methyl halides: \[\mathrm{C_6H_5O^-Na^+ + CH_3-I \longrightarrow C_6H_5-O-CH_3 + NaI}\]

7.2 Chemical Reactions of Ethers: Cleavage of the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) Bond

Ethers are comparatively unreactive, but undergo \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond cleavage when heated with concentrated hydrogen halides (\(\mathrm{HI > HBr > HCl}\)):

\[\mathrm{R-O-R' + HX \longrightarrow R-X + R'-OH}\] If excess hydrogen halide is used at elevated temperatures, the formed alcohol reacts further to yield a second equivalent of alkyl halide: \[\mathrm{R'-OH + HX \longrightarrow R'-X + H_2O}\]

Regioselectivity and Mechanism Rules for Asymmetric Ethers

  1. Case 1: Both Alkyl Groups are Primary or Secondary (\(1^\circ\) or \(2^\circ\)): The reaction proceeds via an \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) mechanism. After protonation of the ether oxygen, the iodide nucleophile (\(\mathrm{I^-}\)) selectively attacks the less sterically hindered alkyl group: \[\mathrm{CH_3-O-CH_2CH_2CH_3 + HI \longrightarrow CH_3-I + CH_3CH_2CH_2-OH}\]
  2. Case 2: One Alkyl Group is Tertiary (\(3^\circ\)): The reaction shifts to an \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) mechanism. After protonation, the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond breaks to form a stable tertiary carbocation, which is then trapped by iodide. Hence, the tertiary group forms the alkyl iodide: \[\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-O-CH_3 + HI \longrightarrow (CH_3)_3C-I + CH_3-OH}\]
  3. Case 3: Alkyl Aryl Ethers (e.g., Anisole): Protonation forms the oxonium ion \(\mathrm{C_6H_5-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH_3}\). The \(\mathrm{sp^2\,C(aromatic)-O}\) bond possesses partial double-bond character from resonance and resists cleavage. Nucleophilic attack by iodide takes place at the \(sp^3\) methyl carbon via \(\mathrm{S_N2}\), yielding phenol and methyl iodide: \[\mathrm{C_6H_5-O-CH_3 + HI \longrightarrow C_6H_5-OH + CH_3-I}\] Phenol does not react further with \(\mathrm{HI}\) to form iodobenzene because the aromatic carbon-oxygen bond cannot be cleaved under these conditions.

7.3 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution in Anisole

In alkyl aryl ethers (such as anisole, \(\mathrm{C_6H_5OCH_3}\)), the methoxy group (\(\mathrm{-OCH_3}\)) is ortho- and para-directing and activates the benzene ring due to the \(+R\) resonance effect of oxygen's lone pairs:

  • Halogenation: Anisole undergoes bromination with bromine in ethanoic acid without any \(\mathrm{FeBr_3}\) catalyst to give 90% para-bromoanisole and minor ortho-bromoanisole.
  • Friedel-Crafts Reactions:
    • Alkylation: Anisole reacts with chloromethane in the presence of anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) to give a mixture of 2-methoxytoluene (minor) and 4-methoxytoluene (major).
    • Acylation: Reaction with ethanoyl chloride (\(\mathrm{CH_3COCl}\)) and anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) yields 2-methoxyacetophenone (minor) and 4-methoxyacetophenone (major).
  • Nitration: Anisole reacts with a mixture of concentrated sulfuric and nitric acids to yield a mixture of 2-nitroanisole (minor) and 4-nitroanisole (major).

8. Commercially Important Alcohols

8.1 Methanol (\(\mathrm{CH_3OH}\), Wood Spirit)

  • Historical Origin: Originally obtained by the destructive distillation of wood.
  • Modern Industrial Manufacture: Catalytic hydrogenation of carbon monoxide in the presence of a zinc oxide-chromium oxide catalyst under high temperature and pressure: \[\mathrm{CO + 2\,H_2 \xrightarrow[200-300\,atm,\,573-673\,K]{ZnO - Cr_2O_3} CH_3OH}\]
  • Toxicity & Biological Hazard: Methanol is highly poisonous. Ingestion of even small quantities (\(10\text{–}30\,\mathrm{mL}\)) can cause blindness, while larger doses cause death due to respiratory failure. In the liver, methanol is oxidized by the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase to methanal (formaldehyde), and subsequently to methanoic acid (formic acid): \[\mathrm{CH_3OH \xrightarrow{Alcohol\,dehydrogenase} HCHO \longrightarrow HCOOH}\] Formaldehyde coagulates cellular protoplasm (analogous to boiling an egg), while formic acid induces severe metabolic acidosis and destroys retinal cells, causing optic neuropathy and permanent blindness.
  • Clinical Antidote: Intravenous infusion of dilute ethanol. Ethanol acts as a competitive substrate for alcohol dehydrogenase (having a much higher affinity for the enzyme), which halts the metabolic oxidation of methanol and allows it to be excreted harmlessly.

8.2 Ethanol (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}\))

  • Industrial Preparation by Fermentation: Fermentation of sugars from molasses, sugarcane, or grains: \[\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} + H_2O \xrightarrow{Invertase} C_6H_{12}O_6\,(Glucose) + C_6H_{12}O_6\,(Fructose)}\] \[\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \xrightarrow{Zymase} 2\,C_2H_5OH + 2\,CO_2 \uparrow}\] Fermentation is strictly anaerobic. If air is admitted, bacterium Acetobacter oxidizes ethanol to acetic acid, spoiling the mixture. Fermentation self-terminates when ethanol concentration reaches \(14\%\) because higher alcohol levels denature zymase.
  • Denaturation of Alcohol: Commercial alcohol is rendered unfit for drinking by adding poisonous compounds like methanol (\(5\%\)) and foul-smelling basic pyridine, along with a blue dye like copper sulfate. This blend is known as denatured spirit / methylated spirit.

9. Canonical Worked Examples (NEET UG Level)

Worked Example 1: Regiochemical Outcome of Alkene Hydration Mechanistic Strategy

Problem: Predict the major organic product formed when 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene undergoes: (a) Acid-catalyzed hydration (\(\mathrm{H_2O / H_2SO_4}\)), and (b) Hydroboration-oxidation (\(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\) followed by \(\mathrm{H_2O_2 / OH^-}\)).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Substrate: \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CH=CH_2}\)

(a) Acid-Catalyzed Hydration:

  1. Electrophilic addition of \(\mathrm{H^+}\) generates a secondary carbocation: \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-\overset{+}{C}H-CH_3}\) (\(2^\circ\)).
  2. A 1,2-methyl shift occurs from the adjacent quaternary carbon to form a more stable tertiary carbocation: \[\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-\overset{+}{C}H-CH_3 \xrightarrow{1,2-shift} (CH_3)_2\overset{+}{C}-CH(CH_3)_2\,(3^\circ)}\]
  3. Attack of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) and deprotonation yields 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol: \[\mathrm{(CH_3)_2C(OH)-CH(CH_3)_2}\]

(b) Hydroboration-Oxidation:

Proceeds via a cyclic 4-membered transition state with concerted addition of \(\mathrm{-H}\) and \(\mathrm{-BH_2}\). No carbocation is formed, precluding rearrangements. The reaction gives the pure anti-Markovnikov product: 3,3-dimethylbutan-1-ol, \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CH_2-CH_2OH}\).

Worked Example 2: Ether Cleavage Analysis \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) vs. \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) Logic

Problem: Identify the products obtained when 1-methoxy-4-(2-methylpropan-2-yl)benzene is treated with one equivalent of concentrated \(\mathrm{HI}\) at room temperature.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Structure: The molecule is \(\mathrm{p\text{-}(CH_3)_3C-C_6H_4-O-CH_3}\) (an alkyl aryl ether).

  1. Protonation occurs at the ether oxygen atom: \(\mathrm{p\text{-}(CH_3)_3C-C_6H_4-\overset{+}{O}(H)-CH_3}\).
  2. The \(\mathrm{C(aromatic)-O}\) bond has partial double-bond character due to resonance with the benzene ring and cannot be cleaved.
  3. The iodide nucleophile (\(\mathrm{I^-}\)) attacks the unhindered \(sp^3\)-hybridized methyl group via an \(\mathrm{S_N2}\) pathway.
  4. Products formed: 4-tert-butylphenol (\(\mathrm{p\text{-}(CH_3)_3C-C_6H_4-OH}\)) and iodomethane (\(\mathrm{CH_3I}\)).

11. Common NEET Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

NEET Exam Pitfall Traps

  • Alkene Hydration vs. Hydroboration-Oxidation: Acid-catalyzed hydration (\(\mathrm{H^+ / H_2O}\)) involves a carbocation intermediate and will rearrange (e.g., 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene gives 2,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol). Hydroboration-oxidation gives the un-rearranged anti-Markovnikov product (3,3-dimethylbutan-1-ol).
  • Williamson Synthesis Pitfall: Do not attempt to prepare a tertiary ether using a tertiary alkyl halide with an alkoxide. Tertiary halides undergo 100% E2 elimination with basic alkoxides to form alkenes. The halide must be \(1^\circ\).
  • Reimer-Tiemann Intermediate: The electrophile in the Reimer-Tiemann reaction is dichlorocarbene (\(:\mathrm{CCl_2}\)), not the trichloromethyl carbanion or chloroform. It contains 6 valence electrons and is electrically neutral.
  • Ether Cleavage with Tertiary Groups: Do not blindly apply the "iodide goes to smaller alkyl group" rule. If either alkyl group is tertiary (\(3^\circ\)), the reaction proceeds via an \(\mathrm{S_N1}\) pathway, and the iodide bonds to the tertiary carbon.
  • Alcohol Acidity vs. Water: Water is more acidic than all aliphatic alcohols except methanol. Methanol is marginally more acidic than water.

Rapid Revision Checklist

  • ☑ Phenol Synthesis from Cumene: Cumene + \(\mathrm{O_2 \longrightarrow}\) Cumene hydroperoxide \(\mathrm{\xrightarrow{H^+} Phenol + Acetone}\).
  • ☑ Bromination of Phenol: \(\mathrm{Br_2 / CS_2 \longrightarrow}\) p-Bromophenol; \(\mathrm{Br_2 / H_2O \longrightarrow}\) 2,4,6-Tribromophenol (white ppt).
  • ☑ Kolbe's vs. Reimer-Tiemann: Kolbe introduces \(\mathrm{-COOH}\) using \(\mathrm{CO_2}\); Reimer-Tiemann introduces \(\mathrm{-CHO}\) using \(\mathrm{CHCl_3 / NaOH}\).
  • ☑ Lucas Test: \(3^\circ\) alcohols \(\longrightarrow\) instant turbidity; \(2^\circ\) \(\longrightarrow\) 5 minutes; \(1^\circ\) \(\longrightarrow\) clear at room temperature.
  • ☑ Dehydrogenation over \(\mathrm{Cu}\) at \(573\,\mathrm{K}\): \(1^\circ \longrightarrow\) Aldehyde; \(2^\circ \longrightarrow\) Ketone; \(3^\circ \longrightarrow\) Alkene (dehydration).
  • ☑ Aspirin Synthesis: Salicylic acid + Acetic anhydride \(\mathrm{\xrightarrow{H^+}}\) Acetylsalicylic acid + Acetic acid.
