NEET UG Organic Chemistry

Aldehydes, Ketones, and Carboxylic Acids

Exam-Oriented Tutorial: Structure, Nomenclature, Synthesis, Mechanisms, Acidity Trends, and Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Overview

Carbonyl (\(>\mathrm{C=O}\)) and carboxyl (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)) compounds form the backbone of synthetic and biological organic chemistry. From natural fragrances such as vanillin, cinnamaldehyde, and salicylaldehyde to industrial solvents like acetone and biomolecules such as fatty acids, mastering their electronic structure allows you to predict virtually all their reactions logically.

What You Will Master for NEET UG

  • Write common and IUPAC names of aliphatic, cyclic, and aromatic aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids.
  • Relate \(sp^2\) hybridization, bond polarity, and hydrogen bonding to boiling points, solubility, and reactivity.
  • Select specific reagents for preparing aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids without over-oxidation or over-reduction.
  • Compare nucleophilic addition reactivity in aldehydes versus ketones using steric and electronic effects.
  • Apply named reactions (Rosenmund, Stephen, Etard, Gattermann–Koch, Clemmensen, Wolff–Kishner, Aldol, Cannizzaro, Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky) and diagnostic tests (2,4-DNP, Tollens', Fehling's, Iodoform, \(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\)).
  • Rank carboxylic acid strengths (\(\mathrm{p}K_a\)) using inductive (\(-I/+I\)) and resonance (\(-R/+R\)) effects.

2. Structure, Polarity & Nomenclature

2.1 Orbital Structure and Polarity of the Carbonyl Group

The carbonyl carbon atom is \(sp^2\)-hybridized and forms three coplanar \(\sigma\)-bonds separated by approximately \(120^\circ\). The unhybridized \(p\)-orbital on carbon overlaps sideways with a \(p\)-orbital on oxygen to form a \(\pi\)-bond, placing the \(\pi\)-electron cloud above and below the molecular plane. Oxygen also carries two non-bonding lone pairs.

Because oxygen is significantly more electronegative than carbon, the \(\mathrm{C=O}\) bond is strongly polarized. Resonance between the neutral covalent structure and the dipolar structure gives the carbonyl carbon a partial positive charge (\(\delta^+\), electrophilic Lewis acid center) and the carbonyl oxygen a partial negative charge (\(\delta^-\), nucleophilic Lewis base center). Consequently, aldehydes and ketones have substantial dipole moments and are more polar than ethers.

Tutorial Diagram 1: Hybridization, bond angles, and resonance-driven polarity of the carbonyl group.

In carboxylic acids (\(-\mathrm{COOH}\)), the carboxyl carbon is also \(sp^2\)-hybridized with \(\approx 120^\circ\) angles, but it is less electrophilic than an aldehyde or ketone carbon because the adjacent \(-\mathrm{OH}\) oxygen donates a lone pair by resonance into the carbonyl group.

2.2 Common and IUPAC Nomenclature Rules

  • Aldehydes: Common names replace the -ic acid ending of the corresponding carboxylic acid with -aldehyde; substituent positions use Greek letters (\(\alpha\) = carbon attached to \(-\mathrm{CHO}\), then \(\beta, \gamma\)). IUPAC names replace -e of the parent alkane with -al, numbering C-1 at the \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) carbon. When \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) is attached to a ring, use the suffix -carbaldehyde (e.g., cyclohexanecarbaldehyde; benzaldehyde is IUPAC-accepted). If three or more \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) groups are attached to an open chain, all are treated equally using -tricarbaldehyde (e.g., \(\mathrm{OHC-CH_2-CH(CHO)-CH_2-CHO}\) is propane-1,2,3-tricarbaldehyde).
  • Ketones: Common names list the two alkyl/aryl groups attached to \(\mathrm{C=O}\) (substituents on the two chains are labeled \(\alpha, \beta\) and \(\alpha', \beta'\)). Alkyl phenyl ketones use the acyl prefix + -phenone (e.g., acetophenone, propiophenone). IUPAC names replace -e with -one, numbering from the end closer to \(\mathrm{C=O}\) (in cyclic ketones, the carbonyl carbon is C-1).
  • Carboxylic Acids: Common names derive from natural sources (formic from ants, acetic from vinegar, butyric from butter). IUPAC names replace -e with -oic acid (C-1 at \(-\mathrm{COOH}\)). Polycarboxylic acids keep the alkane name and add -dioic acid or -tricarboxylic acid.

3. Physical Properties: Boiling Points & Solubility

  • Boiling Point Trend (Comparable Molar Mass): \[ \text{Alkane} < \text{Ether} < \text{Aldehyde} \approx \text{Ketone} < \text{Alcohol} < \text{Carboxylic Acid} \] Alkanes have only weak dispersion forces. Aldehydes and ketones have stronger dipole–dipole attractions than ethers, so they boil higher than hydrocarbons and ethers of similar mass. However, aldehydes and ketones lack \(\mathrm{O-H}\) bonds and cannot form intermolecular hydrogen bonds with themselves, so they boil lower than alcohols. Carboxylic acids boil highest because they form very strong, cyclic hydrogen-bonded dimers that persist even in the vapour phase or aprotic solvents.
  • Water Solubility: Lower aldehydes/ketones (up to 3–4 carbons, e.g., methanal, ethanal, propanone) and lower carboxylic acids (up to 4 carbons) are miscible with water because their carbonyl/carboxyl oxygen forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules. As the hydrophobic alkyl chain lengthens, solubility drops rapidly. Benzoic acid is nearly insoluble in cold water.

4. Methods of Preparation

4.1 Shared Routes to Aldehydes and Ketones

  • Oxidation & Dehydrogenation of Alcohols: Primary alcohols yield aldehydes with mild/anhydrous oxidants such as PCC (\(\mathrm{C_5H_5NH^+CrO_3Cl^-}\)), whereas secondary alcohols yield ketones with \(\mathrm{CrO_3}\) or PCC. Passing alcohol vapours over heated \(\mathrm{Cu}\) or \(\mathrm{Ag}\) dehydrogenates \(1^\circ\) alcohols to aldehydes and \(2^\circ\) alcohols to ketones.
  • Reductive Ozonolysis of Alkenes: Cleavage of \(\mathrm{C=C}\) with \(\mathrm{O_3}\) followed by \(\mathrm{Zn/H_2O}\) gives aldehydes, ketones, or a mixture depending on alkene substitution.
  • Hydration of Alkynes: Addition of water in the presence of \(\mathrm{HgSO_4}\) and dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) converts ethyne to ethanal, while all terminal or internal higher alkynes yield ketones via Markovnikov addition and tautomerism.

4.3 Selective Synthesis of Ketones

  • From Acyl Chlorides & Dialkylcadmium: Reacting Grignard reagent with \(\mathrm{CdCl_2}\) gives \(\mathrm{R_2Cd}\), which reacts with acyl chlorides to form ketones without further addition to tertiary alcohols: \[ 2\,\mathrm{R'COCl} + \mathrm{R_2Cd} \longrightarrow 2\,\mathrm{R'-CO-R} + \mathrm{CdCl_2} \]
  • From Nitriles & Grignard Reagents: \(\mathrm{R-C\equiv N} + \mathrm{R'MgX}\) in dry ether followed by acidic hydrolysis (\(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)) yields \(\mathrm{R-CO-R'}\).
  • Friedel–Crafts Acylation: Benzene or an activated arene treated with \(\mathrm{RCOCl}\) or \(\mathrm{ArCOCl}\) in the presence of anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) yields aromatic ketones (e.g., acetophenone, benzophenone).

4.4 Preparation of Carboxylic Acids

  • Oxidation of \(1^\circ\) Alcohols & Aldehydes: Strong oxidants (\(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) in neutral/acidic/alkaline medium, \(\mathrm{K_2Cr_2O_7/H^+}\), or Jones reagent \(\mathrm{CrO_3\text{-}H_2SO_4}\)) oxidize \(1^\circ\) alcohols to acids. Mild oxidants (Tollens' reagent) oxidize aldehydes to acids.
  • Vigorous Oxidation of Alkylbenzenes: Hot alkaline \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) followed by acidification oxidizes any \(1^\circ\) or \(2^\circ\) alkyl side chain (regardless of length) to \(-\mathrm{COOH}\). Exception: A \(3^\circ\) alkyl group (no benzylic \(\mathrm{H}\), e.g., tert-butylbenzene) is not oxidized.
  • Hydrolysis of Nitriles & Derivatives: Nitriles (\(\mathrm{RCN}\)) hydrolyze with \(\mathrm{H^+}\) or \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) via amides to \(\mathrm{RCOOH}\). Acyl chlorides, anhydrides, and esters also hydrolyze to carboxylic acids (basic hydrolysis gives carboxylate salt, requiring \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\) workup).
  • Grignard Reagent + Dry Ice (\(\mathrm{CO_2}\)): \(\mathrm{RMgX} + \mathrm{CO_2}\) in dry ether, then \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\) gives \(\mathrm{RCOOH}\) with one carbon more than the parent alkyl halide (ascending the series).

5. Chemical Reactions of Aldehydes and Ketones

5.1 Nucleophilic Addition: Mechanism & Reactivity

Unlike alkenes (which undergo electrophilic addition), the polar carbonyl carbon (\(\delta^+\)) is attacked by nucleophiles (\(\mathrm{Nu^-}\)) roughly perpendicular to the \(sp^2\) plane. Carbon rehybridizes from \(sp^2\) to \(sp^3\) to form a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate, which then captures \(\mathrm{H^+}\) to yield the neutral addition product.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Perpendicular nucleophilic attack on sp² carbonyl carbon yielding a tetrahedral sp³ product.

Reactivity Rule: Aldehydes are consistently more reactive than ketones for two reasons: (1) Steric — ketones have two bulky alkyl/aryl groups crowding the transition state; (2) Electronic — two alkyl groups donate more electron density (\(+I\)), reducing the \(\delta^+\) electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon. Aromatic aldehydes (e.g., benzaldehyde) are less reactive than aliphatic aldehydes (e.g., propanal) because benzene ring resonance donates \(\pi\)-electrons into the carbonyl group, lowering its electrophilicity.

Key Nucleophilic Additions & Addition–Elimination Reactions

  • \(\mathrm{HCN}\) (Base-catalyzed): Generates \(\mathrm{CN^-}\), which attacks to form cyanohydrins (\(\mathrm{RCH(OH)CN}\)).
  • \(\mathrm{NaHSO_3}\): Forms crystalline, water-soluble bisulphite adducts (favoured for aldehydes and unhindered methyl ketones). Acid or base regenerates the carbonyl compound, making this useful for purification.
  • Alcohols (Dry \(\mathrm{HCl}\) catalyst): Aldehydes + 1 equiv. \(\mathrm{ROH}\) \(\rightleftharpoons\) hemiacetal \(\xrightarrow{\mathrm{ROH}}\) acetal (gem-dialkoxy). Ketones + ethylene glycol give cyclic ketals. Acetals/ketals are stable to base and hydrolyze back in aqueous acid.
  • Ammonia Derivatives (\(\mathrm{H_2N-Z}\), weak acid catalyst): Reversible addition followed by rapid dehydration gives \(\mathrm{>C=N-Z}\):
    • \(\mathrm{Z = -H}\) (Ammonia) \(\rightarrow\) Imine; \(\mathrm{Z = -R}\) (Amine) \(\rightarrow\) Schiff's base
    • \(\mathrm{Z = -OH}\) (Hydroxylamine) \(\rightarrow\) Oxime; \(\mathrm{Z = -NH_2}\) (Hydrazine) \(\rightarrow\) Hydrazone
    • \(\mathrm{Z = -NHC_6H_3(NO_2)_2}\) (2,4-DNP) \(\rightarrow\) 2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazone (yellow/orange/red solid used to identify carbonyls)
    • \(\mathrm{Z = -NHCONH_2}\) (Semicarbazide) \(\rightarrow\) Semicarbazone (only the terminal \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) furthest from \(\mathrm{C=O}\) attacks, as the other \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) lone pair is delocalized into \(\mathrm{C=O}\))

5.2 Reduction, Oxidation & Distinguishing Tests

  • Reduction to Alcohols: \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\), \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\), or \(\mathrm{H_2/Ni}\) reduces aldehydes to \(1^\circ\) alcohols and ketones to \(2^\circ\) alcohols.
  • Deoxygenation (\(\mathrm{>C=O \rightarrow >CH_2}\)):
    • Clemmensen Reduction: \(\mathrm{Zn\text{-}Hg}\) + conc. \(\mathrm{HCl}\) (avoid if molecule has acid-sensitive groups like \(-\mathrm{OH}\) or alkenes).
    • Wolff–Kishner Reduction: \(\mathrm{NH_2NH_2}\) followed by \(\mathrm{KOH}\)/ethylene glycol, heat (avoid if molecule has base-sensitive groups like esters or halides).
  • Tollens' Test: Ammoniacal \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\), \([\mathrm{Ag(NH_3)_2}]^+\), oxidizes both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes to carboxylate ions while depositing a bright silver mirror (\(\mathrm{Ag}\)). Ketones do not react.
  • Fehling's Test: Alkaline \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) tartrate complex oxidizes aliphatic aldehydes to give a brick-red precipitate of \(\mathrm{Cu_2O}\). Crucial NEET point: Aromatic aldehydes (like benzaldehyde) and ketones do not respond to Fehling's test.
  • Haloform (Iodoform) Reaction: Compounds with a \(\mathrm{CH_3-C(=O)-}\) group (methyl ketones, ethanal) or \(\mathrm{CH_3-CH(OH)-}\) group (oxidized in situ) react with \(\mathrm{I_2/NaOH}\) (\(\mathrm{NaOI}\)) to give a yellow precipitate of \(\mathrm{CHI_3}\) and a carboxylate salt with one carbon less. \(\mathrm{C=C}\) double bonds are untouched.

5.3 Reactions Due to \(\alpha\)-Hydrogen & Disproportionation

  • Aldol Condensation: Aldehydes or ketones with at least one \(\alpha\)-hydrogen react in dilute alkali (\(\mathrm{NaOH}\) or \(\mathrm{Ba(OH)_2}\)) to form a \(\beta\)-hydroxy aldehyde (aldol) or \(\beta\)-hydroxy ketone (ketol), which dehydrates on warming to an \(\alpha,\beta\)-unsaturated carbonyl compound. Cross-aldol between two different carbonyls with \(\alpha\)-\(\mathrm{H}\) gives up to four products; using one partner without \(\alpha\)-\(\mathrm{H}\) (e.g., benzaldehyde + acetophenone) gives a clean major cross-aldol product.
  • Cannizzaro Reaction: Aldehydes with no \(\alpha\)-hydrogen (e.g., \(\mathrm{HCHO}\), \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CHO}\), \(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3CCHO}\)) undergo self-oxidation and reduction (disproportionation) when heated with concentrated alkali, yielding one molecule of alcohol and one molecule of carboxylic acid salt.
  • Electrophilic Ring Substitution: On an aromatic ring, \(-\mathrm{CHO}\) and \(-\mathrm{COR}\) are deactivating and meta-directing.

6. Carboxylic Acids: Acidity & Chemical Reactions

6.1 Acidity & Substituent Effects

Carboxylic acids react with active metals (\(\mathrm{Na}\)), alkalis (\(\mathrm{NaOH}\)), and even weak bases like \(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\) (evolving brisk \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) effervescence—a diagnostic test distinguishing carboxylic acids from phenols). Carboxylic acids (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 4\text{–}5\)) are far stronger acids than phenols (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 10\)) and alcohols (\(\mathrm{p}K_a \approx 16\)) because the carboxylate anion (\(\mathrm{RCOO^-}\)) is stabilized by two equivalent resonance structures where negative charge is delocalized equally over two electronegative oxygen atoms, whereas phenoxide has non-equivalent structures placing negative charge on less electronegative ring carbons.

Substituent Rules: Electron-withdrawing groups (EWG: \(-I, -R\)) stabilize \(\mathrm{RCOO^-}\) and increase acidity (lower \(\mathrm{p}K_a\)); electron-donating groups (EDG: \(+I, +R\)) destabilize \(\mathrm{RCOO^-}\) and decrease acidity (higher \(\mathrm{p}K_a\)). \[ \text{EWG strength: } -\mathrm{CF_3} > -\mathrm{NO_2} > -\mathrm{CN} > -\mathrm{F} > -\mathrm{Cl} > -\mathrm{Br} > -\mathrm{I} > -\mathrm{C_6H_5} \] \[ \text{Acidity: } \mathrm{CF_3COOH} > \mathrm{CCl_3COOH} > \mathrm{FCH_2COOH} > \mathrm{ClCH_2COOH} > \mathrm{HCOOH} > \mathrm{C_6H_5COOH} > \mathrm{CH_3COOH} \]

Note that benzoic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.19\)) is stronger than acetic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.76\)) because the \(sp^2\)-hybridized phenyl carbon is more electronegative than an \(sp^3\) methyl carbon. On a benzene ring, p-nitrobenzoic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 3.41\)) is stronger than benzoic acid, while p-methoxybenzoic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.46\)) is weaker.

6.2 Key Reactions of Carboxylic Acids

  • \(\mathrm{C-OH}\) Cleavage:
    • Anhydride formation: Heating with \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\) or \(\mathrm{P_2O_5}\) removes \(\mathrm{H_2O}\).
    • Esterification: \(\mathrm{RCOOH} + \mathrm{R'OH} \xrightleftharpoons{\mathrm{H^+}} \mathrm{RCOOR'} + \mathrm{H_2O}\) (nucleophilic acyl substitution; removing water or ester drives equilibrium forward).
    • Acyl chloride formation: \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\) is preferred over \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) or \(\mathrm{PCl_3}\) because its byproducts (\(\mathrm{SO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{HCl}\)) are gases that escape, leaving pure \(\mathrm{RCOCl}\).
    • Reaction with \(\mathrm{NH_3}\): Forms ammonium salt, which dehydrates on heating to an amide. Phthalic acid gives phthalamide, which on strong heating loses \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) to form cyclic phthalimide.
  • Reduction: \(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) or \(\mathrm{B_2H_6}\) reduces \(\mathrm{RCOOH}\) to \(\mathrm{RCH_2OH}\). \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) does not reduce carboxylic acids.
  • Decarboxylation: Heating \(\mathrm{RCOONa}\) with soda-lime (\(\mathrm{NaOH + CaO}\), 3:1) gives \(\mathrm{R-H} + \mathrm{Na_2CO_3}\) (step-down by one carbon). Kolbe electrolysis of aqueous \(\mathrm{RCOONa}\) gives \(\mathrm{R-R}\) (doubling the alkyl group).
  • Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) Reaction: Acids with an \(\alpha\)-hydrogen react with \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) or \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in the presence of red phosphorus (followed by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) to give \(\alpha\)-halocarboxylic acids.
  • Ring Substitution: \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) is meta-directing and deactivating (gives m-nitrobenzoic acid, m-bromobenzoic acid). Benzoic acid does not undergo Friedel–Crafts alkylation or acylation because \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) coordinates with the carboxyl group.

7. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 — Reactivity in Nucleophilic Addition

Problem: Arrange the following four carbonyl compounds (chosen as a representative comparison set) in increasing order of reactivity toward \(\mathrm{HCN}\) addition: Acetophenone, Butanone, Propanal, 4-Nitrobenzaldehyde.

Solution:

  1. Ketones are less reactive than aldehydes due to greater steric hindrance and \(+I\) electron donation from two carbon groups. Between the two ketones, acetophenone has resonance donation from the benzene ring into the carbonyl group in addition to steric bulk, making it less reactive than aliphatic butanone.
  2. Between the two aldehydes, 4-nitrobenzaldehyde has a strong \(-R/-I\) nitro group that withdraws electron density and increases the \(\delta^+\) charge on the carbonyl carbon compared with benzaldehyde, making it even more reactive than aliphatic propanal.
  3. Increasing order: Acetophenone < Butanone < Propanal < 4-Nitrobenzaldehyde.
Worked Example 2 — Structural Elucidation

Problem: An assumed organic liquid X (\(\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O}\)) forms an orange-red precipitate with 2,4-DNP, fails to reduce Tollens' reagent, and gives a yellow precipitate with \(\mathrm{I_2/NaOH}\). Vigorous oxidation of X yields a mixture of ethanoic acid and propanoic acid. Identify X.

Solution:

  1. Positive 2,4-DNP + negative Tollens' test confirms X is a ketone (not an aldehyde).
  2. Positive iodoform test (\(\mathrm{I_2/NaOH} \rightarrow \mathrm{CHI_3}\)) proves X is a methyl ketone (\(\mathrm{CH_3-CO-R}\)).
  3. Since the formula is \(\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O}\), \(\mathrm{R}\) must be \(\mathrm{C_3H_7}\). Vigorous oxidation gives ethanoic acid (\(\mathrm{C_2}\)) and propanoic acid (\(\mathrm{C_3}\)), which requires an unbranched propyl chain: pentan-2-one (\(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_2CH_3}\)). (Note: 3-methylbutan-2-one would yield ethanoic acid and propanone/carbon dioxide rather than propanoic acid.)
Worked Example 3 — Acidity Comparison

Problem: Compare the acid strength of three hypothetical equimolar aqueous solutions of: (I) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH(Cl)COOH}\), (II) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH(Cl)CH_2COOH}\), and (III) \(\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2COOH}\).

Solution:

Inductive effects (\(-I\)) weaken rapidly with distance along a saturated carbon chain. In (I), the electron-withdrawing \(-\mathrm{Cl}\) is at the \(\alpha\)-carbon (closest to \(-\mathrm{COOH}\)), strongly stabilizing the carboxylate anion. In (II), \(-\mathrm{Cl}\) is at the \(\beta\)-carbon (weaker \(-I\)). Compound (III) has no \(-I\) group. Thus, acidity order is (III) < (II) < (I).

Worked Example 4 — Chemoselective Reduction

Problem: A chemist wants to reduce the ketone carbonyl of ethyl 3-oxobutanoate (\(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2COOC_2H_5}\)) to a secondary alcohol without touching the ester group. Which reagent should be used?

Solution:

Use \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) followed by \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\). Sodium borohydride selectively reduces aldehydes and ketones to alcohols while leaving ester groups intact. (\(\mathrm{LiAlH_4}\) would reduce both the ketone and the ester to alcohols.)

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: These are original exam-style practice questions designed for NEET UG preparation.

Practice Question 1 — Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following compounds will give a positive Tollens' test but a negative Fehling's test?

  • (A) Ethanal
  • (B) Propanal
  • (C) Benzaldehyde
  • (D) Acetophenone

Answer: (C) Benzaldehyde

Worked Solution: Tollens' reagent oxidizes both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes to give a silver mirror. Fehling's solution is a weaker oxidant and does not oxidize aromatic aldehydes such as benzaldehyde. Ethanal and propanal are positive for both, while acetophenone (a ketone) is negative for both.

Practice Question 2 — Single-Correct MCQ

In the conversion of 4-oxopentanal (\(\mathrm{CH_3COCH_2CH_2CHO}\)) using \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) followed by acidic workup, the major product formed is:

  • (A) Pentane-1,4-diol
  • (B) 4-Hydroxypentanal
  • (C) 5-Hydroxypentan-2-one
  • (D) Pentanoic acid

Answer: (A) Pentane-1,4-diol

Worked Solution: \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) reduces both aldehyde (\(-\mathrm{CHO} \rightarrow -\mathrm{CH_2OH}\)) and ketone (\(\mathrm{>C=O} \rightarrow \mathrm{>CH-OH}\)) functional groups. Therefore, 4-oxopentanal is reduced completely to pentane-1,4-diol.

Practice Question 3 — Single-Correct MCQ

Which reagent sequence converts toluene directly into benzaldehyde via a chromium complex intermediate?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{KMnO_4/KOH}\), heat, then \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{CrO_2Cl_2}\) in \(\mathrm{CS_2}\), then \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{CO, HCl}\) with anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3/CuCl}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Cl_2}/h\nu\) followed by \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) at \(373\,\mathrm{K}\)

Answer: (B) \(\mathrm{CrO_2Cl_2}\) in \(\mathrm{CS_2}\), then \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\)

Worked Solution: This is the Etard reaction: chromyl chloride (\(\mathrm{CrO_2Cl_2}\)) oxidizes the methyl group of toluene to a chromium complex, which hydrolyzes to benzaldehyde. Option (A) gives benzoic acid; (C) starts from benzene (Gattermann–Koch); (D) proceeds via benzal chloride rather than a chromium complex.

Practice Question 4 — Statement-Based MCQ

Read the following two statements and choose the correct option:

Statement I: Carboxylic acids are stronger acids than phenols even though phenoxide ion has more resonance structures than carboxylate ion.
Statement II: In the carboxylate ion, the negative charge is delocalized over two equivalent electronegative oxygen atoms, whereas in phenoxide ion it is delocalized over one oxygen and less electronegative carbon atoms.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Answer: (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.

Worked Solution: Stability of a conjugate base depends on the quality (equivalence and electronegativity of atoms bearing charge) rather than just the count of resonance structures. Carboxylate's two equivalent structures on oxygen make it far more stable than phenoxide.

Practice Question 5 — Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following aldehydes undergoes the Cannizzaro reaction when heated with concentrated \(\mathrm{NaOH}\)?

  • (A) Propanal
  • (B) 2-Methylpropanal
  • (C) 2,2-Dimethylpropanal
  • (D) Phenylacetaldehyde

Answer: (C) 2,2-Dimethylpropanal

Worked Solution: Cannizzaro disproportionation requires an aldehyde with zero \(\alpha\)-hydrogens. In 2,2-dimethylpropanal (\(\mathrm{(CH_3)_3C-CHO}\)), the \(\alpha\)-carbon is bonded to three methyl groups and has no hydrogen atom. Propanal, 2-methylpropanal, and phenylacetaldehyde (\(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_2CHO}\)) all possess \(\alpha\)-hydrogens and undergo aldol condensation instead.

Practice Question 6 — Single-Correct MCQ

What is the correct increasing order of acid strength for the following carboxylic acids?
(i) 4-Methoxybenzoic acid   (ii) Benzoic acid   (iii) 4-Nitrobenzoic acid   (iv) Acetic acid

  • (A) (iv) < (i) < (ii) < (iii)
  • (B) (i) < (iv) < (ii) < (iii)
  • (C) (iv) < (ii) < (i) < (iii)
  • (D) (i) < (ii) < (iv) < (iii)

Answer: (A) (iv) < (i) < (ii) < (iii)

Worked Solution: Known \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) values: acetic acid (\(4.76\)) > 4-methoxybenzoic acid (\(4.46\), \(+R\) EDG weakens benzoic acid) > benzoic acid (\(4.19\)) > 4-nitrobenzoic acid (\(3.41\), \(-R/-I\) EWG strengthens acid). Smaller \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) means stronger acid.

Practice Question 7 — Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): Benzoic acid does not undergo Friedel–Crafts alkylation or acylation.
Reason (R): The carboxyl group is strongly deactivating and the Lewis acid catalyst \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) coordinates with the carboxyl group.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

Worked Solution: Both the strong electron-withdrawing deactivation of \(-\mathrm{COOH}\) and its complexation with anhydrous \(\mathrm{AlCl_3}\) prevent Friedel–Crafts substitution on benzoic acid.

Practice Question 8 — Single-Correct MCQ

Which reagent is preferred industrially and in the laboratory to convert \(\mathrm{RCOOH}\) into \(\mathrm{RCOCl}\) because its byproducts escape as gases?

  • (A) \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\)
  • (B) \(\mathrm{PCl_3}\)
  • (C) \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\)
  • (D) \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) / Red \(\mathrm{P}\)

Answer: (C) \(\mathrm{SOCl_2}\)

Worked Solution: \(\mathrm{RCOOH} + \mathrm{SOCl_2} \rightarrow \mathrm{RCOCl} + \mathrm{SO_2(g)} + \mathrm{HCl(g)}\). Practice Question 9 — Single-Correct MCQ

When tert-butylbenzene is heated with alkaline \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) followed by acidification with dilute \(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\), the expected outcome is:

  • (A) Benzoic acid
  • (B) 2-Methyl-2-phenylpropanoic acid
  • (C) No oxidation of the tert-butyl side chain
  • (D) Phthalic acid

Answer: (C) No oxidation of the tert-butyl side chain

Worked Solution: Side-chain oxidation of alkylbenzenes by \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) requires at least one benzylic hydrogen atom. Primary and secondary alkylbenzenes oxidize to benzoic acid, whereas tertiary alkylbenzenes lack a benzylic hydrogen and remain unoxidized.

Practice Question 10 — Single-Correct MCQ

When propanoic acid is treated with \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) in the presence of a small amount of red phosphorus followed by water, the product obtained is:

  • (A) 3-Bromopropanoic acid
  • (B) 2-Bromopropanoic acid
  • (C) Propanoyl bromide
  • (D) Bromoethane

Answer: (B) 2-Bromopropanoic acid

Worked Solution: This is the Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) reaction, which selectively substitutes an \(\alpha\)-hydrogen of a carboxylic acid with bromine or chlorine, yielding 2-bromopropanoic acid (\(\mathrm{CH_3CH(Br)COOH}\)).

9. Common Pitfalls, Revision Checklist & Final Recap

High-Yield NEET UG Traps to Avoid

  • Benzaldehyde & Fehling's Test: Benzaldehyde gives a positive Tollens' test but fails Fehling's test.
  • \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) Scope: \(\mathrm{NaBH_4}\) reduces aldehydes and ketones, but cannot reduce carboxylic acids or esters.
  • Semicarbazide Regioselectivity: Only the terminal \(-\mathrm{NH_2}\) (not adjacent to \(\mathrm{C=O}\)) acts as a nucleophile to form semicarbazones.
  • 3° Alkylbenzene Oxidation: \(\mathrm{KMnO_4}\) oxidizes \(1^\circ\) and \(2^\circ\) benzylic chains to \(-\mathrm{COOH}\), but leaves \(3^\circ\) alkyl groups unchanged.
  • Clemmensen vs Wolff–Kishner: Use Wolff–Kishner (basic) when acid-sensitive groups (\(-\mathrm{OH}\), \(\mathrm{C=C}\)) are present; use Clemmensen (acidic) when base-sensitive groups (esters, halogens) are present.

Concise Final Recap

Carbonyl reactivity is governed by the electrophilic \(sp^2\) carbon: aldehydes outpace ketones due to lower steric crowding and weaker \(+I\) donation. Nucleophilic addition dominates \(\mathrm{>C=O}\) chemistry, while \(\alpha\)-hydrogens enable aldol condensations (and lack of \(\alpha\)-\(\mathrm{H}\) enables Cannizzaro disproportionation). In carboxylic acids, equivalent resonance in the carboxylate ion drives high acidity, which is systematically tuned by \(-I/-R\) and \(+I/+R\) substituents.
