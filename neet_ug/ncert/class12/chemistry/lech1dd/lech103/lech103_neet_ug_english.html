Chemical Kinetics: Rates, Mechanisms, and Energetics

A standalone, exam-oriented study guide covering reaction rates, integrated rate laws, temperature dependence, catalysis, and collision theory.

1. Learning Objectives & Conceptual Scope

To understand any chemical transformation completely, chemists ask three distinct questions. Each is answered by a separate branch of physical chemistry:

  • Thermodynamics predicts feasibility: at constant temperature and pressure, a process is spontaneous only when \(\Delta G < 0\).
  • Chemical Equilibrium predicts extent: how far the reaction proceeds toward products before dynamic balance is reached.
  • Chemical Kinetics predicts speed and pathway: how fast reactants convert to products and through which molecular steps the transformation occurs.
Key Distinction for NEET UG
Thermodynamic feasibility does not guarantee a fast reaction. For instance, the conversion of diamond to graphite is thermodynamically favorable, yet the rate at room temperature is imperceptibly slow because of a huge activation energy barrier.

By mastering this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Express average and instantaneous reaction rates using stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Distinguish between reaction order (experimental) and molecularity (mechanistic).
  • Apply zero-order and first-order integrated rate equations, including gas-phase pressure forms and half-life relations.
  • Analyze temperature dependence using the Arrhenius equation, Maxwell–Boltzmann curves, and catalyst energy profiles.
  • Interpret bimolecular reaction rates using collision frequency and steric orientation factors.

2. Rate of a Chemical Reaction

Reactions span vast timescales—from nearly instantaneous ionic precipitations (such as \(\mathrm{Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) \to AgCl(s)}\)) to very slow processes (like iron rusting) and moderate-speed molecular reactions (like sucrose inversion).

Average vs. Instantaneous Rate

At constant volume, the rate of a reaction is defined as the positive change in molar concentration of a species per unit time. Because reactant concentration falls (\(\Delta[\mathrm{R}] < 0\)) while product concentration rises (\(\Delta[\mathrm{P}] > 0\)), a minus sign is placed before reactant terms so that reaction rate is always positive.

\[ r_{\mathrm{av}} = -\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{R}]}{\Delta t} = +\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{P}]}{\Delta t} \qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{\mathrm{inst}} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} r_{\mathrm{av}} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{P}]}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

The units of reaction rate are \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) (or \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\)). For gaseous reactions measured via partial pressures at constant temperature, rate is expressed in \(\mathrm{atm\,s^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{bar\,min^{-1}}\).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Average rate (secant slope over a finite interval) versus instantaneous rate (tangent slope at a specific time).

Stoichiometric Normalization of Rate

When stoichiometric coefficients differ from unity, individual species are consumed or formed at different speeds. To obtain a single, unique reaction rate, divide each species' rate of change by its stoichiometric coefficient. For a general balanced equation:

\[ a\,\mathrm{A} + b\,\mathrm{B} \longrightarrow c\,\mathrm{C} + d\,\mathrm{D} \] \[ \text{Rate of reaction} = -\frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{b}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{B}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{c}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{d}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{D}]}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

3. Rate Law, Rate Constant, Order, and Molecularity

Reaction rates generally depend on reactant concentrations (or partial pressures for gases), temperature, and the presence of a catalyst.

Rate Law and Rate Constant

The experimentally determined equation relating reaction rate to molar concentrations of reactants is called the rate law (or differential rate equation):

\[ \text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^{x}[\mathrm{B}]^{y} \]
  • Rate constant (\(k\)): A proportionality constant specific to a reaction at a given temperature. It equals the reaction rate when all reactant concentrations are \(1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\).
  • Exponents (\(x, y\)): The partial orders with respect to \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\). They cannot be deduced from the balanced chemical equation alone and must be measured experimentally.
  • Overall order (\(n\)): The sum of exponents in the rate law, \(n = x + y\). Order can be zero, positive, negative, or fractional.

The general unit of the rate constant for an \(n\)-th order reaction is:

\[ \text{Units of } k = \left(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\right)^{1-n}\mathrm{s^{-1}} \]

Elementary vs. Complex Reactions and Molecularity

An elementary reaction occurs in a single concerted step. A complex reaction proceeds through a sequence of elementary steps (a reaction mechanism) involving transient intermediates. The overall rate of a complex reaction is controlled by its slowest step, called the rate-determining step (RDS).

Property Order of Reaction Molecularity of Reaction
Definition Sum of powers of concentration terms in the experimental rate law. Number of reacting species colliding simultaneously in an elementary step.
Nature Purely experimental quantity. Theoretical/mechanistic concept.
Permitted Values Can be \(0\), positive integer, negative, or fractional. Always a positive integer (\(1, 2,\) or \(3\)); never \(0\) or fractional.
Applicability Applies to both elementary and complex reactions. Meaningful only for elementary steps (not overall complex reactions).

4. Integrated Rate Equations & Half-Life

Integrating differential rate laws gives direct relationships between concentration and elapsed time, allowing easy determination of \(k\) and reaction order.

Zero-Order Reactions

In a zero-order reaction (\(\mathrm{R \to P}\)), the rate is independent of reactant concentration: \(\text{Rate} = -\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]/\mathrm{d}t = k[\mathrm{R}]^0 = k\). Examples include metal-surface catalyzed decompositions at high pressure (such as \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) on hot Pt or \(\mathrm{HI}\) on Au) and enzyme-saturated reactions.

\[ [\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_0 - kt \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0 - [\mathrm{R}]}{t} \qquad\text{and}\qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{2k} \]

Notice that for a zero-order reaction, half-life \(t_{1/2}\) is directly proportional to the initial concentration \([\mathrm{R}]_0\), and the reaction reaches \(100\%\) completion at \(t_{100\%} = [\mathrm{R}]_0 / k = 2 t_{1/2}\).

First-Order Reactions

In a first-order reaction, \(\text{Rate} = -\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]/\mathrm{d}t = k[\mathrm{R}]\). Examples include all natural and artificial radioactive decays, hydrogenation of ethene, and thermal decomposition of \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\).

\[ [\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_0 e^{-kt} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{[\mathrm{R}]} \qquad\text{and}\qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k} \]

Crucially, the half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of initial concentration \([\mathrm{R}]_0\). After \(m\) half-lives, the remaining concentration is \([\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_0 / 2^m\).

Tutorial Diagram 2: Linear graphical signatures used to identify zero-order and first-order kinetics.

Gas-Phase First-Order Kinetics & Pseudo First-Order Reactions

For a gas-phase reaction \(\mathrm{A(g) \to B(g) + C(g)}\) at constant volume and temperature, if \(p_i\) is the initial pressure of \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(p_t\) is the total pressure at time \(t\), then \(p_t = (p_i - x) + x + x = p_i + x\), so \(p_A = p_i - x = 2p_i - p_t\). Thus:

\[ k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{p_i}{2p_i - p_t} \]

A pseudo first-order reaction is an intrinsically higher-order reaction that obeys first-order kinetics because one reactant is present in large excess (its concentration remains virtually constant). Classic examples are acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of ethyl acetate and inversion of cane sugar in excess water.

5. Temperature Dependence, Arrhenius Equation & Catalysis

Reaction rates rise sharply with temperature; as an empirical rule of thumb, a \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (or \(10\,\mathrm{K}\)) temperature rise nearly doubles the rate constant. This occurs because raising the temperature shifts the Maxwell–Boltzmann kinetic energy distribution toward higher energies, dramatically increasing the fraction of molecules possessing energy \(\ge E_a\) (activation energy).

Tutorial Diagram 3: Maxwell–Boltzmann kinetic energy distribution showing why a small temperature rise sharply increases the fraction of molecules exceeding \(E_a\).

Arrhenius Equation

The quantitative dependence of the rate constant \(k\) on absolute temperature \(T\) (in Kelvin) is given by the Arrhenius equation:

\[ k = A e^{-E_a / RT} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) \]
  • \(A\) is the frequency factor (pre-exponential factor), with the same units as \(k\).
  • \(E_a\) is the activation energy (in \(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\)), the minimum extra energy reactants must acquire to form the unstable activated complex.
  • \(e^{-E_a/RT}\) represents the fraction of colliding molecules having kinetic energy \(\ge E_a\).
  • Plotting \(\ln k\) vs. \(1/T\) yields a straight line with \(\text{slope} = -E_a/R\) and \(y\text{-intercept} = \ln A\). (For \(\log_{10} k\) vs. \(1/T\), \(\text{slope} = -E_a / (2.303R)\).)

Comparing rate constants \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) at two temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) (assuming \(E_a\) and \(A\) are constant over the interval):

\[ \log\frac{k_2}{k_1} = \frac{E_a}{2.303\,R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right) = \frac{E_a}{2.303\,R}\left(\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right) \]

Effect of a Catalyst

A catalyst accelerates a reaction without being permanently consumed by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. (A substance that slows a reaction is an inhibitor.)

Critical Exam Facts on Catalysis
A catalyst does not alter \(\Delta H\), \(\Delta G\), or the equilibrium constant \(K_{\mathrm{eq}}\). It lowers both forward and backward activation energies equally, helping a spontaneous reaction reach equilibrium faster without catalyzing non-spontaneous processes.

6. Collision Theory of Chemical Reactions

Developed by Max Trautz and William Lewis, collision theory models reactant molecules as hard spheres. For a bimolecular elementary reaction \(\mathrm{A + B \to Products}\), molecules must collide to react. Let \(Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\) be the collision frequency (number of collisions per second per unit volume). Not every collision produces a reaction; only effective collisions succeed. An effective collision requires two simultaneous criteria:

  1. Energy Criterion: Colliding molecules must possess kinetic energy equal to or greater than the threshold energy (\(E_{\mathrm{th}} = E_a + \text{average energy of reactants}\)).
  2. Orientation Criterion: Molecules must collide with proper spatial orientation so that old bonds can break and new bonds can form. This is accounted for by the steric (probability) factor \(P\).
\[ \text{Rate} = P\,Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\,e^{-E_a / RT} \]

7. Worked Examples

The following worked examples use hypothetical experimental values to illustrate standard NEET UG problem-solving methods.

Worked Example 1 · Stoichiometric Reaction Rates

For the hypothetical gas-phase reaction \(2\,\mathrm{A(g) + B(g) \to 3\,C(g)}\), the concentration of \(\mathrm{A}\) decreases from \(0.80\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) to \(0.50\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) in \(150\,\mathrm{s}\) (assumed values). Calculate the average rate of the reaction and the rate of appearance of \(\mathrm{C}\) during this interval.

Solution:

First, compute the rate of disappearance of \(\mathrm{A}\):

\[ -\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{A}]}{\Delta t} = -\frac{(0.50 - 0.80)\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}}{150\,\mathrm{s}} = \frac{0.30}{150} = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}} \]

Using stoichiometric normalization:

\[ \text{Rate of reaction} = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{A}]}{\Delta t} = \frac{1}{2}(2.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}} \]

Since \(\text{Rate} = +\frac{1}{3}\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{C}]}{\Delta t}\), the rate of appearance of \(\mathrm{C}\) is:

\[ \frac{\Delta[\mathrm{C}]}{\Delta t} = 3 \times (1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 3.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}} \]
Worked Example 2 · Initial Rates Method

For a hypothetical reaction \(\mathrm{X + 2Y \to Z}\) at \(298\,\mathrm{K}\), the following assumed initial rate data were collected:

Run \([\mathrm{X}]_0\) (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) \([\mathrm{Y}]_0\) (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)) Initial Rate (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\))
1\(0.20\)\(0.10\)\(4.0 \times 10^{-4}\)
2\(0.40\)\(0.10\)\(1.6 \times 10^{-3}\)
3\(0.20\)\(0.30\)\(1.2 \times 10^{-3}\)

Determine the rate law, overall order, and rate constant \(k\).

Solution: Let \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{X}]^x[\mathrm{Y}]^y\).

  • Comparing Runs 1 and 2 (\([\mathrm{Y}]_0\) constant): doubling \([\mathrm{X}]_0\) increases the rate by \(1.6 \times 10^{-3} / 4.0 \times 10^{-4} = 4 = 2^2\). Hence \(x = 2\).
  • Comparing Runs 1 and 3 (\([\mathrm{X}]_0\) constant): tripling \([\mathrm{Y}]_0\) increases the rate by \(1.2 \times 10^{-3} / 4.0 \times 10^{-4} = 3 = 3^1\). Hence \(y = 1\).

Thus, the rate law is \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{X}]^2[\mathrm{Y}]\) and the overall order is \(2 + 1 = 3\). Substituting Run 1 values:

\[ k = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-4}}{(0.20)^2(0.10)} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-4}}{4.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 0.10\,\mathrm{L^2\,mol^{-2}\,s^{-1}} \]
Worked Example 3 · Gas-Phase First-Order Kinetics

A hypothetical gas-phase reaction \(\mathrm{X(g) \to Y(g) + Z(g)}\) follows first-order kinetics at \(400\,\mathrm{K}\) in a constant-volume vessel. Starting with pure \(\mathrm{X}\) at an assumed initial pressure of \(0.40\,\mathrm{atm}\), the total pressure rises to \(0.60\,\mathrm{atm}\) after \(200\,\mathrm{s}\). Find the rate constant \(k\).

Solution:

Here \(p_i = 0.40\,\mathrm{atm}\) and \(p_t = 0.60\,\mathrm{atm}\) at \(t = 200\,\mathrm{s}\).

The partial pressure of \(\mathrm{X}\) remaining is \(p_X = 2p_i - p_t = 2(0.40) - 0.60 = 0.20\,\mathrm{atm}\).

\[ k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{p_i}{p_X} = \frac{2.303}{200\,\mathrm{s}}\log\left(\frac{0.40}{0.20}\right) = \frac{2.303 \times 0.3010}{200\,\mathrm{s}} = 3.47 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \]
Worked Example 4 · Arrhenius Activation Energy

A hypothetical reaction has a rate constant \(k_1 = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(k_2 = 8.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) at \(320\,\mathrm{K}\) (assumed values). Calculate the activation energy \(E_a\). (Given: \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\), \(\log 4 = 0.6021\).)

Solution:

\[ \log\frac{k_2}{k_1} = \frac{E_a}{2.303\,R}\left(\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right) \] \[ \log\left(\frac{8.0 \times 10^{-3}}{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}\right) = \frac{E_a}{2.303 \times 8.314}\left(\frac{320 - 300}{300 \times 320}\right) \] \[ 0.6021 = \frac{E_a}{19.147} \times \frac{20}{96000} \implies E_a = \frac{0.6021 \times 19.147 \times 96000}{20} \approx 55,336\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx 55.3\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

Note: These are original practice questions designed in the NEET UG examination format for self-assessment.

Practice Question 1 · Single-Correct MCQ

For the reaction \(3\,\mathrm{A(g)} + 2\,\mathrm{B(g)} \longrightarrow 4\,\mathrm{C(g)}\), if the rate of disappearance of \(\mathrm{A}\) at a certain instant is \(6.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\), what is the rate of appearance of \(\mathrm{C}\) at the same instant?

  1. (A) \(4.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  2. (B) \(6.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  3. (C) \(8.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  4. (D) \(2.4 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: From stoichiometric rate equivalence, \(-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{4}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\). Therefore, \(\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{4}{3} \times (6.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 8.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 2 · Single-Correct MCQ

A chemical reaction involving a single reactant \(\mathrm{X}\) has a rate constant \(k = 4.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{L^2\,mol^{-2}\,s^{-1}}\). If the concentration of \(\mathrm{X}\) is doubled while keeping temperature constant, the reaction rate will increase by a factor of:

  1. (A) \(2\)
  2. (B) \(4\)
  3. (C) \(6\)
  4. (D) \(8\)
Correct Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: Comparing the unit \(\mathrm{L^2\,mol^{-2}\,s^{-1}}\) with \((\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})^{1-n}\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) gives \(1 - n = -2 \implies n = 3\). Since the reaction is third order, \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{X}]^3\). Doubling \([\mathrm{X}]\) multiplies the rate by \(2^3 = 8\).

Practice Question 3 · Statement-Based MCQ

Consider the following two statements:

Statement I: Molecularity of a reaction can be zero or fractional, whereas order of a reaction is always a positive integer.

Statement II: For a complex reaction, the overall order is governed by the slowest elementary step, known as the rate-determining step.

Choose the correct option:

  1. (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: Statement I reverses the two concepts: order is experimental and can be zero or fractional, whereas molecularity is a positive integer (\(1, 2,\) or \(3\)) for elementary steps. Statement II is correct.

Practice Question 4 · Zero-Order Numerical MCQ

For a zero-order reaction \(\mathrm{A \to Products}\), the initial concentration of \(\mathrm{A}\) is \(0.80\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) and the rate constant is \(0.020\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\). What is the concentration of \(\mathrm{A}\) remaining after \(25\,\mathrm{s}\)?

  1. (A) \(0.20\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)
  2. (B) \(0.30\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)
  3. (C) \(0.50\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)
  4. (D) \(0.60\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: Using the zero-order integrated rate law \([\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{A}]_0 - kt\), we get \([\mathrm{A}] = 0.80 - (0.020 \times 25) = 0.80 - 0.50 = 0.30\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 5 · First-Order Half-Life MCQ

A first-order reaction is \(75\%\) complete in \(60\,\mathrm{min}\). How much time will it take for \(87.5\%\) completion of the same reaction at the same temperature?

  1. (A) \(75\,\mathrm{min}\)
  2. (B) \(90\,\mathrm{min}\)
  3. (C) \(120\,\mathrm{min}\)
  4. (D) \(150\,\mathrm{min}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Worked Solution: \(75\%\) completion leaves \(25\% = 1/4 = (1/2)^2\) of the initial reactant, which corresponds to \(2\,t_{1/2} = 60\,\mathrm{min} \implies t_{1/2} = 30\,\mathrm{min}\). For \(87.5\%\) completion, \(12.5\% = 1/8 = (1/2)^3\) remains, requiring \(3\,t_{1/2} = 3 \times 30 = 90\,\mathrm{min}\).

Practice Question 6 · Pseudo First-Order Concept MCQ

In the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of \(0.05\,\mathrm{mol}\) of an ester in \(20\,\mathrm{mol}\) of water, the experimental rate law is \(\text{Rate} = k'[\text{Ester}]\), where \(k' = k[\mathrm{H_2O}]\). If the initial amount of water is doubled to \(40\,\mathrm{mol}\) (at constant temperature), what happens to the apparent rate constant \(k'\)?

  1. (A) \(k'\) remains unchanged because water is in excess.
  2. (B) \(k'\) is halved.
  3. (C) \(k'\) is doubled.
  4. (D) The reaction changes to second order with respect to the ester.
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: Although \([\mathrm{H_2O}]\) stays effectively constant during any single run, the pseudo first-order rate constant \(k' = k[\mathrm{H_2O}]\) is directly proportional to the bulk concentration of water. Doubling \([\mathrm{H_2O}]\) doubles \(k'\).

Practice Question 7 · Assertion–Reason MCQ

Assertion (A): A positive catalyst increases the rate of a reversible reaction without changing the equilibrium constant.

Reason (R): A catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward reaction more than that of the backward reaction.

  1. (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: A catalyst lowers the transition-state energy barrier, reducing the forward and backward activation energies by the exact same amount. Thus both forward and reverse rate constants increase by the same factor, leaving \(K_{\mathrm{eq}} = k_f / k_b\) unchanged. Hence (A) is true and (R) is false.

Practice Question 8 · Arrhenius Graph MCQ

In an Arrhenius plot of \(\ln k\) versus \(1/T\) for a chemical reaction, the straight line has a slope of \(-6000\,\mathrm{K}\). What is the activation energy \(E_a\) of the reaction? (Take \(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\).)

  1. (A) \(4.99\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  2. (B) \(24.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  3. (C) \(49.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
  4. (D) \(114.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Worked Solution: From \(\ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)\), the slope is \(-E_a / R = -6000\,\mathrm{K}\). Thus \(E_a = 6000 \times 8.314 = 49,884\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx 49.9\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\).

Practice Question 10 · Collision Theory MCQ

In collision theory, the rate of a bimolecular reaction is written as \(\text{Rate} = P\,Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\,e^{-E_a/RT}\). Which term specifically represents the fraction of colliding molecules possessing kinetic energy equal to or greater than the activation energy?

  1. (A) \(P\)
  2. (B) \(Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\)
  3. (C) \(P\,Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\)
  4. (D) \(e^{-E_a / RT}\)
Correct Answer: (D)

Worked Solution: According to Boltzmann statistics, \(e^{-E_a/RT}\) is the dimensionless fraction of molecules whose kinetic energy equals or exceeds \(E_a\). \(Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\) is the collision frequency and \(P\) is the steric factor.

9. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Frequent NEET UG Traps to Avoid
  • Forgetting stoichiometric divisors: Never equate \(-\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]/\mathrm{d}t\) directly to \(+\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{B}]/\mathrm{d}t\) unless their stoichiometric coefficients are equal.
  • Assuming order from stoichiometry: Exponents in the rate law come from experiment (or the rate-determining step), not the overall balanced equation.
  • Mixing \(\ln\) and \(\log_{10}\) slopes: \(\ln k\) vs. \(1/T\) has slope \(-E_a/R\), whereas \(\log_{10} k\) vs. \(1/T\) has slope \(-E_a/(2.303R)\).
  • Using \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in Arrhenius equations: Always convert temperature \(T\) to Kelvin (\(\mathrm{K} = ^\circ\mathrm{C} + 273.15\)).
Quick Pre-Exam Revision Checklist
  • Units of \(k\): \((\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})^{1-n}\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) — memorize \(n=0\) (\(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)), \(n=1\) (\(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)), \(n=2\) (\(\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)).
  • Zero-order \(t_{1/2} = [\mathrm{R}]_0/(2k)\); First-order \(t_{1/2} = 0.693/k\) (independent of \([\mathrm{R}]_0\)).
  • Useful first-order benchmark: \(t_{99.9\%} \approx 10\,t_{1/2}\) and \(t_{99\%} = 2\,t_{90\%}\).
  • Catalyst lowers \(E_a\) for both forward and reverse steps equally; \(\Delta H\), \(\Delta G\), and \(K_{\mathrm{eq}}\) remain unchanged.

10. Concise Final Recap

Chemical kinetics quantifies reaction speed via experimentally determined rate laws, distinguishing reaction order from elementary molecularity. Integrated rate equations connect concentration to time—showing linear concentration decay with concentration-dependent half-life for zero-order reactions, and exponential decay with constant half-life for first-order reactions. Temperature accelerates reactions exponentially by expanding the Boltzmann tail above \(E_a\), while catalysts speed equilibration by lowering \(E_a\) without shifting thermodynamic state functions. Finally, collision theory unites energetic (\(e^{-E_a/RT}\)), frequency (\(Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\)), and geometric (\(P\)) requirements for effective molecular transformations.
