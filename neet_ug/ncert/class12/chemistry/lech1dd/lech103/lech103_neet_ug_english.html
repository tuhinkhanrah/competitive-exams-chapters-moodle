Chemical Kinetics

A Comprehensive, Exam-Oriented Master Tutorial with NEET Practice Assessments & Complete Step-by-Step Solutions

1. Syllabus Overview & Learning Objectives

Chemical Kinetics is one of the highest-yielding topics in Physical Chemistry for NEET UG, contributing 2 to 3 questions each year. While chemical thermodynamics establishes whether a transformation is thermodynamically feasible (spontaneous when \(\Delta G < 0\)) and chemical equilibrium predicts the extent to which it proceeds, chemical kinetics governs the speed (reaction velocity) of the chemical change, the pathway (mechanism), and the molecular parameters controlling it.

NEET Learning Checklist
  • Differentiate between average and instantaneous rates of chemical reactions using stoichiometric expressions.
  • Formulate differential rate equations and deduce the overall order and individual reactant orders from experimental rate laws.
  • Distinguish sharply between order and molecularity of elementary and complex reactions.
  • Identify the slowest step as the rate determining step (RDS) in multi-step reaction mechanisms.
  • Derive and apply integrated rate equations and half-life formulas for zero-order and first-order processes.
  • Master gaseous first-order reactions involving total system pressure changes.
  • Understand pseudo-first-order reactions and why abundant solvents lead to apparent first-order kinetics.
  • Analyze the temperature dependence of rate constants using the Arrhenius equation, Maxwell-Boltzmann energy distribution curves, and activation energy (\(E_{\mathrm{a}}\)).
  • Understand how a catalyst creates an alternative lower activation energy pathway without altering \(\Delta G\) or equilibrium constant \(K_{\mathrm{eq}}\).
  • Explain the foundational concepts of Collision Theory: collision frequency (\(Z\)), threshold energy, steric/probability factor (\(P\)), and criteria for effective collisions.

2. Fundamental Concepts & Theoretical Framework

2.1 Rate of a Chemical Reaction

Reactions vary drastically in speed: from instantaneous ionic reactions (such as precipitation of \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) upon mixing aqueous \(\mathrm{AgNO}_3\) and \(\mathrm{NaCl}\)), to extremely slow transformations (such as diamond converting to graphite, or rusting of iron in air and moisture), to moderate reactions (such as the inversion of cane sugar or hydrolysis of ethyl acetate).

The rate of a reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time at constant volume. For a simple general reaction \(\mathrm{R} \rightarrow \mathrm{P}\):

\[\text{Rate of disappearance of R} = -\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{R}]}{\Delta t}\] \[\text{Rate of appearance of P} = +\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{P}]}{\Delta t}\]

Because the concentration of reactant \([\mathrm{R}]\) decreases with time, \(\Delta[\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_2 - [\mathrm{R}]_1 < 0\). The negative sign is explicitly prefixed so that the reaction rate remains a strictly positive quantity.

Units of Reaction Rate

Rate has dimensions of \(\text{concentration} \times \text{time}^{-1}\). In aqueous or solution systems, units are typically \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) (or \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\)). For gaseous reactions where concentration is tracked via partial pressures, units are \(\mathrm{atm\,s^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{bar\,s^{-1}}\).

2.2 Average Rate vs. Instantaneous Rate

  • Average Rate (\(r_{\mathrm{av}}\)): Measured over a finite macroscopic time interval \(\Delta t\): \[r_{\mathrm{av}} = -\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{R}]}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta[\mathrm{P}]}{\Delta t}\] Graphically, \(r_{\mathrm{av}}\) equals the negative slope of the secant line connecting two distinct time points on a \([\mathrm{R}]\) vs. \(t\) curve.
  • Instantaneous Rate (\(r_{\mathrm{inst}}\)): The rate at a specific moment in time, obtained in the limit as \(\Delta t \rightarrow 0\): \[r_{\mathrm{inst}} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \left(-\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{R}]}{\Delta t}\right) = -\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{P}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\] Graphically, \(r_{\mathrm{inst}}\) is equal to the negative slope of the tangent drawn to the concentration-time curve at that specific instant \(t\).
Figure 1: Concentration vs. time curves for reactant disappearance and product appearance, illustrating average rate (secant slope) and instantaneous rate (tangent slope).

2.3 Stoichiometric Expressions of Reaction Rate

When the stoichiometric coefficients of reactants and products are not all unity, the rate of disappearance of each reactant and the rate of appearance of each product must be divided by its respective stoichiometric coefficient so that the overall reaction rate is uniquely defined.

For a general chemical reaction:

\[a\,\mathrm{A} + b\,\mathrm{B} \longrightarrow c\,\mathrm{C} + d\,\mathrm{D}\] \[\text{Rate of Reaction} = -\frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{b}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{B}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{c}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{d}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{D}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\]
NEET Pitfall Alert: Rate of Reaction vs. Rate of Disappearance/Appearance

Always distinguish between:

  • Rate of disappearance of A: \(-\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\) (does NOT include the stoichiometric coefficient \(1/a\)).
  • Rate of appearance of C: \(+\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\) (does NOT include \(1/c\)).
  • Rate of the overall reaction: \(-\frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{c}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\).

2.4 Rate Law, Order of Reaction, and Rate Constant

The rate law is an experimentally determined mathematical expression relating the rate of reaction to the molar concentrations of the reactants. For the reaction \(a\,\mathrm{A} + b\,\mathrm{B} \rightarrow \text{Products}\):

\[\text{Rate} = k\,[\mathrm{A}]^x\,[\mathrm{B}]^y\]
  • \(k\) is the rate constant (or specific reaction rate). When \([\mathrm{A}] = [\mathrm{B}] = 1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\), \(\text{Rate} = k\). Thus, \(k\) represents the reaction velocity when all reactants are at unit molar concentration.
  • \(x\) is the order with respect to reactant \(\mathrm{A}\), and \(y\) is the order with respect to \(\mathrm{B}\).
  • The overall order of reaction is \(n = x + y\).
  • Crucial Rule: The powers \(x\) and \(y\) are determined solely by experiment; they may or may not equal the stoichiometric coefficients \(a\) and \(b\). A balanced stoichiometric equation cannot be used to deduce the rate law theoretically!

Units of Rate Constant for an \(n\)-th Order Reaction

From the rate law:

\[k = \frac{\text{Rate}}{[\text{Concentration}]^n} = \frac{\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}}{(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})^n} = \left(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\right)^{1-n}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = \mathrm{mol}^{1-n}\,\mathrm{L}^{n-1}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\]
Order (\(n\)) Rate Law Format SI / Standard Units of Rate Constant (\(k\)) Gaseous Pressure Units
0 (Zero Order) \(\text{Rate} = k\) \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{atm\,s^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{bar\,s^{-1}}\)
1 (First Order) \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]\) \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) (or \(\mathrm{min^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm{hr^{-1}}\)) \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
2 (Second Order) \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^2\) \(\mathrm{mol^{-1}\,L\,s^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{atm^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
\(3/2\) (Fractional) \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^{3/2}\) \(\mathrm{mol^{-1/2}\,L^{1/2}\,s^{-1}}\) \(\mathrm{atm^{-1/2}\,s^{-1}}\)
\(n\) (General Order) \(\text{Rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^n\) \(\mathrm{mol}^{1-n}\,\mathrm{L}^{n-1}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) \(\mathrm{atm}^{1-n}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\)

2.5 Molecularity vs. Order of Reaction

Chemical reactions can be classified into two fundamental categories:

  • Elementary Reactions: Chemical reactions that occur in a single collision step without intermediates.
  • Complex Reactions: Reactions that proceed through a sequence of two or more elementary steps (the reaction mechanism).

The molecularity of an elementary reaction is defined as the total number of reacting species (atoms, ions, or molecules) that must collide simultaneously in order to bring about the chemical transformation. Simultaneous collisions of more than three molecules are statistically extremely improbable; hence, elementary reactions with molecularity greater than 3 are practically non-existent.

Feature Order of Reaction Molecularity of Reaction
Definition Sum of the powers of concentration terms in the experimental rate law. Number of reacting species that collide simultaneously in an elementary step.
Nature Purely an experimental quantity. A theoretical property derived from the proposed elementary mechanism.
Permissible Values Can be zero, integer (1, 2, 3), negative, or fractional. Can only be positive integers: 1 (unimolecular), 2 (bimolecular), 3 (termolecular). Never zero or fractional.
Applicability Applies to both elementary and complex multi-step reactions. Has meaning only for elementary steps. For complex reactions, molecularity of the overall reaction is meaningless.
Slowest Step Relation For a complex reaction, overall order is given by the stoichiometry of the slowest step (Rate Determining Step). Molecularity of the slowest elementary step is identical to the overall order of a simple multi-step sequence without prior equilibria.

Rate Determining Step (RDS) & Reactive Intermediates

In a multi-step complex reaction, the slowest elementary step acts as the Rate Determining Step (RDS), analogous to the slowest runner in a relay race determining the team's total time.

Consider the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide catalyzed by iodide ion in alkaline medium:

\[2\,\mathrm{H_2O_2} \xrightarrow[\text{alkaline medium}]{\mathrm{I}^-} 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{O_2}\]

The experimentally observed rate law is:

\[\text{Rate} = k\,[\mathrm{H_2O_2}][\mathrm{I}^-]\]

The accepted two-step mechanism is:

  1. \(\mathrm{H_2O_2} + \mathrm{I}^- \longrightarrow \mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{IO}^-\)    (Slow, bimolecular, RDS)
  2. \(\mathrm{H_2O_2} + \mathrm{IO}^- \longrightarrow \mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{I}^- + \mathrm{O_2}\)    (Fast, bimolecular)

Here, the hypoiodite ion \(\mathrm{IO}^-\) is an intermediate: it is produced in step 1 and consumed in step 2, so it does not appear in the overall balanced equation. The catalyst \(\mathrm{I}^-\) participates in step 1 and is regenerated in step 2.

3. Integrated Rate Laws: Derivations, Graphs, and Half-Lives

While differential rate equations express reaction rate as a function of instantaneous concentration, integrating these equations yields direct functional relationships between measurable concentration \([\mathrm{R}]\), elapsed time \(t\), and rate constant \(k\).

3.1 Zero-Order Reactions

A reaction is of zero order when the rate of reaction is completely independent of the reactant concentration:

\[\text{Rate} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = k\,[\mathrm{R}]^0 = k\] \[\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}] = -k\,\mathrm{d}t\]

Integrating both sides between limits \(t = 0\) (where \([\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_0\)) and time \(t\) (where \([\mathrm{R}] = [\mathrm{R}]_t\)):

\[\int_{[\mathrm{R}]_0}^{[\mathrm{R}]_t} \mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}] = -k\int_0^t \mathrm{d}t \implies [\mathrm{R}]_t - [\mathrm{R}]_0 = -kt\] \[[\mathrm{R}]_t = [\mathrm{R}]_0 - kt \quad\iff\quad k = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0 - [\mathrm{R}]_t}{t}\]

Zero-Order Half-Life (\(t_{1/2}\))

The half-life \(t_{1/2}\) is the time required for \([\mathrm{R}]_t\) to drop to \(\frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{R}]_0\):

\[\frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{R}]_0 = [\mathrm{R}]_0 - k\,t_{1/2} \implies t_{1/2} = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{2k}\]

Crucial NEET Takeaway: For a zero-order reaction, \(t_{1/2}\) is directly proportional to initial concentration: \(t_{1/2} \propto [\mathrm{R}]_0\). If initial concentration is doubled, the half-life doubles!

Classic Physical Examples of Zero-Order Reactions

  • Decomposition of gaseous ammonia on a hot platinum surface at high pressure: \[2\,\mathrm{NH_3}(\mathrm{g}) \xrightarrow[1130\,\mathrm{K}]{\mathrm{Pt}} \mathrm{N_2}(\mathrm{g}) + 3\,\mathrm{H_2}(\mathrm{g}) \quad\quad \text{Rate} = k\,[\mathrm{NH_3}]^0 = k\] Why is it zero-order? At high pressure, all catalytic adsorption sites on the platinum surface become fully saturated. Further increases in \(\mathrm{NH}_3\) gas pressure cannot increase the surface concentration of reacting molecules.
  • Thermal decomposition of gaseous \(\mathrm{HI}\) on a gold (\(\mathrm{Au}\)) surface.
  • Enzyme-catalyzed biochemical reactions at substrate saturation.

3.2 First-Order Reactions

In a first-order reaction, the rate is proportional to the first power of reactant concentration:

\[\text{Rate} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = k\,[\mathrm{R}]\] \[\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{[\mathrm{R}]} = -k\,\mathrm{d}t\]

Integrating from \(t = 0\) to \(t\):

\[\int_{[\mathrm{R}]_0}^{[\mathrm{R}]_t} \frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{[\mathrm{R}]} = -k\int_0^t \mathrm{d}t \implies \ln[\mathrm{R}]_t - \ln[\mathrm{R}]_0 = -kt\] \[\ln\frac{[\mathrm{R}]_t}{[\mathrm{R}]_0} = -kt \implies [\mathrm{R}]_t = [\mathrm{R}]_0\,e^{-kt}\]

Converting natural log (\(\ln\)) to common base-10 log (\(\log_{10}\)) using \(\ln x = 2.303\,\log_{10} x\):

\[k = \frac{2.303}{t}\,\log_{10}\left(\frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{[\mathrm{R}]_t}\right) = \frac{2.303}{t}\,\log_{10}\left(\frac{a}{a-x}\right)\]

where \(a = [\mathrm{R}]_0\) is the initial concentration and \(x\) is the concentration of reactant consumed after time \(t\).

First-Order Half-Life (\(t_{1/2}\))

At \(t = t_{1/2}\), \([\mathrm{R}]_t = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{2}\):

\[k = \frac{2.303}{t_{1/2}}\,\log_{10}(2) = \frac{2.303 \times 0.3010}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{t_{1/2}}\] \[t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}\]

Crucial NEET Takeaway: For a first-order reaction, the half-life is strictly independent of the initial concentration \([\mathrm{R}]_0\). Whether you start with \(10\,\mathrm{M}\) or \(0.01\,\mathrm{M}\), it takes the exact same duration for half of the reactant to decompose!

Figure 2: Characteristic linear plots: (A) Zero-order \([\mathrm{R}]\) vs. \(t\); (B) First-order \(\ln[\mathrm{R}]\) vs. \(t\); (C) First-order \(\log([\mathrm{R}]_0/[\mathrm{R}])\) vs. \(t\).

Key Time Proportions for First-Order Reactions (NEET High-Yield)

  • Time for \(75\%\) completion (\(t_{75\%}\)): \[[\mathrm{R}] = 0.25\,[\mathrm{R}]_0 \implies t_{75\%} = \frac{2.303}{k}\log(4) = \frac{2 \times 2.303 \times 0.3010}{k} = 2 \times t_{1/2}\]
  • Time for \(87.5\%\) completion (\(t_{87.5\%}\)): \(t_{87.5\%} = 3 \times t_{1/2}\).
  • Time for \(99.9\%\) completion (\(t_{99.9\%}\)): \[[\mathrm{R}] = 0.001\,[\mathrm{R}]_0 = 10^{-3}\,[\mathrm{R}]_0 \implies t_{99.9\%} = \frac{2.303}{k}\log(10^3) = \frac{3 \times 2.303}{k} = \frac{6.909}{k} \approx 10 \times t_{1/2}\]
  • Time for \(99\%\) vs. \(90\%\) completion: \[t_{99\%} = \frac{2.303}{k}\log(100) = \frac{2 \times 2.303}{k}\] \[t_{90\%} = \frac{2.303}{k}\log(10) = \frac{2.303}{k} \implies t_{99\%} = 2 \times t_{90\%}\]

3.3 First-Order Gas Phase Reactions

Consider a homogeneous gas-phase reaction where reactant and products are all gases:

\[\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{g}) \longrightarrow \mathrm{B}(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{C}(\mathrm{g})\]

Let the initial pressure of pure \(\mathrm{A}\) at \(t = 0\) be \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\). At time \(t\), let \(x\) be the decrease in partial pressure of \(\mathrm{A}\). According to reaction stoichiometry:

Timeline \(p_{\mathrm{A}}\) \(p_{\mathrm{B}}\) \(p_{\mathrm{C}}\) Total Pressure (\(p_t\))
At \(t = 0\) \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\) 0 0 \(p_{\mathrm{i}}\)
At time \(t\) \(p_{\mathrm{i}} - x\) \(x\) \(x\) \((p_{\mathrm{i}} - x) + x + x = p_{\mathrm{i}} + x\)

From total pressure: \(p_t = p_{\mathrm{i}} + x \implies x = p_t - p_{\mathrm{i}}\).

Substituting \(x\) back into the partial pressure of \(\mathrm{A}\):

\[p_{\mathrm{A}} = p_{\mathrm{i}} - (p_t - p_{\mathrm{i}}) = 2p_{\mathrm{i}} - p_t\] \[k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\left(\frac{p_{\mathrm{i}}}{p_{\mathrm{A}}}\right) = \frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\left(\frac{p_{\mathrm{i}}}{2p_{\mathrm{i}} - p_t}\right)\]

3.4 Pseudo-First-Order Reactions

A reaction is termed pseudo-first-order when its true molecularity or intrinsic order is higher (e.g., bimolecular/second order), but under actual experimental conditions, it obeys first-order kinetics because one of the reactants is present in large stoichiometric excess.

1. Acid Hydrolysis of Ethyl Acetate

\[\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H}^+} \mathrm{CH_3COOH} + \mathrm{C_2H_5OH}\]

The true rate equation is \(\text{Rate} = k'\,[\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5}][\mathrm{H_2O}]\). When \(0.01\,\mathrm{mol}\) of ethyl acetate is reacted in \(10\,\mathrm{mol}\) of water, the water concentration decreases only from \(10\,\mathrm{mol}\) to \(9.99\,\mathrm{mol}\)—an insignificant change. Hence, \([\mathrm{H_2O}]\) remains effectively constant:

\[\text{Rate} = k'\,[\mathrm{H_2O}]\,[\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5}] = k\,[\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5}] \quad\quad (\text{where } k = k'\,[\mathrm{H_2O}])\]

2. Inversion of Cane Sugar (Sucrose)

\[\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H}^+} \underset{\text{D-glucose}}{\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}} + \underset{\text{D-fructose}}{\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6}}\] \[\text{Rate} = k\,[\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}}]\]

4. Temperature Dependence, Arrhenius Equation, and Catalysis

4.1 Temperature Coefficient (\(Q_{10}\))

For most chemical reactions, the rate of reaction increases exponentially with temperature. As a general empirical rule, a rise in temperature of \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) (or \(10\,\mathrm{K}\)) nearly doubles or triples the rate constant:

\[\text{Temperature Coefficient } (\mu \text{ or } Q_{10}) = \frac{k_{T+10}}{k_T} \approx 2 \text{ to } 3\]

4.2 The Arrhenius Equation

Svante Arrhenius provided the physical interpretation of temperature dependence based on the formation of an energized intermediate known as the activated complex:

\[k = A\,e^{-E_{\mathrm{a}} / RT}\]
  • \(A\): Arrhenius factor, frequency factor, or pre-exponential factor (has identical units to \(k\)).
  • \(E_{\mathrm{a}}\): Activation energy in \(\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). It represents the minimum extra energy that reactant molecules must absorb to reach the transition state.
  • \(R\): Universal gas constant (\(8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)).
  • \(T\): Absolute temperature in Kelvin (\(\mathrm{K}\)).
  • The Boltzmann factor \(e^{-E_{\mathrm{a}} / RT}\) represents the fraction of molecules having kinetic energy equal to or exceeding the activation energy \(E_{\mathrm{a}}\).

Logarithmic and Two-Temperature Forms

Taking natural logarithm:

\[\ln k = -\frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + \ln A\]

Comparing with the equation of a straight line (\(y = mx + c\)), a plot of \(\ln k\) against \(1/T\) yields a straight line with:

  • \(\text{Slope} = -\frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{R}\)
  • \(\text{Intercept} = \ln A\)

In base-10 logarithms between two temperatures \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) (with rate constants \(k_1\) and \(k_2\)):

\[\log_{10}\left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{2.303\,R}\left[\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right] = \frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{2.303\,R}\left[\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right]\]
Figure 3: (Left) Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curves showing why a \(10\,\mathrm{K}\) temperature rise roughly doubles the fraction of molecules possessing energy \(\ge E_{\mathrm{a}}\); (Right) Arrhenius plot of \(\ln k\) against \(1/T\).

4.3 Effect of Catalyst: Intermediate Complex Theory

A catalyst is a substance that accelerates the rate of a chemical reaction without itself undergoing any permanent chemical change. According to the intermediate complex theory, the catalyst combines temporarily with reactant molecules to form an unstable intermediate complex, which then decomposes into products and regenerates the catalyst intact.

Figure 4: Potential energy profile of an exothermic reaction showing the uncatalyzed pathway versus the lower activation energy pathway provided by a catalyst. Note that reactant energy, product energy, and \(\Delta H\) remain identical.
NEET Master Rules: What a Catalyst DOES and DOES NOT Do
  • Does: Provides an alternative mechanism with a lower potential energy barrier (lowers \(E_{\mathrm{a}}\) for both forward and reverse reactions by the exact same amount).
  • Does: Increases both forward rate constant (\(k_{\mathrm{f}}\)) and backward rate constant (\(k_{\mathrm{b}}\)) to the exact same extent, thereby reducing the time taken to attain equilibrium.
  • Does NOT: Alter the equilibrium constant (\(K_{\mathrm{eq}} = k_{\mathrm{f}} / k_{\mathrm{b}}\) remains unchanged).
  • Does NOT: Alter standard Gibbs free energy (\(\Delta G^\circ\)) or enthalpy (\(\Delta H\)) of the reaction.
  • Does NOT: Make a non-spontaneous reaction (\(\Delta G > 0\)) spontaneous; it only accelerates thermodynamically feasible processes.

4.4 Collision Theory of Chemical Reactions

Developed by Max Trautz and William Lewis (1916–18) based on the kinetic theory of gases, Collision Theory treats molecules as hard rigid spheres. A chemical reaction occurs only when reactant molecules physically collide. However, only a tiny fraction of total collisions lead to chemical reaction.

The Two Necessary Barriers for an Effective Collision

  1. Energy Barrier (Threshold Energy): Colliding molecules must possess a minimum amount of energy called Threshold Energy. \[\text{Threshold Energy} = E_{\mathrm{a}} + \text{Initial kinetic energy possessed by reacting molecules}\] Collisions involving kinetic energy below \(E_{\mathrm{a}}\) are elastic: the molecules simply rebound without reacting.
  2. Orientation Barrier (Steric Factor \(P\)): Even if molecules possess sufficient kinetic energy, bond rearrangement cannot occur unless reacting atoms collide in the correct spatial orientation.
Figure 5: Collision theory model of molecular orientation illustrated for the bimolecular nucleophilic substitution of bromomethane with hydroxide ion.

To incorporate both requirements, the modified collision rate equation is formulated as:

\[\text{Rate} = P\,Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\,e^{-E_{\mathrm{a}} / RT}\]
  • \(Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\): Collision frequency, defined as the number of collisions per second per unit volume of the reaction mixture between species \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\).
  • \(P\): Steric factor or probability factor (\(P \le 1\)), which accounts for the proper orientation of reacting molecules.
  • Arrhenius pre-exponential factor is related as: \(A = P\,Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\).
  • Drawback of Collision Theory: Treats atoms/molecules as hard structureless spheres and completely ignores their internal degrees of freedom, vibrations, and rotational electron cloud distortions.

6. Representative Worked Examples (Step-by-Step)

Worked Example 1: Stoichiometric Rates of Reaction and Appearance

Problem: In the high-temperature decomposition of dinitrogen pentoxide in carbon tetrachloride:

\[2\,\mathrm{N_2O_5}(\mathrm{g}) \longrightarrow 4\,\mathrm{NO_2}(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{O_2}(\mathrm{g})\]

The concentration of \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\) drops from \(2.33\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) to \(2.08\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) in \(184\,\text{minutes}\). Calculate:

  1. The average rate of the reaction in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\) and \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\).
  2. The rate of production of \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) during this period.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Express the overall average reaction rate from stoichiometry:

\[\text{Rate} = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{N_2O_5}]}{\Delta t} = \frac{1}{4}\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{NO_2}]}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta[\mathrm{O_2}]}{\Delta t}\]

Step 2: Substitute concentration changes for \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\):

\[\Delta[\mathrm{N_2O_5}] = 2.08 - 2.33 = -0.25\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\] \[\Delta t = 184\,\mathrm{min}\] \[\text{Rate} = -\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-0.25\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}}{184\,\mathrm{min}}\right) = \frac{0.25}{368} = 6.79 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\]

In seconds:

\[\text{Rate} = \frac{6.79 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}}{60\,\mathrm{s\,min^{-1}}} = 1.13 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\]

Step 3: Calculate rate of formation of \(\mathrm{NO_2}\):

\[\frac{\Delta[\mathrm{NO_2}]}{\Delta t} = 4 \times \text{Rate} = 4 \times (6.79 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}) = 2.72 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 2: Deducing Rate Law from Initial Rate Data

Problem: For the reaction \(2\,\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{B} \rightarrow \mathrm{C} + \mathrm{D}\), the following initial rates were obtained at \(300\,\mathrm{K}\):

Run \([\mathrm{A}]_0 / \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) \([\mathrm{B}]_0 / \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\) Initial Rate of Formation of \(\mathrm{D} / \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\)
I 0.10 0.10 \(6.0 \times 10^{-3}\)
II 0.30 0.20 \(7.2 \times 10^{-2}\)
III 0.30 0.40 \(2.88 \times 10^{-1}\)
IV 0.40 0.10 \(2.40 \times 10^{-2}\)

Determine the order with respect to \(\mathrm{A}\) and \(\mathrm{B}\), write the rate law, and calculate the rate constant \(k\).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Let the rate law be: \(\text{Rate} = k\,[\mathrm{A}]^x\,[\mathrm{B}]^y\).

Step 1: Determine order with respect to \(\mathrm{A}\) (holding \([\mathrm{B}]\) constant):

Compare Run IV and Run I (where \([\mathrm{B}] = 0.10\,\mathrm{M}\)):

\[\frac{\text{Rate IV}}{\text{Rate I}} = \frac{2.40 \times 10^{-2}}{6.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 4.0\] \[\frac{[\mathrm{A}]_{\text{IV}}^x}{[\mathrm{A}]_{\text{I}}^x} = \left(\frac{0.40}{0.10}\right)^x = (4)^x \implies 4^x = 4 \implies x = 1\]

The reaction is first order with respect to A.

Step 2: Determine order with respect to \(\mathrm{B}\) (holding \([\mathrm{A}]\) constant):

Compare Run III and Run II (where \([\mathrm{A}] = 0.30\,\mathrm{M}\)):

\[\frac{\text{Rate III}}{\text{Rate II}} = \frac{2.88 \times 10^{-1}}{7.2 \times 10^{-2}} = \frac{0.288}{0.072} = 4.0\] \[\frac{[\mathrm{B}]_{\text{III}}^y}{[\mathrm{B}]_{\text{II}}^y} = \left(\frac{0.40}{0.20}\right)^y = (2)^y \implies 2^y = 4 \implies y = 2\]

The reaction is second order with respect to B.

Step 3: Write overall rate law and calculate \(k\):

\[\text{Rate} = k\,[\mathrm{A}]\,[\mathrm{B}]^2 \quad\quad (\text{Overall order } = 1 + 2 = 3)\]

Using data from Run I:

\[6.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}} = k\,(0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})\,(0.10\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})^2 = k \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol^3\,L^{-3}}\] \[k = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{-3}}{1.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 6.0\,\mathrm{mol^{-2}\,L^2\,min^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 3: Gas-Phase First-Order Decomposition with Pressure Monitoring

Problem: Thermal decomposition of gaseous \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\) was monitored at constant volume at \(318\,\mathrm{K}\):

\[2\,\mathrm{N_2O_5}(\mathrm{g}) \longrightarrow 2\,\mathrm{N_2O_4}(\mathrm{g}) + \mathrm{O_2}(\mathrm{g})\]

The initial total pressure at \(t = 0\) was \(0.500\,\mathrm{atm}\). After \(100\,\mathrm{s}\), the total pressure rose to \(0.512\,\mathrm{atm}\). Calculate the first-order rate constant \(k\).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Let the initial pressure of \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\) be \(p_0 = 0.500\,\mathrm{atm}\). Let \(2x\) be the pressure of \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\) that decomposes in \(100\,\mathrm{s}\):

\[p_{\mathrm{N_2O_5}} = 0.500 - 2x\] \[p_{\mathrm{N_2O_4}} = 2x, \quad\quad p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = x\] \[p_t = p_{\mathrm{N_2O_5}} + p_{\mathrm{N_2O_4}} + p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = (0.500 - 2x) + 2x + x = 0.500 + x\]

Given \(p_t = 0.512\,\mathrm{atm}\):

\[x = 0.512 - 0.500 = 0.012\,\mathrm{atm}\]

The remaining partial pressure of \(\mathrm{N_2O_5}\) is:

\[p_{\mathrm{N_2O_5}} = 0.500 - 2(0.012) = 0.500 - 0.024 = 0.476\,\mathrm{atm}\]

For a first-order reaction:

\[k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\left(\frac{p_0}{p_{\mathrm{N_2O_5}}}\right) = \frac{2.303}{100}\log_{10}\left(\frac{0.500}{0.476}\right) = \frac{2.303}{100}\log_{10}(1.0504)\] \[\log_{10}(1.0504) \approx 0.02136\] \[k = \frac{2.303}{100} \times 0.02136 = 4.92 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 4: Arrhenius Activation Energy and Two-Temperature Calculation

Problem: The rate constant for the decomposition of ethyl iodide (\(\mathrm{C_2H_5I} \rightarrow \mathrm{C_2H_4} + \mathrm{HI}\)) at \(600\,\mathrm{K}\) is \(1.60 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Its activation energy is \(209\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). Calculate the rate constant at \(700\,\mathrm{K}\) (\(R = 8.314\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Using the Arrhenius two-temperature relation:

\[\log_{10}\left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{2.303\,R}\left[\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right]\]

Substitute known values: \(E_{\mathrm{a}} = 209000\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}\), \(T_1 = 600\,\mathrm{K}\), \(T_2 = 700\,\mathrm{K}\):

\[\frac{E_{\mathrm{a}}}{2.303\,R} = \frac{209000}{2.303 \times 8.314} = \frac{209000}{19.147} \approx 10915.5\,\mathrm{K}\] \[\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2} = \frac{700 - 600}{600 \times 700} = \frac{100}{420000} = \frac{1}{4200}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}\] \[\log_{10}\left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{10915.5}{4200} = 2.599\]

Taking antilog:

\[\frac{k_2}{k_1} = 10^{2.599} = 397.2\] \[k_2 = 397.2 \times k_1 = 397.2 \times (1.60 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}) = 6.36 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 1 Single Correct MCQ

For the gaseous reaction \(2\,\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{B} \longrightarrow 3\,\mathrm{C}\), the rate of formation of \(\mathrm{C}\) is \(0.060\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\). The rate of disappearance of \(\mathrm{A}\) and the overall rate of reaction are, respectively:

  • (A) \(0.040\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) and \(0.020\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(0.090\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) and \(0.060\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(0.040\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) and \(0.060\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(0.020\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\) and \(0.040\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: According to reaction stoichiometry:

\[\text{Overall Rate} = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{B}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{3}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\]

Given \(\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{C}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0.060\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\):

\[\text{Overall Rate} = \frac{1}{3}(0.060) = 0.020\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\]

The rate of disappearance of \(\mathrm{A}\) is \(-\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\):

\[-\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2 \times \text{Overall Rate} = 2 \times 0.020 = 0.040\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\]
Practice Question 2 Single Correct MCQ

The rate constant of a certain reaction is given as \(k = 4.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1/2}\,L^{1/2}\,s^{-1}}\). The overall order of this reaction is:

  • (A) \(1/2\)
  • (B) \(3/2\)
  • (C) \(2\)
  • (D) \(-1/2\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: The general dimensional formula for rate constant is:

\[\text{Units of } k = \mathrm{mol}^{1-n}\,\mathrm{L}^{n-1}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\]

Equating exponents of \(\mathrm{L}\):

\[n - 1 = \frac{1}{2} \implies n = 1 + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{2}\]

Verifying with exponent of \(\mathrm{mol}\): \(1 - n = 1 - 3/2 = -1/2\). Hence, order is \(3/2\).

Practice Question 3 Single Correct MCQ

A first-order reaction is \(20\%\) complete in \(10\,\mathrm{minutes}\). How much time will it take to reach \(80\%\) completion? [Given: \(\log_{10}(2) = 0.3010\)]

  • (A) \(40.0\,\mathrm{min}\)
  • (B) \(72.1\,\mathrm{min}\)
  • (C) \(62.2\,\mathrm{min}\)
  • (D) \(80.0\,\mathrm{min}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: For a first order reaction, \(k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\left(\frac{a}{a-x}\right)\).

At \(20\%\) completion: \(a - x = 0.80\,a\), \(t_1 = 10\,\mathrm{min}\):

\[k = \frac{2.303}{10}\log\left(\frac{1}{0.80}\right) = \frac{2.303}{10}\log(1.25)\] \[\log(1.25) = \log(10/8) = 1 - 3\log 2 = 1 - 0.9030 = 0.0970\]

At \(80\%\) completion: \(a - x = 0.20\,a\):

\[k = \frac{2.303}{t_2}\log\left(\frac{1}{0.20}\right) = \frac{2.303}{t_2}\log(5)\] \[\log(5) = 1 - \log 2 = 1 - 0.3010 = 0.6990\]

Dividing the two equations:

\[\frac{t_2}{10} = \frac{\log 5}{\log 1.25} = \frac{0.6990}{0.0970} \approx 7.206 \implies t_2 = 72.06 \approx 72.1\,\mathrm{min}\]

  • (B) \(4\)

  • (D) \(2\)

8. Common Pitfalls & NEET High-Yield Revision Checklist

Frequent Student Mistakes to Avoid
  • Confusing Rate of Disappearance with Overall Rate: Remember that in \(2\mathrm{A} \rightarrow \mathrm{Products}\), \(-\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{A}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2 \times \text{Rate of Reaction}\).
  • Predicting Rate Law from Stoichiometry: Stoichiometric coefficients tell you nothing about the rate law unless you are explicitly told the reaction is elementary.
  • Unit Confusion in Rate Constants: Always use \(\mathrm{mol}^{1-n}\,\mathrm{L}^{n-1}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) to deduce reaction order from rate constant units.
  • Believing Catalysts Change Equilibrium: A catalyst speeds up equilibrium attainment; it NEVER shifts equilibrium position or alters \(K_{\mathrm{eq}}\).
  • Forgetting Absolute Temperature in Kelvin: In Arrhenius equations, temperatures must always be converted to Kelvin (\(\mathrm{K} = \,^\circ\mathrm{C} + 273.15\)).
Rapid Revision Memory Hooks
  • Zero Order: \(t_{1/2} = \frac{[\mathrm{R}]_0}{2k}\)  →  Linear graph: \([\mathrm{R}]\) vs \(t\) (Slope \(= -k\)).
  • First Order: \(t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}\)  →  Linear graph: \(\ln[\mathrm{R}]\) vs \(t\) (Slope \(= -k\)).
  • First Order Benchmarks: \(t_{75\%} = 2 \times t_{50\%}\), \(t_{87.5\%} = 3 \times t_{50\%}\), \(t_{99.9\%} \approx 10 \times t_{50\%}\).
  • Arrhenius Plot: \(\ln k\) vs \(1/T\) gives a straight line with \(\text{Slope} = -E_{\mathrm{a}} / R\).
  • Effective Collision: Requires BOTH (1) Energy \(\ge E_{\mathrm{a}}\) AND (2) Proper spatial orientation.

9. Concise Final Recap

Chemical Kinetics provides the indispensable quantitative bridge between molecular dynamics and macroscopic chemical change. In summary:

  • The rate of reaction is defined symmetrically via stoichiometry: \(\text{Rate} = -\frac{1}{\nu_{\mathrm{R}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{R}]}{\mathrm{d}t} = +\frac{1}{\nu_{\mathrm{P}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}[\mathrm{P}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\).
  • The order of reaction is an empirical quantity (zero, integer, or fractional) representing concentration sensitivity; molecularity is a theoretical non-zero integer (1, 2, 3) representing colliding species in an elementary step.
  • For complex reactions, the slowest elementary step acts as the rate-determining bottleneck.
  • Zero-order reactions proceed at constant speed until the reactant is exhausted; their half-life is directly proportional to initial concentration.
  • First-order reactions exhibit exponential decay; their half-life is completely independent of initial reactant concentration. All radioactive decay processes obey first-order kinetics.
  • In pseudo-first-order reactions, an abundant reagent (such as a solvent) maintains nearly constant concentration, reducing higher-order rate laws to apparent first-order behavior.
  • The Arrhenius equation (\(k = A\,e^{-E_{\mathrm{a}}/RT}\)) mathematically encapsulates the exponential sensitivity of reaction velocity to temperature, explained by the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution where a \(10\,\mathrm{K}\) rise approximately doubles the population of molecules with energy exceeding \(E_{\mathrm{a}}\).
  • A catalyst accelerates reactions purely kinetically by opening an alternative pathway with a lower \(E_{\mathrm{a}}\); it leaves equilibrium state, \(\Delta G\), and \(\Delta H\) strictly unaltered.
  • Collision Theory links macroscopic rate constants to microscopic molecular collisions through collision frequency (\(Z_{\mathrm{AB}}\)), activation energy (\(E_{\mathrm{a}}\)), and steric orientation probability (\(P\)).
