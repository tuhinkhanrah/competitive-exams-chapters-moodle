NEET Examination Profile: In the NEET UG Chemistry paper, Coordination Compounds represents one of the highest-weightage chapters in Class 12 Inorganic Chemistry, routinely contributing 2 to 3 questions (8 to 12 marks) every year. NEET questions in this chapter are renowned for testing precision in conceptual fundamentals: identifying primary vs. secondary valences, deducing IUPAC nomenclature, evaluating isomer counts (especially optical activity of chelated complexes), predicting magnetic moments using VBT and CFT, calculating crystal field stabilization energy (CFSE), and understanding synergic bonding in metal carbonyls.

Target Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish rigorously between double salts and complex ions based on their aqueous dissociation behavior.
  • Identify and classify ligands according to charge, denticity (unidentate, didentate, hexadentate), chelation (and the resulting chelate effect), and ambidentate behavior.

  • Apply current IUPAC nomenclature conventions to name mononuclear complexes and formulate chemical representations unambiguously.
  • Classify and enumerate structural isomers (ionisation, solvate/hydrate, linkage, coordination) and stereoisomers (geometrical cis/trans and fac/mer; optical enantiomers).
  • Predict hybridisation schemes, spatial geometries, spin states (inner vs. outer orbital), and spin-only magnetic moments using Valence Bond Theory (VBT).
  • Explain Crystal Field Theory (CFT) orbital splitting in octahedral (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)) and tetrahedral (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\)) environments, apply the spectrochemical series, evaluate pairing energies (\(\mathrm{P}\)), calculate CFSE, and rationalize \(d-d\) transitions, complementary colors, and gemstone pigmentation.
  • Describe synergic bonding in transition metal carbonyls (\(\mathrm{M}-\mathrm{C}\) \(\sigma\)-bond and \(\mathrm{M} \rightarrow \mathrm{CO}\) \(\pi\)-backbonding) and its structural/vibrational consequences.
  • Identify high-yield analytical, metallurgical, biological, industrial, and medicinal applications of coordination compounds.

2. Alfred Werner’s Theory of Coordination Compounds

Alfred Werner (1866–1919), recognized as the founder of modern coordination chemistry, explained the unique bonding and stoichiometry in transition metal addition compounds by proposing that metal ions exhibit two distinct types of valency:

Postulates of Werner’s Theory

  1. Two Types of Linkages: In coordination compounds, central metals exhibit two types of linkages (valences): primary valence and secondary valence.
  2. Primary Valence: Corresponds to the oxidation state of the metal ion. It is ionisable, non-directional in space, and is satisfied strictly by negative ions (anions).
  3. Secondary Valence: Corresponds to the coordination number of the metal ion. It is non-ionisable and directional in space. It is satisfied by either neutral molecules (e.g., \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)) or negative ions (e.g., \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), \(\mathrm{CN^-}\)). The secondary valence is fixed for a given metal in a specific oxidation state (e.g., cobalt(III) universally exhibits a secondary valence of 6).
  4. Spatial Directionality (Coordination Polyhedra): The groups bound by secondary linkages occupy definite, directional spatial positions around the central metal ion, generating characteristic geometries such as octahedral, tetrahedral, and square planar.

Experimental Validation: Cobalt(III) Chloride–Ammonia Complexes

NEET High-Yield Distinction: Double Salts vs. Coordination Complexes

  • Double Salts: Addition compounds formed by stoichiometric combinations of stable salts that dissociate completely into individual constituent simple ions when dissolved in water. They retain identity only in the solid state.
    • Carnallite: \(\mathrm{KCl \cdot MgCl_2 \cdot 6H_2O \rightarrow K^+ + Mg^{2+} + 3Cl^- + 6H_2O}\)
    • Mohr’s Salt: \(\mathrm{FeSO_4 \cdot (NH_4)_2SO_4 \cdot 6H_2O \rightarrow Fe^{2+} + 2NH_4^+ + 2SO_4^{2-} + 6H_2O}\) (gives characteristic test for \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\))
    • Potash Alum: \(\mathrm{KAl(SO_4)_2 \cdot 12H_2O \rightarrow K^+ + Al^{3+} + 2SO_4^{2-} + 12H_2O}\)
  • Coordination Complexes: Retain their molecular identity in both solid and aqueous states. The complex entity enclosed in square brackets does not dissociate into simple ions.
    • Potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II), \(\mathrm{K_4[Fe(CN)_6] \rightarrow 4K^+ + [Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}}\). The solution gives no test for free \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) or \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) ions.
    • Tetraamminecopper(II) sulphate, \(\mathrm{[Cu(NH_3)_4]SO_4 \rightarrow [Cu(NH_3)_4]^{2+} + SO_4^{2-}}\), giving no test for free \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\).

3. Essential Definitions and Structural Terminology

(a) Coordination Entity & Central Atom/Ion

A coordination entity constitutes a central metal atom or cation bonded to a fixed number of ions or neutral molecules. For example, \([\mathrm{CoCl}_3(\mathrm{NH}_3)_3]\) is a neutral coordination entity, while \([\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{CN})_6]^{4-}\) is an anionic entity, and \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_6]^{3+}\) is a cationic entity.

The central atom or ion is the transition metal center to which a fixed number of donor groups are coordinated in a definite geometrical arrangement. Because it accepts electron pairs from ligands, the central atom/ion functions as a Lewis acid.

(b) Ligands and Their Classification

The ions or neutral molecules directly bonded to the central metal atom/ion in a coordination entity are called ligands. Because they donate lone pairs of electrons to form coordinate covalent \(\sigma\)-bonds with the central metal, ligands function as Lewis bases.

  • Unidentate (Monodentate) Ligands: Ligands that bind to the metal center through a single donor atom. Examples: \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), \(\mathrm{Br^-}\), \(\mathrm{F^-}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) (donor: \(\mathrm{O}\)), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) (donor: \(\mathrm{N}\)), \(\mathrm{OH^-}\), \(\mathrm{CN^-}\), \(\mathrm{CO}\).
  • Didentate (Bidentate) Ligands: Ligands possessing two donor atoms that can coordinate simultaneously to a single metal center.
    • Ethane-1,2-diamine (\(\mathrm{H_2NCH_2CH_2NH_2}\), abbreviated as ‘en’): Neutral ligand with two donor nitrogen atoms.
    • Oxalate ion (\(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\), abbreviated as ‘ox’): Symmetrical dinegative anion coordinating through two negatively charged oxygen atoms.
  • Polydentate Ligands: Contain multiple donor atoms.
    • Triaminotriethylamine (\(\mathrm{N(CH_2CH_2NH_2)_3}\)): Branched tetradentate ligand coordinating through four nitrogen atoms.
    • Ethylenediaminetetraacetate ion (\(\mathrm{EDTA^{4-}}\)): An important hexadentate ligand that coordinates through two nitrogen atoms and four carboxylate oxygen atoms.
  • Chelating Ligands & The Chelate Effect: When a di- or polydentate ligand binds a single metal ion through two or more donor atoms simultaneously, it forms a 5- or 6-membered heterocyclic ring known as a chelate ring. Complexes containing chelate rings are called chelate complexes.
    The Chelate Effect: Chelate complexes exhibit markedly greater thermodynamic stability than analogous complexes formed by monodentate ligands. This enhanced stability is predominantly an entropy-driven phenomenon (\(\Delta S^\circ > 0\)), as displacement of multiple monodentate molecules by a single polydentate ligand increases the net number of independent particles in solution: \[ [\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{H_2O})_6]^{2+} + 3\,\mathrm{en} \rightleftharpoons [\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{en})_3]^{2+} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O} \quad (\Delta S^\circ > 0) \]
  • Ambidentate Ligands: Monodentate ligands having two distinct potential donor atoms, either of which can coordinate to the central metal atom/ion:
    • Nitrite ion (\(\mathrm{NO_2^-}\)): Can bind through nitrogen (\(\mathrm{M \leftarrow NO_2}\), named nitrito-N or nitro) or through oxygen (\(\mathrm{M \leftarrow ONO}\), named nitrito-O).
    • Thiocyanate ion (\(\mathrm{SCN^-}\)): Can bind through sulfur (\(\mathrm{M \leftarrow SCN}\), named thiocyanato-S) or through nitrogen (\(\mathrm{M \leftarrow NCS}\), named isothiocyanato or thiocyanato-N).

(c) Coordination Number (CN)

The coordination number of a central metal ion is defined as the total number of ligand donor atoms directly bonded to the metal ion by \(\sigma\)-bonds.

NEET Calculation Rule: \(\text{Coordination Number} = \sum (\text{number of ligands} \times \text{denticity})\).
• In \([\mathrm{PtCl}_6]^{2-}\), \(\mathrm{CN} = 6 \times 1 = 6\).
• In \([\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_4]^{2+}\), \(\mathrm{CN} = 4 \times 1 = 4\).
• In \([\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{O}_4)_3]^{3-}\), oxalate is didentate: \(\mathrm{CN} = 3 \times 2 = 6\).
• In \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{en})_3]^{3+}\), ‘en’ is didentate: \(\mathrm{CN} = 3 \times 2 = 6\).
• In \([\mathrm{Ca}(\mathrm{EDTA})]^{2-}\), \(\mathrm{EDTA^{4-}}\) is hexadentate: \(\mathrm{CN} = 1 \times 6 = 6\).
Crucial note: \(\pi\)-bonds between metal and ligand are never counted toward the coordination number.

(d) Coordination Sphere, Counter Ions, & Coordination Polyhedra

The central metal atom/ion and its coordinated ligands are enclosed within square brackets, \([\dots]\), collectively forming the non-ionisable coordination sphere. Ionisable species written outside the brackets are counter ions (e.g., in \(\mathrm{K_4[Fe(CN)_6]}\), \([\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{CN})_6]^{4-}\) is the coordination sphere and \(4\,\mathrm{K^+}\) are the counter ions).

(e) Oxidation Number of the Central Atom

The oxidation number of the central atom in a complex is defined as the residual charge it would carry if all coordinated ligands were removed along with the shared electron pairs. It is denoted by a Roman numeral in parentheses immediately following the metal name:

  • In \([\mathrm{Cu}(\mathrm{CN})_4]^{3-}\): \(x + 4(-1) = -3 \implies x = +1 \implies \mathrm{Cu(I)}\).
  • In \([\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{CN})_6]^{4-}\): \(x + 6(-1) = -4 \implies x = +2 \implies \mathrm{Fe(II)}\).
  • In \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_4(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})\mathrm{Cl}]^{2+}\): \(x + 4(0) + 1(0) + 1(-1) = +2 \implies x = +3 \implies \mathrm{Co(III)}\).
  • In \([\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{CO})_4]\): \(x + 4(0) = 0 \implies x = 0 \implies \mathrm{Ni(0)}\).

(f) Homoleptic vs. Heteroleptic Complexes

  • Homoleptic Complexes: Complexes in which the central metal atom/ion is coordinated to only one kind of donor group. Examples: \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_6]^{3+}\), \([\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{CO})_4]\), \([\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{CN})_6]^{3-}\).
  • Heteroleptic Complexes: Complexes in which the central metal atom/ion is bound to more than one kind of donor group. Examples: \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_4\mathrm{Cl}_2]^+\), \([\mathrm{PtCl}_2(\mathrm{NH}_3)_2]\). (Heteroleptic complexes can exhibit geometrical and optical isomerism).

4. IUPAC Nomenclature of Mononuclear Coordination Compounds

To communicate structures and isomerism unambiguously, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) has established standardized principles of additive nomenclature for coordination entities.

IUPAC Rules for Writing Chemical Formulas

  1. The central metal atom is placed first inside the square brackets.
  2. Ligands are then listed in strict alphabetical order according to their chemical symbols/abbreviations, regardless of their ionic charge. (In case of abbreviations like ‘en’ or ‘ox’, the first letter of the abbreviation determines its alphabetical position).
  3. The entire coordination entity is enclosed in square brackets \([\dots]\). Polyatomic ligands and abbreviations are individually enclosed in parentheses, e.g., \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_4(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})\mathrm{Cl}]\mathrm{Cl}_2\).
  4. No space is left between the central metal symbol and the ligands inside the square brackets.
  5. When writing a charged entity without its counter ion, the net charge is indicated outside the square brackets as a right superscript with the numeral preceding the sign: \([\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_6]^{3+}\), \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{CN})_6]^{3-}\).
  6. The total charge of the cations must equal and balance the total charge of the anions.

IUPAC Rules for Systematic Naming

  1. Order of Naming Ions: The positive ion (cation) is always named first, followed by the negative ion (anion), regardless of whether the complex entity is cationic, anionic, or neutral.
  2. Ligand Order: Ligands are named in strict alphabetical order before the name of the central metal atom/ion. (Numerical multipliers such as di-, tri-, tetra- are ignored when determining alphabetical order).
  3. Ligand Name Endings:
    • Anionic ligands: End in -o (or -ido according to 2004 IUPAC recommendations): chloride \(\rightarrow\) chlorido (or chloro), cyanide \(\rightarrow\) cyanido, hydroxide \(\rightarrow\) hydroxido, oxalate \(\rightarrow\) oxalato, sulphate \(\rightarrow\) sulphato, nitrite \(\rightarrow\) nitrito-N (or nitro) or nitrito-O.
    • Neutral ligands: Retain their molecular names, with four key exceptions: \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) is named aqua, \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) is named ammine (note the double ‘m’), \(\mathrm{CO}\) is named carbonyl, and \(\mathrm{NO}\) is named nitrosyl.
  4. Numerical Prefixes:
    • Simple ligands take prefixes mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa-.
    • Complex ligands (especially those whose names already contain a numerical prefix, e.g., ethane-1,2-diamine, triphenylphosphine) or polydentate chelating ligands take multiplicative prefixes bis- (2), tris- (3), tetrakis- (4), with the ligand name placed in parentheses: e.g., \(\mathrm{tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)cobalt(III)}\).
  5. Central Metal Name:
    • In a Cationic or Neutral Entity: The metal retains its regular English elemental name: cobalt, chromium, platinum, iron, nickel, copper.
    • In an Anionic Entity: The metal name ends in the characteristic suffix -ate. For certain metals, the Latin root is used: iron \(\rightarrow\) ferrate, copper \(\rightarrow\) cuprate, lead \(\rightarrow\) plumbate, silver \(\rightarrow\) argentate, gold \(\rightarrow\) aurate, tin \(\rightarrow\) stannate, platinum \(\rightarrow\) platinate, cobalt \(\rightarrow\) cobaltate.
  6. Oxidation State: The oxidation state of the central metal is indicated by a Roman numeral in parentheses immediately following the metal name, with no space: e.g., \(\mathrm{cobalt(III)}\), \(\mathrm{nickel(0)}\).

5. Isomerism in Coordination Compounds

Isomers are two or more compounds that possess identical chemical formulas but display different structural arrangements of atoms or differing spatial orientations, resulting in distinct physical and chemical properties.

5.1 Structural Isomerism

Structural isomers possess identical overall chemical formulas but differ in the connectivity of chemical bonds within the coordination sphere and counter ions:

  • 1. Ionisation Isomerism: Occurs when an ionisable counter ion displaces a ligand from within the coordination sphere, exchanging roles. When dissolved in water, the two isomers produce distinctly different ions in solution.
    • Diagnostic chemical test: \[ [\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_5(\mathrm{SO}_4)]\mathrm{Br} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{BaCl}_2} \text{No precipitate} \quad \Big| \quad [\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_5(\mathrm{SO}_4)]\mathrm{Br} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{AgNO}_3} \mathrm{AgBr}\downarrow \text{ (pale yellow precipitate)} \] \[ [\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_5\mathrm{Br}]\mathrm{SO}_4 \xrightarrow{\mathrm{BaCl}_2} \mathrm{BaSO}_4\downarrow \text{ (white precipitate)} \quad \Big| \quad [\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_5\mathrm{Br}]\mathrm{SO}_4 \xrightarrow{\mathrm{AgNO}_3} \text{No precipitate} \]
  • 2. Solvate (Hydrate) Isomerism: A specific form of ionisation isomerism where water acts as either a coordinated ligand within the coordination sphere or as uncoordinated solvent of crystallization held in the crystal lattice.
    • \([\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_6]\mathrm{Cl}_3\) (Violet): 6 coordinated waters; gives \(3\ \mathrm{mol}\ \mathrm{AgCl}\) per mol of complex.
    • \([\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_5\mathrm{Cl}]\mathrm{Cl}_2 \cdot \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) (Grey-green): 5 coordinated waters; gives \(2\ \mathrm{mol}\ \mathrm{AgCl}\) per mol of complex.
    • \([\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_4\mathrm{Cl}_2]\mathrm{Cl} \cdot 2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) (Bright green): 4 coordinated waters; gives \(1\ \mathrm{mol}\ \mathrm{AgCl}\) per mol of complex.
  • 3. Linkage Isomerism: Arises exclusively in complexes containing ambidentate ligands that possess two different donor atoms:
    • Pentaamminenitritocobalt(III) chloride exists as two distinct linkage isomers discovered by S. M. Jørgensen:
    – \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_5(\mathrm{NO}_2)]\mathrm{Cl}_2\) (Yellow form): Nitro isomer coordinated through nitrogen (\(\mathrm{Co-NO_2}\)).
    – \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_5(\mathrm{ONO})]\mathrm{Cl}_2\) (Red form): Nitrito isomer coordinated through oxygen (\(\mathrm{Co-ONO}\)).
    • Thiocyanate complexes: \(\mathrm{M-SCN}\) (thiocyanato-S) vs. \(\mathrm{M-NCS}\) (thiocyanato-N or isothiocyanato).
  • 4. Coordination Isomerism: Occurs in salts where both the cation and the anion are complex entities, arising from the interchange of ligands between the two metal centers.
    • Example: \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_6][\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{CN})_6]\) (where \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) coordinates to \(\mathrm{Co(III)}\) and \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) to \(\mathrm{Cr(III)}\)) and its coordination isomer \([\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_6][\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{CN})_6]\) (where \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) coordinates to \(\mathrm{Cr(III)}\) and \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) to \(\mathrm{Co(III)}\)).

5.2 Stereoisomerism

Stereoisomers possess identical chemical bonds and connectivity, but differ in the spatial orientation of these bonds.

A. Geometrical Isomerism

Arises in heteroleptic complexes due to different possible spatial distributions of coordinating ligands:

  • Square Planar Complexes (\(\mathrm{CN}=4\)):
    • Type \([\mathrm{MA}_2\mathrm{B}_2]\): Exhibits 2 geometrical isomers: cis (identical donor atoms occupy adjacent positions at \(90^\circ\)) and trans (identical donor atoms occupy opposite positions at \(180^\circ\)).
    – Classic example: \([\mathrm{PtCl}_2(\mathrm{NH}_3)_2]\). The cis-isomer (Cisplatin) is an effective antitumor drug, whereas the trans-isomer is clinically inactive.
    • Type \([\mathrm{MABCD}]\): A square planar complex containing four different monodentate ligands (e.g., \([\mathrm{Pt}(\mathrm{NH}_3)(\mathrm{Br})(\mathrm{Cl})(\mathrm{py})]\)) exhibits 3 geometrical isomers (two cis and one trans with respect to any chosen reference ligand).
  • Why Tetrahedral Complexes Cannot Show Geometrical Isomerism: In a regular tetrahedral geometry, all four coordination positions are mutually adjacent and equidistant from one another, subtending identical bond angles of \(109.5^\circ\). Therefore, no cis/trans distinction is geometrically possible.
  • Octahedral Complexes (\(\mathrm{CN}=6\)):
    • Type \([\mathrm{MA}_4\mathrm{B}_2]\): Shows 2 isomers: cis (the two \(\mathrm{B}\) ligands are adjacent at \(90^\circ\)) and trans (the two \(\mathrm{B}\) ligands are opposite at \(180^\circ\)). Example: \([\mathrm{CoCl}_2(\mathrm{NH}_3)_4]^+\) (cis is violet, trans is green).
    • Type \([\mathrm{MA}_3\mathrm{B}_3]\): Displays a distinctive pair of geometrical isomers:
    – Facial (fac) Isomer: The three identical donor atoms occupy adjacent positions at the corners of a single triangular octahedral face (all mutually adjacent at \(90^\circ\)).
    – Meridional (mer) Isomer: The three identical donor atoms lie in a plane that passes through the metal center, tracing a meridian around the octahedron (two ligands trans at \(180^\circ\), one cis at \(90^\circ\)). Example: \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_3(\mathrm{NO}_2)_3]\).
    • Type \([\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{AA})_2\mathrm{B}_2]\): Complexes with two symmetrical didentate chelating ligands and two monodentate ligands exhibit cis and trans forms, such as in \([\mathrm{CoCl}_2(\mathrm{en})_2]^+\).

B. Optical Isomerism

Optical isomers (enantiomers) are stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of one another. Molecules or ions that lack an improper axis of rotation (specifically lacking a plane of symmetry \(\sigma\) and a center of inversion \(i\)) are chiral.

  • Chiral coordination entities rotate the plane of plane-polarized light in a polarimeter: the isomer rotating the plane to the right is dextrorotatory (\(d\)), while that rotating to the left is laevorotatory (\(l\)).
  • Tris-chelate complexes of the type \([\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{AA})_3]^{n+}\): (e.g., \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{en})_3]^{3+}\), \([\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{O}_4)_3]^{3-}\)) have no plane of symmetry; they are universally chiral and exist as an enantiomeric pair of \(d\)- and \(l\)-forms. They show no geometrical isomerism.
  • Bis-chelate complexes of the type \([\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{AA})_2\mathrm{B}_2]^{n+}\): (e.g., \([\mathrm{CoCl}_2(\mathrm{en})_2]^+\), \([\mathrm{PtCl}_2(\mathrm{en})_2]^{2+}\), \([\mathrm{CrCl}_2(\mathrm{ox})_2]^{3-}\)):
    • cis-isomer: Has no plane of symmetry or inversion center. Its mirror image cannot be superimposed; thus, cis is chiral and optically active (resolvable into \(d\) and \(l\) enantiomers).
    • trans-isomer: Possesses both a plane of symmetry and a center of inversion. It is superimposable on its mirror image; thus, trans is achiral and optically inactive.

6. Bonding in Coordination Compounds: Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

Linus Pauling extended the Valence Bond Theory to explain the geometries and magnetic properties of coordination entities. According to VBT, under the influence of approaching ligands, the central metal atom or ion makes available empty valence atomic orbitals that undergo hybridisation to yield an equivalent set of hybrid orbitals directed along the symmetry axes of the coordination polyhedron. Each ligand then donates a lone pair of electrons into an empty hybrid orbital to establish a metal–ligand coordinate covalent \(\sigma\)-bond.

The magnetic susceptibility of a complex reveals the number of unpaired electrons (\(n\)) present in the metal \(d\)-orbitals. The theoretical spin-only magnetic moment (\(\mu_{\mathrm{s}}\)) is calculated as:

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{s}} = \sqrt{n(n + 2)}\ \mathrm{Bohr\ Magnetons\ (BM)} \]

Key reference numerical values for NEET:

High-Yield VBT Case Studies

Case 1: Inner Orbital vs. Outer Orbital Cobalt(III) Complexes (\(3d^6\))

Cobalt in ground state: \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^7 4s^2\). For \(\mathrm{Co^{3+}}\): \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^6 4s^0 4p^0 4d^0\).

  • \([\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{NH}_3)_6]^{3+}\) (Diamagnetic, Inner Orbital):
    In the presence of the strong-field ligand \(\mathrm{NH}_3\), the six electrons in the \(3d\) subshell are forced to pair up completely: \[ 3d^6 \rightarrow [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\quad][\quad] \] This vacates two inner \(3d\) orbitals. The two vacant \(3d\), one \(4s\), and three \(4p\) orbitals hybridize to form six equivalent \(d^2sp^3\) hybrid orbitals. Six lone pairs from six \(\mathrm{NH}_3\) ligands fill these hybrid orbitals.
    Magnetic State: Diamagnetic (\(n=0\), \(\mu = 0\ \mathrm{BM}\)). Because inner \((n-1)d\) orbitals are utilized, it is termed an inner orbital complex, low spin complex, or spin-paired complex.
  • \([\mathrm{CoF}_6]^{3-}\) (Paramagnetic, Outer Orbital):
    In the presence of the weak-field ligand \(\mathrm{F^-}\), electron pairing in the \(3d\) subshell cannot be induced: \[ 3d^6 \rightarrow [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow][\uparrow][\uparrow][\uparrow] \quad (n = 4) \] No inner \(3d\) orbitals are available. The metal ion instead hybridizes its outer \(4s\), three \(4p\), and two outer \(4d\) orbitals to form six \(sp^3d^2\) hybrid orbitals.
    Magnetic State: Strongly paramagnetic (\(n=4\), \(\mu = \sqrt{4(6)} = 4.90\ \mathrm{BM}\)). Because outer \(nd\) orbitals are utilized, it is termed an outer orbital complex, high spin complex, or spin-free complex.
Case 2: Four-Coordinate Nickel Complexes (\(3d^8\))

Nickel in ground state: \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^8 4s^2\). For \(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}\): \([\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^8 4s^0 4p^0\).

  • \([\mathrm{NiCl}_4]^{2-}\) (Tetrahedral, Paramagnetic):
    \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) is a weak-field ligand unable to pair the two unpaired electrons in \(3d^8\): \[ 3d^8 \rightarrow [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow][\uparrow] \quad (n = 2) \] The vacant \(4s\) and three \(4p\) orbitals undergo \(sp^3\) hybridisation, yielding a tetrahedral geometry.
    Magnetic Moment: \(\mu = \sqrt{2(4)} = 2.83\ \mathrm{BM}\) (paramagnetic).
  • \([\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{CN})_4]^{2-}\) (Square Planar, Diamagnetic):
    \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) is a strong-field ligand that forces pairing of the two unpaired \(3d\) electrons: \[ 3d^8 \rightarrow [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\quad] \quad (n = 0) \] One inner \(3d\) orbital is vacated. The vacant \(3d\), one \(4s\), and two \(4p\) orbitals undergo \(dsp^2\) hybridisation, creating a square planar geometry.
    Magnetic Moment: Diamagnetic (\(n=0\), \(\mu = 0\ \mathrm{BM}\)).
  • \([\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{CO})_4]\) (Tetrahedral, Diamagnetic, \(\mathrm{Ni(0)}\)):
    In zero oxidation state, nickel has the configuration \(3d^8 4s^2\). Under the influence of the extraordinarily strong ligand \(\mathrm{CO}\), the two \(4s\) electrons are pushed completely into the \(3d\) subshell, pairing with the unpaired electrons to generate a fully filled \(3d^{10}\) configuration: \[ 3d^{10} \rightarrow [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow] \quad 4s^0 \quad 4p^0 \] The now vacant \(4s\) and three \(4p\) orbitals undergo \(sp^3\) hybridisation, producing a tetrahedral geometry.
    Magnetic Moment: Diamagnetic (\(n=0\), \(\mu = 0\ \mathrm{BM}\)).

Limitations of Valence Bond Theory

Although VBT provides a qualitative model for geometries and spin states, it has severe limitations:

  • It involves multiple empirical assumptions and offers no quantitative interpretation of magnetic susceptibility data.
  • It cannot explain or predict the electronic absorption spectra and characteristic colors of transition metal complexes.
  • It gives no quantitative interpretation of the thermodynamic or kinetic stabilities of complexes.
  • It cannot reliably predict whether 4-coordinate \(d^8\) complexes (such as \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) or \(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}\)) will adopt tetrahedral or square planar geometry.
  • It provides no physical mechanism to explain why some ligands are strong-field (pairing) while others are weak-field.

7. Crystal Field Theory (CFT)

Crystal Field Theory (CFT) is an electrostatic model that treats the metal–ligand interaction as purely ionic, arising entirely from electrostatic attractions between the positively charged central metal cation and the negative point charges (anionic ligands like \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), \(\mathrm{CN^-}\)) or point dipoles (neutral dipolar molecules like \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) directed with their negative ends toward the metal).

In an isolated, gaseous transition metal atom or ion, the five \(d\)-orbitals (\(d_{xy}\), \(d_{yz}\), \(d_{xz}\), \(d_{x^2-y^2}\), \(d_{z^2}\)) are completely degenerate (possessing identical energy). When surrounded by a hypothetical spherically symmetrical field of negative charges, all five \(d\)-orbitals are raised equally in energy. However, in an actual complex, the ligands approach along specific geometric directions. The electrostatic repulsion between ligand electrons and metal \(d\)-electrons is non-uniform, lifting the degeneracy and causing the \(d\)-orbitals to split into sets of differing energies. This phenomenon is termed crystal field splitting.

7.1 Crystal Field Splitting in Octahedral Complexes (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\))

In an octahedral coordination entity, six ligands approach the central metal ion along the Cartesian axes (\(x\), \(y\), and \(z\)):

  • Axial Orbitals (\(e_{\mathrm{g}}\) set): The \(d_{x^2-y^2}\) and \(d_{z^2}\) orbitals have their electron density lobes pointing directly along the Cartesian axes. Consequently, they experience maximum electrostatic repulsion from the incoming ligands and are destabilized, rising in energy by \(+\frac{3}{5}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) (\(+0.6\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)) above the spherical barycentre. The doubly degenerate set is designated \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\).
  • Non-Axial Orbitals (\(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) set): The \(d_{xy}\), \(d_{yz}\), and \(d_{xz}\) orbitals have their lobes directed between the Cartesian axes (at \(45^\circ\) to the axes). They experience significantly less repulsion and are stabilized, dropping in energy by \(-\frac{2}{5}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) (\(-0.4\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)) below the barycentre. The triply degenerate set is designated \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\).

The total energy separation between the \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) and \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\) levels is defined as the octahedral crystal field splitting energy, denoted by \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) (or \(10\,\mathrm{Dq}\)).

7.2 Crystal Field Splitting in Tetrahedral Complexes (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\))

In a tetrahedral complex, four ligands approach along the alternate corners of a circumscribed cube. The coordinate axes pass through the centers of the cube faces. Hence, the four ligands approach between the Cartesian axes:

  • The non-axial orbitals (\(d_{xy}\), \(d_{yz}\), \(d_{xz}\)) point more closely toward the approaching ligands than the axial orbitals, experiencing greater repulsion. Therefore, the \(t_2\) set is destabilized by \(+\frac{2}{5}\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\) (\(+0.4\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\)).
  • The axial orbitals (\(d_{x^2-y^2}\), \(d_{z^2}\)) point between the ligands and are stabilized by \(-\frac{3}{5}\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\) (\(-0.6\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\)).
  • Note on symmetry labels: Because a regular tetrahedron lacks a center of inversion symmetry (centrosymmetry), the ‘g’ (gerade) subscript is omitted: the levels are designated strictly as \(e\) (lower energy, doubly degenerate) and \(t_2\) (higher energy, triply degenerate).
NEET Fundamental Formula: For the same metal ion, same ligands, and identical metal–ligand bond lengths: \[ \Delta_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{4}{9}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} \] Because \(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\) is less than half of \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\), the crystal field splitting in tetrahedral complexes is never large enough to overcome the electron pairing energy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}} < \mathrm{P}\) universally). Consequently, tetrahedral complexes are virtually always high-spin (spin-free)!

7.3 The Spectrochemical Series

The magnitude of \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) depends on both the oxidation state of the metal ion and the field strength of the coordinated ligands. Ligands that produce large \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) splitting are termed strong field ligands, while those producing small splitting are weak field ligands. Arranged in order of increasing crystal field splitting (experimentally derived from absorption spectra), they constitute the spectrochemical series:

\[ \mathrm{I^- < Br^- < SCN^- < Cl^- < S^{2-} < F^- < OH^- < C_2O_4^{2-} < H_2O < NCS^- < edta^{4-} < NH_3 < en < CN^- < CO} \]

NEET Memory Anchor: Halides are weak field ligands \(\rightarrow\) Oxygen-donor ligands are intermediate-to-weak \(\rightarrow\) Nitrogen-donor ligands are strong \(\rightarrow\) Carbon-donor ligands (\(\mathrm{CN^-}\), \(\mathrm{CO}\)) are the strongest field ligands.

7.4 Electronic Configurations & Crystal Field Stabilization Energy (CFSE)

In an octahedral field, the first three electrons enter the lower-energy \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) orbitals singly in accordance with Hund’s rule: \(d^1 \rightarrow t_{2\mathrm{g}}^1\), \(d^2 \rightarrow t_{2\mathrm{g}}^2\), \(d^3 \rightarrow t_{2\mathrm{g}}^3\). For a \(d^4\) ion, a competition arises between the crystal field splitting energy (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)) and the mean pairing energy (\(\mathrm{P}\)) (the electrostatic repulsion energy required to pair two electrons in the same orbital):

  • Case 1: Weak Field Ligands (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} < \mathrm{P}\)):
    The cost of pairing (\(\mathrm{P}\)) exceeds the energy gap (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)). The fourth electron enters the higher-energy \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\) orbital: \[ \text{Configuration: } t_{2\mathrm{g}}^3 e_{\mathrm{g}}^1 \quad (\text{High Spin / Spin Free}) \]

7.5 Origin of Colour and \(d-d\) Transitions

Transition metal complexes frequently exhibit vivid colours. When white light passes through a solution of a complex, a photon whose energy corresponds precisely to the crystal field splitting (\(h\nu = \Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)) is absorbed, promoting an electron from the lower \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) level to the empty or half-filled \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\) level (a \(d-d\) electronic transition). NEET High-Yield Principles of Complex Colour

  • Requirement for \(d-d\) Transitions: In the total absence of ligands, no crystal field splitting occurs; the \(d\)-orbitals remain degenerate, preventing \(d-d\) absorption. Thus, anhydrous \(\mathrm{CuSO_4}\) is white, whereas hydrated \(\mathrm{CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O}\) is blue. Heating \([\mathrm{Ti}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_6]\mathrm{Cl}_3\) drives off water ligands, rendering it completely colourless.
  • \(d^0\) and \(d^{10}\) Configurations: Ions with empty \(d\)-orbitals (\(d^0\), e.g., \(\mathrm{Sc^{3+}}\), \(\mathrm{Ti^{4+}}\)) or fully occupied \(d\)-orbitals (\(d^{10}\), e.g., \(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\), \(\mathrm{Cd^{2+}}\)) cannot undergo \(d-d\) transitions and are universally colourless.
  • Colour Tuning by Ligand Substitution: Adding ethane-1,2-diamine progressively to green \([\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_6]^{2+}\) causes a stepwise increase in \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\): \[ [\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_6]^{2+} \text{ (green)} \xrightarrow{+\mathrm{en}} [\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_4(\mathrm{en})]^{2+} \text{ (pale blue)} \xrightarrow{+\mathrm{en}} [\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O})_2(\mathrm{en})_2]^{2+} \text{ (blue/purple)} \xrightarrow{+\mathrm{en}} [\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{en})_3]^{2+} \text{ (violet)} \]
  • Gemstones:
    • Ruby: Formed by mineral corundum (\(\mathrm{Al_2O_3}\)) containing \(0.5\text{--}1\%\) \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) (\(3d^3\)) substituting in octahedral \(\mathrm{Al^{3+}}\) sites. Strong \(d-d\) transitions absorb green/yellow light, imparting a brilliant red color.
    • Emerald: Mineral beryl (\(\mathrm{Be_3Al_2Si_6O_{18}}\)) where \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) occupies octahedral sites with slightly larger metal–ligand distances (smaller \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)), shifting the absorption bands to yellow-red and blue, thus transmitting vibrant green light.

7.6 Limitations of Crystal Field Theory

  • Because CFT treats ligands as negative point charges, it incorrectly predicts that anionic ligands should exert the largest splitting. In reality, halides are at the lowest (weak-field) end of the spectrochemical series.
  • It completely ignores the covalent character of metal–ligand bonds, neglecting \(\pi\)-bonding and orbital overlap (addressed in Ligand Field and Molecular Orbital theories).

8. Bonding and Structure in Metal Carbonyls

Transition metals form a distinct class of homoleptic compounds with carbon monoxide (\(\mathrm{CO}\)) called metal carbonyls, wherein the metal center is formally in the zero oxidation state. These compounds possess well-defined molecular geometries:

  • \([\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{CO})_4]\): Mononuclear, tetrahedral geometry (\(\mathrm{Ni(0)}\), \(sp^3\)).
  • \([\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{CO})_5]\): Mononuclear, trigonal bipyramidal geometry (\(\mathrm{Fe(0)}\), \(dsp^3\)).
  • \([\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{CO})_6]\): Mononuclear, octahedral geometry (\(\mathrm{Cr(0)}\), \(d^2sp^3\)).
  • \([\mathrm{Mn}_2(\mathrm{CO})_{10}]\): Dinuclear decacarbonyl, composed of two square pyramidal \(\mathrm{Mn(CO)_5}\) units joined by a direct \(\mathrm{Mn-Mn}\) single bond (bond length \(292\,\mathrm{pm}\)), without bridging carbonyls.
  • \([\mathrm{Co}_2(\mathrm{CO})_8]\): Dinuclear octacarbonyl; in the solid state it features a \(\mathrm{Co-Co}\) single bond bridged by two carbonyl ligands, with each cobalt bearing three terminal \(\mathrm{CO}\) groups.

The Synergic Bonding Mechanism

The metal–carbon (\(\mathrm{M-C}\)) bond in metal carbonyls exhibits unique mutual reinforcement through a two-way synergic interaction involving both \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) bonding components:

Components of the Synergic Bond

  1. \(\mathrm{M \leftarrow C}\) \(\sigma\)-Bond: Formed by the donation of the lone pair of electrons residing in the non-bonding \(sp\)-hybridized orbital on the carbonyl carbon into a vacant \(d\)-orbital of the metal atom.
  2. \(\mathrm{M \rightarrow C}\) \(\pi\)-Backbond: Formed by the back-donation of a pair of electrons from a filled metal \(d\)-orbital of suitable \(\pi\)-symmetry into the empty, lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO)—the antibonding \(\pi^*\) molecular orbital of carbon monoxide.

NEET High-Yield Deductions on Synergic Bonding

  • Mutual Strengthening: The forward \(\sigma\)-donation builds up negative electron density on the metal, which enhances the metal’s tendency to back-donate into the \(\pi^*\) orbitals of \(\mathrm{CO}\). This synergic effect dramatically strengthens and shortens the \(\mathrm{M-C}\) bond.
  • Consequence on the Carbon–Oxygen (\(\mathrm{C-O}\)) Bond: Because electrons are accepted into the antibonding \(\pi^*\) orbital of \(\mathrm{CO}\), the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond order decreases below 3. Consequently:
    • The \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length increases compared to free \(\mathrm{CO}\).
    • The \(\mathrm{C-O}\) stretching frequency (\(\nu_{\mathrm{CO}}\)) decreases.
  • Influence of Metal Charge (Isoelectronic Carbonyls):
    Consider the isoelectronic series \([\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^-\), \([\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{CO})_6]\), and \([\mathrm{Mn}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^+\):
    • The negatively charged complex \([\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^-\) possesses the highest electron density on the metal center \(\rightarrow\) exhibits the greatest \(\pi\)-backbonding \(\rightarrow\) has the strongest \(\mathrm{M-C}\) bond and the weakest / longest \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond (lowest \(\nu_{\mathrm{CO}}\))\(\text{.}\)

    • • The positively charged complex \([\mathrm{Mn}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^+\) holds its electrons tightly \(\rightarrow\) displays the least \(\pi\)-backbonding \(\rightarrow\) has the weakest \(\mathrm{M-C}\) bond and the strongest / shortest \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond (highest \(\nu_{\mathrm{CO}}\))\(\text{.}\)

9. Importance and Practical Applications of Coordination Compounds

Coordination compounds play foundational roles across chemical analysis, extractive metallurgy, biological life processes, industrial catalysis, and pharmacology.

1. Analytical Chemistry & Water Hardness

  • Complexometric Titrations: Estimation of total water hardness caused by dissolved \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\) ions is carried out by volumetric titration with standard disodium EDTA (\(\mathrm{Na_2EDTA}\)). Selective estimation is achievable because the stability constant of \([\mathrm{Ca}(\mathrm{EDTA})]^{2-}\) is significantly greater than that of \([\mathrm{Mg}(\mathrm{EDTA})]^{2-}\).
  • Qualitative Analysis Reagents:
    • Dimethylglyoxime (DMG): Used for the specific detection and gravimetric estimation of nickel(II) ion in ammoniacal medium, forming a characteristic scarlet red precipitate of bis(dimethylglyoximato)nickel(II), stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonds.
    • Other analytical chelating reagents include \(\alpha\)-nitroso-\(\beta\)-naphthol (for cobalt) and cupron (for copper).

2. Extractive Metallurgy and Metal Refining

  • Cyanide Extraction of Gold and Silver (Mac-Arthur Forrest Process): Native gold and silver are selectively leached from their ores by aerated dilute aqueous potassium/sodium cyanide: \[ 4\,\mathrm{Au} + 8\,\mathrm{CN^-} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} + \mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow 4\,[\mathrm{Au}(\mathrm{CN})_2]^- + 4\,\mathrm{OH^-} \] The pure metal is subsequently displaced from the soluble dicyanidoaurate(I) complex by reduction with zinc dust: \[ 2\,[\mathrm{Au}(\mathrm{CN})_2]^- + \mathrm{Zn} \rightarrow [\mathrm{Zn}(\mathrm{CN})_4]^{2-} + 2\,\mathrm{Au}\downarrow \]
  • Mond Process for Nickel Refining: Impure nickel reacts reversibly with carbon monoxide at \(330\text{--}350\,\mathrm{K}\) to yield volatile tetracarbonylnickel(0), which on heating to \(450\text{--}470\,\mathrm{K}\) decomposes into pure nickel: \[ \mathrm{Ni} + 4\,\mathrm{CO} \xrightarrow{330-350\,\mathrm{K}} [\mathrm{Ni}(\mathrm{CO})_4] \xrightarrow{450-470\,\mathrm{K}} \mathrm{Ni}\ (\text{pure}) + 4\,\mathrm{CO} \]

3. Biological Systems

  • Chlorophyll: The green photosynthetic pigment of plants is a coordination complex of magnesium (\(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\)) with a substituted porphyrin ring.
  • Haemoglobin: The red oxygen-transport pigment in red blood cells is a coordination complex of iron (\(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\)) with a porphyrin ring (heme).
  • Vitamin \(\mathrm{B_{12}}\) (Cyanocobalamin): The anti-pernicious anaemia factor is a coordination complex of cobalt (\(\mathrm{Co^{3+}}\)) with a corrin ring.
  • Metalloenzymes: Enzymes such as carbonic anhydrase (catalyzing \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) hydration) and carboxypeptidase A (hydrolyzing peptide bonds) contain coordinated zinc (\(\mathrm{Zn^{2+}}\)) ions at their active sites.

4. Industrial Catalysis & Chemical Technology

  • Wilkinson’s Catalyst: Chloridotris(triphenylphosphine)rhodium(I), \([(\mathrm{Ph_3P})_3\mathrm{RhCl}]\), is a square planar complex utilized as a homogeneous catalyst for the selective hydrogenation of alkenes at ambient temperature and pressure.
  • Electroplating: Articles are electroplated with silver or gold much more smoothly, adherent, and uniformly from baths containing \([\mathrm{Ag}(\mathrm{CN})_2]^-\) or \([\mathrm{Au}(\mathrm{CN})_2]^-\) than from solutions containing uncomplexed simple metal ions, because the low equilibrium concentration of free metal ions prevents rapid, uneven dendritic crystallization.
  • Black & White Photography: The exposed and developed film is fixed by washing with hypo solution (aqueous sodium thiosulphate, \(\mathrm{Na_2S_2O_3}\)), which dissolves unexposed, insoluble silver bromide by forming the soluble complex ion: \[ \mathrm{AgBr} + 2\,\mathrm{S_2O_3^{2-}} \rightarrow [\mathrm{Ag}(\mathrm{S_2O_3})_2]^{3-} + \mathrm{Br^-} \]

5. Medicinal Chemistry & Chelation Therapy

  • Removal of Toxic Metals:
    • Lead Poisoning: Chelation therapy with calcium disodium EDTA (\(\mathrm{CaNa_2EDTA}\)) removes toxic lead ions as stable, soluble, non-toxic lead-EDTA chelates excreted in urine.
    • Copper Toxicity (Wilson’s Disease): Excess copper is chelated and eliminated using D-penicillamine.
    • Iron Overload: Excess iron is selectively scavenged by the hexadentate siderophore chelator desferrioxime B.
  • Antitumor Chemotherapy: Platinum coordination complexes effectively inhibit the proliferation of malignant tumors. Cisplatin, cis-\([\mathrm{PtCl}_2(\mathrm{NH}_3)_2]\), coordinates to nuclear DNA, cross-linking adjacent guanine bases to prevent DNA replication in rapidly dividing cancer cells. (The trans-isomer is pharmacologically inactive).

10. Comprehensive Worked Examples (Step-by-Step Problem Solving)

Worked Example 1: Werner’s Formulations & Precipitation Stoichiometry

Problem: On treating \(0.01\ \mathrm{mol}\) of each of the following coordination compounds with excess cold aqueous \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\), the masses of \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) precipitated were determined as follows:

  • Compound A (\(\mathrm{PdCl_2 \cdot 4NH_3}\)): Precipitates \(2.87\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{AgCl}\).
  • Compound B (\(\mathrm{PtCl_4 \cdot 2HCl}\)): Precipitates \(0.00\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{AgCl}\).
  • Compound C (\(\mathrm{CoCl_3 \cdot 4NH_3}\)): Precipitates \(1.435\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{AgCl}\).

Deduce the coordination formula, secondary valence of the metal, and the number of ions formed in solution for each compound. (Molar mass of \(\mathrm{AgCl} = 143.5\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)).

Solution Strategy:

Worked Example 6: Synergic Bonding and Carbon–Oxygen Bond Order

Problem: Arrange the following isoelectronic transition metal hexacarbonyl complexes in order of increasing carbon–oxygen (\(\mathrm{C-O}\)) bond length: \[ [\mathrm{Mn}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^+,\quad [\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{CO})_6],\quad [\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^- \] Provide the scientific rationale based on the synergic bonding model.

Solution:

  1. All three complexes are octahedral, homoleptic, and isoelectronic (\(d^6\) transition metal centers).
  2. In transition metal carbonyls, the \(\mathrm{M \rightarrow CO}\) \(\pi\)-backbonding transfers electron density from filled metal \(d\)-orbitals into the antibonding \(\pi^*\) molecular orbitals of carbon monoxide.
  3. As the negative charge on the complex increases (or positive charge decreases): \[ [\mathrm{Mn}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^+ < [\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{CO})_6] < [\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^- \] the electron density on the central metal ion increases markedly.
  4. Greater electron density on \(\mathrm{V^-}\) results in maximum \(\pi\)-back-donation into the \(\pi^*\) orbitals of \(\mathrm{CO}\), which lowers the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond order to the greatest extent and causes the greatest lengthening of the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond.
  5. Conversely, \(\mathrm{Mn^+}\) holds its \(d\)-electrons tightly, exhibiting the least \(\pi\)-back-donation; hence the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond in \([\mathrm{Mn}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^+\) remains closest to a formal triple bond (shortest bond length).

Order of Increasing \(\mathrm{C-O}\) Bond Length: \[ \mathbf{[\mathrm{Mn}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^+ < [\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{CO})_6] < [\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^-} \] Corresponding Order of \(\mathrm{C-O}\) Bond Strength / Stretching Frequency (\(\nu_{\mathrm{CO}}\)): \[ [\mathrm{V}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^- < [\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{CO})_6] < [\mathrm{Mn}(\mathrm{CO})_6]^+ \]

12. Common NEET Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

Frequent Traps & How to Avoid Them

  • Double Counting in Coordination Number: Remember that coordination number equals the number of \(\sigma\)-bonds, not the number of ligands. Always multiply the number of ligands by their denticity (e.g., in \([\mathrm{Fe}(\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{O}_4)_3]^{3-}\), \(\mathrm{CN} = 3 \times 2 = 6\), not 3!).
  • IUPAC Multipliers for Anions: In naming ionic coordination salts, never include numerical prefixes for counter ions (e.g., \(\mathrm{K_3[Fe(CN)_6]}\) is potassium hexacyanidoferrate(III), never tripotassium).
  • Ambidentate vs. Chelating: Ambidentate ligands (e.g., \(\mathrm{NO_2^-}\), \(\mathrm{SCN^-}\)) have two donor atoms but only coordinate through one at a time to a single metal. They do not form chelate rings; they produce linkage isomers.
  • Optical Activity of Square Planar Complexes: Square planar complexes are planar and always possess a molecular plane of symmetry; therefore, they never exhibit optical activity.
  • Tetrahedral Isomerism: Tetrahedral complexes can never exhibit geometrical isomerism because all four positions are mutually adjacent (\(109.5^\circ\)).
  • trans-Isomers with Chelates: For \([\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{AA})_2\mathrm{B}_2]\) (e.g., \([\mathrm{CoCl}_2(\mathrm{en})_2]^+\)), only the cis-isomer is chiral and optically active. The trans-isomer has a plane of symmetry and is optically inactive.
  • Tetrahedral Spin States: Never assign a low-spin configuration to a tetrahedral complex. Because \(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{4}{9}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} < \mathrm{P}\), tetrahedral complexes are always high-spin.
  • Zero CFSE for High-Spin \(d^5\): For \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}^3 e_{\mathrm{g}}^2\), \(\mathrm{CFSE} = \left[-0.4(3) + 0.6(2)\right]\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} = 0\).
  • Synergic Backbonding & C-O Bond: Increased \(\pi\)-backbonding strengthens the \(\mathrm{M-C}\) bond but weakens the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond, increasing the \(\mathrm{C-O}\) bond length and lowering its vibrational frequency (\(\nu_{\mathrm{CO}}\)).

NEET High-Yield Quick Revision Formula Sheet

  • Spin-Only Magnetic Moment: \(\mu_{\mathrm{s}} = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \mathrm{BM}\) (\(n = \text{unpaired electrons}\)).
  • Tetrahedral vs. Octahedral Splitting: \(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{4}{9}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\).
  • Octahedral CFSE: \(\mathrm{CFSE}_{\mathrm{oct}} = \left[-0.4\,n(t_{2\mathrm{g}}) + 0.6\,n(e_{\mathrm{g}})\right]\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} + m\mathrm{P}\).
  • Tetrahedral CFSE: \(\mathrm{CFSE}_{\mathrm{tet}} = \left[-0.6\,n(e) + 0.4\,n(t_2)\right]\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\).
  • Spectrochemical Series: \(\mathrm{I^- < Br^- < SCN^- < Cl^- < S^{2-} < F^- < OH^- < C_2O_4^{2-} < H_2O < NCS^- < edta^{4-} < NH_3 < en < CN^- < CO}\).

13. Final Conceptual Summary

Coordination chemistry bridges fundamental inorganic bonding and modern materials/biochemical sciences. Alfred Werner established that transition metals satisfy both an ionisable primary valence (oxidation state) and a directional, non-ionisable secondary valence (coordination number). Ligands donate electron pairs into hybrid metal orbitals (VBT) or generate an electrostatic crystal field that lifts the degeneracy of the metal \(d\)-orbitals (CFT), splitting them into \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) and \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\) sets in octahedral fields, or inverted \(e\) and \(t_2\) sets in tetrahedral fields (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{4}{9}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)). The magnitude of splitting determines high-spin vs. low-spin electronic configurations, magnetic moments, and electronic absorption spectra responsible for the complementary colors of complexes and gemstones. In metal carbonyls, a unique synergic cycle of \(\sigma\)-donation and \(\pi\)-backbonding stabilizes zero-valent complexes. Across nature and industry, coordination compounds govern vital processes ranging from photosynthesis (chlorophyll) and oxygen transport (haemoglobin) to industrial alkene hydrogenation (Wilkinson’s catalyst) and targeted cancer chemotherapy (cisplatin).
