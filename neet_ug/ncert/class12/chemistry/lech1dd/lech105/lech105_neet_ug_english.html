NEET UG Inorganic Chemistry

Coordination Compounds

Comprehensive Concept Synthesis, Bonding Models, Isomerism & Exam-Oriented Practice

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Overview

Coordination compounds—also known as complex compounds—form the structural foundation of bio-inorganic systems, industrial catalysis, analytical separations, and modern metallurgy. For the NEET UG examination, this chapter is consistently high-yield, testing conceptual precision in IUPAC nomenclature, counting stereoisomers, predicting hybridisation and magnetic moments, applying Crystal Field Theory (CFT), and identifying real-world applications.

Target Competencies
  • Distinguish between double salts and coordination complexes using solution dissociation behaviour.
  • Apply Werner's postulates to deduce primary and secondary valencies from precipitation and conductivity data.
  • Identify ligand denticity, ambidentate behaviour, coordination number, oxidation state, and homoleptic/heteroleptic nature.
  • Write unambiguous IUPAC names and chemical formulas for mononuclear coordination entities.
  • Classify and count structural isomers and stereoisomers (geometrical and optical) in 4- and 6-coordinate complexes.
  • Predict geometry, spin state, magnetic moment, and colour using Valence Bond Theory (VBT) and Crystal Field Theory (CFT).
  • Explain synergic bonding in homoleptic metal carbonyls and recall key biological, medicinal, and industrial uses.

2. Addition Compounds & Werner's Coordination Theory

2.1 Double Salts vs. Coordination Complexes

When two or more stable stoichiometric salts crystallise together from an aqueous solution, they form addition compounds. Depending on their behaviour upon redissolving in water, they fall into two distinct classes:

Property Double Salt Coordination Complex
Dissociation in Water Dissociates completely into all constituent simple ions. Retains the complex ion intact; only counter ions dissociate.
Identity in Solution Loses its solid-state identity; gives positive tests for every constituent ion. Retains complex-ion identity; ligands inside brackets do not respond to simple-ion tests.
Representative Examples Carnallite (\(\mathrm{KCl \cdot MgCl_2 \cdot 6H_2O}\)), Mohr's salt (\(\mathrm{FeSO_4 \cdot (NH_4)_2SO_4 \cdot 6H_2O}\)), Potash alum (\(\mathrm{KAl(SO_4)_2 \cdot 12H_2O}\)) Potassium ferrocyanide (\(\mathrm{K_4[Fe(CN)_6]}\)), Tetramminecopper(II) sulphate (\(\mathrm{[Cu(NH_3)_4]SO_4}\))

2.2 Postulates of Werner's Theory

Alfred Werner (1898) explained the structure and reactivity of transition-metal complexes by proposing that a central metal ion exhibits two fundamentally different types of valency:

  1. Primary Valency (Ionisable): Satisfied exclusively by negative ions (anions), corresponds to the oxidation state of the metal, is non-directional, and is written outside the coordination sphere as counter ions when ionisable.
  2. Secondary Valency (Non-ionisable): Satisfied by neutral molecules or negative ions (ligands), corresponds to the fixed coordination number of the metal, and is strictly directional in space, giving rise to definite polyhedral shapes (octahedral, tetrahedral, square planar).
Interpreting AgNO₃ Precipitation & Conductivity Data

For a cobalt(III) chloride–ammonia adduct with empirical composition \(\mathrm{CoCl_3 \cdot \mathit{x}NH_3}\), cobalt maintains a fixed secondary valency (coordination number) of 6. Only chloride ions outside the square brackets precipitate immediately as \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) with cold aqueous \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\):

  • \(\mathrm{CoCl_3 \cdot 6NH_3} \equiv \mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_6]Cl_3}\): yields 3 mol \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) per mol complex; 4 total ions (1:3 electrolyte).
  • \(\mathrm{CoCl_3 \cdot 5NH_3} \equiv \mathrm{[CoCl(NH_3)_5]Cl_2}\): yields 2 mol \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) per mol complex; 3 total ions (1:2 electrolyte).
  • \(\mathrm{CoCl_3 \cdot 4NH_3} \equiv \mathrm{[CoCl_2(NH_3)_4]Cl}\): yields 1 mol \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) per mol complex; 2 total ions (1:1 electrolyte); exists as green (trans) and violet (cis) isomers.

3. Core Terminology & Structural Anatomy

3.1 Key Components of a Complex

  • Coordination Entity: A central metal atom or ion bonded to a fixed number of surrounding ions or molecules, e.g., \(\mathrm{[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}}\) or \(\mathrm{[Ni(CO)_4]}\).
  • Central Atom/Ion: The metal species that accepts electron pairs from surrounding groups; it acts as a Lewis acid.
  • Coordination Sphere & Counter Ions: The central atom and its directly attached ligands are enclosed inside square brackets \([\,\dots\,]\) as the non-ionisable coordination sphere. Ionisable ions outside the brackets are counter ions (e.g., \(\mathrm{K^+}\) in \(\mathrm{K_3[Fe(C_2O_4)_3]}\)).
  • Coordination Number (CN): The total number of ligand donor atoms forming sigma (\(\sigma\)) bonds directly with the central metal atom/ion. Any \(\pi\)-bonds present between metal and ligand are not counted toward CN.
  • Oxidation Number: The hypothetical charge left on the central atom if all ligands are removed along with their shared electron pairs. For \(\mathrm{[Cu(CN)_4]^{3-}}\), \(x + 4(-1) = -3 \Rightarrow x = +1\), written as \(\mathrm{Cu(I)}\).
  • Homoleptic vs. Heteroleptic: Complexes containing only one type of donor group are homoleptic (e.g., \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}}\)), whereas those with more than one type of donor group are heteroleptic (e.g., \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_4Cl_2]^+}\)).

3.2 Classification of Ligands

Ligands act as Lewis bases by donating one or more electron pairs to the central metal:

  • Unidentate (Monodentate): Bind through a single donor atom, e.g., \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{CO}\), \(\mathrm{CN^-}\).
  • Didentate (Bidentate): Bind simultaneously through two donor atoms, e.g., ethane-1,2-diamine (\(\mathrm{en}\), \(\mathrm{H_2NCH_2CH_2NH_2}\)) via two N atoms, and oxalate (\(\mathrm{ox^{2-}}\), \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\)) via two O atoms.
  • Polydentate: Possess multiple donor sites. A prime example is ethylenediaminetetraacetate (\(\mathrm{EDTA^{4-}}\)), a hexadentate ligand binding through 2 N atoms and 4 carboxylate O atoms.
  • Chelate Ligands & Chelate Effect: When a di- or polydentate ligand uses two or more donor atoms simultaneously to bind a single metal ion, it forms a ring structure called a chelate. Chelate complexes are thermodynamically much more stable than comparable complexes with unidentate ligands (the chelate effect).
  • Ambidentate Ligands: Unidentate ligands possessing two different donor atoms, capable of coordinating through either atom (one at a time). Examples: \(\mathrm{NO_2^-}\) (via N in nitrito-N, \(-\mathrm{NO_2}\), or via O in nitrito-O, \(-\mathrm{ONO}\)) and \(\mathrm{SCN^-}\) (via S in thiocyanato-S, \(-\mathrm{SCN}\), or via N in thiocyanato-N, \(-\mathrm{NCS}\)).

4. IUPAC Nomenclature & Formula Writing

4.1 Rules for Writing Formulas

  1. Write the central metal symbol first.
  2. List all ligands in alphabetical order of their chemical formulas/abbreviations (irrespective of their charge).
  3. Enclose polyatomic ligands (e.g., \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{C_2O_4}\)) and abbreviated ligands (e.g., \(\mathrm{en}\)) in parentheses when more than one is present or for clarity.
  4. Enclose the entire coordination entity in square brackets with no spaces inside.
  5. Indicate the net charge of a complex ion as a right superscript outside the bracket with the magnitude before the sign (e.g., \(\mathrm{[Co(CN)_6]^{3-}}\)).

4.2 Rules for Naming Coordination Compounds

  1. Name the cation first, followed by the anion (whether complex or simple).
  2. Within the coordination sphere, name ligands in alphabetical order (ignoring numerical prefixes like di-, tri-) before the central metal.
  3. Anionic ligands end in -ido (or -o): e.g., \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) = chlorido, \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) = cyanido, \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) = hydroxido, \(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\) = oxalato, \(\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}\) = carbonato, \(\mathrm{NO_2^-}\) = nitrito-N (or nitrito-O for \(-\mathrm{ONO}\)).
  4. Neutral ligands retain their usual names except: \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) = aqua, \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) = ammine (note double 'm'), \(\mathrm{CO}\) = carbonyl, \(\mathrm{NO}\) = nitrosyl.
  5. Use prefixes mono-, di-, tri-, tetra- for simple ligands. If the ligand name itself contains a numerical prefix (e.g., ethane-1,2-diamine, triphenylphosphine), use bis-, tris-, tetrakis- and place the ligand name in parentheses.
  6. For a cationic or neutral complex, use the standard English name of the metal. For an anionic complex, add the suffix -ate to the metal name (using Latin roots where conventional: ferrate for Fe, cuprate for Cu, argentate for Ag, aurate for Au, plumbate for Pb, stannate for Sn).
  7. Follow the metal name immediately with its oxidation state in Roman numerals inside parentheses, e.g., \(\mathrm{(III)}\), \(\mathrm{(0)}\).

5. Isomerism in Coordination Compounds

Compounds sharing the same molecular formula but differing in atom connectivity or spatial orientation exhibit isomerism. Broadly, isomerism is divided into structural isomerism (different bonds) and stereoisomerism (same bonds, different 3D arrangement).

5.1 Structural Isomerism

  • Ionisation Isomerism: Arises when a counter ion is itself a potential ligand and exchanges places with a ligand inside the coordination sphere. These isomers yield different ions in solution. Example: \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_5Br]SO_4}\) (gives white \(\mathrm{BaSO_4}\) precipitate with \(\mathrm{BaCl_2}\)) and \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_5(SO_4)]Br}\) (gives pale yellow \(\mathrm{AgBr}\) precipitate with \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\)).
  • Solvate (Hydrate) Isomerism: A special case of ionisation isomerism where solvent (water) molecules differ in whether they are coordinated to the metal or present as free lattice water. Example: \(\mathrm{[Cr(H_2O)_6]Cl_3}\) (violet, 3 ionisable \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\)) vs. \(\mathrm{[Cr(H_2O)_5Cl]Cl_2 \cdot H_2O}\) (grey-green, 2 ionisable \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\)).
  • Linkage Isomerism: Occurs in complexes containing an ambidentate ligand that can bind through two different donor atoms. Example: \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_5(NO_2)]Cl_2}\) (yellow, N-bound) vs. \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_5(ONO)]Cl_2}\) (red, O-bound).
  • Coordination Isomerism: Occurs in salts where both cation and anion are complex ions; ligands interchange between the two different metal centres. Example: \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_6][Cr(CN)_6]}\) and \(\mathrm{[Cr(NH_3)_6][Co(CN)_6]}\).

5.2 Stereoisomerism: Geometrical & Optical

  • Tetrahedral Complexes (\(\mathrm{CN = 4}\)): Do not show geometrical isomerism because all four ligand positions are equidistant and adjacent to one another. (Optical isomerism is possible only if all four ligands are different or unsymmetrical chelates are present.)
  • Square Planar Complexes (\(\mathrm{CN = 4}\)):
    • \(\mathrm{[Ma_2b_2]}\) (e.g., \(\mathrm{[Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2]}\)): 2 isomers — cis (same ligands adjacent, 90° apart) and trans (same ligands opposite, 180° apart).
    • \(\mathrm{[Mabcd]}\) (four different unidentate ligands): 3 geometrical isomers (fix one ligand and place the other three opposite to it one by one).
  • Octahedral Complexes (\(\mathrm{CN = 6}\)):
    • \(\mathrm{[Ma_4b_2]}\) and \(\mathrm{[Ma_2(L\text{–}L)_2]}\): 2 geometrical isomers (cis and trans).
    • \(\mathrm{[Ma_3b_3]}\): 2 geometrical isomers — facial (fac) (three identical ligands occupy corners of one triangular face, all 90° to each other) and meridional (mer) (three identical ligands lie along a meridian, with one pair 180° apart).
  • Optical Isomerism: Exhibited by chiral complexes lacking a plane or centre of symmetry, forming non-superimposable mirror images called enantiomers (dextro / d and laevo / l). Common in octahedral chelate complexes:
    • \(\mathrm{[M(en)_3]^{3+}}\) is optically active (exists as d and l pair).
    • \(\mathrm{\textit{cis}-[PtCl_2(en)_2]^{2+}}\) is chiral and optically active, whereas \(\mathrm{\textit{trans}-[PtCl_2(en)_2]^{2+}}\) possesses a plane of symmetry and is optically inactive.

6. Bonding Models, Magnetism & Colour

6.1 Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

According to VBT, the central metal ion hybridises a set of vacant \((n-1)d\), \(ns\), \(np\), or \(nd\) orbitals to match the coordination number and geometry, then overlaps them with filled ligand orbitals to form coordinate covalent bonds.

CN Hybridisation Geometry Key Examples & Spin Type
4 \(sp^3\) Tetrahedral \(\mathrm{[NiCl_4]^{2-}}\) (paramagnetic, 2 unpaired \(e^-\)), \(\mathrm{[Ni(CO)_4]}\) (diamagnetic)
4 \(dsp^2\) Square planar \(\mathrm{[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}}\), \(\mathrm{[PtCl_4]^{2-}}\) (diamagnetic)
5 \(sp^3d\) Trigonal bipyramidal \(\mathrm{[Fe(CO)_5]}\) (diamagnetic)
6 \(d^2sp^3\) Octahedral (inner orbital) \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}}\), \(\mathrm{[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}}\) (low spin / spin-paired)
6 \(sp^3d^2\) Octahedral (outer orbital) \(\mathrm{[CoF_6]^{3-}}\), \(\mathrm{[FeF_6]^{3-}}\) (high spin / spin-free)
Spin-Only Magnetic Moment: \[\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mathrm{BM}\] where \(n\) is the number of unpaired electrons and \(\mathrm{BM}\) denotes Bohr Magneton.

Limitations of VBT: It relies on ad-hoc assumptions, cannot quantitatively predict magnetic moments or thermodynamic/kinetic stabilities, does not explain the colour of complexes, and fails to distinguish strong-field from weak-field ligands.

6.2 Crystal Field Theory (CFT)

CFT treats metal–ligand bonding as purely electrostatic, modelling anionic ligands as point charges and neutral ligands as point dipoles. In an isolated gaseous metal ion, all five \(d\) orbitals are degenerate (equal in energy). In an asymmetric ligand field, this degeneracy is lifted:

  • Octahedral Field (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)): Six ligands approach along the \(\pm x, \pm y, \pm z\) axes. Orbitals pointing directly at ligands (\(d_{x^2-y^2}, d_{z^2}\), called the \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\) set) experience greater repulsion and rise in energy by \(+\frac{3}{5}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) (\(+0.6\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)). Orbitals directed between the axes (\(d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{xz}\), called the \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) set) are lowered by \(-\frac{2}{5}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) (\(-0.4\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)).
  • Tetrahedral Field (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\)): Four ligands approach between the axes, inverting the splitting pattern: the doubly degenerate \(e\) set lies lower by \(-\frac{3}{5}\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\) and the triply degenerate \(t_2\) set lies higher by \(+\frac{2}{5}\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\). (The 'g' subscript is omitted because a tetrahedron lacks a centre of symmetry.) For identical metal and ligands: \[\Delta_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{4}{9}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\] Because \(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}}\) is small, \(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}} < P\) almost always holds, so tetrahedral complexes are nearly always high spin.

6.3 Spectrochemical Series & Spin Configurations

Experimentally determined from light absorption spectra, ligands are ranked in order of increasing crystal field splitting strength (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)):

\[\mathrm{I^- < Br^- < SCN^- < Cl^- < S^{2-} < F^- < OH^- < C_2O_4^{2-} < H_2O < NCS^- < EDTA^{4-} < NH_3 < en < CN^- < CO}\]

For octahedral \(d^4\) to \(d^7\) configurations, electron distribution depends on the competition between \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) and pairing energy \(P\):

  • Weak-field ligands (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} < P\)): Electrons occupy \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\) before pairing in \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) → high-spin complexes (e.g., \(d^4 \rightarrow t_{2\mathrm{g}}^3 e_{\mathrm{g}}^1\)).
  • Strong-field ligands (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} > P\)): Electrons pair up in \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) before entering \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\) → low-spin complexes (e.g., \(d^4 \rightarrow t_{2\mathrm{g}}^4 e_{\mathrm{g}}^0\)).

6.4 Colour in Coordination Compounds

When a transition-metal complex absorbs visible light of energy \(h\nu = \Delta\), an electron is excited from a lower-energy \(d\) orbital to a higher-energy \(d\) orbital (\(d\text{–}d\) transition). The observed colour is the complementary colour of the absorbed wavelength. For example, \(\mathrm{[Ti(H_2O)_6]^{3+}}\) (\(3d^1\)) absorbs blue-green light (~498 nm) for the \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}^1 e_{\mathrm{g}}^0 \rightarrow t_{2\mathrm{g}}^0 e_{\mathrm{g}}^1\) transition and appears violet. Stronger-field ligands increase \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) and shift absorption to shorter wavelengths (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} = hc/\lambda\)). Complexes with \(d^0\) or \(d^{10}\) configurations (or anhydrous salts lacking ligands) cannot undergo \(d\text{–}d\) transitions and are typically colourless.

Limitations of CFT: Treating ligands purely as point charges incorrectly predicts that anionic ligands should cause the largest splitting (whereas in reality they lie at the weak end of the spectrochemical series), and CFT ignores covalent character in metal–ligand bonds.

6.5 Bonding in Metal Carbonyls

Homoleptic carbonyls such as \(\mathrm{[Ni(CO)_4]}\) (tetrahedral), \(\mathrm{[Fe(CO)_5]}\) (trigonal bipyramidal), \(\mathrm{[Cr(CO)_6]}\) (octahedral), \(\mathrm{[Mn_2(CO)_{10}]}\) (two square-pyramidal \(\mathrm{Mn(CO)_5}\) units linked by a \(\mathrm{Mn\text{–}Mn}\) bond), and \(\mathrm{[Co_2(CO)_8]}\) (one \(\mathrm{Co\text{–}Co}\) bond plus two bridging \(\mathrm{CO}\) groups) exhibit synergic bonding:

  1. \(\sigma\)-bond: Donation of a lone pair from the carbonyl carbon into a vacant metal orbital (\(\mathrm{M \leftarrow C}\)).
  2. \(\pi\)-backbond: Donation of an electron pair from a filled metal \(d\) orbital into the vacant antibonding \(\pi^*\) orbital of \(\mathrm{CO}\) (\(\mathrm{M \rightarrow C}\)).

This mutual reinforcement strengthens the \(\mathrm{M\text{–}C}\) bond while slightly weakening the \(\mathrm{C\equiv O}\) bond.

8. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1 — Werner's Theory & Electrolyte Type (Hypothetical Complex)

Problem: A newly synthesised chromium(III) complex has the empirical composition \(\mathrm{CrCl_3 \cdot 5H_2O \cdot NH_3}\) (assuming a fixed coordination number of 6 for \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\)). When 1 mol of this compound is treated with excess cold \(\mathrm{AgNO_3}\), 2 mol of \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) precipitate immediately. Write its coordination formula, identify the electrolyte type, and state its IUPAC name.

Solution:

  1. Precipitation of 2 mol \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) per mole of complex indicates 2 ionisable \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) counter ions outside the coordination sphere.
  2. Since \(\mathrm{Cr^{3+}}\) has \(\mathrm{CN = 6}\), the coordination sphere must contain 1 \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), 1 \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), and 4 \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) molecules (leaving 1 \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) as lattice water of crystallisation).
  3. Formula: \(\mathrm{[Cr(H_2O)_4(NH_3)Cl]Cl_2 \cdot H_2O}\) (ligands listed alphabetically by formula: \(\mathrm{Cl}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) → \(\mathrm{[CrCl(H_2O)_4(NH_3)]Cl_2 \cdot H_2O}\)).
  4. Electrolyte type: Dissociates into 1 complex cation and 2 \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) anions → 1:2 electrolyte (3 ions total).
  5. IUPAC Name: Tetraammine? No, there is 1 \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and 4 \(\mathrm{H_2O}\): ammine­tetraaquachloridochromium(III) chloride monohydrate.
Worked Example 2 — IUPAC Nomenclature & Oxidation State

Problem: Write the IUPAC name of \(\mathrm{K_2[Ni(CN)_2(C_2O_4)]}\) and the chemical formula of sodium dicyanidoaurate(I).

Solution:

  • For \(\mathrm{K_2[Ni(CN)_2(C_2O_4)]}\): Let the oxidation state of Ni be \(x\). With two \(\mathrm{K^+}\) counter ions, the complex anion has charge \(-2\). Thus, \(x + 2(-1) + (-2) = -2 \Rightarrow x = +2\). Alphabetical ligands: cyanido before oxalato. Since the complex is anionic, Ni becomes nickelate(II). Name: potassium dicyanidooxalatonickelate(II).
  • For sodium dicyanidoaurate(I): Gold (Au) is in \(+1\) state with two \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) ligands, giving the complex anion \(\mathrm{[Au(CN)_2]^-}\). Balancing with one \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) gives \(\mathrm{Na[Au(CN)_2]}\).
Worked Example 3 — Counting Stereoisomers

Problem: How many total stereoisomers (geometrical + optical) exist for the hypothetical octahedral ion \(\mathrm{[CoBr_2(en)_2]^+}\)? Which of them are optically active?

Solution:

  1. The complex is of the type \(\mathrm{[MX_2(L\text{–}L)_2]}\), which exhibits two geometrical isomers: cis and trans.
  2. The trans isomer has a plane of symmetry passing through the two \(\mathrm{en}\) rings and is therefore optically inactive (1 stereoisomer).
  3. The cis isomer lacks any plane or centre of symmetry and exists as a pair of non-superimposable mirror images (d and l enantiomers).
  4. Total stereoisomers: \(1\text{ (trans)} + 2\text{ (cis }d, l\text{)} = \mathbf{3}\). Only the cis enantiomers are optically active.
Worked Example 4 — CFT Configuration, Magnetic Moment & Absorption

Problem: Compare the \(t_{2\mathrm{g}} / e_{\mathrm{g}}\) electronic configurations and spin-only magnetic moments of \(\mathrm{[Fe(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}}\). Which complex absorbs light of shorter wavelength?

Solution:

  • In both complexes, iron is \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) (\(3d^6\)).
  • \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) is a weak-field ligand (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} < P\)): high-spin configuration \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}^4 e_{\mathrm{g}}^2\), with \(n = 4\) unpaired electrons. Magnetic moment: \(\mu = \sqrt{4(4+2)} = \sqrt{24} \approx 4.90\,\mathrm{BM}\).
  • \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) is a strong-field ligand (\(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} > P\)): low-spin configuration \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}^6 e_{\mathrm{g}}^0\), with \(n = 0\) unpaired electrons. Magnetic moment: \(\mu = 0\,\mathrm{BM}\) (diamagnetic).
  • Since \(\mathrm{CN^-}\) produces a much larger \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) than \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} = hc/\lambda\), \(\mathrm{[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}}\) absorbs light of shorter wavelength.

10. Answer Key & Worked Solutions

Q1: (A) 3 mol \(\mathrm{AgCl}\) implies 3 ionisable \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) counter ions. The remaining 1 \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) and 5 \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) molecules are inside the coordination sphere: \(\mathrm{[PtCl(NH_3)_5]Cl_3}\). Thus, secondary valency (CN) = 6, and it dissociates into 1 complex cation + 3 \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) = 4 ions.
Q2: (D) \(\mathrm{SCN^-}\) is an ambidentate unidentate ligand—it can donate through either S or N, but uses only one donor atom at a time (denticity = 1).
Q3: (A) Alphabetical order of ligands: ammine, aqua, bromido. Cobalt oxidation state: \(x + 0 + 0 + (-1) = +2 \Rightarrow x = +3\). Since the complex is cationic, the metal is named cobalt(III), followed by counter anion sulphate.
Q4: (C) \(\mathrm{[Pt(NH_3)_4Cl_2]Br_2}\) and \(\mathrm{[Pt(NH_3)_4Br_2]Cl_2}\) exchange ligands (\(\mathrm{Cl^-}\) and \(\mathrm{Br^-}\)) between the coordination sphere and counter-ion position, producing different ions in solution. (A) shows solvate isomerism, (B) linkage isomerism, and (D) coordination isomerism.
Q5: (B) A square planar complex of the type \(\mathrm{[Mabcd]}\) with four different unidentate ligands has 3 geometrical isomers (obtained by fixing one ligand, say \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), and placing \(\mathrm{H_2O}\), \(\mathrm{Cl^-}\), or \(\mathrm{NO_2^-}\) trans to it).
Q6: (A) Both statements are correct. Because every pair of ligands in a regular tetrahedron subtends the same 109.5° angle, no distinct cis or trans positions exist.
Q7: (C) \(\mathrm{\textit{cis}-[CrCl_2(ox)_2]^{3-}}\) lacks any plane or centre of symmetry and forms a non-superimposable mirror-image pair (d and l). The trans isomers and \(\mathrm{\textit{fac}-[Co(NH_3)_3(NO_2)_3]}\) possess planes of symmetry and are achiral.
Q8: (A) In \(\mathrm{[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}}\) (\(\mathrm{Co^{3+}}\), \(3d^6\)), \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) pairs the electrons to give \(d^2sp^3\) (inner-orbital, diamagnetic). In \(\mathrm{[CoF_6]^{3-}}\), weak-field \(\mathrm{F^-}\) leaves 4 unpaired electrons, using \(4d\) orbitals for \(sp^3d^2\) (outer-orbital, paramagnetic).
Q9: (B) \(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = 5.92\,\mathrm{BM} \Rightarrow n = 5\) unpaired electrons, matching high-spin \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) (\(3d^5\)). With CN = 4 and no \(d\)-electron pairing, \(\mathrm{Mn^{2+}}\) undergoes \(sp^3\) hybridisation (tetrahedral).
Q10: (B) When \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}} > P\) in an octahedral field, all 5 electrons enter the lower \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}\) level before occupying \(e_{\mathrm{g}}\), giving \(t_{2\mathrm{g}}^5 e_{\mathrm{g}}^0\) with 1 unpaired electron.
Q11: (B) From the spectrochemical series, ligand field strength increases as \(\mathrm{H_2O < NH_3 < en}\), so \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) increases in the same order. Since absorbed wavelength \(\lambda \propto 1/\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\), the wavelength increases in the reverse order: \(\mathrm{[Ni(en)_3]^{2+} < [Ni(NH_3)_6]^{2+} < [Ni(H_2O)_6]^{2+}}\).
Q12: (A) In metal carbonyls, \(\mathrm{CO \rightarrow M}\) \(\sigma\)-donation and \(\mathrm{M \rightarrow CO}\) \(\pi\)-backbonding reinforce each other synergistically, explaining why the \(\mathrm{M\text{–}C}\) bond has both \(\sigma\) and \(\pi\) character.
Q13: (A) Wilkinson's catalyst \(\mathrm{[(Ph_3P)_3RhCl]}\) hydrogenates alkenes (q); cis-platin is an anti-tumour agent (s); \(\mathrm{Na_2EDTA}\) treats lead poisoning (p); \(\mathrm{[Ag(S_2O_3)_2]^{3-}}\) forms when hypo fixes photographic film (r).
Q14: (C) In \(\mathrm{[Ni(CO)_4]}\), Ni is in oxidation state 0 (\(3d^8 4s^2\)). Strong-field \(\mathrm{CO}\) forces the \(4s\) electrons into the \(3d\) subshell, yielding \(3d^{10}\) (0 unpaired electrons, diamagnetic) and \(sp^3\) hybridisation (tetrahedral). (\(\mathrm{[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}}\) is diamagnetic but square planar.)

11. Common Pitfalls & NEET Revision Checklist

Frequent Exam Traps to Avoid
  • Formula vs. Name Alphabetisation: Both formulas and names list ligands alphabetically, not by charge! In formulas, ignore numerical prefixes; in names, also ignore di-/tri-/bis- when alphabetising.
  • Counting CN with Didentate Ligands: In \(\mathrm{[Fe(C_2O_4)_3]^{3-}}\), there are 3 oxalate ligands, each donating 2 atoms, so \(\mathrm{CN = 6}\) (not 3).
  • \(\mathrm{[Ni(CO)_4]}\) vs. \(\mathrm{[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}}\) vs. \(\mathrm{[NiCl_4]^{2-}}\): All three are 4-coordinate Ni complexes, but \(\mathrm{[Ni(CO)_4]}\) is tetrahedral & diamagnetic (\(\mathrm{Ni^0}\)), \(\mathrm{[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}}\) is square planar & diamagnetic (\(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}, dsp^2\)), and \(\mathrm{[NiCl_4]^{2-}}\) is tetrahedral & paramagnetic (\(\mathrm{Ni^{2+}}, sp^3\), 2 unpaired \(e^-\)).
  • \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) vs. \(\lambda\) Direction: Stronger ligand = larger \(\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\) = shorter wavelength (\(\lambda\)) absorbed.
Quick Pre-Exam Checklist:
  • Can I compute primary valency (oxidation state) and secondary valency (CN) in under 15 seconds?
  • Do I remember the spectrochemical series order and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{t}} = \frac{4}{9}\Delta_{\mathrm{o}}\)?
  • Can I distinguish cis/trans, fac/mer, and optical chirality in \(\mathrm{[M(en)_3]^{n+}}\) and \(\mathrm{[MX_2(en)_2]^{n+}}\)?
  • Have I memorised the metal centres in Chlorophyll (Mg), Haemoglobin (Fe), Vitamin B₁₂ (Co), cis-platin (Pt), and Wilkinson's catalyst (Rh)?

12. Concise Final Recap

Coordination compounds consist of a central Lewis-acid metal ion bonded to Lewis-base ligands inside a non-ionisable coordination sphere. Werner's theory distinguishes ionisable primary valencies (oxidation state) from directional secondary valencies (coordination number). Systematic IUPAC naming places the cation first, lists ligands alphabetically with -ido endings for anions, and appends -ate for anionic complexes. Structural and stereoisomerism (geometrical and optical) govern spatial diversity, while VBT and CFT connect ligand field strength to spin state, magnetic moment (\(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mathrm{BM}\)), and \(d\text{–}d\) electronic colour. Finally, synergic \(\sigma/\pi\) bonding stabilises metal carbonyls, underpinning vital roles in biology, medicine, catalysis, and metallurgy.
