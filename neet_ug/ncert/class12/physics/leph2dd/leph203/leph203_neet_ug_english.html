Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter

Exam Context: In NEET UG, this unit is a high-yield Modern Physics topic that consistently tests conceptual clarity on the photoelectric effect, graphical interpretation of stopping potential and photocurrent, photon energy-momentum relations, and de Broglie matter waves.

1. Learning Objectives & Overview

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Explain how cathode-ray and oil-drop experiments established the electron as a universal, quantised constituent of matter.
  • Define work function (\(\phi_0\)) and distinguish between thermionic, field, and photoelectric electron emission.
  • Interpret all experimental laws and characteristic graphs of the photoelectric effect.
  • Contrast classical wave theory predictions with Einstein's photon theory of light.
  • Apply Einstein's photoelectric equation to calculate stopping potential, threshold frequency, and maximum kinetic energy.
  • Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of subatomic and macroscopic particles and explain why wave nature is observable only at microscopic scales.

2. Discovery of the Electron & Electron Emission

2.1 Universality and Quantisation of Electric Charge

Late nineteenth-century experiments on low-pressure gas discharges (around \(0.001\,\mathrm{mm}\) of mercury) revealed glowing streams emerging from the negative electrode (cathode), known as cathode rays. Using mutually perpendicular electric and magnetic fields, J. J. Thomson measured the specific charge (charge-to-mass ratio, \(e/m\)) of these particles and found it to be \(1.76 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\), with speeds roughly \(0.1\) to \(0.2\) times the speed of light (\(c = 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)).

Crucially, \(e/m\) was identical regardless of the cathode metal or the gas inside the tube, and matched the particles emitted during ultraviolet irradiation or intense heating of metals. This proved that electrons are fundamental, universal building blocks of all matter. Later, in 1913, R. A. Millikan's oil-drop experiment demonstrated that electric charge always occurs in integer multiples of an elementary charge \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), establishing charge quantisation and enabling calculation of the electron mass (\(m_e \approx 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)).

2.2 Work Function and Types of Electron Emission

Inside a metal, conduction electrons move freely among positive metal ions, but they cannot spontaneously leave the surface. As soon as an electron attempts to exit, the metal surface becomes positively charged and pulls it back. To escape, an electron must receive at least a minimum amount of energy called the work function (\(\phi_0\)).

  • Unit of Work Function: Work function is commonly expressed in electron volts (\(\mathrm{eV}\)). One electron volt is the kinetic energy gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference of \(1\,\mathrm{V}\): \[1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\]
  • Material Dependence: \(\phi_0\) depends on the nature of the metal and the condition of its surface.

The minimum escape energy can be supplied through three primary physical mechanisms:

  1. Thermionic Emission: Heating the metal supplies sufficient thermal energy to free electrons so they overcome the surface barrier.
  2. Field Emission: Applying a very strong electric field (of the order of \(10^8\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\)) pulls electrons directly out of the metal surface.
  3. Photoelectric Emission: Illuminating a metal surface with electromagnetic radiation of suitably high frequency transfers energy to electrons, ejecting them as photoelectrons.

3. Experimental Study of the Photoelectric Effect

3.1 Historical Milestones & Apparatus

In 1887, Heinrich Hertz noticed that ultraviolet (UV) light falling on a spark-gap emitter enhanced high-voltage sparks. Wilhelm Hallwachs and Philipp Lenard subsequently investigated this effect in evacuated tubes: Hallwachs showed that UV illumination caused a negatively charged zinc plate to lose its negative charge, an uncharged plate to become positively charged, and a positively charged plate to become even more positive—confirming the ejection of negatively charged particles. Lenard showed that a photocurrent flows in an evacuated two-electrode tube only when light above a certain minimum threshold frequency strikes the emitter.

Alkali metals (lithium, sodium, potassium, caesium, rubidium) respond even to visible light, whereas metals such as zinc, cadmium, and magnesium require shorter-wavelength UV light.

3.2 Four Key Experimental Laws

By varying light intensity, light frequency (\(\nu\)), and collector potential (\(V\)) with the commutator and potential divider, four fundamental experimental laws emerge:

  1. Effect of Intensity on Photocurrent: For a fixed frequency (\(\nu > \nu_0\)) and fixed positive collector potential, the photoelectric current increases linearly with incident light intensity. Since photocurrent measures the number of photoelectrons emitted per second, higher intensity means more electrons ejected per second.
  2. Saturation Current & Stopping Potential: As the positive (accelerating) potential on collector A increases, photocurrent rises until all emitted electrons reach A. This plateau is the saturation current. When A is made negative (retarding) relative to C, only the fastest electrons reach A. At a sharp negative voltage \(-V_0\), even the most energetic electrons are stopped and current drops to zero. This minimum retarding potential \(V_0\) is the stopping (cut-off) potential: \[K_{\max} = eV_0\] Increasing intensity raises the saturation current proportionally, but leaves \(V_0\) (and hence \(K_{\max}\)) unchanged.
  3. Effect of Frequency on Stopping Potential: Keeping intensity fixed and increasing frequency (\(\nu_3 > \nu_2 > \nu_1\)) yields the same saturation current (or more precisely, stopping potential becomes more negative: \(V_{03} > V_{02} > V_{01}\)). A plot of \(V_0\) versus \(\nu\) is a straight line with a cut-off threshold frequency (\(\nu_0\)) below which no emission occurs, regardless of intensity.
  4. Instantaneous Emission: Whenever \(\nu > \nu_0\), photoemission begins instantaneously (within \(\sim 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}\) or less), even if the light is extremely dim.

4. Wave Theory Failure vs. Einstein's Photon Picture

4.1 Why Classical Wave Theory Fails

In classical electromagnetism, light spreads energy continuously across its wavefront. Higher intensity means larger electric and magnetic field amplitudes, so electrons at the metal surface should absorb more energy as intensity rises. This leads to three predictions that flatly contradict experiment:

  • Wave theory predicts \(K_{\max}\) should increase with intensity, whereas experiment shows \(K_{\max}\) is independent of intensity.
  • Wave theory predicts any frequency should eject electrons if the beam is intense enough, contradicting the existence of a sharp threshold frequency \(\nu_0\).
  • Because energy is distributed over many surface atoms on a wavefront, a single electron would need hours to accumulate enough energy to overcome \(\phi_0\), contradicting the observed instantaneous emission.

4.2 Einstein's Photoelectric Equation & Photon Properties

In 1905, Albert Einstein resolved these contradictions by proposing that electromagnetic radiation interacts with matter in discrete energy packets called quanta (later named photons), each carrying energy \(E = h\nu\), where \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\) is Planck's constant.

When a single electron absorbs one photon of energy \(h\nu\), part of that energy overcomes the surface work function \(\phi_0\), and the remainder appears as the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electron:

\[K_{\max} = \frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\max}^2 = eV_0 = h\nu - \phi_0 = h(\nu - \nu_0)\] \[V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \frac{\phi_0}{e}\]

R. A. Millikan verified this linear relation with high precision across alkali metals in 1916, measuring the slope \(h/e\) to confirm Planck's constant \(h\). Later, A. H. Compton's X-ray scattering experiment (1924) further confirmed the particle-like momentum of photons.

Summary of the Photon Picture

  • Energy & Momentum: Each photon of frequency \(\nu\) and wavelength \(\lambda\) travels at speed \(c\) in vacuum with energy \(E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}\) and momentum \(p = \frac{h\nu}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}\).
  • Independence from Intensity: Increasing intensity at a fixed frequency increases the number of photons crossing unit area per second, not the energy of an individual photon.
  • Electrical Neutrality: Photons carry zero electric charge and are undeflected by electric or magnetic fields.
  • Collisions: In photon-particle collisions, total energy and total momentum are conserved, but the number of photons need not be conserved (photons can be absorbed or newly created).

5. Wave Nature of Matter (de Broglie Hypothesis)

Light exhibits dual character: wave behaviour in interference, diffraction, and polarisation, and particle behaviour in the photoelectric and Compton effects. Guided by symmetry in nature, Louis Victor de Broglie proposed in 1924 that moving material particles should likewise exhibit wave-like properties under suitable conditions.

\[\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}\]

Here \(\lambda\) is the de Broglie wavelength of the matter wave, \(m\) is the particle's mass, and \(v\) is its speed. Notice that \(\lambda = h/p\) also holds for a photon (\(p = h\nu/c \implies h/p = c/\nu = \lambda\)), and for a material particle it is independent of electric charge.

  • Microscopic vs. Macroscopic Scales: Because \(h \approx 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\) is extraordinarily tiny, macroscopic objects (such as a moving ball or dust grain) have wavelengths around \(10^{-34}\,\mathrm{m}\), far smaller than a proton and completely undetectable. Subatomic particles like electrons have very small masses, giving wavelengths comparable to atomic-plane spacings in crystals (\(\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{nm}\) or \(1\,\mathrm{Å}\)), making their wave nature experimentally measurable.
  • NEET Kinetic Energy Connection: Since non-relativistic kinetic energy is \(K = \frac{p^2}{2m}\), momentum is \(p = \sqrt{2mK}\), giving \(\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mK}}\). If a charged particle of charge \(q\) is accelerated from rest through a potential difference \(V\), \(K = qV\), so \(\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mqV}}\).

High-Yield NEET Calculation Tips

  • Fast Photon Energy Conversion: Using \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\), \(c = 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\), we get \(hc \approx 1.989 \times 10^{-25}\,\mathrm{J\,m} \approx 1243\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}\) (often approximated as \(1240\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}\) or \(1242\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}\) depending on rounding). Always check the options' precision and substitute exact constants when needed.
  • Graph Slopes: On any \(V_0\) vs. \(\nu\) plot, the slope is always \(h/e\) regardless of the metal. On a \(K_{\max}\) vs. \(\nu\) plot, the slope is \(h\).

7. Worked Examples

Note: The scenarios and numerical values in the worked examples below are hypothetical assumptions created for practice.

Worked Example 1: Photon Energy, Momentum, and Emission Rate

A hypothetical green laser emits monochromatic light of wavelength \(\lambda = 500\,\mathrm{nm}\) at a power of \(P = 4.0\,\mathrm{mW}\). Take \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\), \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\). Find (a) the energy of each photon in joules and in \(\mathrm{eV}\), (b) the momentum of each photon, and (c) the number of photons emitted per second.

Solution:

(a) Energy per photon: \[E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{500 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.978 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\] In electron volts: \[E = \frac{3.978 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 2.49\,\mathrm{eV}\]

(b) Momentum per photon: \[p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{500 \times 10^{-9}} = 1.326 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\]

(c) Since power \(P = N E\), where \(N\) is the number of photons emitted per second: \[N = \frac{P}{E} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-3}}{3.978 \times 10^{-19}} \approx 1.01 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{photons\,s^{-1}}\]

Worked Example 2: Stopping Potential and Threshold Frequency

Suppose ultraviolet light of wavelength \(\lambda = 300\,\mathrm{nm}\) falls on a metal plate whose hypothetical work function is \(\phi_0 = 2.20\,\mathrm{eV}\). Calculate (a) the maximum kinetic energy of emitted photoelectrons, (b) the stopping potential, and (c) the threshold frequency of the metal.

Solution:

(a) Incident photon energy: \[E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{300 \times 10^{-9}} = 6.63 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}\,\mathrm{eV} \approx 4.14\,\mathrm{eV}\] Using Einstein's photoelectric equation: \[K_{\max} = E - \phi_0 = 4.14\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.20\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.94\,\mathrm{eV}\]

(b) Stopping potential \(V_0\): \[eV_0 = K_{\max} \implies V_0 = \frac{1.94\,\mathrm{eV}}{e} = 1.94\,\mathrm{V}\]

(c) Threshold frequency \(\nu_0\): \[\nu_0 = \frac{\phi_0}{h} = \frac{2.20 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} = \frac{3.52 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} \approx 5.31 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\]

Worked Example 3: Determining Planck's Constant from Two Frequencies

In a hypothetical photoelectric setup, light of frequency \(\nu_1 = 6.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\) requires a stopping potential \(V_{01} = 0.50\,\mathrm{V}\), while light of frequency \(\nu_2 = 8.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\) requires \(V_{02} = 1.33\,\mathrm{V}\). Estimate Planck's constant \(h\) and the metal's work function \(\phi_0\). (Take \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)).

Solution:

Subtracting \(eV_{01} = h\nu_1 - \phi_0\) from \(eV_{02} = h\nu_2 - \phi_0\) eliminates \(\phi_0\): \[h = \frac{e(V_{02} - V_{01})}{\nu_2 - \nu_1} = \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times (1.33 - 0.50)}{(8.0 - 6.0) \times 10^{14}} = \frac{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.83}{2.0 \times 10^{14}} = 6.64 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\]

Substituting back to find \(\phi_0\): \[\phi_0 = h\nu_1 - eV_{01} = (6.64 \times 10^{-34} \times 6.0 \times 10^{14}) - (1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.50) = 3.184 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} \approx 1.99\,\mathrm{eV}\]

Worked Example 4: Microscopic vs. Macroscopic de Broglie Wavelengths

Compare the de Broglie wavelength of an electron (\(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) moving at \(v_e = 2.0 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) with that of a hypothetical pollen grain of mass \(m_p = 2.0 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{kg}\) drifting at \(v_p = 5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Solution:

For the electron: \[\lambda_e = \frac{h}{m_e v_e} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 2.0 \times 10^6} \approx 3.64 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m} = 3.64\,\mathrm{Å}\]

For the pollen grain: \[\lambda_p = \frac{h}{m_p v_p} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{2.0 \times 10^{-10} \times 5.0} = 6.63 \times 10^{-25}\,\mathrm{m}\]

The electron's wavelength is of atomic dimensions and readily observable via diffraction, whereas the pollen grain's wavelength is far below any measurable scale.

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

All items below are original practice questions aligned with the NEET UG examination pattern.

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following statements regarding electron emission from a metal surface is correct?
  • (A) Field emission requires an electric field of the order of \(10^2\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\).
  • (B) The work function of a metal depends on the intensity of incident light.
  • (C) Work function is the minimum energy required by an electron to escape from the metal surface.
  • (D) Photoelectrons are emitted with identical kinetic energies from a given metal.
Answer: (C)

Explanation: Work function (\(\phi_0\)) is defined as the minimum energy needed for an electron to overcome the surface ion attraction and escape. Field emission requires \(\sim 10^8\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\), \(\phi_0\) depends only on the metal and surface nature (not light intensity), and emitted photoelectrons have a range of kinetic energies from \(0\) up to \(K_{\max}\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ): Monochromatic light of frequency above the threshold frequency illuminates a photoelectric emitter. If the intensity of the light is doubled while keeping the frequency constant, then:
  • (A) Both saturation current and stopping potential double.
  • (B) Saturation current doubles, while stopping potential remains unchanged.
  • (C) Saturation current remains unchanged, while stopping potential doubles.
  • (D) Both saturation current and maximum kinetic energy remain unchanged.
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Doubling intensity doubles the number of photons arriving per second, which doubles the number of photoelectrons emitted per second and hence doubles the saturation current. Since frequency is unchanged, each photon still carries the same energy \(h\nu\), so \(K_{\max}\) and \(V_0\) remain unchanged.

Practice Question 3 (Numerical MCQ): A hypothetical monochromatic light source of wavelength \(663\,\mathrm{nm}\) emits \(6.0\,\mathrm{mW}\) of optical power. Taking \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\) and \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), how many photons are emitted per second?
  • (A) \(2.0 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(2.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(3.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(1.5 \times 10^{17}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: Energy of one photon: \[E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^8}{663 \times 10^{-9}} = 3.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\] Number of photons per second: \[N = \frac{P}{E} = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{-3}}{3.0 \times 10^{-19}} = 2.0 \times 10^{16}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 4 (Numerical MCQ): A hypothetical metal has a work function of \(2.18\,\mathrm{eV}\). When photons of energy \(4.00\,\mathrm{eV}\) strike its surface, what is the maximum speed of the emitted photoelectrons? (Take \(m_e = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\)).
  • (A) \(4.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(6.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(8.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
  • (D) \(1.2 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
Answer: (C)

Explanation: \[K_{\max} = E - \phi_0 = 4.00\,\mathrm{eV} - 2.18\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.82\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.82 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} = 2.912 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\] Using \(K_{\max} = \frac{1}{2}m_e v_{\max}^2\): \[v_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 2.912 \times 10^{-19}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}}} = \sqrt{6.4 \times 10^{11}} = 8.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]

Practice Question 5 (Graph-Based MCQ): Graphs of stopping potential \(V_0\) versus incident frequency \(\nu\) are plotted for two metals \(P\) and \(Q\). Their threshold frequencies are \(\nu_{0P} = 4.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(\nu_{0Q} = 6.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\). Which statement is correct?
  • (A) The graph for \(Q\) has a steeper slope than that for \(P\).
  • (B) Both graphs are parallel straight lines of slope \(h/e\), and \(Q\) has a larger work function than \(P\).
  • (C) Both graphs are parallel straight lines of slope \(h\), and \(P\) has a larger work function than \(Q\).
  • (D) The two lines intersect at a frequency above \(6.0 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\).
Answer: (B)

Explanation: From \(V_0 = \left(\frac{h}{e}\right)\nu - \frac{\phi_0}{e}\), the slope is \(h/e\) for every metal, so the two lines are parallel. Since \(\phi_0 = h\nu_0\) and \(\nu_{0Q} > \nu_{0P}\), metal \(Q\) has the larger work function.

Practice Question 6 (Assertion–Reason): Read the Assertion and Reason carefully and choose the correct option.

Assertion (A): Photoelectric emission starts instantaneously without appreciable time lag whenever the incident frequency exceeds the threshold frequency, even for very dim light.
Reason (R): In Einstein's photon model, emission occurs via single-step absorption of one photon by one electron, and intensity determines only the photon arrival rate.

  • (A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  • (C) A is true, but R is false.
  • (D) A is false, but R is true.
Answer: (A)

Explanation: Because the elementary process is the instantaneous absorption of a single quantum \(h\nu\) by a single electron, even a dim beam causes immediate emission (though fewer electrons are emitted per second).

Statement II: Classical wave theory predicts that increasing light intensity increases the electric field amplitude and should therefore increase the maximum kinetic energy of emitted photoelectrons.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Explanation: Both statements accurately describe the predictions of classical wave theory that were contradicted by photoelectric experiments.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ): Which of the following statements about photons is INCORRECT?
  • (A) A photon of wavelength \(\lambda\) carries energy \(hc/\lambda\) and momentum \(h/\lambda\).
  • (B) Photons are electrically neutral and are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.
  • (C) In a photon-electron collision, total energy and total momentum are conserved.
  • (D) In every photon-electron interaction, the total number of photons is always conserved.
Answer: (D)

Explanation: While total energy and total momentum are conserved in photon-particle collisions, photon number is not necessarily conserved—photons may be absorbed (as in the photoelectric effect) or newly created.

Practice Question 9 (Conceptual Ratio MCQ): An electron and a proton are moving with the same speed \(v\). If the proton mass is approximately \(1836\) times the electron mass, what is the ratio of their de Broglie wavelengths \(\lambda_e / \lambda_p\)?
  • (A) \(1 : 1836\)
  • (B) \(1836 : 1\)
  • (C) \(1 : 1\)
  • (D) \(\sqrt{1836} : 1\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: From \(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}\), for the same speed \(v\), \(\lambda \propto \frac{1}{m}\). Therefore, \[\frac{\lambda_e}{\lambda_p} = \frac{m_p}{m_e} \approx \frac{1836}{1}\]

Practice Question 10 (Numerical MCQ): Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron (\(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) moving at a speed of \(1.0 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). (Take \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\)).
  • (A) \(0.73\,\mathrm{Å}\)
  • (B) \(7.28\,\mathrm{Å}\)
  • (C) \(14.5\,\mathrm{Å}\)
  • (D) \(72.8\,\mathrm{Å}\)
Answer: (B)

Explanation: \[\lambda = \frac{h}{m_e v} = \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 1.0 \times 10^6} \approx 7.28 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m} = 7.28\,\mathrm{Å}\]

9. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

  • Confusing Intensity with Frequency: Intensity controls the number of photons per second (and thus saturation current), whereas frequency controls the energy of each photon (and thus \(K_{\max}\) and \(V_0\)).
  • Unit Mismatch in \(K_{\max} = h\nu - \phi_0\): Never subtract \(\phi_0\) in \(\mathrm{eV}\) directly from \(h\nu\) in joules. Convert both quantities to either \(\mathrm{J}\) or \(\mathrm{eV}\) first.
  • Forgetting the \(1/e\) Factor in Graph Slope: The slope of \(V_0\) vs. \(\nu\) is \(h/e\) (in \(\mathrm{V\,s}\)), whereas the slope of \(K_{\max}\) vs. \(\nu\) is \(h\) (in \(\mathrm{J\,s}\)).
  • Assuming Photon Number is Conserved: Total energy and momentum are conserved in photon-electron collisions, but photons can be completely absorbed.
  • Charge Independence of \(\lambda\): The de Broglie wavelength \(\lambda = h/(mv)\) depends on mass and speed (momentum), not on whether the particle is charged or neutral.

10. Concise Final Recap

Electrons are universal, negatively charged constituents of matter with quantised charge \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\). To escape a metal surface, an electron must overcome the work function \(\phi_0\). In the photoelectric effect, light behaves as discrete photons of energy \(E = h\nu\) and momentum \(p = h/\lambda\). One-to-one photon-electron absorption gives Einstein's equation \(eV_0 = K_{\max} = h\nu - \phi_0\), explaining the threshold frequency \(\nu_0 = \phi_0/h\), instantaneous emission, and the independence of stopping potential from intensity. Symmetrically, moving matter exhibits wave properties with de Broglie wavelength \(\lambda = h/p = h/(mv)\), which is measurable for subatomic particles and negligible for macroscopic objects.
