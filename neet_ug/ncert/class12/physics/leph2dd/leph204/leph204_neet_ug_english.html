Atoms: Nuclear Scattering, Atomic Spectra & Bohr's Quantum Model

A complete, exam-focused tutorial covering atomic models, α-particle scattering, energy quantisation, hydrogen spectral series, and de Broglie standing waves.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Roadmap

Understanding the internal structure of the atom bridges classical electromagnetism and quantum physics. For NEET UG, questions from this topic frequently test proportionalities (how radius, speed, time period, and energy scale with principal quantum number \(n\)), distance of closest approach, energy level transitions, and conceptual limitations of classical and Bohr models.

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Contrast Thomson's plum-pudding model with Rutherford's nuclear model and explain why classical electrodynamics fails to explain atomic stability.
  • Calculate the distance of closest approach and relate impact parameter to scattering angle in α-particle scattering.
  • Apply Bohr's three postulates to derive orbital radii, speeds, and quantised energy levels of hydrogen and hydrogenic ions.
  • Identify spectral series (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, etc.) and compute photon frequencies and wavelengths for electronic transitions.
  • Explain angular momentum quantisation using de Broglie's circular standing-wave condition.

2. Early Atomic Models & Alpha-Particle Scattering

2.1 Thomson's Model vs. the Need for a Nuclear Picture

Following the discovery of the electron in 1897, it became clear that electrically neutral atoms contain identical negatively charged electrons balanced by positive charge. J. J. Thomson proposed in 1898 that the positive charge is spread uniformly throughout the atomic volume (a sphere of radius roughly \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)), with electrons embedded inside like seeds in a watermelon. Although this "plum-pudding" model kept electrons in electrostatic equilibrium in the ground state, it could not account for large-angle scattering of energetic α-particles or discrete atomic emission spectra.

2.2 The Geiger–Marsden Alpha-Particle Scattering Experiment

Acting on Ernest Rutherford's proposal, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden (1911) directed a narrow, collimated beam of \(5.5\,\mathrm{MeV}\) α-particles (helium nuclei, charge \(+2e\)) from a radioactive \(^{214}_{83}\mathrm{Bi}\) source onto an ultra-thin gold foil (thickness \(\approx 2.1 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}\)) inside an evacuated chamber. A rotatable zinc sulphide (\(\mathrm{ZnS}\)) screen paired with a microscope detected tiny light flashes (scintillations) at various scattering angles \(\theta\).

Key Experimental Observations & Nuclear Deductions
  • Most α-particles pass straight through without noticeable deflection: the atom is overwhelmingly empty space, and lightweight atomic electrons cannot appreciably deflect heavy α-particles.
  • Small-angle deflection is rare: only about \(0.14\%\) of incident α-particles scatter by more than \(1°\).
  • Large-angle backscattering occurs: roughly \(1\) in \(8000\) α-particles deflects by more than \(90°\) (some nearly \(180°\)). Because an α-particle experiences an intense repulsive Coulomb force to reverse direction, almost the entire mass and all positive charge of the atom must be concentrated in a tiny, dense central core—the nucleus.
  • Scale comparison: Nuclear radius is about \(10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m}\), whereas atomic radius is about \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)—roughly \(10^4\) to \(10^5\) times larger.

2.3 Impact Parameter and Scattering Trajectory

Because the gold foil is extremely thin, each α-particle undergoes at most a single elastic Coulomb encounter with a stationary gold nucleus (\(Z = 79\), which is about \(50\) times more massive than an α-particle). The repulsive electrostatic force at separation \(r\) is:

\[F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(2e)(Ze)}{r^2}\]

The path of an α-particle is governed by its impact parameter \(b\)—the perpendicular distance between the nucleus and the initial velocity line of the incoming α-particle:

  • Large \(b\): The α-particle passes far from the nucleus and suffers negligible deflection (\(\theta \approx 0°\)).
  • Small \(b\): The α-particle approaches close to the nucleus, experiences strong repulsion, and deflects through a large angle.
  • Head-on collision (\(b = 0\)): The α-particle slows down along the line joining the centres, comes momentarily to rest at the distance of closest approach \(d\), and rebounds straight back (\(\theta = 180°\)).
Tutorial Diagram 1: Coulomb scattering of α-particles by a stationary target nucleus. Smaller impact parameters \(b\) yield larger deflection angles \(\theta\), while a head-on encounter (\(b = 0\)) stops at distance \(d\) before reversing.

2.4 Distance of Closest Approach

For a head-on collision, the α-particle's initial kinetic energy \(K\) converts entirely into electrostatic potential energy at the turning point \(r = d\). By conservation of mechanical energy:

\[K = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(2e)(Ze)}{d} \implies d = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 K}\]

Because \(d\) gives the closest distance reached before repulsion reverses the particle, \(d\) sets an upper bound on the nuclear radius (for example, \(7.7\,\mathrm{MeV}\) α-particles on gold give \(d \approx 30\,\mathrm{fm}\), whereas the actual gold nuclear radius is \(\approx 6\,\mathrm{fm}\)).

3. Classical Electron Orbits & Why Rutherford's Model Fails

In Rutherford's planetary picture of the hydrogen atom, an electron (mass \(m\), charge \(-e\)) revolves around a stationary proton (charge \(+e\)) in a circular orbit of radius \(r\) at speed \(v\). The electrostatic attraction provides the centripetal force:

\[\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{e^2}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \implies mv^2 = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\]

From this force balance, the kinetic energy \(K\), electrostatic potential energy \(U\), and total mechanical energy \(E\) of the bound electron are:

\[K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}, \qquad U = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}, \qquad E = K + U = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\]

Notice the fundamental energy relation for any inverse-square bound orbit: \(E = -K = \frac{U}{2}\). The negative total energy confirms that the electron is bound to the nucleus.

Two Fatal Flaws of the Classical Nuclear Model
  1. Atomic Instability: An electron moving in a circle undergoes continuous centripetal acceleration. According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle radiates energy continuously as electromagnetic waves. Consequently, the electron would lose energy, spiral inward, and collapse into the nucleus within a fraction of a second—contradicting the observed stability of matter.
  2. Continuous vs. Line Spectrum: As the electron spirals inward, its orbital frequency increases continuously, so classical theory predicts a continuous smear of emitted frequencies. Experimentally, however, isolated atoms emit only sharp, discrete wavelengths.

4. Atomic Line Spectra & Bohr's Model of Hydrogen

4.1 Emission and Absorption Line Spectra

Dense solids, liquids, and high-pressure gases emit a continuous spectrum because closely packed atoms interact strongly. In contrast, a rarefied gas excited electrically or thermally emits light only at specific discrete wavelengths, forming an emission line spectrum (bright lines on a dark background). Conversely, when white light passes through a cool, rarefied gas, dark absorption lines appear at the exact same wavelengths that the gas emits. These discrete lines act as an elemental fingerprint.

4.2 Bohr's Three Postulates (1913)

To reconcile Rutherford's nuclear atom with discrete line spectra, Niels Bohr proposed three postulates for single-electron (hydrogenic) atoms:

  1. Stationary Orbits: An electron can revolve around the nucleus in certain allowed circular orbits without emitting electromagnetic radiation. Each stationary orbit has a fixed total energy.
  2. Angular Momentum Quantisation: Only those orbits are allowed in which the orbital angular momentum \(L\) of the electron is an integral multiple of \(\frac{h}{2\pi}\): \[L = mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\] where \(n\) is the principal quantum number and \(h \approx 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\) is Planck's constant.
  3. Frequency Condition for Transitions: An atom radiates or absorbs a photon only when an electron jumps between two stationary states of energies \(E_i\) and \(E_f\) (\(E_i > E_f\)): \[h\nu = E_i - E_f\]

4.3 Quantised Radii, Speeds, and Energy Levels

Combining the centripetal force condition \(mv^2 = \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\) with Bohr's angular momentum quantisation \(mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\) yields the radius and speed of the \(n^{\text{th}}\) orbit for a hydrogenic atom (nuclear charge \(+Ze\)):

\[r_n = \frac{n^2 h^2 \varepsilon_0}{\pi m Z e^2} = a_0 \frac{n^2}{Z}, \qquad v_n = \frac{Z e^2}{2\varepsilon_0 n h} \propto \frac{Z}{n}\]

For hydrogen (\(Z = 1\)), the smallest orbit (\(n = 1\)) has the Bohr radius:

\[a_0 = r_1 = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.53\,\mathrm{Å}\]

Substituting \(r_n\) into the total energy expression \(E = -\frac{Ze^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\) gives the quantised energy levels:

\[E_n = -\frac{m Z^2 e^4}{8 n^2 \varepsilon_0^2 h^2} = -13.6\,\frac{Z^2}{n^2}\,\mathrm{eV}\]

For hydrogen (\(Z = 1\)):

  • Ground state (\(n = 1\)): \(E_1 = -13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\). The minimum energy needed to completely free the electron (\(r \to \infty, E_\infty = 0\,\mathrm{eV}\)) is the ionisation energy, \(13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\).
  • First excited state (\(n = 2\)): \(E_2 = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\). Excitation from \(n = 1\) to \(n = 2\) requires \(10.2\,\mathrm{eV}\).
  • Second excited state (\(n = 3\)): \(E_3 = -1.51\,\mathrm{eV}\). Excitation from \(n = 1\) to \(n = 3\) requires \(12.09\,\mathrm{eV}\).
  • Higher states: \(E_4 = -0.85\,\mathrm{eV}\), \(E_5 = -0.54\,\mathrm{eV}\), converging toward \(0\,\mathrm{eV}\) as \(n \to \infty\). Above \(E = 0\,\mathrm{eV}\), the electron is unbound and energies form a continuum.
Tutorial Diagram 3: Quantised energy levels of the hydrogen atom showing emission transitions for the Lyman (ultraviolet), Balmer (visible), and Paschen (infrared) series.

4.4 Spectral Series of Hydrogen

When an electron transitions from an upper state \(n_i\) to a lower state \(n_f\) (\(n_i > n_f\)), the emitted photon energy is \(h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = 13.6\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)\,\mathrm{eV}\). Grouping transitions by their final state \(n_f\) produces the spectral series:

  • Lyman series (\(n_f = 1\)): Ultraviolet region (\(91\,\mathrm{nm}\) to \(122\,\mathrm{nm}\)).
  • Balmer series (\(n_f = 2\)): Visible region (\(365\,\mathrm{nm}\) to \(656\,\mathrm{nm}\)).
  • Paschen series (\(n_f = 3\)): Near-infrared region (\(820\,\mathrm{nm}\) to \(1875\,\mathrm{nm}\)).

5. de Broglie's Explanation of Quantisation & Bohr's Limitations

5.1 Standing Matter Waves on Circular Orbits

In 1923, Louis de Broglie explained why angular momentum must be quantised. Treating the orbiting electron as a matter wave of wavelength \(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv_n}\), only those orbits persist in which the electron wave forms a closed standing wave without destructive interference. This requires the orbit circumference \(2\pi r_n\) to accommodate an exact integer number \(n\) of de Broglie wavelengths:

\[2\pi r_n = n\lambda = n\frac{h}{mv_n} \implies m v_n r_n = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\]

Thus, Bohr's quantisation rule emerges naturally from wave-particle duality.

Tutorial Diagram 4: A resonant standing matter wave for \(n = 4\), where exactly four de Broglie wavelengths fit around the circular orbit (\(2\pi r_4 = 4\lambda\)).

5.2 Limitations of Bohr's Model

  • Only valid for single-electron (hydrogenic) atoms: It fails even for two-electron helium (\(\mathrm{He}\)) because it ignores electron–electron electrostatic repulsion, which is comparable in strength to electron–nucleus attraction.
  • Cannot explain spectral line intensities: Some transitions are bright and others faint; Bohr's model predicts frequencies accurately but cannot explain transition probabilities.
  • Classical trajectory conflict: Precise circular orbits contradict the Heisenberg uncertainty principle; modern quantum mechanics replaces fixed orbits with three-dimensional probability distributions described by four quantum numbers.

7. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Distance of Closest Approach

Problem: Suppose a beam of α-particles, each having a kinetic energy of \(6.4\,\mathrm{MeV}\) (assumed value), is directed head-on toward a stationary silver nucleus (\(Z = 47\)). Calculate the distance of closest approach \(d\). (Take \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9.0 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{N\cdot m^2/C^2}\) and \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)).

Solution:

Convert kinetic energy to joules:

\[K = 6.4 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{eV} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J/eV} = 1.024 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J}\]

At the distance of closest approach \(d\), total kinetic energy converts to electrostatic potential energy:

\[d = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 K} = \frac{2 \times 9.0 \times 10^9 \times 47 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{1.024 \times 10^{-12}}\] \[d = \frac{18 \times 47 \times 2.56 \times 10^{-29}}{1.024 \times 10^{-12}} = 2115 \times 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 2.12 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m} = 21.2\,\mathrm{fm}\]
Worked Example 2: Energy & Radius in an Excited State

Problem: An electron in a hydrogen atom occupies the \(n = 3\) stationary state. Using \(a_0 = 0.53\,\mathrm{Å}\) and \(E_1 = -13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\), determine its orbital radius, kinetic energy, and electrostatic potential energy.

Solution:

  • Orbital radius: \(r_3 = n^2 a_0 = 3^2 \times 0.53\,\mathrm{Å} = 4.77\,\mathrm{Å} = 4.77 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\).
  • Total energy: \(E_3 = -\dfrac{13.6}{3^2}\,\mathrm{eV} = -1.51\,\mathrm{eV}\).
  • Kinetic energy: \(K_3 = -E_3 = +1.51\,\mathrm{eV}\).
  • Potential energy: \(U_3 = 2E_3 = -3.02\,\mathrm{eV}\).
Worked Example 3: Photon Frequency & Wavelength of a Balmer Transition

Problem: A hydrogen atom transitions from the \(n_i = 4\) state to the \(n_f = 2\) state. Calculate the frequency and wavelength of the emitted photon. (Assume \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\cdot s}\), \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\)).

Solution:

The energies of the initial and final states are:

\[E_4 = -\frac{13.6}{4^2} = -0.85\,\mathrm{eV}, \qquad E_2 = -\frac{13.6}{2^2} = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\]

Photon energy:

\[\Delta E = E_4 - E_2 = -0.85 - (-3.40) = 2.55\,\mathrm{eV} = 2.55 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J} = 4.08 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\]

Frequency of emitted light:

\[\nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{4.08 \times 10^{-19}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} \approx 6.15 \times 10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}\]

Wavelength of emitted light:

\[\lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{6.15 \times 10^{14}} \approx 4.88 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} = 488\,\mathrm{nm}\]
Worked Example 4: Excitation by Electron Bombardment

Problem: Gaseous hydrogen at room temperature is bombarded with a beam of \(12.8\,\mathrm{eV}\) electrons (assumed energy). Which spectral series and how many distinct spectral lines can be emitted during de-excitation?

Solution:

At room temperature, hydrogen atoms are in the ground state (\(E_1 = -13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\)). After absorbing up to \(12.8\,\mathrm{eV}\), the highest reachable energy is \(-13.6 + 12.8 = -0.80\,\mathrm{eV}\).

Comparing with allowed levels: \(E_4 = -0.85\,\mathrm{eV}\) (requires \(12.75\,\mathrm{eV}\)) and \(E_5 = -0.54\,\mathrm{eV}\) (requires \(13.06\,\mathrm{eV}\)). Thus, electrons can be excited up to \(n = 4\), but not \(n = 5\).

From \(n = 4\), downward transitions can end at \(n_f = 1\) (Lyman series), \(n_f = 2\) (Balmer series), and \(n_f = 3\) (Paschen series). The total number of distinct spectral lines possible is:

\[N = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{4 \times 3}{2} = 6\text{ lines}\]

8. NEET UG Practice Questions

The following are original practice questions designed in the NEET UG format. Attempt each question before reviewing its answer and worked solution.

Practice Question 1. In Rutherford's α-particle scattering experiment, if the impact parameter \(b\) of an incident α-particle is gradually reduced toward zero, how does its scattering angle \(\theta\) change?
  • (A) It decreases toward \(0°\)
  • (B) It remains constant at \(90°\)
  • (C) It increases toward \(180°\)
  • (D) It drops abruptly to zero at the nuclear boundary
Answer: (C)

Solution: A smaller impact parameter \(b\) means the α-particle passes closer to the positively charged nucleus, experiencing a stronger Coulomb repulsion and hence a larger deflection \(\theta\). In the limit \(b \to 0\) (head-on collision), the particle rebounds straight back (\(\theta = 180°\)).

Practice Question 2. An α-particle of kinetic energy \(K\) approaches a stationary nucleus of atomic number \(Z\) head-on and stops at a distance of closest approach \(d\). If both the kinetic energy of the α-particle and the atomic number of the target nucleus are doubled (to \(2K\) and \(2Z\), respectively), what is the new distance of closest approach?
  • (A) \(d/2\)
  • (B) \(d\)
  • (C) \(2d\)
  • (D) \(4d\)
Answer: (B)

Solution: The distance of closest approach is \(d = \dfrac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 K}\), so \(d \propto \dfrac{Z}{K}\). When \(Z \to 2Z\) and \(K \to 2K\), the factor of \(2\) cancels: \(d' = \dfrac{2Z}{2K}\left(\dfrac{2e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\right) = d\).

Practice Question 3. In a hydrogen atom, an electron revolves in a stationary orbit where its total energy is \(-3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\). What are its kinetic energy \(K\) and electrostatic potential energy \(U\) in this orbit?
  • (A) \(K = +3.40\,\mathrm{eV},\; U = -6.80\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (B) \(K = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV},\; U = +6.80\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (C) \(K = +1.70\,\mathrm{eV},\; U = -5.10\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (D) \(K = +6.80\,\mathrm{eV},\; U = -10.20\,\mathrm{eV}\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: For a bound circular Coulomb orbit, \(K = -E\) and \(U = 2E\). Given \(E = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\), we obtain \(K = +3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\) and \(U = 2(-3.40\,\mathrm{eV}) = -6.80\,\mathrm{eV}\).

Practice Question 4. According to Bohr's model of the hydrogen atom, what is the ratio of the orbital time period of an electron in the \(n = 2\) state to that in the \(n = 1\) state?
  • (A) \(2 : 1\)
  • (B) \(4 : 1\)
  • (C) \(8 : 1\)
  • (D) \(16 : 1\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: The orbital time period is \(T_n = \dfrac{2\pi r_n}{v_n}\). Since \(r_n \propto n^2\) and \(v_n \propto \dfrac{1}{n}\), it follows that \(T_n \propto n^3\). Therefore, \(\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\dfrac{2}{1}\right)^3 = \dfrac{8}{1}\).

Practice Question 5. If the de Broglie wavelength of an electron in the ground state (\(n = 1\)) of a hydrogen atom is \(\lambda_0\), what is its de Broglie wavelength in the second excited state (\(n = 3\))?
  • (A) \(\lambda_0 / 3\)
  • (B) \(\lambda_0\)
  • (C) \(3\lambda_0\)
  • (D) \(9\lambda_0\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: By de Broglie's standing-wave condition, \(n\lambda_n = 2\pi r_n\). Since \(r_n = n^2 a_0\), we get \(\lambda_n = \dfrac{2\pi n^2 a_0}{n} = 2\pi a_0 n = n\lambda_0\). For the second excited state (\(n = 3\)), \(\lambda_3 = 3\lambda_0\).

Practice Question 6. Which of the following electronic transitions in a hydrogen atom emits a photon of the shortest wavelength?
  • (A) \(n = 2 \to n = 1\)
  • (B) \(n = 3 \to n = 2\)
  • (C) \(n = 4 \to n = 3\)
  • (D) \(n = 5 \to n = 2\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: The shortest wavelength corresponds to the largest emitted photon energy \(\Delta E = E_i - E_f\). Computing each: (A) \(13.6(1 - 1/4) = 10.2\,\mathrm{eV}\); (B) \(13.6(1/4 - 1/9) \approx 1.89\,\mathrm{eV}\); (C) \(13.6(1/9 - 1/16) \approx 0.66\,\mathrm{eV}\); (D) \(13.6(1/4 - 1/25) \approx 2.86\,\mathrm{eV}\). Transition \(n = 2 \to n = 1\) releases the greatest energy and hence the shortest wavelength.

Practice Question 7 (Assertion–Reason).
Assertion (A): In the Geiger–Marsden α-particle scattering experiment, nearly all α-particles pass through the gold foil with negligible deflection, while only about \(1\) in \(8000\) deflects by more than \(90°\).
Reason (R): Most of the atom's volume is empty space, whereas its entire positive charge and almost all of its mass are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus.
  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Answer: (A)

Solution: Because the nucleus occupies a tiny fraction of the atomic volume (radius \(\sim 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\) compared to atomic radius \(\sim 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\)), most α-particles miss the nucleus and pass straight through. Only the rare α-particles that pass very close to the dense positive nucleus experience sufficient Coulomb repulsion to scatter beyond \(90°\).

Practice Question 8 (Statement-Based).
Statement I: Bohr's atomic model is applicable to single-electron hydrogenic species such as \(\mathrm{H}\), \(\mathrm{He}^+\), and \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\).
Statement II: Bohr's model accurately predicts the relative intensities of spectral lines emitted by hydrogen atoms.
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: (C)

Solution: Bohr's model works for any single-electron atom or ion (where electron–electron repulsion is absent), so Statement I is correct. However, Bohr's model cannot account for why some spectral lines are brighter than others (relative intensities), making Statement II incorrect.

Practice Question 9. A hydrogen atom is initially in its first excited state (\(n = 2\)). What is the minimum energy required to ionise the atom from this state?
  • (A) \(13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (B) \(10.2\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (C) \(3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\)
  • (D) \(1.51\,\mathrm{eV}\)
Answer: (C)

Solution: In the \(n = 2\) state, the electron's energy is \(E_2 = -\dfrac{13.6}{2^2} = -3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\). To ionise the atom (\(E_\infty = 0\,\mathrm{eV}\)), the minimum energy needed is \(0 - (-3.40\,\mathrm{eV}) = 3.40\,\mathrm{eV}\).

Practice Question 10. An electron in a hydrogen atom jumps from an orbit of angular momentum \(\dfrac{3h}{2\pi}\) to an orbit of angular momentum \(\dfrac{h}{2\pi}\). What is the ratio of its orbital speed in the initial orbit to that in the final orbit?
  • (A) \(1 : 3\)
  • (B) \(3 : 1\)
  • (C) \(1 : 9\)
  • (D) \(9 : 1\)
Answer: (A)

Solution: From \(L = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\), the initial and final quantum numbers are \(n_i = 3\) and \(n_f = 1\). Since orbital speed scales as \(v_n \propto \dfrac{1}{n}\), the speed ratio is \(\dfrac{v_i}{v_f} = \dfrac{n_f}{n_i} = \dfrac{1}{3}\).

9. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Common Mistakes to Avoid in NEET UG
  • Confusing "first excited state" with \(n = 1\): \(n = 1\) is the ground state; the first excited state is \(n = 2\), and the second excited state is \(n = 3\).
  • Sign errors in \(K\), \(U\), and \(E\): Kinetic energy \(K\) is always positive (\(K = -E\)), while potential energy \(U\) and total energy \(E\) are negative for bound states (\(U = 2E\)).
  • Assuming \(d\) equals the nuclear radius: The distance of closest approach \(d\) is always greater than or equal to the sum of the α-particle and target nuclear radii if no nuclear contact occurs; it provides an upper limit on nuclear size.
  • Applying Bohr's formula to multi-electron atoms: Never use \(E_n = -13.6\,Z^2/n^2\,\mathrm{eV}\) for neutral helium (\(\mathrm{He}\)) or lithium (\(\mathrm{Li}\)); it applies only to single-electron species (\(\mathrm{H}\), \(\mathrm{He}^+\), \(\mathrm{Li}^{2+}\)).
Quick Revision Checklist
  • Nuclear radius \(\sim 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\) to \(10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m}\); atomic radius \(\sim 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\).
  • \(r_n \propto n^2/Z\), \(v_n \propto Z/n\), \(T_n \propto n^3/Z^2\), \(E_n \propto -Z^2/n^2\).
  • Hydrogen ground state energy \(= -13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\); ionisation energy \(= 13.6\,\mathrm{eV}\).
  • Lyman (\(n_f = 1\), UV), Balmer (\(n_f = 2\), Visible), Paschen (\(n_f = 3\), IR).
  • de Broglie standing-wave condition: \(2\pi r_n = n\lambda \iff mvr = nh/2\pi\).

10. Concise Final Recap

Rutherford's α-particle scattering experiment established that an atom consists of a tiny, massive, positively charged nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space, but classical electrodynamics predicted that orbiting electrons would spiral into the nucleus and emit continuous radiation. Bohr resolved this paradox for hydrogenic atoms by postulating non-radiating stationary states with quantised angular momentum \(L = nh/2\pi\) and photon emission/absorption governed by \(h\nu = E_i - E_f\). This yielded quantised radii (\(r_n \propto n^2\)) and energies (\(E_n = -13.6/n^2\,\mathrm{eV}\) for hydrogen) that accurately explained the Lyman, Balmer, and Paschen series, while de Broglie's matter-wave hypothesis (\(2\pi r_n = n\lambda\)) later provided the physical basis for angular momentum quantisation.
