Magnetism and Matter

Magnetic dipoles, Gauss's law for magnetism, magnetisation, magnetic intensity, and the classification of magnetic materials.

1. Learning Objectives and Big-Picture Overview

Magnetic phenomena appear across every scale in nature, from planetary magnetic fields to the orbital and spin motion of electrons inside atoms. While moving electric charges and steady currents generate magnetic fields, bulk matter also responds to and modifies those fields. This tutorial builds a complete, exam-ready understanding of bar magnets as magnetic dipoles, the universal law governing magnetic flux, and how materials are classified by their magnetic response.

  • The equivalence between a bar magnet and a current-carrying finite solenoid, along with the electrostatic dipole analogy.

  • Definitions, units, dimensions, and exact algebraic links among \(\mathbf{B}\), \(\mathbf{H}\), \(\mathbf{M}\), \(\chi\), \(\mu_r\), and \(\mu\).

2. Bar Magnets, Field Lines, and the Equivalent Solenoid

2.1 Fundamental Properties of a Bar Magnet

A bar magnet acts as a permanent magnetic dipole with two inseparable poles: a North (N) pole and a South (S) pole. When freely suspended, the magnet aligns roughly along the geographic north-south axis; the tip pointing toward geographic north is the North pole, and the tip pointing toward geographic south is the South pole. Like poles repel each other, whereas unlike poles attract.

A defining feature of magnetism is that magnetic monopoles do not exist. If a bar magnet is sliced either transversely (across its length) or longitudinally (along its length), each piece becomes a complete, smaller dipole with both a North pole and a South pole. Cutting a magnet never isolates a single pole.

2.2 Magnetic Field Lines

Magnetic field lines provide a visual map of the magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) in space. Their key properties distinguish them sharply from electrostatic field lines:

  • Closed continuous loops: Magnetic field lines form unbroken closed loops. Outside a magnet (or solenoid), they run from the North pole to the South pole; inside the magnet, they continue from the South pole back to the North pole. Electrostatic field lines, by contrast, originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges (or extend to infinity) without forming closed loops.
  • Tangent gives direction: At any point, the tangent to a field line points in the direction of the net magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) at that location.
  • Density indicates strength: Regions where field lines are crowded together correspond to a stronger magnetic field magnitude; regions where lines are spread apart have a weaker field.
  • No intersection: Two magnetic field lines can never cross. If they intersected, the magnetic field at the crossing point would point in two different directions simultaneously, which is physically impossible.
Terminology Caution: Field Lines vs. Lines of Force

Calling magnetic field lines "lines of force" is misleading. In electrostatics, the force on a positive test charge is parallel to \(\mathbf{E}\). In magnetism, the magnetic force on a moving charge \(q\) with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\), which is always perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\), not along the field line. A magnetic field line only indicates the direction in which a tiny compass needle aligns.

2.3 Bar Magnet as an Equivalent Solenoid

According to Ampère's hypothesis, all magnetic effects originate from circulating electric currents. In a bar magnet, atomic-scale circulating currents (due to electron motion) sum up to produce a macroscopic magnetic field identical to that of a closely wound, current-carrying finite solenoid.

At distances \(r\) much larger than the size \(l\) of the magnet or solenoid (\(r \gg l\)), the magnetic field along the axis has magnitude:

\[ B_A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{2m}{r^3} \]

Here, \(m\) is the magnetic dipole moment (in \(\mathrm{A\,m^2}\) or \(\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}\)), and \(\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}}\) is the permeability of free space. For a solenoid with \(N\) turns, cross-sectional area \(A\), and steady current \(I\), the equivalent magnetic moment is \(m = NIA\).

3. Magnetic Dipole in a Uniform Field and the Electrostatic Analogy

3.1 Torque and Potential Energy

When a magnetic needle or short bar magnet of magnetic moment \(\mathbf{m}\) is placed in a uniform external magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) at an angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf{m}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\), the two poles experience equal and opposite forces. Consequently, the net translational force is zero, but the magnet experiences a restoring torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) tending to align \(\mathbf{m}\) with \(\mathbf{B}\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{m} \times \mathbf{B}, \qquad \tau = mB\sin\theta \]

By integrating the restoring torque with respect to angle and choosing the reference zero of potential energy at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (when \(\mathbf{m} \perp \mathbf{B}\)), the magnetic potential energy \(U_m\) is:

\[ U_m = -\mathbf{m} \cdot \mathbf{B} = -mB\cos\theta \]

The work \(W_{\text{ext}}\) required by an external agent to slowly rotate the dipole from an initial angle \(\theta_1\) to a final angle \(\theta_2\) equals the change in potential energy:

\[ W_{\text{ext}} = U_m(\theta_2) - U_m(\theta_1) = mB(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2) \]
Tutorial Diagram 2: A magnetic dipole oriented at angle \(\theta\) to a uniform magnetic field experiences a restoring torque \(\tau = mB\sin\theta\) and zero net force.

3.2 Non-Uniform Field Behaviour

If the external magnetic field is non-uniform (for instance, near the pole of a bar magnet), an unmagnetised iron nail experiences an induced magnetic moment. Because the induced opposite pole lies closer to the magnet's pole than the induced like pole, the attractive force exceeds the repulsive force, producing both a net attractive force and a torque.

3.3 The Electrostatic Dipole Analogy

For a short dipole of size \(l\) at distance \(r \gg l\), the magnetic field equations match the electrostatic dipole equations under the substitutions \(\mathbf{E} \to \mathbf{B}\), \(\mathbf{p} \to \mathbf{m}\), and \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \to \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\). Notice that at the same large distance \(r\), the axial field is twice as strong as the equatorial field and points along \(\mathbf{m}\), whereas the equatorial field points opposite to \(\mathbf{m}\).

4. Gauss's Law for Magnetism

Because magnetic field lines always form closed loops, any closed Gaussian surface \(S\) has exactly as many magnetic field lines entering it as leaving it. Summing the magnetic flux \(\Delta\phi_B = \mathbf{B}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{S}\) over every small area element \(\Delta\mathbf{S}\) of a closed surface gives zero:

\[ \phi_B = \sum_{\text{all }\Delta\mathbf{S}} \mathbf{B} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{S} = 0 \]

In electrostatics, \(\sum \mathbf{E}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{S} = q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\) can be non-zero because isolated positive or negative charges exist. In magnetism, \(\phi_B = 0\) for every closed surface directly reflects the absence of magnetic monopoles. If hypothetical magnetic monopoles of charge \(q_m\) existed, the right-hand side would become \(\mu_0 q_m\).

How to Identify Invalid Magnetic Field Configurations
  • Field lines radiating outward from or converging into a point or plate imply non-zero net flux through a surrounding closed surface, which violates Gauss's law for magnetism.
  • Static magnetic field lines cannot form closed loops in empty space without enclosing a current (by Ampère's law); conversely, inside a toroid, closed circular field lines are valid because they enclose the winding currents.
  • Magnetic field lines between flat pole pieces or at the ends of a finite solenoid must fringe/curve out to form closed loops rather than terminating abruptly as perfectly straight segments.

5. Magnetisation, Magnetic Intensity, and Susceptibility

To describe how a material core alters the magnetic field inside a solenoid, we separate the external current's contribution from the material's internal response.

  • Magnetisation (\(\mathbf{M}\)): The net magnetic dipole moment induced or aligned per unit volume of the sample: \[ \mathbf{M} = \frac{\mathbf{m}_{\text{net}}}{V} \] Its SI unit is \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\) and its dimensions are \([\mathrm{L}^{-1}\mathrm{A}]\).
  • Magnetic Intensity (\(\mathbf{H}\)): Defined to represent the external magnetising agency (such as free current in a solenoid, where \(H = nI\) for \(n\) turns per unit length carrying current \(I\)): \[ \mathbf{H} = \frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0} - \mathbf{M} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M}) \] Here \(\mathbf{B}\) is the total magnetic field inside the material, \(\mathbf{B}_0 = \mu_0\mathbf{H}\) is the field due to free currents alone, and \(\mathbf{B}_m = \mu_0\mathbf{M}\) is the additional field contributed by the magnetised core. \(\mathbf{H}\) also has units \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\) and dimensions \([\mathrm{L}^{-1}\mathrm{A}]\).
  • Magnetic Susceptibility (\(\chi\)): For linear isotropic materials, magnetisation is proportional to magnetic intensity: \[ \mathbf{M} = \chi \mathbf{H} \] \(\chi\) is a dimensionless scalar measuring how strongly and in what direction a material responds to an applied field.
  • Relative Permeability (\(\mu_r\)) and Permeability (\(\mu\)): Substituting \(\mathbf{M} = \chi \mathbf{H}\) into \(\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M})\) yields: \[ \mathbf{B} = \mu_0(1 + \chi)\mathbf{H} = \mu_0 \mu_r \mathbf{H} = \mu \mathbf{H} \] where \(\mu_r = 1 + \chi\) is dimensionless and \(\mu = \mu_0\mu_r = \mu_0(1 + \chi)\).

6. Classification of Magnetic Materials

Based on the sign and magnitude of \(\chi\) (and \(\mu_r\)), materials fall into three primary classes: diamagnetic, paramagnetic, and ferromagnetic.

6.1 Superconductors and the Meissner Effect

Superconductors are metals cooled to very low temperatures that exhibit both zero electrical resistance and perfect diamagnetism. Inside a superconductor, magnetic field lines are completely expelled: \(\chi = -1\), \(\mu_r = 0\), and \(\mu = 0\). This total expulsion of magnetic flux is called the Meissner effect. Because diamagnetism arises in all materials, it is universal, though in paramagnetic and ferromagnetic substances the weak diamagnetic effect is masked by much stronger positive effects.

6.2 Hard vs. Soft Ferromagnets and Temperature Effects

  • Hard ferromagnets (such as Alnico—an alloy of Al, Ni, Co, Cu, and Fe—and lodestone) retain their magnetisation after the external field is removed, making them ideal for permanent magnets.
  • Soft ferromagnets (such as soft iron) lose their magnetisation rapidly once the external field is switched off, making them suitable for electromagnet cores and transformers.
  • Effect of temperature: In paramagnetic materials, lowering the temperature or increasing the external field increases magnetisation until saturation is reached. In ferromagnetic materials, heating gradually weakens the alignment; at a sufficiently high temperature, the domain structure disintegrates and the ferromagnet transitions into a paramagnet.

7. Worked Examples

Note: All scenarios and numerical values in these worked examples are hypothetical assumptions designed to illustrate NEET UG problem-solving methods.

Worked Example 1: Torque, Potential Energy, and Work Done on a Dipole

Problem (Assumed values): A short bar magnet of magnetic moment \(m = 0.80\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}\) is placed in a uniform magnetic field \(B = 0.40\,\mathrm{T}\). Calculate: (a) the maximum torque it can experience, (b) its potential energy in stable equilibrium, and (c) the work required by an external agent to rotate it slowly from stable equilibrium to a position perpendicular to the field.

Solution:

(a) Torque is \(\tau = mB\sin\theta\). It is maximum when \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (\(\sin 90^\circ = 1\)): \[ \tau_{\max} = (0.80\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}})(0.40\,\mathrm{T}) = 0.32\,\mathrm{N\,m} \]

(b) Stable equilibrium occurs at \(\theta = 0^\circ\): \[ U_{\min} = -mB\cos 0^\circ = -(0.80)(0.40)(1) = -0.32\,\mathrm{J} \]

(c) Rotating from \(\theta_1 = 0^\circ\) to \(\theta_2 = 90^\circ\): \[ W_{\text{ext}} = mB(\cos 0^\circ - \cos 90^\circ) = 0.32(1 - 0) = 0.32\,\mathrm{J} \]

Worked Example 2: Axial vs. Equatorial Field of a Short Magnet

Problem (Assumed values): A short bar magnet has magnetic moment \(m = 0.60\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}\). Find the ratio and magnitudes of the magnetic field at a distance \(r = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\) from its centre on (a) the axial line and (b) the equatorial line.

Solution:

Using \(\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} = 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}}\) and \(r^3 = (0.20)^3 = 8 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\):

(a) Axial field magnitude (directed along \(\mathbf{m}\)): \[ B_A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{r^3} = \frac{10^{-7} \times 2 \times 0.60}{8 \times 10^{-3}} = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{T} \]

(b) Equatorial field magnitude (directed opposite to \(\mathbf{m}\)): \[ B_E = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{m}{r^3} = \frac{B_A}{2} = 7.5 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{T} \] Hence, \(B_A : B_E = 2 : 1\).

Worked Example 3: Solenoid with a Ferromagnetic Core

Problem (Assumed values): A long solenoid has \(n = 500\,\mathrm{turns\,m^{-1}}\) and carries a current \(I = 3.0\,\mathrm{A}\). Its interior is filled with a core of relative permeability \(\mu_r = 250\). Calculate (a) magnetic intensity \(H\), (b) magnetic susceptibility \(\chi\), (c) magnetisation \(M\), and (d) total magnetic field \(B\) inside the core.

Solution:

(a) \(H\) depends only on the free current and winding density: \[ H = nI = 500 \times 3.0 = 1.5 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}} \]

(b) Susceptibility \(\chi = \mu_r - 1 = 250 - 1 = 249\).

(c) Magnetisation \(M = \chi H = 249 \times 1.5 \times 10^3 = 3.735 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\).

(d) Total magnetic field \(B = \mu_0\mu_r H = (4\pi \times 10^{-7})(250)(1.5 \times 10^3) = 0.15\pi \approx 0.47\,\mathrm{T}\).

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ
A bar magnet of magnetic moment \(m\) is cut into two equal halves along its length, and one of these halves is then cut into two equal halves transverse to its length. What is the magnetic moment of each of the smallest pieces?
  • (A) \(m\)
  • (B) \(m/2\)
  • (C) \(m/4\)
  • (D) \(m/8\)
Answer: (C) \(m/4\)

Explanation: Cutting a bar magnet along its length halves its cross-sectional area while keeping length unchanged, so each half has magnetic moment \(m/2\). Cutting that piece transversely halves its length while keeping area unchanged, halving the moment again to \((m/2)/2 = m/4\).

Practice Question 2 • Single-Correct MCQ
A short bar magnet of magnetic moment \(0.5\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}\) is placed in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.2\,\mathrm{T}\). What is the work done by an external agent in rotating the magnet slowly from its most stable equilibrium position to its most unstable equilibrium position?
  • (A) \(0.10\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(0.20\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(-0.20\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(0\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (B) \(0.20\,\mathrm{J}\)

Explanation: Most stable equilibrium is \(\theta_1 = 0^\circ\) (\(U_1 = -mB\)), and most unstable equilibrium is \(\theta_2 = 180^\circ\) (\(U_2 = +mB\)). The work done is \(W = U_2 - U_1 = 2mB = 2 \times 0.5 \times 0.2 = 0.20\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 3 • Statement-Based (NEET UG Format)
Given below are two statements:

Statement II: Isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist.
In the light of the above statements, choose the most appropriate answer:
  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.

Reason (R): Magnetic field lines are slightly concentrated inside a paramagnetic material because its relative permeability is slightly greater than unity.

Practice Question 8 • Single-Correct MCQ
At a distance \(r\) from the centre of a short bar magnet (where \(r\) is much larger than the magnet's length), the magnetic field on the axial line has magnitude \(B_0\). What is the magnitude of the magnetic field on the equatorial line at a distance \(2r\) from the centre of the same magnet?
  • (A) \(B_0 / 4\)
  • (B) \(B_0 / 8\)
  • (C) \(B_0 / 16\)
  • (D) \(B_0 / 2\)
Answer: (C) \(B_0 / 16\)

Explanation: \(B_A(r) = B_0 = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{r^3}\). At distance \(2r\) on the equatorial line, \(B_E(2r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{m}{(2r)^3} = \frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{m}{r^3}\right) = \frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{B_0}{2}\right) = \frac{B_0}{16}\).

Practice Question 9 • Single-Correct MCQ

Practice Question 11 • Single-Correct MCQ
A solenoid with \(n = 400\,\mathrm{turns\,m^{-1}}\) carries a current of \(2.0\,\mathrm{A}\). When a soft iron core of relative permeability \(\mu_r = 500\) is inserted, what is the magnetisation \(M\) inside the core?
  • (A) \(8.0 \times 10^2\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\)
  • (B) \(4.0 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\)
  • (C) \(3.992 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}\)

Practice Question 12 • Single-Correct MCQ
What happens to a ferromagnetic material when it is heated to a sufficiently high temperature?
  • (A) It becomes a superconductor.
  • (B) Its domain structure disintegrates and it becomes paramagnetic.
  • (C) Its relative permeability increases indefinitely.

Answer: (B) Its domain structure disintegrates and it becomes paramagnetic.

Explanation: Thermal agitation disrupts the cooperative alignment of atomic dipoles within domains. Above a high enough temperature, the domain structure breaks down and the ferromagnet behaves as a paramagnet.

9. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common Exam Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Confusing \(\chi\) and \(\mu_r\): Always remember \(\mu_r = 1 + \chi\), not \(\mu_r = \chi\). For a diamagnet, \(\chi\) is negative, so \(0 \le \mu_r < 1\); \(\mu_r\) is never negative.
  • Using \(M = \mu_r H\) instead of \(M = \chi H = (\mu_r - 1)H\): In high-\(\mu_r\) cores, subtracting \(1\) matters when exact options like \(399H\) vs. \(400H\) are tested.
  • Assuming a uniform field exerts a net force on a magnet: A uniform \(\mathbf{B}\) produces zero net force on a magnetic dipole; only a non-uniform field produces a net translational force.
  • Mixing up axial and equatorial directions: On the axis, \(\mathbf{B}_A\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{m}\); on the equatorial bisector, \(\mathbf{B}_E\) is anti-parallel to \(\mathbf{m}\) and half as strong at the same distance \(r \gg l\).
  • Treating magnetic field lines as open curves: Unlike electric field lines, magnetic field lines never start or end on poles—they continue through the interior of the magnet from S to N.

  • Know the exact equilibrium angles: \(\theta = 0^\circ\) (stable, \(U = -mB\)) and \(\theta = 180^\circ\) (unstable, \(U = +mB\)).

10. Concise Final Recap

A bar magnet behaves like a finite current-carrying solenoid and forms closed magnetic field loops, reflecting the absence of magnetic monopoles (\(\phi_B = 0\) over any closed surface). In a uniform magnetic field, a dipole of moment \(\mathbf{m}\) experiences zero net force, a restoring torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}\), and potential energy \(U_m = -\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{B}\).

Inside matter, the total field is \(\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{M}) = \mu_0(1+\chi)\mathbf{H}\). Materials are diamagnetic (\(\chi < 0\), weakly repelled; superconductors have \(\chi = -1, \mu_r = 0\)), paramagnetic (\(\chi > 0\) small, weakly attracted), or ferromagnetic (\(\chi \gg 1\), domain alignment, strongly attracted, transitioning to paramagnetic at high temperatures).
