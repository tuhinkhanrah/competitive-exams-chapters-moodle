2. Electrostatic Potential Energy & Potential Difference

2.1 Conservative Nature of Electrostatic Force

The Coulomb force between two static charges has an inverse-square dependence on distance (\(F \propto 1/r^2\)), identical in mathematical form to Newton's law of gravitation. Consequently, the Coulomb force is a conservative force. Work done by or against the electrostatic field in moving a test charge between two points is strictly path-independent and depends solely on the initial and final coordinates.

When an external agent applies a force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}\) to displace a small test charge \(q\) from an initial point \(R\) to a final point \(P\) without acceleration (\(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} = -\mathbf{F}_E = -q\mathbf{E}\)), the work done by the external agency is:

Work Done by External Force
\[ W_{RP} = \int_{R}^{P} \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\int_{R}^{P} \mathbf{F}_E \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\int_{R}^{P} q\mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \]

This external work is completely stored as the change in electrostatic potential energy (\(\Delta U\)) of the system:

\[ \Delta U = U_P - U_R = W_{RP} = -W_{\mathrm{electric}} \]
Work Sign Convention (High-Yield NEET Alert):
  • Work done by electric force: \(W_{\mathrm{electric}} = -\Delta U = -(U_P - U_R) = U_R - U_P\).
  • Work done by external force (slowly/no \(\Delta K\)): \(W_{\mathrm{ext}} = +\Delta U = U_P - U_R\).
  • If like charges are brought closer, repulsive force opposes motion \(\Rightarrow W_{\mathrm{ext}} > 0\), \(W_{\mathrm{electric}} < 0\), and \(U\) increases (\(\Delta U > 0\)).
  • If unlike charges are brought closer, attractive force aids motion \(\Rightarrow W_{\mathrm{electric}} > 0\), \(W_{\mathrm{ext}} < 0\), and \(U\) decreases (\(\Delta U < 0\)).

2.2 Electrostatic Potential (\(V\))

Electrostatic potential energy is proportional to the magnitude of the test charge \(q\). To obtain a field characteristic independent of the test charge, we define the electrostatic potential (\(V\)) as the potential energy per unit test charge.

Electrostatic Potential Difference
\[ V_P - V_R = \frac{U_P - U_R}{q} = \frac{W_{RP}}{q} = -\int_R^P \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \]

Because only potential differences are physically meaningful, the reference level is arbitrary. By standard convention, potential at infinity is taken as zero: \(V(\infty) = 0\) and \(U(\infty) = 0\).

Definition of Electric Potential at a Point: The electrostatic potential at any point \(P\) is the work done by an external agency in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point at constant speed (without acceleration):

\[ V_P = \frac{W_{\infty P}}{q} \]

SI Unit: volt (\(\mathrm{V}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{V} = 1\,\mathrm{J\,C^{-1}} = 1\,\mathrm{N\,m\,C^{-1}}\).
Dimensional Formula: \([V] = \frac{[W]}{[q]} = \frac{[\mathrm{M L^2 T^{-2}}]}{[\mathrm{A T}]} = [\mathrm{M^1 L^2 T^{-3} A^{-1}}]\).

3. Potential Formulations for Standard Configurations

3.1 Potential Due to an Isolated Point Charge

Consider a point charge \(Q\) located at the origin. To find the potential at a distance \(r\), we calculate the work done in bringing a unit test charge (\(+1\,\mathrm{C}\)) from \(\infty\) to \(r\) radially inward against the repulsive force:

\[ W = -\int_{\infty}^{r} \frac{Q (1)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r'^2} dr' = \left[ \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r'} \right]_{\infty}^{r} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} \]
Potential of a Point Charge
\[ V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r} \]

Note: For \(Q > 0\), \(V > 0\); for \(Q < 0\), \(V < 0\). Unlike the electric field vector (\(\mathbf{E} \propto 1/r^2\)), potential is a scalar quantity decaying as \(1/r\).

Tutorial Figure 1: Variation of electrostatic potential \(V \propto 1/r\) and electric field magnitude \(E \propto 1/r^2\) with radial distance \(r\). Note that for \(r < 1\), \(E > V\), while for \(r > 1\), \(V > E\) in normalized units.

3.2 Potential Due to an Electric Dipole

An electric dipole consists of charges \(-q\) and \(+q\) separated by distance \(2a\). The dipole moment vector is \(\mathbf{p} = q(2a)\hat{\mathbf{p}}\), directed from \(-q\) to \(+q\). For a field point \(P\) located at distance \(r\) from the dipole center at angle \(\theta\) relative to \(\mathbf{p}\):

\[ r_1^2 = r^2 + a^2 - 2ar\cos\theta \approx r^2\left(1 - \frac{2a\cos\theta}{r}\right) \] \[ r_2^2 = r^2 + a^2 + 2ar\cos\theta \approx r^2\left(1 + \frac{2a\cos\theta}{r}\right) \]

Applying the binomial expansion for \(r \gg a\):

\[ \frac{1}{r_1} \approx \frac{1}{r}\left(1 + \frac{a\cos\theta}{r}\right), \quad \frac{1}{r_2} \approx \frac{1}{r}\left(1 - \frac{a\cos\theta}{r}\right) \] \[ V(r,\theta) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2a\cos\theta}{r^2} \]
Electric Dipole Potential (\(r \gg a\))
\[ V(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{p \cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\mathbf{p} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2} \]
Tutorial Figure 2: Geometry for determining potential at point \(P(r, \theta)\) due to an electric dipole of length \(2a\).

Key Dipole Potential Properties for NEET:

  • Axial Line (\(\theta = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\)): \(V_{\mathrm{axial}} = \pm \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^2}\). Positive near \(+q\), negative near \(-q\).
  • Equatorial Line (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)): \(V_{\mathrm{equatorial}} = 0\). The entire equatorial plane is an equipotential surface of zero potential. Work done in carrying any charge along the equatorial plane is identically zero!
  • Distance Dependence: Monopole potential scales as \(1/r\); dipole potential scales as \(1/r^2\); quadrupole potential scales as \(1/r^3\).
  • Axial Symmetry: Rotating point \(P\) around the dipole axis while keeping \(\theta\) and \(r\) constant forms a cone of equal potential.

3.3 Potential Due to a Uniformly Charged Thin Spherical Shell

For a thin spherical conducting shell or insulating shell of radius \(R\) carrying uniform surface charge \(q\):

  • Outside the Shell (\(r \ge R\)): By Gauss's Law, the external field behaves as though the entire charge were concentrated at the center: \[ V_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r} \]
  • At the Surface (\(r = R\)): \[ V_{\mathrm{surface}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R} \]
  • Inside the Shell (\(r < R\)): Since \(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inside}} = 0\) everywhere inside, no work is done against electric forces in moving a charge from the surface to any interior point. Thus, the interior is equipotential: \[ V_{\mathrm{in}} = V_{\mathrm{surface}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R} = \mathrm{constant} \]
NEET Visual Trap (Discontinuity vs Continuity): The electric field \(E\) is discontinuous across the shell boundary (\(E = 0\) inside; \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0\) just outside). However, the electrostatic potential \(V\) is everywhere continuous across the surface.

4. Equipotential Surfaces & Gradient Relation

4.1 Definition and Crucial Properties

An equipotential surface is a locus of all points having the same electric potential. If \(A\) and \(B\) are two points on an equipotential surface, \(V_A = V_B\), which yields:

\[ W_{AB} = q_0 (V_B - V_A) = 0 \]

Since \(W_{AB} = \int_A^B \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0\) for any displacement \(d\mathbf{l}\) lying on the surface, we have:

\[ \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = E\, dl \cos\alpha = 0 \implies \alpha = 90^\circ \]
Fundamental Rules of Equipotential Surfaces:
  1. Electric field lines are always perpendicular to the equipotential surface at every point.
  2. No work is done in moving a charge along an equipotential surface.
  3. Equipotential surfaces never intersect. If they did, two different potential values would exist at the intersection point, implying two distinct electric field directions at a single location, which is physically impossible.
  4. Spacing indicates field strength: Since \(|\mathbf{E}| = |\Delta V / \Delta l|\), regions where equipotential surfaces are crowded denote strong electric fields, whereas widely spaced surfaces denote weak electric fields.
Tutorial Figure 3: Equipotential geometries for an isolated point charge (concentric spheres), a uniform field (parallel planes), and an electric dipole (with central equatorial plane \(V=0\)).

4.2 Mathematical Relation Between \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(V\)

Consider two closely spaced equipotential surfaces \(A\) and \(B\) with potentials \(V\) and \(V + \delta V\), separated by normal distance \(\delta l\). The work done per unit positive charge in moving from \(B\) to \(A\) against \(\mathbf{E}\) is:

\[ |\mathbf{E}| \delta l = V - (V + \delta V) = -\delta V \implies |\mathbf{E}| = -\frac{\delta V}{\delta l} \]
Field-Potential Differential Relations
\[ E = -\frac{dV}{dr} \quad \text{and in 3D Cartesian coordinates:} \quad \mathbf{E} = -\left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\hat{\mathbf{k}} \right) \]

Physical Deductions:

  1. Direction: Electric field points in the direction of the steepest decrease of electric potential.
  2. Magnitude: The magnitude of the electric field equals the rate of decrease of potential per unit displacement normal to the equipotential surface.

5. Electrostatic Potential Energy of Systems & External Fields

5.1 Assembly of a System of Point Charges

The potential energy of a system of charges equals the total external work required to assemble the charges from an initial infinite mutual separation to their specified locations without acceleration.

  • Two-Charge System: Bringing \(q_1\) from \(\infty\) requires \(W_1 = 0\) (no field present). Bringing \(q_2\) to distance \(r_{12}\) requires \(W_2 = q_2 V_1(r_2) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}\): \[ U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} \]
  • Three-Charge System: \[ U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left( \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1 q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2 q_3}{r_{23}} \right) \]
  • System of \(N\) Charges: Sum over all unique interaction pairs: \[ U = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j \neq i}^{N} \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} = \sum_{1 \le i < j \le N} \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} \] The total number of pairwise interaction terms is \(\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\). For a square arrangement of 4 charges, there are \(\frac{4 \times 3}{2} = 6\) pairs (4 sides and 2 diagonals).

5.2 Charges in an External Potential Field \(V_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{r})\)

When external sources maintain an ambient potential \(V(\mathbf{r})\):

  • Single Charge: Potential energy \(U = q V(\mathbf{r})\).
  • System of Two Charges in External Field: \[ U = q_1 V(\mathbf{r}_1) + q_2 V(\mathbf{r}_2) + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} \]

5.3 Dipole in a Uniform External Electric Field

In a uniform field \(\mathbf{E}\), the net force on an electric dipole is zero (\(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = q\mathbf{E} - q\mathbf{E} = 0\)). However, it experiences a restoring torque:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E} \implies \tau = pE\sin\theta \]

Work done by an external torque in rotating the dipole slowly from angle \(\theta_0\) to \(\theta\) is:

\[ W_{\mathrm{ext}} = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta} \tau_{\mathrm{ext}} d\theta = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta} pE\sin\theta\,d\theta = pE(\cos\theta_0 - \cos\theta) \]

Choosing the standard reference \(\theta_0 = 90^\circ\) (\(\pi/2\,\mathrm{rad}\)), where \(U(90^\circ) = 0\):

Potential Energy of a Dipole in an Electric Field
\[ U(\theta) = -pE\cos\theta = -\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{E} \]
Orientation (\(\theta\)) Torque (\(\tau = pE\sin\theta\)) Potential Energy (\(U = -pE\cos\theta\)) Equilibrium State & Physics
\(\theta = 0^\circ\) (\(\mathbf{p} \parallel \mathbf{E}\)) \(0\) \(-pE\) (Minimum) Stable Equilibrium: Small displacements result in a restoring torque directed back toward \(\theta = 0\).
\(\theta = 90^\circ\) (\(\mathbf{p} \perp \mathbf{E}\)) \(pE\) (Maximum) \(0\) Maximum restoring torque, non-equilibrium.
\(\theta = 180^\circ\) (\(\mathbf{p} \ anti\parallel \mathbf{E}\)) \(0\) \(+pE\) (Maximum) Unstable Equilibrium: Small angular displacement produces torque accelerating the dipole away from \(180^\circ\).

Work Required to Rotate Dipole: The work required to rotate the dipole from orientation \(\theta_1\) to \(\theta_2\) is \(W = U(\theta_2) - U(\theta_1) = pE(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2)\). Specifically, rotating from stable equilibrium (\(0^\circ\)) to unstable equilibrium (\(180^\circ\)) requires \(W = pE(1 - (-1)) = 2pE\).

6. Electrostatics of Conductors & Shielding

Metallic conductors possess abundant mobile conduction electrons. Under static conditions (current \(I = 0\)), conductors exhibit six fundamental properties:

  1. Inside a conductor, the electrostatic field is zero (\(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{inside}} = 0\)): If an internal field existed, free charges would accelerate under \(\mathbf{F} = -e\mathbf{E}\). Charge migration continues until surface charges establish an internal opposing field that completely neutralizes the applied field inside.
  2. At the surface of a charged conductor, \(\mathbf{E}\) is strictly perpendicular to the surface at every point: Any tangential component \(E_{\parallel}\) would exert tangential forces on surface electrons, causing surface currents. Since static charges are at rest, \(E_{\parallel} = 0\).
  3. The interior of a conductor has no excess charge: By Gauss's Law, applying \(\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\) to any Gaussian surface enclosed wholly inside the conductor (where \(\mathbf{E} = 0\)) yields \(q_{\mathrm{enc}} = 0\). Any excess charge resides exclusively on the outer surface.
  4. Potential is constant throughout the volume and surface of a conductor: Since \(\mathbf{E} = 0\) throughout the bulk and \(E_{\parallel} = 0\) on the surface, \(\Delta V = -\int \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0\). Thus, every conductor is an equipotential body.
  5. Electric field just outside a charged conductor is \(E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\): By choosing a tiny pillbox Gaussian surface half inside and half outside the conductor: \[ \Phi = E\,\delta S + 0 = \frac{\sigma\,\delta S}{\varepsilon_0} \implies \mathbf{E} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf{n}} \] where \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is the outward unit normal.
  6. Electrostatic Shielding: In any empty cavity within a conductor (free of enclosed charges), \(\mathbf{E} = 0\) identically, regardless of external charges or potentials applied to the conductor. The interior cavity is perfectly isolated from external electrical influences.
Tutorial Figure 4: Electrostatic equilibrium of a charged conductor with an empty internal cavity. Charges reside strictly on the exterior surface; the electric field inside the conductor bulk and cavity is zero.
High-Yield NEET Conceptual Scenarios:
  • Bird on a High-Voltage Line: Both feet rest on the same wire at virtually identical potential (\(\Delta V \approx 0\)), hence zero current traverses its body. A human on the ground touches the wire and completes a circuit to zero ground potential, receiving a lethal shock.
  • Lightning Safety: It is significantly safer inside a closed metal car than under an open tree during a thunderstorm; the metal chassis acts as an electrostatic shield cavity.
  • Asymmetry of Shielding: A conductor cavity shields internal objects from external fields. However, placing a charge \(+q\) inside the cavity induces \(-q\) on the cavity wall and \(+q\) on the outer surface, producing external fields unless the outer shell is grounded.

7. Dielectrics and Dielectric Polarization

7.1 Polar vs Non-Polar Molecules

Dielectrics are insulating substances containing essentially zero mobile charge carriers. They respond to applied electric fields via displacement of bound charges.

  • Non-Polar Dielectrics (e.g., \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{N_2}\), \(\mathrm{O_2}\), \(\mathrm{CO_2}\)): The centers of positive and negative charge distributions coincide due to molecular symmetry. Permanent dipole moment is zero. An applied field \(\mathbf{E}_0\) stretches the electron cloud, inducing a dipole moment parallel to the field.
  • Polar Dielectrics (e.g., \(\mathrm{HCl}\), \(\mathrm{H_2O}\)): Centers of positive and negative charges are separated by asymmetric chemical bonds, producing a permanent intrinsic dipole moment. In the absence of an external field, thermal agitation causes random dipole orientations (\(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{net}} = 0\)). An applied field \(\mathbf{E}_0\) exerts a torque aligning the dipoles with the field against thermal agitation.

7.2 Polarization Vector (\(\mathbf{P}\)) and Electric Susceptibility

The polarization \(\mathbf{P}\) is defined as the net induced dipole moment per unit volume:

\[ \mathbf{P} = \frac{\sum \mathbf{p}}{\Delta V} \quad (\text{SI Unit: }\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}) \]

For linear isotropic dielectrics, \(\mathbf{P}\) is directly proportional to the total macroscopic electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) in the medium:

Polarization Vector & Susceptibility
\[ \mathbf{P} = \varepsilon_0 \chi_e \mathbf{E} \]

where \(\chi_e\) is the dimensionless electric susceptibility of the medium.

7.3 Induced Bound Surface Charge Density (\(\sigma_p\)) & Dielectric Constant

Inside a uniformly polarized dielectric slab, positive and negative ends of adjacent dipoles cancel macroscopically (\(\rho_{\mathrm{bound}} = 0\)). However, at the unneutralized boundary faces, bound surface charges appear with densities \(+\sigma_p\) and \(-\sigma_p\). These bound surface charges produce an opposing induced electric field \(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{in}}\):

\[ E_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{\sigma_p}{\varepsilon_0} \]

The net reduced electric field inside the dielectric medium is:

\[ E = E_0 - E_{\mathrm{in}} = E_0 - \frac{\sigma_p}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{E_0}{K} \]

where \(K\) is the dielectric constant (relative permittivity \(\varepsilon_r = \varepsilon/\varepsilon_0\)). Since \(\mathbf{P} = \sigma_p \hat{\mathbf{n}}\):

\[ \sigma_p = \sigma\left(1 - \frac{1}{K}\right) \quad \text{and} \quad K = 1 + \chi_e \]
Tutorial Figure 5: Dielectric slab in an external field. Induced surface charge density \(\sigma_p\) reduces the macroscopic interior electric field to \(E = E_0/K\).

8. Capacitors and the Parallel Plate Capacitor

8.1 Fundamental Definition of Capacitance

A capacitor consists of two conducting plates separated by an insulating gap. Transferring charge \(Q\) between the plates creates a potential difference \(V\). Since \(\mathbf{E} \propto Q\) by Coulomb's law, \(V \propto Q\). The constant of proportionality is the capacitance (\(C\)):

Capacitance Equation
\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} \]

Crucial Physical Fact: Capacitance \(C\) is independent of \(Q\) and \(V\). It is determined purely by the geometric dimensions (area, spacing, shape) and the permittivity of the intervening medium.
SI Unit: farad (\(\mathrm{F}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{F} = 1\,\mathrm{C\,V^{-1}}\). Practical micro-units include \(1\,\mathrm{\mu F} = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{F}\), \(1\,\mathrm{nF} = 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{F}\), and \(1\,\mathrm{pF} = 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F}\).
Dielectric Strength: The maximum electric field that a dielectric medium can withstand without electrical breakdown (ionizing discharge). For dry air at atmospheric pressure, dielectric strength is approximately \(3 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\).

8.2 Parallel Plate Capacitor in Vacuum

Consider two parallel conducting plates of large area \(A\) separated by a small gap \(d\) (\(d^2 \ll A\)), carrying surface charge densities \(+\sigma\) and \(-\sigma\) (\(\sigma = Q/A\)):

  • Outer Region I (above plate 1): \(E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} - \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = 0\).
  • Outer Region II (below plate 2): \(E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} - \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = 0\).
  • Inner Gap Region: Electric fields from both plates reinforce: \[ E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} + \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A} \]

The potential difference between plates is \(V = Ed = \frac{Q d}{\varepsilon_0 A}\). Substituting into \(C = Q/V\):

Capacitance of Parallel Plate Capacitor (Vacuum)
\[ C_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} \]

8.3 Effect of Dielectrics on Parallel Plate Capacitor

When the intervening space is completely filled with a dielectric of constant \(K\), the interior field drops to \(E = E_0/K\), and voltage drops to \(V = V_0/K\). Hence:

\[ C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{Q}{V_0 / K} = K C_0 = \frac{K \varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d} \]

Partially Filled Dielectric Slab (Thickness \(t < d\)):

If a dielectric slab of thickness \(t\) and constant \(K\) is inserted into the plate gap \(d\):

\[ V = E_0 (d - t) + E_{\mathrm{dielectric}} t = E_0 (d - t) + \frac{E_0}{K} t = E_0 \left( d - t + \frac{t}{K} \right) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}\left( d - t + \frac{t}{K} \right) \]
Capacitor with Partially Filled Dielectric Slab
\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t + \frac{t}{K}} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t\left(1 - \frac{1}{K}\right)} \]
Special Case: Conducting Slab (\(K = \infty\)) of Thickness \(t\): Inside a conducting metal slab, \(E = 0\) (\(K \to \infty\)). The effective plate separation decreases to \((d - t)\): \[ C_{\mathrm{metal}} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t} \] Notice that \(C \to \infty\) if the conducting slab touches both plates (\(t = d\)), short-circuiting the capacitor.

8.4 Battery Connected vs Disconnected (The Classic NEET Grid)

Analyzing what happens to physical quantities when a dielectric slab is inserted under two experimental protocols is a perennial NEET favorite:

Physical Parameter Case 1: Battery Remains Connected (\(V = \text{const}\)) Case 2: Battery Disconnected (\(Q = \text{const}\))
Capacitance (\(C\)) Increases: \(C = K C_0\) Increases: \(C = K C_0\)
Potential Difference (\(V\)) Constant: \(V = V_0\) Decreases: \(V = V_0 / K\)
Charge (\(Q\)) Increases: \(Q = K Q_0\) (battery supplies extra charge) Constant: \(Q = Q_0\) (isolated plates)
Electric Field (\(E\)) Constant: \(E = V/d = V_0/d = E_0\) Decreases: \(E = E_0 / K\)
Stored Energy (\(U\)) Increases: \(U = \frac{1}{2} C V^2 = K U_0\) Decreases: \(U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = U_0 / K\)

9. Combinations of Capacitors

9.1 Capacitors in Series

In a series combination, capacitors are connected head-to-tail such that the charge on every plate has the identical magnitude \(Q\) by electrostatic induction, while the total applied voltage divides across the components:

\[ V = V_1 + V_2 + \dots + V_n = \frac{Q}{C_1} + \frac{Q}{C_2} + \dots + \frac{Q}{C_n} \]
Equivalent Capacitance in Series
\[ \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{eq}}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{C_n} \]

Special Case for Two Capacitors: \(C_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}\).
Voltage Division Rule: \(V_1 = V \left(\frac{C_2}{C_1 + C_2}\right)\) and \(V_2 = V \left(\frac{C_1}{C_1 + C_2}\right)\). Note inverse proportionality: the smaller capacitor carries the larger voltage drop!

9.2 Capacitors in Parallel

In a parallel combination, all positive plates connect to a common high-potential node and all negative plates connect to a common low-potential node. The potential difference across each capacitor is identical (\(V\)), while charges add up:

\[ Q_{\mathrm{total}} = Q_1 + Q_2 + \dots + Q_n = C_1 V + C_2 V + \dots + C_n V \]
Equivalent Capacitance in Parallel
\[ C_{\mathrm{eq}} = C_1 + C_2 + \dots + C_n \]

Charge Division Rule: \(Q_1 = Q_{\mathrm{total}} \left(\frac{C_1}{C_1 + C_2}\right)\). Direct proportionality: the larger capacitor stores greater charge.

10. Energy Stored in a Capacitor & Redistribution Loss

10.1 Derivation of Electrostatic Energy Stored

To charge a capacitor, infinitesimal charge elements \(\delta Q'\) are incrementally transferred from the negative plate to the positive plate against the momentary potential difference \(V' = Q'/C\). The incremental work is:

\[ \delta W = V' \delta Q' = \frac{Q'}{C}\delta Q' \]

Integrating from an initially uncharged state (\(Q'=0\)) to final charge \(Q\):

\[ W = \int_0^Q \frac{Q'}{C}dQ' = \frac{1}{C}\left[\frac{Q'^2}{2}\right]_0^Q = \frac{Q^2}{2C} \]
Electrostatic Energy Stored
\[ U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}QV \]
The "Factor of 1/2" Battery Paradox: When a battery of electromotive force \(V\) charges a capacitor to charge \(Q\), the work done by the battery is \(W_{\mathrm{battery}} = Q V\). However, the energy stored in the capacitor is only \(\frac{1}{2}QV\). Exactly \(50\%\) of the energy supplied by the battery is dissipated as heat in the connecting leads (or electromagnetic radiation) during the charging transient, irrespective of wire resistance!

10.2 Energy Density of an Electrostatic Field

Energy stored in a parallel plate capacitor can be expressed in terms of the uniform electric field \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)\) occupying volume \(\mathrm{Vol} = Ad\):

\[ U = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{(\sigma A)^2}{\varepsilon_0 A / d} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 \left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\right)^2 (Ad) = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 (Ad) \]

Dividing by volume \(Ad\) yields the energy density (\(u\)):

Electric Energy Density
\[ u = \frac{U}{\mathrm{Volume}} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2 \quad (\text{In a dielectric medium: } u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon E^2 = \frac{1}{2}K\varepsilon_0 E^2) \]

10.3 Redistribution of Charge & Energy Loss on Connecting Two Capacitors

When a charged capacitor \(C_1\) at potential \(V_1\) is connected in parallel to a capacitor \(C_2\) at potential \(V_2\), charge transfers until they reach a common potential (\(V_{\mathrm{common}}\)):

Common Potential
\[ V_{\mathrm{common}} = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{total}}}{C_{\mathrm{total}}} = \frac{C_1 V_1 + C_2 V_2}{C_1 + C_2} \]

If plates of opposite polarities are connected together, \(V_{\mathrm{common}} = \frac{|C_1 V_1 - C_2 V_2|}{C_1 + C_2}\).

Energy Loss During Sharing (\(\Delta U_{\mathrm{loss}}\)):

The initial total energy is \(U_i = \frac{1}{2}C_1 V_1^2 + \frac{1}{2}C_2 V_2^2\). The final total energy is \(U_f = \frac{1}{2}(C_1 + C_2)V_{\mathrm{common}}^2\). The loss in stored electrostatic energy, dissipated as heat and sparks, is:

Loss of Electrostatic Energy on Sharing
\[ \Delta U_{\mathrm{loss}} = U_i - U_f = \frac{C_1 C_2}{2(C_1 + C_2)}(V_1 - V_2)^2 \]

Notice that \(\Delta U_{\mathrm{loss}} \ge 0\) always; energy is never gained. Energy is conserved globally: the lost potential energy converts to thermal energy in the connecting wires.

11. Comprehensive Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Zero Potential Coordinates for Dipole System

Two point charges \(q_1 = +3 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}\) and \(q_2 = -2 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{C}\) are located \(15\,\mathrm{cm}\) apart on the \(x\)-axis. Taking \(V(\infty) = 0\), find the points on the line joining the two charges where the net electrostatic potential is zero.

Solution:

Let \(q_1\) be placed at the origin \(x = 0\) and \(q_2\) at \(x = 15\,\mathrm{cm}\). Since potential is a scalar, \(V(x) = V_1(x) + V_2(x) = 0\):

\[ \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{3 \times 10^{-8}}{|x|} + \frac{-2 \times 10^{-8}}{|x - 15|}\right] = 0 \implies \frac{3}{|x|} = \frac{2}{|x - 15|} \]

Case 1: Interior point between the charges (\(0 < x < 15\,\mathrm{cm}\)):

\[ \frac{3}{x} = \frac{2}{15 - x} \implies 3(15 - x) = 2x \implies 45 - 3x = 2x \implies 5x = 45 \implies x = 9\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Case 2: Exterior point beyond the smaller charge (\(x > 15\,\mathrm{cm}\)):

\[ \frac{3}{x} = \frac{2}{x - 15} \implies 3(x - 15) = 2x \implies 3x - 45 = 2x \implies x = 45\,\mathrm{cm} \]

Note: For \(x < 0\), \(\frac{3}{|x|} > \frac{2}{|x - 15|}\) always, so potential cannot vanish to the left of \(q_1\).
Answer: The potential is zero at \(9\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(45\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the positive charge along the direction of the negative charge.

Worked Example 2: Energy Stored and Field Gradient in Partially Filled Capacitor

A parallel plate capacitor with plate area \(A = 100\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) and separation \(d = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) is connected to a \(120\,\mathrm{V}\) battery. A dielectric slab of thickness \(t = 0.5\,\mathrm{cm}\) and dielectric constant \(K = 4\) is inserted between the plates. Calculate: (a) the original capacitance \(C_0\), (b) the new capacitance \(C\), and (c) the total charge supplied by the battery during insertion.

Solution:

(a) Initial capacitance \(C_0\):

\[ A = 100 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2} = 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2}, \quad d = 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} \] \[ C_0 = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 10^{-2}}{10^{-2}} = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 8.85\,\mathrm{pF} \]

(b) Capacitance with dielectric slab:

\[ C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t\left(1 - \frac{1}{K}\right)} \] \[ d - t\left(1 - \frac{1}{K}\right) = 1.0\,\mathrm{cm} - 0.5\,\mathrm{cm}\left(1 - \frac{1}{4}\right) = 1.0 - 0.5(0.75) = 1.0 - 0.375 = 0.625\,\mathrm{cm} = 6.25 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} \] \[ C = \frac{8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 10^{-2}}{6.25 \times 10^{-3}} = 1.417 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{F} \approx 14.17\,\mathrm{pF} \]

(c) Extra charge supplied by the battery:

\[ \Delta Q = (C - C_0)V = (14.17 \times 10^{-12} - 8.85 \times 10^{-12}) \times 120 = 5.32 \times 10^{-12} \times 120 = 6.38 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{C} = 0.638\,\mathrm{nC} \]
Worked Example 3: Charge Redistribution & Energy Dissipation

A \(600\,\mathrm{pF}\) capacitor is charged by a \(200\,\mathrm{V}\) power supply. It is then disconnected from the supply and connected in parallel across an uncharged \(600\,\mathrm{pF}\) capacitor. (a) What is the common potential difference? (b) How much electrostatic energy is lost in the process?

Solution:

(a) Common Potential:

\[ C_1 = 600\,\mathrm{pF}, \quad V_1 = 200\,\mathrm{V}, \quad C_2 = 600\,\mathrm{pF}, \quad V_2 = 0\,\mathrm{V} \] \[ V_{\mathrm{common}} = \frac{C_1 V_1 + C_2 V_2}{C_1 + C_2} = \frac{600 \times 200 + 0}{600 + 600} = \frac{120000}{1200} = 100\,\mathrm{V} \]

(b) Energy Loss:

\[ \Delta U_{\mathrm{loss}} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{2(C_1 + C_2)}(V_1 - V_2)^2 = \frac{(600 \times 10^{-12})(600 \times 10^{-12})}{2(1200 \times 10^{-12})}(200 - 0)^2 \] \[ \Delta U_{\mathrm{loss}} = \frac{36 \times 10^{-20}}{24 \times 10^{-10}} \times 40000 = 1.5 \times 10^{-10} \times 4 \times 10^4 = 6.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{J} = 6\,\mathrm{\mu J} \]

Check: Initial energy \(U_i = \frac{1}{2} C_1 V_1^2 = \frac{1}{2}(600 \times 10^{-12})(40000) = 12\,\mathrm{\mu J}\). Final energy \(U_f = \frac{1}{2}(C_1 + C_2)V_{\mathrm{common}}^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1200 \times 10^{-12})(10000) = 6\,\mathrm{\mu J}\). Exactly \(6\,\mathrm{\mu J}\) (\(50\%\)) is dissipated as heat and electromagnetic radiation.

13. Common NEET Pitfalls & High-Yield Revision Checklist

Trap 1: Confusing Scalar \(V\) with Vector \(\mathbf{E}\) When calculating potential due to multiple charges, do NOT resolve into vector components. Sum potentials algebraically with their algebraic signs included: \[ V_{\mathrm{net}} = \sum \frac{q_i}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_i} \]
Trap 2: Forgetting Sign in \(W = -q\Delta V\) Work done by the electric field is \(W_E = -q(V_f - V_i)\). Work done by an external agent is \(W_{\mathrm{ext}} = +q(V_f - V_i)\). Always double-check whose work is requested!
Trap 3: Battery "Connected" vs "Disconnected" Before calculating new values after dielectric insertion:
  • If battery is connected: \(V\) is pinned constant.
  • If battery is disconnected: \(Q\) is trapped constant.
Rapid Revision Checklist (Formulas at a Glance):
  • Point charge: \(V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r}\)
  • Dipole: \(V = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\); \(V_{\mathrm{eq}} = 0\)
  • Shell (\(r \le R\)): \(V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} = \text{const}\); \(E_{\mathrm{in}} = 0\)
  • Potential Gradient: \(E = -dV/dr\)
  • Dipole in field: \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{E}\); \(U = -\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{E}\)
  • Capacitor: \(C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\); With dielectric: \(C = \frac{K\varepsilon_0 A}{d}\)
  • Slab \(t\): \(C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t(1 - 1/K)}\)
  • Energy: \(U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}QV\)
  • Energy density: \(u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2\)
  • Common Potential: \(V_c = \frac{C_1 V_1 + C_2 V_2}{C_1 + C_2}\)
  • Loss on sharing: \(\Delta U = \frac{C_1 C_2}{2(C_1 + C_2)}(V_1 - V_2)^2\)

14. Concise Final Recap

Electrostatics can be fully understood through the duality between vector field concepts (\(\mathbf{E}\), forces) and scalar energetic concepts (\(V\), potential energy). Electrostatic potential \(V\) characterizes space around charge distributions, allowing the calculation of work via simple scalar arithmetic. The electric field is the negative spatial gradient of potential (\(\mathbf{E} = -\boldsymbol{\nabla} V\)), directing from regions of high potential to low potential perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.

In materials, conductors act as equipotential reservoirs with zero internal field and complete electrostatic shielding, while dielectrics undergo molecular polarization, reducing macroscopic internal fields by factor \(K\). In capacitors, this effect allows increased charge storage at lower potential differences, storing energy within the electric field at density \(u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2\). Mastering these connections ensures complete confidence for NEET UG.
