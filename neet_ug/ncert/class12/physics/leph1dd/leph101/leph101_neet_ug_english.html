2. Nature and Fundamental Properties of Electric Charge

Electrostatics is the branch of physics dealing with forces, fields, and potentials arising from static (stationary) charges. The ancient Greeks noticed that amber rubbed with wool attracts lightweight objects like dry leaves or bits of straw. The root word elektron in Greek means amber.

Careful experiments show that rubbing pairs of insulating bodies results in two distinct states of electrification:

  • A glass rod rubbed with silk acquires a positive charge; the silk cloth acquires an equal magnitude of negative charge.
  • A plastic (or ebonite) rod rubbed with cat's fur acquires a negative charge; the fur acquires an equal magnitude of positive charge.
  • Fundamental Law of Electrostatics: Like charges repel each other; unlike charges attract each other.
Tutorial Figure 1: Polarity of electric charges showing repulsion between like charges and mutual attraction between unlike charges.

Microscopic Origin of Charging

All normal matter consists of neutral atoms comprising positively charged nuclei surrounded by negatively charged electrons. When two neutral bodies are rubbed together, frictional work transfers the most loosely bound valence electrons from the material with lower work function to the material with higher electron affinity.

  • Deficit of electrons: Leaves the body with net positive charge.
  • Excess of electrons: Endows the body with net negative charge.
  • No new charge is created. The total number of protons and electrons across the interacting pair remains absolutely constant.
  • Because electrons possess mass (\(m_e \approx 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}\)), transfer of negative charge strictly involves a minuscule transfer of mass. A negatively charged body gains a tiny amount of mass, while a positively charged body loses mass.

Conductors, Insulators, and Electroscopes

Materials are categorized electrically by the mobility of their internal charges:

  • Conductors: Contain free electrons that roam throughout the bulk material (e.g., metals, aqueous electrolyte solutions, human body, Earth). Added charge spreads immediately over the entire outer boundary surface.
  • Insulators (Dielectrics): Have tightly bound electrons with virtually no mobility (e.g., glass, porcelain, rubber, dry wood, nylon). Added charge remains confined to the exact region where it was deposited.
  • Semiconductors: Possess electrical resistance intermediate between conductors and insulators (e.g., silicon, germanium).

A gold-leaf electroscope detects electric charge. It comprises a vertical conducting brass rod supported by an insulating rubber stopper inside a glass box, with two thin flexible gold leaves attached at its base. When a charged rod contacts the metal knob at the top, like charges flow down to both gold leaves. The leaves repel each other and diverge; the angular separation of divergence provides a qualitative measure of the amount of charge.

Three Invariant Axioms of Electric Charge

  1. Additivity of Charge: Electric charge is a scalar quantity. The total charge of an assembly of discrete point charges is the algebraic sum of individual charges taking proper algebraic signs into account: \[ Q_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i = q_1 + q_2 + q_3 + \dots + q_n \]
  2. Conservation of Charge: The algebraic sum of all electric charges in an isolated system remains constant over time. Although charged particles can be created or annihilated (such as pair production \(\gamma \to e^- + e^+\), or beta decay of a neutron \(n \to p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e\)), the algebraic sum of charges before and after the process is invariant.
  3. Quantisation of Charge: All observable free charges exist exclusively in integral multiples of the elementary fundamental quantum \(e\): \[ q = n e, \quad \text{where } n \in \mathbb{Z} \quad (n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots) \] where \(e = 1.602192 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}\).
NEET High-Yield Insight: Macroscopic vs. Microscopic Scale

At the macroscopic scale, typical charges are on the order of microcoulombs (\(1\,\mathrm{µC} = 10^{-6}\,\text{C}\)). The number of elementary charges in \(1\,\mathrm{µC}\) is: \[ n = \frac{10^{-6}\,\text{C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} \approx 6.25 \times 10^{12} \] Because \(n\) is vast, adding or removing a few thousand electrons produces a fractional change far below experimental detection. Thus, at macroscopic scales, charge appears continuous, and quantisation can be safely ignored. At the atomic or nuclear scale (a few tens or hundreds of \(e\)), discreteness dominates and quantisation cannot be neglected.

3. Coulomb's Law and Electrostatic Interactions

In 1785, Charles Augustin de Coulomb used a sensitive torsion balance to measure the mechanical force between small charged spheres. He formulated the quantitative fundamental law:

Coulomb's Law (Scalar Form)

The magnitude of the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges in vacuum is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance separating them:

\[ F = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2} \]

Here, \(\varepsilon_0\) is the permittivity of free space (vacuum). In SI units: \[ \varepsilon_0 \approx 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{C}^2\,\text{N}^{-1}\,\text{m}^{-2} \quad (\text{or }\text{F m}^{-1}) \] \[ k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 8.987 \times 10^9 \approx 9.0 \times 10^9\,\text{N}\,\text{m}^2\,\text{C}^{-2} \]

Definition of One Coulomb (SI Unit)

Setting \(q_1 = q_2 = 1\,\text{C}\) and \(r = 1\,\text{m}\) in vacuum: \[ F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{(1)(1)}{1^2} = 9 \times 10^9\,\text{N} \] One Coulomb is defined as that quantity of static charge which, when placed in vacuum at a distance of \(1\,\text{meter}\) from an identical equal charge, experiences a mutual electrostatic repulsive force of \(9 \times 10^9\,\text{N}\).

Vector Form and Newton's Third Law

Let position vectors of charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) relative to an origin \(O\) be \(\mathbf{r}_1\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2\). The displacement vector pointing from \(q_1\) to \(q_2\) is \(\mathbf{r}_{21} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1\), with magnitude \(r_{21} = |\mathbf{r}_{21}|\) and unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21} = \mathbf{r}_{21}/r_{21}\).

The electrostatic force \(\mathbf{F}_{21}\) exerted on charge \(q_2\) by charge \(q_1\) is: \[ \mathbf{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{21}^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21} \] Similarly, the force \(\mathbf{F}_{12}\) exerted on charge \(q_1\) by charge \(q_2\) is: \[ \mathbf{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12} \] Since \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12} = -\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21}\) and \(r_{12} = r_{21}\), we obtain: \[ \mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21} \] Coulomb's law is fully consistent with Newton's third law of motion: electrostatic forces between two point charges form an action-reaction pair directed along the mutual line of centers (central forces).

Tutorial Figure 2: Action-reaction force vectors between two like point charges.

Comparison: Electrostatic Force vs. Gravitational Force

Both Coulomb's law and Newton's law of gravitation are inverse-square central forces depending on distance as \(1/r^2\). However, two vital distinctions exist:

  • Sign & Polarity: Gravitational force is strictly attractive, whereas electrostatic force can be attractive or repulsive. Because macroscopic bodies contain almost perfectly balanced quantities of positive and negative charges, bulk electrical forces cancel, permitting the far weaker gravitational force to dominate celestial dynamics.
  • Relative Strength: For an electron and a proton separated by distance \(r\): \[ \frac{F_e}{F_G} = \frac{\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}}{\frac{G m_p m_e}{r^2}} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 G m_p m_e} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{39} \] For two protons, \(F_e / F_G \approx 1.3 \times 10^{36}\). Electrostatic interaction is unimaginably stronger than gravity.

Principle of Superposition for Multiple Charges

Coulomb's law describes the pairwise interaction between two point charges. When an assembly of \(n\) stationary point charges \(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\) interacts, experiments demonstrate that the presence of third or fourth charges does not modify the mutual pairwise interaction between any two charges.

Principle of Superposition

The net electrostatic force exerted on any individual charge \(q_1\) is the vector sum of all individual Coulomb forces exerted on it by each of the other charges evaluated independently:

\[ \mathbf{F}_1 = \mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{13} + \dots + \mathbf{F}_{1n} = \frac{q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=2}^{n} \frac{q_i}{r_{1i}^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{1i} \]

4. The Electric Field

To eliminate the philosophically challenging concept of "action-at-a-distance," Michael Faraday introduced the concept of the electric field. A source charge \(Q\) modifies the physical state of the space around it, creating an electric vector field \(\mathbf{E}\). When another charge \(q\) is placed at a point, it experiences a mechanical force through direct local interaction with the electric field at that position.

Operational Definition

The electric field \(\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})\) at a spatial point \(\mathbf{r}\) is formally defined as the electrostatic force experienced per unit positive test charge placed at that location:

\[ \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{F}}{q_0} \]

The limit \(q_0 \to 0\) guarantees that the test charge is infinitesimal, ensuring it does not disturb or redistribute the source charges generating the field.

  • Field of a Point Charge \(Q\) at the origin: \[ \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \]
  • For \(Q > 0\), \(\mathbf{E}\) points radially outward; for \(Q < 0\), \(\mathbf{E}\) points radially inward.
  • SI Unit: \(\text{N C}^{-1}\) (newtons per coulomb) or equivalently \(\text{V m}^{-1}\) (volts per meter).
  • Dimensions: \([\text{M L T}^{-3} \text{A}^{-1}]\).

Physical Reality and Finite Propagation Speed

In static situations, the electric field can be viewed as an elegant calculational framework. However, in time-dependent dynamics, if a source charge accelerates, the disturbance does not affect distant charges instantaneously. Information propagates outward as an electromagnetic wave at the finite speed of light \(c\). The electric field is an authentic physical entity with its own independent equations of motion, momentum, and energy density.

Motion of a Charged Particle in a Uniform Electric Field

When a particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) enters a uniform electric field \(\mathbf{E}\), it experiences a constant electrostatic force \(\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{E}\), resulting in an acceleration: \[ \mathbf{a} = \frac{q\mathbf{E}}{m} \] Because \(a \propto q/m\) (the specific charge), particles with distinct charge-to-mass ratios follow distinct trajectories.

5. Electric Field Lines

An electric field line is an imaginary continuous path or curve drawn through a region of space such that the tangent drawn at any point gives the direction of the net electric field vector \(\mathbf{E}\) at that point.

Fundamental Rules Governing Electric Field Lines

  1. Origin and Termination: Field lines start from positive charges and terminate on negative charges. If there is an isolated single charge, field lines start from the positive charge and extend to infinity, or start from infinity and terminate on the negative charge.
  2. Continuity: In any charge-free region of space, field lines are smooth, continuous curves without sudden breaks or gaps.
  3. Non-Intersection Property: Two field lines can never intersect or cross each other. If they crossed at a point, two distinct tangents could be drawn at that single geometric location, which would imply two contradictory directions for the net electric field—a physical impossibility.
  4. No Electrostatic Closed Loops: Electrostatic field lines can never form closed loops. This property is a direct consequence of the conservative nature of static electric forces (the round-trip line integral \(\oint \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{l} = 0\)). Closed loops would permit net work to be extracted from a static field in violation of energy conservation.
  5. Field Density and Solid Angle: The relative crowding (density) of field lines per unit cross-sectional area perpendicular to the lines is directly proportional to the magnitude of \(\mathbf{E}\). Near a point charge, the area of a sphere subtending solid angle \(\Delta \Omega\) grows as \(r^2 \Delta \Omega\). Because the total number of radial lines within \(\Delta \Omega\) is constant, the number of lines per unit area scales as \(1/r^2\), matching the inverse-square law.

6. Electric Flux

By analogy to the volumetric rate of fluid flow through a boundary surface, electric flux measures the total number of electric field lines penetrating through a given area.

Area Vector Convention

A planar surface area element of magnitude \(\Delta S\) is treated mathematically as a vector \(\Delta \mathbf{S} = \Delta S\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is a unit normal vector perpendicular to the surface.

  • Open Surfaces: The unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) can be chosen along either side of the surface, provided the choice is maintained consistently throughout the calculation.
  • Closed Surfaces (Gauss's Surfaces): By international physical convention, \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is always directed along the outward-drawn normal.
Mathematical Formulation of Electric Flux

The electric flux \(\Delta \phi\) crossing a planar area element \(\Delta \mathbf{S}\) immersed in electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) is:

\[ \Delta \phi = \mathbf{E} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{S} = E\,\Delta S \cos\theta \]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) and the area normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\).

For an arbitrary extended surface \(S\), the total flux is obtained by surface integration:

\[ \phi = \int_S \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} \]
Tutorial Figure 5: Electric flux geometry showing the angle \(\theta\) between electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) and outward unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\).
  • If \(\theta < 90^\circ\): \(\cos\theta > 0 \implies \phi > 0\) (outward, emerging flux).
  • If \(\theta = 90^\circ\): \(\cos\theta = 0 \implies \phi = 0\) (field is parallel to the surface; no lines penetrate).
  • If \(\theta > 90^\circ\): \(\cos\theta < 0 \implies \phi < 0\) (inward, entering flux).
  • SI Unit of Flux: \(\text{N m}^2\,\text{C}^{-1}\) or equivalently \(\text{V m}\) (volt-meters).
  • Dimensions of Flux: \([\text{M L}^3 \text{T}^{-3} \text{A}^{-1}]\).

7. The Electric Dipole

An electric dipole is a system consisting of two equal and opposite point charges \(q\) and \(-q\) separated by a finite small distance \(2a\).

Electric Dipole Moment Vector

The dipole moment \(\mathbf{p}\) is mathematically defined as:

\[ \mathbf{p} = q(2\mathbf{a}) \]

Magnitude: \(p = q \times 2a\).
Direction: By strict convention, \(\mathbf{p}\) points along the dipole axis from the negative charge \(-q\) to the positive charge \(+q\).
SI Unit: \(\text{C m}\) (coulomb-meter); Dimensions: \([\text{L T A}]\).

Field of an Electric Dipole

Although the net charge of an electric dipole is zero (\(q - q = 0\)), its electric field is not identically zero because the two charges are spatially separated. At large distances (\(r \gg a\)), the opposing fields nearly, but not completely, cancel, resulting in an inverse-cube falloff (\(E \propto 1/r^3\)).

Case 1: Axial Field (End-on Position)

Consider point \(P\) along the dipole axis at distance \(r\) from the dipole center \(O\) on the side of \(+q\): \[ E_{+q} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{(r - a)^2}, \quad E_{-q} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{(r + a)^2} \] Taking the vector difference along \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}\): \[ \mathbf{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{1}{(r - a)^2} - \frac{1}{(r + a)^2} \right] \hat{\mathbf{p}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{4 a r}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \hat{\mathbf{p}} \] For a short dipole or far-field limit (\(r \gg a\)), neglecting \(a^2\) compared to \(r^2\): \[ \mathbf{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2\mathbf{p}}{r^3} \quad (r \gg a) \] Direction: Along the dipole moment vector \(\mathbf{p}\).

Case 2: Equatorial Field (Broadside-on Position)

Consider point \(Q\) on the equatorial plane (perpendicular bisector of the dipole axis) at distance \(r\) from center \(O\): \[ E_{+q} = E_{-q} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2 + a^2} \] The components perpendicular to the dipole axis cancel each other by symmetry. The components parallel to the dipole axis reinforce each other in the direction opposite to \(\mathbf{p}\): \[ E_{\text{eq}} = 2 E_{+q} \cos\theta = 2 \left( \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2 + a^2} \right) \left( \frac{a}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}} \right) = \frac{2qa}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \] For \(r \gg a\): \[ \mathbf{E}_{\text{eq}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\mathbf{p}}{r^3} \quad (r \gg a) \] Direction: Antiparallel to the dipole moment vector \(\mathbf{p}\).

NEET High-Yield Comparison: Axial vs. Equatorial

For identical large distances \(r \gg a\) from a short dipole: \[ |\mathbf{E}_{\text{axial}}| = 2 |\mathbf{E}_{\text{eq}}| \] The axial field has twice the magnitude of the equatorial field, and their vector directions are exactly antiparallel to each other!

Tutorial Figure 6: Electric dipole vector fields at axial point \(P\) (collinear with \(\mathbf{p}\)) and equatorial point \(Q\) (antiparallel to \(\mathbf{p}\)).

Electric Dipole in an External Field

Consider an electric dipole of moment \(\mathbf{p}\) placed in a uniform electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) making an angle \(\theta\) with the field vectors:

  • Net Force: \[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{F}_+ + \mathbf{F}_- = q\mathbf{E} + (-q\mathbf{E}) = \mathbf{0} \] In a uniform electric field, the translational net force on any electric dipole is strictly zero.
  • Net Torque: Because the two equal and opposite forces act along distinct lines of action separated by lever arm \(2a\sin\theta\), they form a mechanical couple: \[ \tau = (qE)(2a\sin\theta) = (q \times 2a)E\sin\theta = pE\sin\theta \] Vectorially: \[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E} \] This torque acts to align the dipole moment \(\mathbf{p}\) parallel to the external field \(\mathbf{E}\).
  • Equilibrium States:
    • \(\theta = 0^\circ\) (\(\mathbf{p} \parallel \mathbf{E}\)): \(\tau = 0\). This is stable equilibrium.
    • \(\theta = 180^\circ\) (\(\mathbf{p} \parallel -\mathbf{E}\)): \(\tau = 0\). This is unstable equilibrium.

Dipole in a Non-Uniform Electric Field

If the electric field is non-uniform, \(|\mathbf{E}_+| \neq |\mathbf{E}_-|\), so the net force is non-zero:

  • When \(\mathbf{p}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{E}\), the dipole experiences a net attractive force toward the region of stronger electric field.
  • When \(\mathbf{p}\) is antiparallel to \(\mathbf{E}\), the dipole experiences a net repulsive force toward the region of weaker electric field.
  • Physical Phenomenon: A plastic comb run through dry hair acquires static charge by friction. When brought near neutral bits of paper, the non-uniform field of the comb polarizes the paper molecules (inducing \(\mathbf{p}\) aligned with \(\mathbf{E}\)). Because the field is non-uniform, the paper experiences a net attractive force toward the comb.

8. Continuous Charge Distributions

When dealing with macroscopic charged bodies containing billions of electrons, specifying discrete point locations becomes intractable. We define smooth continuous charge density functions:

Distribution Type Definition Formula SI Unit Dimensional Formula
Linear Charge Density (\(\lambda\)) Charge per unit length along a thin wire or linear line \(\lambda = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta l}\) \(\text{C m}^{-1}\) \([\text{L}^{-1}\text{T A}]\)
Surface Charge Density (\(\sigma\)) Charge per unit area over a 2D surface or conducting sheet \(\sigma = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta S}\) \(\text{C m}^{-2}\) \([\text{L}^{-2}\text{T A}]\)
Volume Charge Density (\(\rho\)) Charge per unit volume throughout a 3D dielectric body \(\rho = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta V}\) \(\text{C m}^{-3}\) \([\text{L}^{-3}\text{T A}]\)

The total electric field at observation point \(P\) is evaluated by integrating over all volume elements \(\mathrm{d}V\): \[ \mathbf{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int_V \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}' \, \mathrm{d}V \]

9. Gauss's Law

Gauss's law is one of the four fundamental Maxwell equations governing classical electromagnetism. It establishes a profound connection between the net electric flux through any closed geometric surface and the enclosed algebraic charge.

Gauss's Law

The total electric flux \(\phi\) through any closed surface \(S\) in vacuum is equal to \(1/\varepsilon_0\) times the total net charge \(q_{\text{enclosed}}\) contained within the interior of that surface:

\[ \Phi_E = \oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = \frac{q_{\text{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0} \]

Essential Tenets of Gauss's Law

  1. Arbitrary Shape & Size: Gauss's law holds true for any closed surface (called a Gaussian surface), regardless of its shape, geometric curvature, or size.
  2. Enclosed Charge Only: The term \(q_{\text{enclosed}}\) represents exclusively the net algebraic sum of charges located inside the boundary surface. Charges outside do not contribute to \(q_{\text{enclosed}}\).
  3. Origin of Field \(\mathbf{E}\): On the left side of Gauss's law, the electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) evaluated in the dot product \(\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S}\) is the total resultant electric field produced by ALL charges—both those inside and those outside the Gaussian surface.
  4. No Discrete Charges on Surface: A Gaussian surface must not pass directly through a discrete point charge, because the electric field of a point charge is mathematically singular (\(\mathbf{E} \to \infty\)) at its exact location. However, it can pass smoothly through continuous line, surface, or volume charge distributions.
  5. Inverse-Square Dependence: Gauss's law is a mathematical consequence of the inverse-square law (\(1/r^2\)) of Coulomb's law. Any experimental departure from Gauss's law would indicate a violation of the inverse-square law.

10. Applications of Gauss's Law

Gauss's law is universally valid for all charge configurations. However, it serves as a powerful practical tool for computing \(\mathbf{E}\) specifically when the charge distribution possesses a high degree of coordinate symmetry (spherical, cylindrical, or planar).

Application 1: Field Due to an Infinitely Long Uniformly Charged Wire

Consider a thin, straight, infinitely long wire carrying uniform linear charge density \(\lambda\).

  • Symmetry: The wire has cylindrical symmetry. The electric field must be directed radially outward perpendicular to the wire, and its magnitude can depend only on radial distance \(r\).
  • Gaussian Surface: A coaxial circular cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(l\), closed by two flat circular end caps.
Tutorial Figure 7: Cylindrical Gaussian surface surrounding an infinite uniformly charged wire.
  • Flux through Flat End Caps: At the flat circular caps, \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is parallel to the wire, so \(\mathbf{E} \perp \hat{\mathbf{n}} \implies \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = 0\). The flux through both flat caps is zero.
  • Flux through Curved Surface: At every point on the curved cylinder, \(\mathbf{E}\) is perpendicular to the surface (\(\mathbf{E} \parallel \hat{\mathbf{n}}\)), and \(|\mathbf{E}|\) is constant: \[ \Phi_E = E \times (2\pi r l) \]
  • Enclosed Charge: \(q_{\text{enclosed}} = \lambda l\).
  • Applying Gauss's Law: \[ E (2\pi r l) = \frac{\lambda l}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} \] Vectorially: \(\mathbf{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} \hat{\mathbf{n}}\) where \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is the radial unit vector.

Application 2: Field Due to a Uniformly Charged Infinite Plane Sheet

Let an infinite plane non-conducting sheet carry a uniform surface charge density \(\sigma\).

  • Symmetry: By planar symmetry, \(\mathbf{E}\) must be perpendicular to the sheet, pointing away from it on both sides if \(\sigma > 0\), and its magnitude cannot depend on coordinates parallel to the sheet.
  • Gaussian Surface: A pillbox (cylinder or rectangular parallelepiped) of cross-sectional area \(A\) penetrating symmetrically through the plane with faces parallel to the sheet at distance \(x\).
Tutorial Figure 8: Cylindrical pillbox Gaussian surface crossing an infinite charged plane sheet.
  • Flux Calculation: Field lines are parallel to the curved cylinder wall, so flux through the curved side is zero. At both flat end caps, \(\mathbf{E} \parallel \hat{\mathbf{n}}\), each contributing flux \(EA\). \[ \Phi_{\text{net}} = EA + EA = 2EA \]
  • Enclosed Charge: \(q_{\text{enclosed}} = \sigma A\).
  • Applying Gauss's Law: \[ 2EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} \]
  • Key Insight: The electric field produced by an infinite sheet is completely independent of the distance \(x\) from the sheet!

Application 3: Field Due to a Uniformly Charged Thin Spherical Shell

Consider a thin spherical shell of radius \(R\) holding a total charge \(q = 4\pi R^2 \sigma\) distributed uniformly over its surface.

(i) Outside the Shell (\(r > R\))

Choose a concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius \(r > R\). By spherical symmetry, \(\mathbf{E}\) is directed radially outward, with constant magnitude over the entire Gaussian sphere: \[ \Phi_E = \oint \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = E \times (4\pi r^2) \] The enclosed charge is the total charge on the shell, \(q_{\text{enclosed}} = q\): \[ E (4\pi r^2) = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} \quad (r \ge R) \] Conclusion: For all external points, a uniformly charged spherical shell behaves electrostatically as if its entire charge were concentrated as a point charge at its center!

(ii) Inside the Shell (\(r < R\))

Choose a concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius \(r < R\). \[ \Phi_E = E \times (4\pi r^2) \] Because all charges reside on the shell at radius \(R\), the interior contains no charge: \(q_{\text{enclosed}} = 0\). \[ E (4\pi r^2) = 0 \implies E = 0 \quad (r < R) \] Conclusion: The electrostatic field is identically zero everywhere inside a uniformly charged thin spherical shell (electrostatic shielding).

12. Step-by-Step Worked Representative Examples

Worked Example 1: Charge Transfer and Quantisation Time Scale

If \(10^9\) electrons transfer from a neutral body \(A\) to another neutral body \(B\) every second, calculate the time required for body \(B\) to accumulate a net negative charge of \(1.0\,\text{C}\).

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate charge transferred per unit time (\(\mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}t\)):

\[ \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta t} = n_e \times e = 10^9\,\text{s}^{-1} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C} = 1.602 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{C s}^{-1} \]

Step 2: Calculate the time \(t\) to accumulate \(Q = 1.0\,\text{C}\):

\[ t = \frac{Q}{\Delta q/\Delta t} = \frac{1.0\,\text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{C s}^{-1}} \approx 6.242 \times 10^9\,\text{s} \]

Step 3: Convert seconds into years:

\[ t = \frac{6.242 \times 10^9}{365.25 \times 24 \times 3600} \approx 197.8\,\text{years} \approx 198\,\text{years} \]

NEET Takeaway: One coulomb is an enormous unit of charge in electrostatics. Even at the immense transfer rate of one billion electrons per second, nearly 200 years are needed to accumulate \(1\,\text{C}\).

Worked Example 2: Vector Superposition on an Equilateral Triangle

Three identical positive point charges \(q_1 = q_2 = q_3 = +q\) are fixed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side length \(l\). A point charge \(Q\) having the same sign as \(q\) is placed at the centroid \(O\) of the triangle. What is the net electrostatic force acting on charge \(Q\)?

Solution:

Step 1: Determine the distance \(r\) from each vertex to centroid \(O\):

In an equilateral triangle of side \(l\), altitude \(h = l\cos 30^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}l\). The centroid divides each median in a \(2:1\) ratio, so the distance from any vertex to \(O\) is:

\[ r = \frac{2}{3}h = \frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}l\right) = \frac{l}{\sqrt{3}} \]

Step 2: Evaluate force magnitudes from each vertex on \(Q\):

\[ F_A = F_B = F_C = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Qq}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{3Qq}{l^2} = F_0 \]

Step 3: Vector addition using planar symmetry:

The three force vectors \(\mathbf{F}_A, \mathbf{F}_B, \mathbf{F}_C\) point radially away from vertices \(A, B, C\) toward centroid \(O\). The angle between any adjacent pair of vectors is \(120^\circ\).

The resultant of \(\mathbf{F}_B\) and \(\mathbf{F}_C\) has magnitude:

\[ F_{BC} = \sqrt{F_0^2 + F_0^2 + 2F_0^2\cos 120^\circ} = \sqrt{2F_0^2 + 2F_0^2(-1/2)} = F_0 \]

By the parallelogram law, \(\mathbf{F}_{BC}\) is directed along the angle bisector directly opposite to \(\mathbf{F}_A\). Therefore:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{F}_A + (\mathbf{F}_B + \mathbf{F}_C) = F_0\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}_A - F_0\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}_A = \mathbf{0} \]

NEET Takeaway: By rotational symmetry, any test charge placed at the geometric centroid of a regular planar polygon with identical charges at all vertices experiences zero net electrostatic force.

Worked Example 3: Non-Uniform Field and Gauss's Law through a Cube

The electric field in a region is given by \(\mathbf{E} = (\alpha x^{1/2})\,\hat{\mathbf{i}}\), with \(E_y = E_z = 0\), where constant \(\alpha = 800\,\text{N C}^{-1}\,\text{m}^{-1/2}\). A cube with edge length \(a = 0.1\,\text{m}\) is situated with its faces parallel to the coordinate planes, its left face located at \(x = a\) and its right face located at \(x = 2a\). Determine (a) the net electric flux through the cube, and (b) the total charge enclosed within the cube.

Solution:

(a) Net Electric Flux:

Because \(\mathbf{E}\) points strictly along \(\hat{\mathbf{i}}\), \(\mathbf{E} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 0\) for the top, bottom, front, and back faces. Only the left and right faces perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis contribute flux:

  • Left face (\(x = a\)): Normal vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_L = -\hat{\mathbf{i}}\), Area \(S = a^2\): \[ \phi_L = \mathbf{E}_L \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}_L\,a^2 = -E_L a^2 = -(\alpha a^{1/2}) a^2 = -\alpha a^{5/2} \]
  • Right face (\(x = 2a\)): Normal vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_R = +\hat{\mathbf{i}}\), Area \(S = a^2\): \[ \phi_R = \mathbf{E}_R \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}_R\,a^2 = +E_R a^2 = +[\alpha (2a)^{1/2}] a^2 = +\sqrt{2}\alpha a^{5/2} \]

The total net outward flux is:

\[ \phi_{\text{net}} = \phi_R + \phi_L = \alpha a^{5/2} (\sqrt{2} - 1) \]

Substitute the given numerical parameters (\(\alpha = 800\), \(a = 0.1\,\text{m}\)):

\[ a^{5/2} = (10^{-1})^{5/2} = 10^{-2.5} = 10^{-2}\sqrt{0.1} \approx 3.162 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m}^{5/2} \] \[ \phi_{\text{net}} = 800 \times (3.162 \times 10^{-3}) \times (1.4142 - 1) \approx 2.53 \times 0.4142 \approx 1.05\,\text{N m}^2\,\text{C}^{-1} \]

(b) Enclosed Charge via Gauss's Law:

\[ q_{\text{enclosed}} = \varepsilon_0 \phi_{\text{net}} = (8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{C}^2\,\text{N}^{-1}\,\text{m}^{-2}) \times (1.05\,\text{N m}^2\,\text{C}^{-1}) \approx 9.27 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{C} = 9.27\,\text{pC} \]
Worked Example 4: Electric Field of Opposite Uniformly Charged Parallel Plates

Two large, thin, conducting metal plates are held parallel and close to each other. On their inner faces, the plates carry surface charge densities of opposite signs and equal magnitude \(\sigma = 17.7 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{C m}^{-2}\). Calculate the magnitude of the electric field: (a) in the outer region to the left of the plates, (b) in the outer region to the right of the plates, and (c) in the region between the plates.

Solution:

Let Plate 1 have \(+\sigma\) and Plate 2 have \(-\sigma\). The electric field produced by an infinite plane sheet of charge is \(E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\), directed away from the positive sheet and toward the negative sheet.

  • (a) Outer Region I (Left of Plate 1): \(\mathbf{E}_+\) points to the left, and \(\mathbf{E}_-\) points to the right: \[ E_I = E_+ - E_- = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} - \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = 0 \]
  • (b) Outer Region III (Right of Plate 2): \(\mathbf{E}_+\) points to the right, and \(\mathbf{E}_-\) points to the left: \[ E_{III} = E_+ - E_- = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} - \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = 0 \]
  • (c) Interior Region II (Between the Plates): Both \(\mathbf{E}_+\) and \(\mathbf{E}_-\) point in the same direction (from positive plate toward negative plate): \[ E_{II} = E_+ + E_- = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} + \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} \] Substituting values: \[ E_{II} = \frac{17.7 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{C m}^{-2}}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{C}^2\,\text{N}^{-1}\,\text{m}^{-2}} = 2.0\,\text{N C}^{-1} \]

NEET Takeaway: This configuration forms the foundation of a parallel-plate capacitor, where the external field vanishes while a uniform field \(E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0\) is confined between the plates.

Practice Question 1 Single Option Correct • Charge Properties

A polythene piece rubbed with wool is found to have acquired a negative charge of \(3.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{C}\). Which of the following statements is physically correct? (Take \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}\), \(m_e = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}\))

  • (A) \(2 \times 10^{12}\) electrons are transferred from wool to polythene, with a negligible mass gain of \(1.82 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{kg}\).
  • (B) \(2 \times 10^{12}\) electrons are transferred from polythene to wool, with a mass decrease of polythene.
  • (C) \(5 \times 10^{11}\) protons are transferred from wool to polythene.
  • (D) \(2 \times 10^{14}\) electrons are transferred from wool to polythene with no change in mass.
Correct Answer: (A)

Detailed Explanation:

By quantisation of charge, \(q = ne\). The number of electrons transferred is:

\[ n = \frac{|q|}{e} = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\text{C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 2.0 \times 10^{12} \]

Because polythene acquires a negative charge, it has gained electrons transferred from the wool. The mass transferred to the polythene is:

\[ \Delta m = n \times m_e = (2.0 \times 10^{12}) \times (9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\text{kg}) = 1.82 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{kg} \]

Protons reside tightly within atomic nuclei and are never transferred during triboelectric rubbing.

Practice Question 2 Single Option Correct • Coulomb's Law & Conduction

Two identical small conducting spheres \(A\) and \(B\) carry charges \(+q\) and \(-3q\) and are separated by distance \(r\) (\(r \gg\) radius of spheres), experiencing a mutual electrostatic force \(F_1\). The spheres are brought into direct physical contact and then returned to their original positions. The new electrostatic force between them is \(F_2\). What is the ratio \(F_1 / F_2\), and what is the nature of \(F_2\)?

  • (A) \(3 : 1\), attractive
  • (B) \(3 : 1\), repulsive
  • (C) \(12 : 1\), repulsive
  • (D) \(4 : 3\), repulsive
Correct Answer: (B)

Detailed Explanation:

Initial State: Magnitude of attractive force is:

\[ F_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|(+q)(-3q)|}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{3q^2}{r^2} \]

After Contact: By conservation of charge, total charge \(Q_{\text{net}} = (+q) + (-3q) = -2q\). Because the spheres are identical, charge redistributes equally by symmetry:

\[ q_A' = q_B' = \frac{Q_{\text{net}}}{2} = \frac{-2q}{2} = -q \]

New Force: Both spheres now have like charges of \(-q\), so the force is repulsive:

\[ F_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|(-q)(-q)|}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{r^2} \]

Taking the ratio:

\[ \frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{3q^2/r^2}{q^2/r^2} = \frac{3}{1} \]

Thus, the ratio is \(3:1\) and the force is repulsive.

Practice Question 3 Single Option Correct • Dipole Field & Distance Scaling

An electric dipole of moment \(\mathbf{p}\) produces an electric field of magnitude \(E\) at a far-away point on its axis at distance \(d\) from its center (\(d \gg 2a\)). If the distance from the center along the axis is doubled to \(2d\), the new electric field magnitude will be:

  • (A) \(E/2\)
  • (B) \(E/4\)
  • (C) \(E/8\)
  • (D) \(E/16\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Detailed Explanation:

The electric field at a far-away axial point of a short dipole is inversely proportional to the cube of the distance:

\[ E \propto \frac{1}{r^3} \]

When the distance increases from \(d\) to \(2d\):

\[ E' = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2p}{(2d)^3} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2p}{8d^3} = \frac{E}{8} \]

Unlike a point charge field which falls off as \(1/r^2\), an electric dipole field falls off as \(1/r^3\).

Practice Question 4 Single Option Correct • Gauss's Law & Symmetry

A point charge \(+q\) is placed at a distance \(a/2\) directly above the center of a horizontal square surface of side length \(a\). The total electric flux passing through the square surface is:

  • (A) \(\frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}\)
  • (B) \(\frac{q}{4\varepsilon_0}\)
  • (C) \(\frac{q}{6\varepsilon_0}\)
  • (D) \(\frac{q}{8\varepsilon_0}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Detailed Explanation:

Construct a fictitious symmetrical closed Gaussian cube of edge length \(a\) enclosing the charge. If the given square is placed as the bottom face of this cube, a distance \(a/2\) above its center is precisely the geometric center of the cube.

By Gauss's law, the total flux emerging through all 6 faces of the cube is:

\[ \Phi_{\text{total}} = \frac{q_{\text{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} \]

Since the charge is at the exact center, the flux is distributed equally across all 6 identical square faces by spherical symmetry:

\[ \Phi_{\text{one face}} = \frac{1}{6}\Phi_{\text{total}} = \frac{q}{6\varepsilon_0} \]
Practice Question 5 Single Option Correct • Torque and Potential Work

An electric dipole of moment \(\mathbf{p} = (4 \times 10^{-9}\,\text{C m})\,\hat{\mathbf{i}}\) is placed in a uniform electric field \(\mathbf{E} = (3 \times 10^4\,\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4 \times 10^4\,\hat{\mathbf{j}})\,\text{N C}^{-1}\). The magnitude of the torque acting on the dipole is:

  • (A) \(1.2 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{N m}\)
  • (B) \(1.6 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{N m}\)
  • (C) \(2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{N m}\)
  • (D) \(2.8 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{N m}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Detailed Explanation:

The torque vector is evaluated via the vector cross-product:

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E} = (4 \times 10^{-9}\,\hat{\mathbf{i}}) \times (3 \times 10^4\,\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 4 \times 10^4\,\hat{\mathbf{j}}) \]

Using unit vector cross products (\(\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times \hat{\mathbf{i}} = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{i}} \times \hat{\mathbf{j}} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\)):

\[ \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{0} + (4 \times 10^{-9})(4 \times 10^4)\,\hat{\mathbf{k}} = (1.6 \times 10^{-4}\,\hat{\mathbf{k}})\,\text{N m} \]

The magnitude is \(|\boldsymbol{\tau}| = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{N m}\).

Practice Question 6 Assertion-Reason • Field Lines & Loops

Assertion (A): Electrostatic field lines never form closed continuous loops.
Reason (R): The electrostatic field is a conservative vector field, meaning the line integral of electric field around any closed loop is identically zero.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Detailed Explanation:

If an electrostatic field line formed a closed loop, a test charge moving along the loop in the direction of \(\mathbf{E}\) would experience a tangential force \(q\mathbf{E}\) at every point. The work done over one full cycle would be \(\oint q\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{l} > 0\), violating the conservative nature of static electric fields. Because the electrostatic force is conservative (\(\oint \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{l} = 0\)), static field lines cannot form closed loops.

Practice Question 7 Assertion-Reason • Gauss's Law Validity

Assertion (A): If the net electric flux through an imaginary closed Gaussian surface is zero, there can be no discrete charges residing anywhere inside that surface.
Reason (R): Gauss's law states that \(\oint \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = q_{\text{enclosed}}/\varepsilon_0\).

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (D)

Detailed Explanation:

By Gauss's law, \(\Phi = q_{\text{enclosed}}/\varepsilon_0\). If \(\Phi = 0\), it implies only that the algebraic sum of the enclosed charges is zero: \(q_{\text{enclosed}} = \sum q_i = 0\). For instance, an electric dipole (\(+q\) and \(-q\)) inside the surface has \(q_{\text{enclosed}} = +q - q = 0\), producing zero net flux despite charges being present inside. Thus, Assertion (A) is false, while Reason (R) is a correct statement of Gauss's law.

Practice Question 8 Single Option Correct • Spherical Shell Electrostatics

A hollow spherical conducting shell of inner radius \(r_1\) and outer radius \(r_2\) carries a net charge \(+Q\). A point charge \(+q\) is placed at the center of the cavity. What are the surface charge densities on the inner surface and outer surface of the shell, respectively?

  • (A) \(\sigma_{\text{in}} = -\frac{q}{4\pi r_1^2}\), \quad \sigma_{\text{out}} = +\frac{Q + q}{4\pi r_2^2}\)
  • (B) \(\sigma_{\text{in}} = +\frac{q}{4\pi r_1^2}\), \quad \sigma_{\text{out}} = +\frac{Q - q}{4\pi r_2^2}\)
  • (C) \(\sigma_{\text{in}} = 0\), \quad \sigma_{\text{out}} = +\frac{Q}{4\pi r_2^2}\)
  • (D) \(\sigma_{\text{in}} = -\frac{q}{4\pi r_1^2}\), \quad \sigma_{\text{out}} = +\frac{Q}{4\pi r_2^2}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Detailed Explanation:

Inside the conducting material (\(r_1 < r < r_2\)), the static electric field must be zero (\(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}\)). Consider a spherical Gaussian surface within the conducting shell:

\[ \oint \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = 0 \implies q_{\text{enclosed}} = 0 \]

Since the central charge is \(+q\), an induced charge \(q_{\text{inner}} = -q\) must reside on the inner surface. Therefore, the inner surface charge density is:

\[ \sigma_{\text{in}} = \frac{-q}{4\pi r_1^2} \]

By conservation of charge, because the shell had an initial charge \(+Q\), the charge on its outer surface becomes \(q_{\text{outer}} = Q - q_{\text{inner}} = Q - (-q) = Q + q\). Thus, the outer surface charge density is:

\[ \sigma_{\text{out}} = \frac{Q + q}{4\pi r_2^2} \]

14. Common Traps, Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Frequent Conceptual Misconceptions
  • Trap: Confusing \(\mathbf{E}\) in Gauss's law with the field of enclosed charges only. Correction: \(\mathbf{E}\) is the total field from all charges, but \(\Phi\) depends only on \(q_{\text{enclosed}}\).
  • Trap: Forgetting the vector sign of dipole moment \(\mathbf{p}\). Correction: In physics, \(\mathbf{p}\) points from negative to positive (\(-q \to +q\)), opposite to the chemistry convention.
  • Trap: Assuming electric field inside any charged body is zero. Correction: Field is zero inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium or inside a hollow shell, but is non-zero inside a uniformly charged non-conducting sphere.
  • Trap: Missing the \(1/r^3\) dependence for dipoles. Correction: For a point charge, \(E \propto 1/r^2\); for a dipole, \(E \propto 1/r^3\).
Quick Revision Checklist
  • \(q = ne\) where \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}\).
  • \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{C}^2\,\text{N}^{-1}\,\text{m}^{-2}\).
  • \(k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9 \times 10^9\,\text{N m}^2\,\text{C}^{-2}\).
  • \(\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21}\) (Newton's 3rd law).
  • \(E_{\text{axial}} = 2 E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\).
  • \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = \mathbf{0}\) in uniform \(\mathbf{E}\).
  • Line charge: \(E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\).
  • Infinite sheet: \(E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\).
  • Shell: \(E_{\text{in}} = 0\), \(E_{\text{out}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\).

15. Final Exam Recap

In summary, Electric Charges and Fields establishes the foundation for all electromagnetism:

  • Charge is conserved, additive, quantised, and relativistically invariant (unaffected by motion, unlike mass).
  • Coulomb's law governs static pairwise interactions via an inverse-square relationship; the superposition principle allows vector addition for any number of charges.
  • The electric field represents force per unit test charge, mapped visually by continuous non-intersecting field lines that start on positive charges, end on negative charges, and never form closed loops.
  • An electric dipole consists of charges \(\pm q\) separated by \(2a\). Its far-field falls off as \(1/r^3\), with \(E_{\text{axial}} = 2E_{\text{eq}}\). In a uniform field, a dipole experiences zero net force but a restoring torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E}\).
  • Gauss's law equates closed surface flux to \(q_{\text{enclosed}}/\varepsilon_0\), providing direct analytic solutions for symmetric systems: \(E \propto 1/r\) for infinite lines, \(E = \text{constant}\) for infinite sheets, and \(E = 0\) inside hollow spherical shells.
