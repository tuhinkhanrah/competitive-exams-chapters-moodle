NEET UG Physics • Electrostatics

Electric Charges and Fields

Complete Concept Tutorial, Analytical Methods, Worked Examples, and NEET UG Practice Assessment

1. Learning Objectives and Exam Context

Electrostatics investigates forces, fields, and interactions produced by electric charges that remain stationary in a chosen reference frame. In the NEET UG examination, this unit tests both rapid numerical execution (using Coulomb's law, dipole formulas, and Gauss's law) and sharp conceptual clarity (field line geometry, charge conservation, and symmetry arguments).

After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:
  • Explain frictional charging via electron transfer and distinguish conductors, insulators, and semiconductors.
  • Apply additivity, conservation, and quantisation of electric charge (\(q = ne\)) to microscopic and macroscopic problems.
  • Compute electrostatic forces between point charges and multi-charge assemblies using Coulomb's law and vector superposition.
  • Determine electric fields, sketch and interpret electric field lines, and evaluate dipole fields and torques.
  • Calculate electric flux and apply Gauss's law to symmetric charge distributions: infinite line, infinite plane sheet, and thin spherical shell.

2. Nature of Electric Charge and Material Behaviour

2.1 Two Polarities and Charging by Friction

Everyday phenomena—such as sparks from synthetic fabric in dry air or attraction of light paper bits to a rubbed plastic comb—arise from static electric charge. Careful experiments reveal that electric charge exists in exactly two opposite kinds, named positive and negative by Benjamin Franklin:

  • Like charges repel each other (two glass rods rubbed with silk repel; two plastic rods rubbed with fur repel).
  • Unlike charges attract each other (a rubbed glass rod attracts a rubbed plastic rod).
  • By convention, the charge acquired by a glass rod rubbed with silk is taken as positive, while that on a plastic rod rubbed with fur is negative.

Ordinary matter is electrically neutral because the total positive nuclear charge (protons) equals the total negative electronic charge. Charging a solid body does not create new charge; instead, it transfers loosely bound outer electrons from one surface to another. A body losing electrons becomes positively charged, whereas a body gaining electrons becomes negatively charged.

2.2 Conductors, Insulators, and Semiconductors

Materials are classified according to how freely electric charges move within them:

  • Conductors: Substances such as metals, human and animal bodies, and the Earth contain mobile charge carriers (free electrons in metals; ions in electrolytes). Charge placed on a conductor spreads rapidly over its outer surface.
  • Insulators: Substances such as glass, plastic, nylon, porcelain, and dry wood strongly resist charge flow. Charge deposited on an insulator remains localized at the spot of contact.
  • Semiconductors: Materials whose electrical conductivity lies intermediate between conductors and insulators.

A gold-leaf electroscope detects net charge using a vertical metal rod with a top knob and two thin gold leaves enclosed in a protective housing. When a charged object touches the knob, like charge flows down to both leaves, causing them to repel and diverge; the angle of divergence indicates the magnitude of charge.

3. Fundamental Properties of Electric Charge

When charged objects have dimensions negligible compared with the distances separating them, they are modelled as point charges. Every electric charge obeys three universal laws:

  1. Additivity: Charge is a scalar quantity (and invariant across inertial frames in relative motion). The net charge of a system containing charges \(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\) is their algebraic sum, keeping proper signs: \[q_{\mathrm{net}} = q_1 + q_2 + \dots + q_n\]
  2. Conservation: The total electric charge of an isolated system remains constant over time. Processes such as rubbing redistribute electrons, and particle reactions (such as a neutron transforming into a proton and an electron) create equal and opposite charges so that net charge is unchanged.
  3. Quantisation: Every observable free charge is an integral multiple of the elementary charge \(e\): \[q = ne, \qquad n \in \{0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots\}\] where \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) (often approximated as \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) in NEET UG calculations). A proton carries \(+e\) and an electron carries \(-e\).
Macroscopic Continuity vs. Microscopic Discreteness

One coulomb (\(\mathrm{C}\)) is defined as the charge transported by a steady current of \(1\,\mathrm{A}\) in \(1\,\mathrm{s}\), corresponding to roughly \(6.25 \times 10^{18}\) electrons. Because a typical laboratory charge of \(1\,\mathrm{µC}\) involves nearly \(6.25 \times 10^{12}\) elementary charges, the discrete step size \(e\) is unnoticeable at macroscopic scales, allowing us to treat charge as continuous.

4. Coulomb's Law and the Principle of Superposition

4.1 Scalar and Vector Forms

Coulomb's law states that the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) separated by distance \(r\) in vacuum acts along the line joining them, is directly proportional to \(|q_1 q_2|\), and is inversely proportional to \(r^2\):

\[F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2} = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}\]

In SI units, the permittivity of free space is \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2\,N^{-1}\,m^{-2}}\), and the electrostatic constant is \(k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\).

If \(\mathbf{r}_1\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2\) are the position vectors of \(q_1\) and \(q_2\), let \(\mathbf{r}_{21} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1\) be the displacement vector from \(q_1\) to \(q_2\), with magnitude \(r_{21}\) and unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21} = \mathbf{r}_{21}/r_{21}\). The force exerted on \(q_2\) by \(q_1\) is:

\[\mathbf{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{21}^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21}\]

Notice two built-in features: (i) if \(q_1 q_2 > 0\) (like charges), \(\mathbf{F}_{21}\) points along \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21}\) (repulsion); if \(q_1 q_2 < 0\) (unlike charges), \(\mathbf{F}_{21}\) points along \(-\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{21}\) (attraction); (ii) interchanging indices gives \(\mathbf{F}_{12} = -\mathbf{F}_{21}\), confirming consistency with Newton's third law.

4.2 Electrostatic vs. Gravitational Force

Both Coulomb's law and Newton's law of gravitation follow an inverse-square dependence on distance. However, electrostatic forces are enormously stronger at the atomic scale. For an electron (mass \(m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)) and a proton (mass \(m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\)), the dimensionless ratio is:

\[\left|\frac{F_e}{F_G}\right| = \frac{k e^2}{G m_e m_p} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{39}\]

For two protons, this ratio is about \(1.3 \times 10^{36}\). Gravity dominates on astronomical scales only because bulk matter is nearly electrically neutral, allowing opposite electric charges to cancel, whereas mass is always positive and purely attractive.

4.3 Principle of Superposition

In a system of \(n\) stationary charges \(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\), the electrostatic force between any pair is completely unaffected by the presence of other charges. The net force on \(q_1\) is the vector sum of the individual pairwise forces:

\[\mathbf{F}_1 = \mathbf{F}_{12} + \mathbf{F}_{13} + \dots + \mathbf{F}_{1n} = \frac{q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=2}^{n} \frac{q_i}{r_{1i}^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{1i}\]

5. Electric Field and Electric Field Lines

5.1 Defining Electric Field

A source charge \(Q\) modifies the space around it by establishing an electric field \(\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})\). Operationally, \(\mathbf{E}\) at a point is the electrostatic force experienced per unit positive test charge \(q_0\) placed at that point in the limit \(q_0 \to 0\) (so the test charge does not disturb the source configuration):

\[\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{F}}{q_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}\]

Its SI unit is \(\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) (equivalent to \(\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\)). A charge \(q\) placed in a field \(\mathbf{E}\) experiences a force \(\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{E}\). For a positive source \(Q > 0\), \(\mathbf{E}\) points radially outward; for \(Q < 0\), \(\mathbf{E}\) points radially inward. By superposition, the net field due to charges \(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n\) at point P is:

\[\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}_P) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{q_i}{r_{iP}^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{iP}\]

Beyond static convenience, electric (and magnetic) fields are real physical entities that propagate disturbances at the speed of light \(c\) and transport energy.

5.2 Properties of Electric Field Lines

Electric field lines provide a visual map of the vector field. The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of \(\mathbf{E}\) there, and the relative closeness (density per unit perpendicular area) indicates field strength.

  • Field lines originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges (or extend to infinity for an isolated charge).
  • In a charge-free region, field lines are continuous curves without sudden breaks.
  • Two field lines can never intersect; an intersection would imply two distinct directions for \(\mathbf{E}\) at the same point, which is impossible.
  • Electrostatic field lines never form closed loops, reflecting the conservative nature of the electrostatic field.

6. Electric Dipole and Behaviour in External Fields

6.1 Dipole Moment and Far-Field Expressions

An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite point charges, \(+q\) and \(-q\), separated by a small distance \(2a\). Its electric dipole moment \(\mathbf{p}\) is a vector of magnitude \(p = 2qa\) directed from \(-q\) toward \(+q\):

\[\mathbf{p} = (2qa)\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}\]

Although the net charge of a dipole is zero, its electric field is non-zero because the charges are spatially separated. At a distance \(r\) from the dipole centre:

  • On the dipole axis: \[\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2\mathbf{p}r}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \xrightarrow{r \gg a} \frac{2\mathbf{p}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\] directed along \(\mathbf{p}\).
  • On the equatorial plane (perpendicular bisector): \[\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eq}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\mathbf{p}}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \xrightarrow{r \gg a} -\frac{\mathbf{p}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\] directed opposite to \(\mathbf{p}\).

Key NEET UG takeaway: at large distances (\(r \gg a\)), a dipole field decays as \(1/r^3\) (faster than the \(1/r^2\) decay of a point charge), and \(E_{\mathrm{axial}} = 2E_{\mathrm{eq}}\) for the same distance \(r\).

Molecules such as \(\mathrm{CO}_2\) and \(\mathrm{CH}_4\) have coinciding positive and negative charge centres (zero permanent dipole moment, non-polar), whereas \(\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}\) has separated charge centres and a permanent dipole moment (polar).

6.2 Torque and Force on a Dipole

When a permanent dipole \(\mathbf{p}\) is placed in a uniform electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) at angle \(\theta\), the forces \(+q\mathbf{E}\) and \(-q\mathbf{E}\) are equal and opposite, so the net translational force is zero. However, their lines of action are separated by \(2a\sin\theta\), producing a restoring torque:

\[\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E}, \qquad \tau = pE\sin\theta\]

This torque tends to align \(\mathbf{p}\) parallel to \(\mathbf{E}\) (stable equilibrium at \(\theta = 0°\); unstable equilibrium at \(\theta = 180°\)). If the external field is non-uniform, the dipole experiences both a torque and a net force: when \(\mathbf{p}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{E}\), the net force pulls the dipole toward the stronger field; when antiparallel, it pushes toward the weaker field. This explains why a charged comb induces dipole moments in neutral paper bits and attracts them into its non-uniform field.

7. Continuous Charge, Electric Flux, and Gauss's Law

7.1 Continuous Charge Densities

For macroscopic bodies carrying huge numbers of charges, we define three continuous charge densities:

  • Linear charge density: \(\lambda = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta l}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-1}}\))
  • Surface charge density: \(\sigma = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta S}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}\))
  • Volume charge density: \(\rho = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta V}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-3}}\))

7.2 Electric Flux

An area element \(\Delta S\) is represented as a vector \(\Delta \mathbf{S} = \Delta S\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is the unit normal. For a closed surface, \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is always chosen along the outward normal. The electric flux \(\Delta \phi\) passing through \(\Delta \mathbf{S}\) is:

\[\Delta \phi = \mathbf{E} \cdot \Delta \mathbf{S} = E\,\Delta S\,\cos\theta\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\). The SI unit of electric flux is \(\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\) (or \(\mathrm{V\,m}\)). Flux is maximum when \(\mathbf{E}\) is normal to the surface (\(\theta = 0°\)) and zero when \(\mathbf{E}\) grazes the surface (\(\theta = 90°\)).

7.3 Gauss's Law and Its Applications

Gauss's law states that the total outward electric flux through any closed surface \(S\) (called a Gaussian surface) equals \(1/\varepsilon_0\) times the net charge \(q_{\mathrm{enc}}\) enclosed inside \(S\):

\[\Phi = \oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \frac{q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}\]

Crucial subtleties: (i) the flux depends only on \(q_{\mathrm{enc}}\), not on the shape or size of \(S\) or where charges sit inside \(S\); (ii) \(\mathbf{E}\) in the integral is the net field due to all charges (both inside and outside \(S\)); (iii) a Gaussian surface must never pass through a discrete point charge (where \(\mathbf{E}\) diverges), though it may pass through a continuous charge distribution.

By exploiting symmetry, Gauss's law yields three cornerstone results:

  1. Infinitely long straight uniformly charged wire (linear density \(\lambda\), at perpendicular distance \(r\)): using a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\) and length \(l\), flux escapes only through the curved surface (\(E \cdot 2\pi r l = \lambda l/\varepsilon_0\)): \[\mathbf{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\]
  2. Uniformly charged infinite plane sheet (surface density \(\sigma\)): using a pillbox Gaussian surface of cross-sectional area \(A\) piercing the sheet, flux exits both flat faces (\(2EA = \sigma A/\varepsilon_0\)): \[\mathbf{E} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf{n}}\] Notice that \(\mathbf{E}\) is uniform and completely independent of distance from the sheet.
  3. Uniformly charged thin spherical shell (radius \(R\), total charge \(q = 4\pi R^2\sigma\)):
    • Outside (\(r \ge R\)): \(\mathbf{E} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\hat{\mathbf{r}}\) (behaves as if all charge is concentrated at the centre).
    • Inside (\(r < R\)): \(q_{\mathrm{enc}} = 0 \implies E = 0\) everywhere inside the shell.

9. Original Worked Examples

All numerical values in the following examples are hypothetical assumptions designed to build speed and accuracy for NEET UG.

Worked Example 1 • Quantisation and Mass Transfer

Problem: Suppose a glass rod is rubbed with silk so that it acquires a positive charge of \(+4.8\,\mathrm{µC}\). (a) How many electrons were transferred, and in which direction? (b) By how much does the mass of the silk change? (Take \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) and electron mass \(m_e = 9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\)).

Solution:

(a) Since the glass rod becomes positively charged, electrons are transferred from the glass rod to the silk. Using \(q = ne\):

\[n = \frac{q}{e} = \frac{4.8 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}} = 3.0 \times 10^{13}\text{ electrons}\]

(b) The silk gains \(3.0 \times 10^{13}\) electrons, so its mass increases by:

\[\Delta m = n m_e = (3.0 \times 10^{13}) \times (9.1 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}) = 2.73 \times 10^{-17}\,\mathrm{kg}\]
Worked Example 2 • Superposition on a Square

Problem: Assume four point charges are placed at the corners of a square of side \(d = 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\) in order: \(q_A = +2\,\mathrm{µC}\), \(q_B = -4\,\mathrm{µC}\), \(q_C = +2\,\mathrm{µC}\), and \(q_D = -4\,\mathrm{µC}\). Find the net electrostatic force on a test charge \(q_0 = +1\,\mathrm{µC}\) placed at the centre O of the square.

Solution:

The centre O is equidistant from all four corners (\(r = d/\sqrt{2}\)).

  • The charges at opposite corners A and C are equal (\(+2\,\mathrm{µC}\)). They exert forces of equal magnitude on \(q_0\) in opposite directions along diagonal AC, so \(\mathbf{F}_A + \mathbf{F}_C = \mathbf{0}\).
  • Similarly, the charges at opposite corners B and D are equal (\(-4\,\mathrm{µC}\)). They exert equal and opposite attractive forces on \(q_0\) along diagonal BD, so \(\mathbf{F}_B + \mathbf{F}_D = \mathbf{0}\).

Therefore, by symmetry and vector superposition, the net force on \(q_0\) is zero.

Worked Example 3 • Electric Dipole Field and Torque

Problem: An assumed electric dipole consists of charges \(\pm 4.0\,\mathrm{µC}\) separated by \(2.0\,\mathrm{mm}\). (a) Find the electric field magnitude at a point on its equatorial plane \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the centre. (b) If this dipole is placed in a uniform field of \(5.0 \times 10^4\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) at \(30°\) to the field, find the torque acting on it.

Solution:

(a) Dipole moment magnitude: \(p = q(2a) = (4.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{C})(2.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}) = 8.0 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{C\,m}\).

Since \(r = 0.20\,\mathrm{m} \gg a = 0.001\,\mathrm{m}\), we use the far-field equatorial formula:

\[E_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3} = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-9}}{(0.20)^3} = \frac{72}{8.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 9.0 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\]

(b) Torque magnitude:

\[\tau = pE\sin 30° = (8.0 \times 10^{-9})(5.0 \times 10^4)(0.5) = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N\,m}\]
Worked Example 4 • Gauss's Law and Symmetry

Problem: Suppose a point charge \(q = +12\,\mathrm{µC}\) is placed at a perpendicular distance of \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) directly above the centre of a square of side \(8.0\,\mathrm{cm}\). Calculate the magnitude of electric flux passing through the square. (Take \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2\,N^{-1}\,m^{-2}}\)).

Solution:

Notice that the height \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) is exactly half the side length \(8.0\,\mathrm{cm}\). Thus, we can imagine the square as one face of a closed cube of side \(8.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) with the charge \(+12\,\mathrm{µC}\) located at the exact geometric centre of the cube.

By Gauss's law, the total flux through all 6 faces of the cube is \(\Phi_{\mathrm{total}} = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}\). By symmetry, each of the 6 identical faces receives one-sixth of the total flux:

\[\Phi_{\mathrm{square}} = \frac{q}{6\varepsilon_0} = \frac{12 \times 10^{-6}}{6 \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12}} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-6}}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}} \approx 2.26 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\]

10. NEET UG Practice Questions

Attempt all 10 original practice questions before checking the worked solutions. These are modelled on standard NEET UG single-correct, statement-based, and assertion-reason formats.

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ

Two identical conducting spheres A and B carry charges \(+6\,\mathrm{µC}\) and \(-2\,\mathrm{µC}\), respectively, and attract each other with a force \(F\) when separated by distance \(r\). They are brought into contact and then separated to a new distance \(r/2\). The new electrostatic force between them is:

  • (1) \(F/3\), attractive
  • (2) \(4F/3\), repulsive
  • (3) \(F/3\), repulsive
  • (4) \(4F/3\), attractive
Correct Answer: (2) \(4F/3\), repulsive

Initial force magnitude: \(F = \frac{k(6)(2)}{r^2} = \frac{12k}{r^2}\) (attractive, in \(\mathrm{µC^2}\) units). On touching, total charge \(+6 + (-2) = +4\,\mathrm{µC}\) divides equally, giving \(+2\,\mathrm{µC}\) on each sphere. At distance \(r/2\), the new force is repulsive with magnitude:

\[F' = \frac{k(2)(2)}{(r/2)^2} = \frac{16k}{r^2} = \frac{4}{3}F\]
Practice Question 2 • Single-Correct MCQ

Which of the following electric charges CANNOT exist on an isolated macroscopic or microscopic body in nature?

  • (1) \(3.2 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (2) \(8.0 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (3) \(2.4 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (4) \(6.4 \times 10^{-18}\,\mathrm{C}\)
Correct Answer: (3) \(2.4 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\)

By quantisation of charge, \(q = ne\) where \(n\) must be an integer and \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\). Dividing \(2.4 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) by \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) gives \(n = 1.5\), which is not an integer. All other options give integers (\(2, 5, 40\)).

Practice Question 3 • Single-Correct MCQ

Two point charges \(+q\) and \(+4q\) are fixed at a separation \(L\). Where should a third charge \(Q\) be placed on the line joining them so that \(Q\) remains in equilibrium?

  • (1) At distance \(L/3\) from \(+q\) between the charges
  • (2) At distance \(L/4\) from \(+q\) between the charges
  • (3) At distance \(L/3\) from \(+4q\) between the charges
  • (4) At distance \(L\) from \(+q\) outside the segment
Correct Answer: (1) At distance \(L/3\) from \(+q\) between the charges

Since both fixed charges have the same sign, the null point where their fields cancel must lie between them. Let \(x\) be the distance from \(+q\):

\[\frac{kq}{x^2} = \frac{k(4q)}{(L-x)^2} \implies \frac{1}{x} = \frac{2}{L-x} \implies L - x = 2x \implies x = \frac{L}{3}\]
Practice Question 4 • Single-Correct MCQ

An electron (charge \(-e\), mass \(m_e\)) and a proton (charge \(+e\), mass \(m_p\)) are released from rest in the same uniform electric field \(E\). After travelling the same distance \(h\), the ratio of their times of fall \(t_e / t_p\) is:

  • (1) \(1\)
  • (2) \(m_e / m_p\)
  • (3) \(\sqrt{m_e / m_p}\)
  • (4) \(\sqrt{m_p / m_e}\)
Correct Answer: (3) \(\sqrt{m_e / m_p}\)

The acceleration of a particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(e\) is \(a = eE/m\). Starting from rest over distance \(h = \frac{1}{2}at^2\), we get \(t = \sqrt{\frac{2hm}{eE}}\). Since \(h\), \(e\), and \(E\) are identical, \(t \propto \sqrt{m}\), so \(t_e / t_p = \sqrt{m_e / m_p}\).

Practice Question 5 • Assertion-Reason

Assertion (A): Two electrostatic field lines can never intersect each other.
Reason (R): At any point in space, the net electric field has a unique direction given by the tangent to the field line.

  • (1) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  • (2) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
  • (3) A is true, but R is false.
  • (4) A is false, but R is true.
Correct Answer: (1) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

If two field lines crossed at a point, two tangents could be drawn at that point, implying two different directions for the electric field simultaneously—contradicting the uniqueness of \(\mathbf{E}\).

Practice Question 6 • Single-Correct MCQ

The electric field at a far distance \(r\) on the axial line of a short electric dipole is \(E_1\), and at the same distance \(r\) on its equatorial line is \(E_2\). Which relation is correct?

  • (1) \(\mathbf{E}_1 = 2\mathbf{E}_2\)
  • (2) \(\mathbf{E}_1 = -2\mathbf{E}_2\)
  • (3) \(\mathbf{E}_1 = -\mathbf{E}_2\)
  • (4) \(\mathbf{E}_2 = 2\mathbf{E}_1\)
Correct Answer: (2) \(\mathbf{E}_1 = -2\mathbf{E}_2\)

For \(r \gg a\), \(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{axial}} = \frac{2\mathbf{p}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\) (parallel to \(\mathbf{p}\)) and \(\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{eq}} = -\frac{\mathbf{p}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\) (antiparallel to \(\mathbf{p}\)). Hence \(\mathbf{E}_1 = -2\mathbf{E}_2\).

Practice Question 7 • Single-Correct MCQ

A square frame of side \(20\,\mathrm{cm}\) is held in a uniform electric field \(\mathbf{E} = 4.0 \times 10^3\,\hat{\mathbf{i}}\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) such that the normal to the square makes an angle of \(60°\) with the \(x\)-axis. The electric flux through the square is:

  • (1) \(80\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (2) \(160\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (3) \(80\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\)
  • (4) \(40\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (1) \(80\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\)

Area \(S = (0.20\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 0.04\,\mathrm{m^2}\). Angle between \(\mathbf{E}\) and normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is \(\theta = 60°\). Thus:

\[\Phi = ES\cos 60° = (4.0 \times 10^3)(0.04)(0.5) = 80\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-1}}\]
Practice Question 8 • Statement-Based MCQ

Statement I: If the net outward electric flux through a closed surface is zero, there cannot be any electric charges inside the surface.
Statement II: The electric field at points on a Gaussian surface depends on both the charges inside and the charges outside the surface.

  • (1) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  • (2) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  • (3) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  • (4) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Correct Answer: (4) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.

Zero net flux implies zero net enclosed charge (\(q_{\mathrm{enc}} = 0\)); equal positive and negative charges (such as an electric dipole) can still be present inside. Statement II is correct because \(\mathbf{E}\) in Gauss's law is the total field from all charges.

Practice Question 9 • Single-Correct MCQ

An infinitely long straight wire with uniform linear charge density \(\lambda\) produces an electric field of \(1.8 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}}\) at a perpendicular distance of \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\). The linear charge density \(\lambda\) is (take \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}\)):

  • (1) \(0.2\,\mathrm{µC\,m^{-1}}\)
  • (2) \(0.4\,\mathrm{µC\,m^{-1}}\)
  • (3) \(0.8\,\mathrm{µC\,m^{-1}}\)
  • (4) \(4.0\,\mathrm{µC\,m^{-1}}\)
Correct Answer: (2) \(0.4\,\mathrm{µC\,m^{-1}}\)

Using \(E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}\):

\[\lambda = \frac{Er}{2k} = \frac{(1.8 \times 10^5)(0.04)}{2 \times 9 \times 10^9} = \frac{7.2 \times 10^3}{1.8 \times 10^{10}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{C\,m^{-1}} = 0.4\,\mathrm{µC\,m^{-1}}\]
Practice Question 10 • Single-Correct MCQ

Two large, thin, parallel non-conducting sheets carry uniform surface charge densities \(+\sigma\) and \(-\sigma\). What is the magnitude of the electric field in the region between the plates and in the outer region?

  • (1) \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) between plates; \(0\) outside
  • (2) \(\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\) between plates; \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) outside
  • (3) \(0\) between plates; \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) outside
  • (4) \(\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\) between plates; \(0\) outside
Correct Answer: (1) \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) between plates; \(0\) outside

Each infinite sheet produces a uniform field of magnitude \(\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\). Between the plates, the field from \(+\sigma\) (away from it) and the field from \(-\sigma\) (toward it) point in the same direction, adding up to \(\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} + \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\). Outside the plates, the two fields point in opposite directions and cancel to zero.

11. Common Pitfalls and NEET UG Revision Checklist

Avoid These Frequent Exam Mistakes
  • Angle in Electric Flux: Always measure \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf{E}\) and the normal to the surface, not the plane of the surface itself.
  • Dipole Direction Confusion: Dipole moment \(\mathbf{p}\) points from \(-q\) to \(+q\). On the equatorial plane, \(\mathbf{E}\) is opposite to \(\mathbf{p}\).
  • Zero Flux vs. Zero Charge: \(\Phi = 0\) means net enclosed charge is zero, not that no charges exist inside the Gaussian surface.
  • Gaussian Surface Radius: Doubling the radius of a sphere enclosing a point charge does not change the total flux; flux depends only on \(q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\).
  • Infinite Sheet vs. Conducting Plate: A single thin sheet has \(E = \sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\) on each side, whereas two oppositely charged parallel sheets produce \(\sigma/\varepsilon_0\) between them.

12. Concise Final Recap

Electric charge is conserved, additive, and quantised in steps of \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\). Point charges interact via Coulomb's inverse-square law and obey linear vector superposition. The electric field \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{F}/q_0\) maps the force per unit positive charge, while electric dipoles exhibit a characteristic \(1/r^3\) far-field decay and experience a torque \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{p} \times \mathbf{E}\) in uniform fields. Finally, Gauss's law (\(\Phi = q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\)) provides an exact, rapid method to compute fields whenever charge distributions possess cylindrical, planar, or spherical symmetry.
