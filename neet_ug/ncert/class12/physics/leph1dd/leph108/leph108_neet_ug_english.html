1. Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

By the end of this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • State Maxwell's four equations in vacuum and connect their symmetry to the self-sustaining propagation of electromagnetic (EM) waves.

2. Displacement Current and the Ampere–Maxwell Law

2.1 The Inconsistency in Ampere's Circuital Law

Ampere's circuital law originally stated that the line integral of the magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) around any closed loop equals \(\mu_0\) times the conduction current \(i_c\) passing through a surface bounded by that loop:

\[ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 i_c \]

Consider a parallel-plate capacitor being charged by a time-varying current \(i(t)\). Suppose we draw a circular Amperian loop of radius \(r\) centered on the connecting wire just outside the left plate. We can choose two different surfaces sharing this exact same circular boundary:

  • Flat circular disc surface (\(S_1\)): The connecting wire pierces this flat surface, so the conduction current passing through it is \(i_c = i(t)\), giving \(B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 i(t)\).
  • Cup-shaped surface (\(S_2\)): This surface bulges into the air gap between the capacitor plates without touching any wire. Because no charges cross the vacuum/air gap, \(i_c = 0\) through \(S_2\), which naively predicts \(B = 0\) on the very same loop!

A single physical point on the loop cannot simultaneously have a non-zero magnetic field and a zero magnetic field. James Clerk Maxwell resolved this paradox by recognizing that while no conduction current crosses \(S_2\), a time-varying electric flux \(\Phi_E\) does.

2.2 Derivation and Properties of Displacement Current

For a parallel-plate capacitor with plate area \(A\) and instantaneous charge \(Q\), the electric field between the plates is \(E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}\). The electric flux through the cross-section between the plates is:

\[ \Phi_E = E A = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \]

Differentiating both sides with respect to time \(t\):

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_E}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} \implies i_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_E}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

Here, \(i_d\) is called Maxwell's displacement current. Adding this term to the conduction current gives the Ampere–Maxwell Law:

\[ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 \left( i_c + \varepsilon_0 \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_E}{\mathrm{d}t} \right) = \mu_0 (i_c + i_d) \]
Key Physical Insights for NEET UG
  • Continuity of Current: In a charging capacitor circuit, \(i_c = i\) and \(i_d = 0\) in the connecting wires, whereas \(i_c = 0\) and \(i_d = i\) in the ideal gap between the plates. Thus, the total current \(i_c + i_d\) is continuous everywhere, and Kirchhoff's junction rule remains valid at each capacitor plate.
  • No Actual Charge Flow: Displacement current does not involve the physical transport of charges across a vacuum gap; it is a current-equivalent term arising purely from a time-varying electric field.
  • Magnetic Effect: Displacement current produces a magnetic field inside the capacitor gap in the exact same manner as a conduction current of equal magnitude.
  • Steady DC vs. AC: For a steady direct current (constant \(E\)), \(\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_E}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0\), so \(i_d = 0\). For an alternating current, \(i_d = C \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t}\).

2.3 Maxwell's Equations in Vacuum

Maxwell unified all fundamental laws of electricity and magnetism into four integral equations (shown here in vacuum):

Law Equation Physical Meaning
Gauss's Law for Electricity \(\displaystyle \oint \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}\) Electric charges are sources/sinks of electric field lines.
Gauss's Law for Magnetism \(\displaystyle \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} = 0\) Magnetic monopoles do not exist; magnetic field lines always form closed loops.
Faraday's Law of Induction \(\displaystyle \oint \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{l} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_B}{\mathrm{d}t}\) A time-varying magnetic field induces an electric field.
Ampere–Maxwell Law \(\displaystyle \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{l} = \mu_0 i_c + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_E}{\mathrm{d}t}\) Both conduction currents and time-varying electric fields produce magnetic fields.

3. Electromagnetic Waves: Origin, Nature, and Properties

3.1 How Electromagnetic Waves Are Produced

  • Stationary charge (\(v = 0\)): Produces only a static electric field; no magnetic field and no EM radiation.
  • Charge in uniform motion (\(v = \text{constant}, a = 0\)): Produces both electric and steady magnetic fields, but does not radiate EM waves.
  • Accelerated charge (\(a \neq 0\)): Radiates electromagnetic waves! An oscillating electric charge (an oscillating electric dipole) is a classic source of EM waves whose frequency \(\nu\) equals the frequency of oscillation of the charge.

Historically, Heinrich Hertz (1887) first generated and detected radio-frequency EM waves in the laboratory, confirming Maxwell's prediction. Shortly thereafter, Jagdish Chandra Bose produced much shorter waves (\(5\,\mathrm{mm}\) to \(25\,\mathrm{mm}\)), and Guglielmo Marconi pioneered long-distance wireless communication.

3.2 Transverse Nature and Wave Equations

In an electromagnetic wave, the oscillating electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) and magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) are mutually perpendicular to each other and also perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. Therefore, EM waves are strictly transverse waves.

For a linearly polarised plane EM wave propagating along the \(+z\)-direction, with \(\mathbf{E}\) along the \(x\)-axis and \(\mathbf{B}\) along the \(y\)-axis, the fields vary sinusoidally in space and time:

\[ E_x = E_0 \sin(kz - \omega t) = E_0 \sin\left[2\pi\left(\frac{z}{\lambda} - \nu t\right)\right] \] \[ B_y = B_0 \sin(kz - \omega t) = B_0 \sin\left[2\pi\left(\frac{z}{\lambda} - \nu t\right)\right] \]

Notice that \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) have the exact same phase \((kz - \omega t)\): both reach their maxima and zero crossings simultaneously! Furthermore, the direction of propagation is always given by the cross product \(\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}\).

3.3 Speed, Field Ratio, Energy Density, and Momentum

  • Speed in Vacuum: All EM waves—regardless of wavelength or frequency—travel through vacuum at the same constant speed:
    \[ c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} \approx 3 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m/s} \]
  • Speed in a Material Medium: In a medium of permittivity \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r\) and permeability \(\mu = \mu_0 \mu_r\), the wave speed decreases to:
    \[ v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu \varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r \varepsilon_r}} \implies n = \frac{c}{v} = \sqrt{\mu_r \varepsilon_r} \]
    where \(n\) is the refractive index of the medium.
  • Electric-to-Magnetic Field Ratio: At every instant and every point in vacuum, the magnitudes of \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are locked together by:
    \[ \frac{E_0}{B_0} = \frac{E}{B} = c \]
  • Equal Energy Partition: The average energy density of the electric field equals the average energy density of the magnetic field:
    \[ \langle u_E \rangle = \frac{1}{4}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_{\mathrm{rms}}^2, \qquad \langle u_B \rangle = \frac{B_0^2}{4\mu_0} = \frac{B_{\mathrm{rms}}^2}{2\mu_0} \]
    Since \(E_0 = c B_0\) and \(c^2 = 1/(\mu_0\varepsilon_0)\), we have \(\langle u_E \rangle = \langle u_B \rangle\). Therefore, the total average energy density is:
    \[ u_{\mathrm{avg}} = \langle u_E \rangle + \langle u_B \rangle = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \frac{B_0^2}{2\mu_0} = \varepsilon_0 E_{\mathrm{rms}}^2 \]
  • Intensity and Radiation Pressure: EM waves carry both energy and momentum. The intensity (energy crossing unit area per unit time) is \(I = u_{\mathrm{avg}} c\). When a wave of energy \(U\) is completely absorbed by a surface, it transfers momentum \(p = U/c\); if completely reflected, it transfers \(p = 2U/c\).
  • No Deflection by Fields: Because EM waves are uncharged field oscillations, they are not deflected by external electric or magnetic fields.

4. The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum classifies EM waves continuously according to wavelength \(\lambda\) or frequency \(\nu\) (where \(\nu \lambda = c\)). There are no sharp boundaries between adjacent bands; rather, regions overlap and are distinguished primarily by how the waves are generated and detected.

6. Problem-Solving Methods for NEET UG

Method A: Determining Field Directions and Wave Equation Parameters
  1. Identify propagation axis & direction: In \(\sin(kz - \omega t)\), the signs of \(kz\) and \(\omega t\) are opposite, so the wave travels in the \(+z\)-direction. In \(\sin(kx + \omega t)\), both signs are the same, so the wave travels in the \(-x\)-direction.
  2. Extract \(k\) and \(\omega\): Read the coefficient of the spatial variable as \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\) and the coefficient of \(t\) as \(\omega = 2\pi\nu\). Verify \(\omega/k = c\) (in vacuum).
  3. Find the missing field direction: Use the right-hand cross-product rule: \(\hat{E} \times \hat{B} = \hat{k}\) (unit propagation vector).
  4. Match the phase: Copy the exact phase argument \((kz \mp \omega t)\) from the given field to the unknown field, and compute the amplitude using \(E_0 = c B_0\).
Method B: Magnetic Field Between Charging Capacitor Plates
  1. Find the displacement current \(i_d\) (which equals the conduction current \(i_c\) in the leads).
  2. For a circular plate of radius \(R\), the displacement current density is uniform: \(J_d = \frac{i_d}{\pi R^2}\).
  3. For an interior circular loop of radius \(r \le R\), the enclosed displacement current is \(i_{d,\mathrm{enc}} = i_d \left(\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)\).
  4. Apply Ampere–Maxwell law: \(B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 i_d \frac{r^2}{R^2} \implies B = \frac{\mu_0 i_d r}{2\pi R^2}\) (linearly proportional to \(r\) inside, and \(B \propto 1/r\) outside for \(r > R\)).

7. Original Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Displacement Current & Magnetic Field in a Capacitor

(Assumed hypothetical values) A parallel-plate capacitor with circular plates of radius \(R = 10\,\mathrm{cm}\) and capacitance \(C = 200\,\mathrm{pF}\) is connected to an AC source of \(V_{\mathrm{rms}} = 200\,\mathrm{V}\) at angular frequency \(\omega = 500\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). Calculate: (a) the rms displacement current, and (b) the amplitude of the magnetic field at a point \(4.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the central axis between the plates.

Solution:

(a) The rms conduction current in the circuit equals the rms displacement current between the plates:

\[ i_{d,\mathrm{rms}} = i_{c,\mathrm{rms}} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{rms}}}{X_C} = \omega C V_{\mathrm{rms}} = (500\,\mathrm{rad/s})(200 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F})(200\,\mathrm{V}) = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{A} = 20\,\mathrm{µA} \]

(b) The peak (amplitude) displacement current is \(i_{d,0} = \sqrt{2}\, i_{d,\mathrm{rms}} = 2\sqrt{2} \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{A}\). At \(r = 4.0\,\mathrm{cm} = 0.04\,\mathrm{m}\) (inside \(R = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}\)):

\[ B_0 = \frac{\mu_0 i_{d,0} r}{2\pi R^2} = \frac{(4\pi \times 10^{-7})(2\sqrt{2} \times 10^{-5})(0.04)}{2\pi (0.10)^2} = 1.6\sqrt{2} \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{T} \approx 2.26 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{T} \]
Worked Example 2: Extracting Parameters from a Wave Equation

(Assumed hypothetical values) The electric field of a plane electromagnetic wave in vacuum is given by: \[ \mathbf{E} = 90 \sin(2.0 \times 10^2\,y + 6.0 \times 10^{10}\,t)\,\hat{k}\;\mathrm{V/m} \] Find: (a) the direction of propagation, (b) the wavelength \(\lambda\) and frequency \(\nu\), and (c) the expression for the magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\).

Solution:

(a) Both \(y\) and \(t\) terms carry the same \((+)\) sign, so the wave propagates along the \(-y\)-direction (\(\hat{k}_{\mathrm{prop}} = -\hat{j}\)).

(b) Comparing with \(E_0 \sin(ky + \omega t)\), we have \(k = 2.0 \times 10^2\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\) and \(\omega = 6.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{rad/s}\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{200} \approx 3.14 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 3.14\,\mathrm{cm}, \qquad \nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{6.0 \times 10^{10}}{2\pi} \approx 9.55 \times 10^9\,\mathrm{Hz} = 9.55\,\mathrm{GHz} \]

(c) Amplitude \(B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{90}{3 \times 10^8} = 3.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T}\). For direction, \(\hat{E} \times \hat{B} = \hat{k}_{\mathrm{prop}}\), so \(\hat{k} \times \hat{B} = -\hat{j}\). Since \(\hat{k} \times \hat{i} = +\hat{j}\), we must have \(\hat{B} = -\hat{i}\). Thus:

\[ \mathbf{B} = -3.0 \times 10^{-7} \sin(2.0 \times 10^2\,y + 6.0 \times 10^{10}\,t)\,\hat{i}\;\mathrm{T} \]
Worked Example 3: Energy Density and Wave Speed in a Medium

(Assumed hypothetical values) A plane EM wave in vacuum has an electric field amplitude \(E_0 = 60\,\mathrm{V/m}\). (a) Calculate its total average energy density. (b) If this wave enters a non-magnetic dielectric medium (\(\mu_r = 1\)) with relative permittivity \(\varepsilon_r = 2.25\), what is its speed in the medium? (Take \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{C^2 N^{-1} m^{-2}}\).)

Solution:

(a) Total average energy density in vacuum:

\[ u_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}(8.85 \times 10^{-12})(60)^2 = 1.593 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{J/m^3} \]

(b) Speed in the dielectric medium:

\[ v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r \varepsilon_r}} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{\sqrt{1 \times 2.25}} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{1.5} = 2.0 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m/s} \]

Practice Question 1 • Single-Correct MCQ
A parallel-plate capacitor with plate area \(A\) and plate separation \(d\) is being charged by a constant current \(I\). The rate of change of electric field \(\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t}\) between the plates is:
  1. \(\displaystyle \frac{I}{\varepsilon_0 A}\)
  2. \(\displaystyle \frac{\varepsilon_0 I}{A}\)
  3. \(\displaystyle \frac{I d}{\varepsilon_0 A}\)
  4. \(\displaystyle \frac{I A}{\varepsilon_0}\)
Answer: A

Worked Solution: Inside the capacitor, the electric flux is \(\Phi_E = E A\). Displacement current equals the charging current: \(I = i_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_E}{\mathrm{d}t} = \varepsilon_0 A \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t}\). Rearranging gives \(\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{I}{\varepsilon_0 A}\).

Practice Question 2 • Single-Correct MCQ
A plane electromagnetic wave travels in vacuum along the \(+x\)-direction. At a certain point and instant, its electric field vector is \(\mathbf{E} = 45\,\hat{j}\;\mathrm{V/m}\). What is the magnetic field vector \(\mathbf{B}\) at that point and instant?
  1. \(1.5 \times 10^{-7}\,\hat{k}\;\mathrm{T}\)
  2. \(-1.5 \times 10^{-7}\,\hat{k}\;\mathrm{T}\)
  3. \(1.5 \times 10^{-7}\,\hat{i}\;\mathrm{T}\)
  4. \(-1.5 \times 10^{-7}\,\hat{j}\;\mathrm{T}\)
Answer: A

Worked Solution: Magnitude \(B = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{45}{3 \times 10^8} = 1.5 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T}\). For direction, \(\hat{E} \times \hat{B} = \hat{k}_{\mathrm{prop}}\), so \(\hat{j} \times \hat{B} = +\hat{i}\). Since \(\hat{j} \times \hat{k} = +\hat{i}\), \(\mathbf{B}\) is along \(+\hat{k}\).

Practice Question 3 • Single-Correct MCQ
Which of the following statements about electromagnetic waves is incorrect?
  1. They are produced by accelerated electric charges.
  2. In vacuum, the electric and magnetic field oscillations differ in phase by \(\pi/2\).
  3. They carry both energy and linear momentum.
  4. Their speed in vacuum is independent of wavelength and frequency.
Answer: B

Worked Solution: In an electromagnetic wave propagating in vacuum, \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are always in phase (phase difference is zero), reaching their maxima and minima simultaneously. Thus, statement (B) is incorrect.

Practice Question 4 • Single-Correct MCQ
A circular parallel-plate capacitor of plate radius \(R = 6.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) is charged by a steady current of \(0.24\,\mathrm{A}\). What is the ratio of the magnetic field at a radial distance \(r_1 = 3.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) to that at \(r_2 = 12.0\,\mathrm{cm}\) from the axis?
  1. \(1 : 1\)
  2. \(1 : 2\)
  3. \(2 : 1\)
  4. \(4 : 1\)
Answer: A

Worked Solution: At \(r_1 = 3.0\,\mathrm{cm} < R\), \(B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 i_d r_1}{2\pi R^2}\). At \(r_2 = 12.0\,\mathrm{cm} > R\) (outside the plates), the entire displacement current is enclosed, so \(B_2 = \frac{\mu_0 i_d}{2\pi r_2}\). Taking the ratio: \(\frac{B_1}{B_2} = \frac{r_1 r_2}{R^2} = \frac{3.0 \times 12.0}{6.0^2} = \frac{36}{36} = 1 : 1\).

Practice Question 5 • Single-Correct MCQ
What is the ratio of the average electric energy density to the total average energy density in a plane electromagnetic wave?
  1. \(1 : 1\)
  2. \(1 : 2\)
  3. \(1 : 4\)
  4. \(2 : 1\)
Answer: B

Worked Solution: Energy is equally partitioned between the electric and magnetic fields: \(\langle u_E \rangle = \langle u_B \rangle\). Since \(u_{\mathrm{total}} = \langle u_E \rangle + \langle u_B \rangle = 2\langle u_E \rangle\), the ratio \(\frac{\langle u_E \rangle}{u_{\mathrm{total}}} = \frac{1}{2}\).

Practice Question 6 • Single-Correct MCQ
Arrange the following electromagnetic radiations in order of increasing wavelength:
1. X-rays    2. Microwaves    3. Ultraviolet rays    4. Infrared rays
  1. 1, 3, 4, 2
  2. 2, 4, 3, 1

Practice Question 7 • Single-Correct MCQ
The dimensional formula of \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}}\) is identical to that of:
  1. \(\displaystyle \frac{B}{E}\)
  2. \(\displaystyle \frac{E}{B}\)
  3. \(\displaystyle E \cdot B\)
  4. \(\displaystyle \frac{E^2}{B^2}\)
Answer: B

Worked Solution: \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}} = c\) (speed of light, \([\mathrm{L T^{-1}}]\)). Since \(E = c B\), the ratio \(\frac{E}{B} = c\) also has dimensions \([\mathrm{L T^{-1}}]\).

Practice Question 8 • Assertion–Reason MCQ
Assertion (A): Ordinary glass windows prevent a person sitting indoors from getting sunburned by sunlight.
Reason (R): Ordinary glass absorbs ultraviolet (UV) radiation, which is responsible for tanning and sunburn.
  1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
  2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
  3. A is true, but R is false.
  4. A is false, but R is true.
Answer: A

Worked Solution: Sunburn and skin tanning (melanin production) are caused by UV rays from the Sun. Because ordinary glass strongly absorbs UV radiation while transmitting visible light, a person behind a glass window does not get sunburned. Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A.

Practice Question 9 • Single-Correct MCQ
A radiation of energy \(E\) falls normally on a perfectly reflecting mirror. What is the momentum transferred to the mirror?
  1. \(\displaystyle \frac{E}{c}\)
  2. \(\displaystyle \frac{2E}{c}\)
  3. \(\displaystyle E c\)
  4. Zero
Answer: B

Worked Solution: The incident radiation carries momentum \(p_i = +\frac{E}{c}\). Upon complete normal reflection, its momentum reverses to \(p_f = -\frac{E}{c}\). By conservation of momentum, the magnitude of momentum transferred to the mirror is \(|\Delta p| = \frac{E}{c} - \left(-\frac{E}{c}\right) = \frac{2E}{c}\).

Practice Question 10 • Statement-Based MCQ
Read the following two statements carefully:
Statement I: Microwaves are used in domestic microwave ovens because their frequency matches the resonant frequency of water molecules.
Statement II: Infrared waves are used in LASIK eye surgery because they can be focused into extremely narrow beams for high-precision tissue cutting.
  1. Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
  2. Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
  3. Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect.
  4. Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct.
Answer: C

Worked Solution: Statement I is correct: microwave ovens match the resonant frequency of water molecules to transfer energy efficiently to food. Statement II is incorrect: ultraviolet (UV) radiation—not infrared—is used in LASIK eye surgery because of its much shorter wavelength, which allows focusing into very narrow, high-precision beams.

9. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Watch Out for These Frequent NEET Traps
  • Peak vs. RMS confusion: Always check whether a question gives \(E_0\) (peak) or \(E_{\mathrm{rms}} = E_0 / \sqrt{2}\). Remember \(u_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \varepsilon_0 E_{\mathrm{rms}}^2\).
  • Sign in the phase: In \(\sin(kz - \omega t)\), the wave moves along \(+z\). In \(\sin(kz + \omega t)\), it moves along \(-z\).
  • Cross-product order: Propagation is along \(\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}\), not \(\mathbf{B} \times \mathbf{E}\).
  • Magnetic field inside vs. outside a capacitor: Inside the plates (\(r \le R\)), \(B \propto r\); outside (\(r > R\)), \(B \propto 1/r\).
  • UV vs. IR applications: UV is used in LASIK and water purifiers (and blocked by glass and ozone); IR is "heat waves" (greenhouse effect, remote controls, physical therapy).

10. Concise Final Recap

Maxwell's addition of displacement current \(i_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_E}{\mathrm{d}t}\) completed the symmetry between electricity and magnetism, revealing that time-varying electric and magnetic fields sustain each other as transverse electromagnetic waves. Accelerated charges radiate these waves, which travel through vacuum at \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = E_0/B_0\) with \(\mathbf{E} \perp \mathbf{B} \perp \mathbf{k}\) and equal average energy densities in both fields. Spanning from high-energy gamma rays to long-wavelength radio waves, the electromagnetic spectrum underpins modern communication, medicine, and atmospheric physics.
