NEET UG Physics • Class 12 Syllabus

Electromagnetic Induction

Conceptual Foundations, Quantitative Laws, Inductance, AC Generation, and Exam-Oriented Practice

1. Learning Objectives and Chapter Overview

Electric currents produce magnetic fields, a discovery that unified electricity and magnetism in the early nineteenth century. Michael Faraday in England and Joseph Henry in the United States independently demonstrated the converse phenomenon around 1830: a changing magnetic field generates an electromotive force (emf) and, in a closed circuit, an electric current. This phenomenon, called electromagnetic induction, forms the operating principle of electrical generators, transformers, induction cooktops, and modern power grids.

Learning Objectives for NEET UG
  • Define magnetic flux \(\Phi_B\) and compute it for planar and non-uniform surfaces.
  • Apply Faraday's law of induction to determine the magnitude of induced emf, induced current, and total induced charge.
  • Use Lenz's law and energy conservation to predict the direction of induced currents and magnetic polarities.
  • Analyze motional emf in translating and rotating conductors using both flux-rate and Lorentz-force perspectives.
  • Calculate self-inductance \(L\), mutual inductance \(M\), and magnetic energy density \(u_B\) for solenoids and coaxial coils.
  • Explain the working principle and instantaneous emf equation of an alternating current (AC) generator.

2. Magnetic Flux

Before quantifying induced emf, we must measure how much magnetic field passes through a given surface. Magnetic flux (\(\Phi_B\)) through a surface is analogous to electric flux: it measures the total number of magnetic field lines crossing that surface perpendicularly.

For a flat surface of area \(A\) placed in a uniform magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), we define an area vector \(\mathbf{A} = A\,\hat{n}\) directed perpendicular (normal) to the plane of the surface. If \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{B}\) and the normal vector \(\mathbf{A}\), the magnetic flux is the scalar dot product:

\[ \Phi_B = \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{A} = B A \cos\theta \]

If the surface is curved or the magnetic field varies from point to point, we divide the surface into tiny planar elements \(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{A}_i\) and sum the contributions:

\[ \Phi_B = \sum_{\text{all}} \mathbf{B}_i \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A}_i = \int \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{A} \]
  • Nature: Magnetic flux is a scalar quantity (positive, negative, or zero depending on \(\theta\)).
  • SI Unit: weber (\(\mathrm{Wb}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{Wb} = 1\,\mathrm{T \cdot m^2}\).
  • CGS Unit: maxwell (\(\mathrm{Mx}\)), where \(1\,\mathrm{Wb} = 10^8\,\mathrm{Mx}\).
  • Dimensional Formula: \([\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}\,A^{-1}}]\).
Tutorial Diagram 1: Uniform magnetic field B crossing a planar loop of area A at an angle θ to its normal.
Angle Trap in NEET Questions

Always verify whether the problem gives the angle with the normal to the plane (\(\theta\)) or with the plane of the coil itself (\(\alpha\)). Since the normal is perpendicular to the plane, \(\theta = 90° - \alpha\), so \(\cos\theta = \sin\alpha\). When a magnetic field is parallel to the plane of a loop, \(\theta = 90°\) and \(\Phi_B = 0\).

3. Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction

Experimental Insights

Classic coil-and-galvanometer experiments reveal three equivalent ways to induce an electric current in a closed conducting loop:

  1. Relative motion between a magnet and a coil: Pushing a bar magnet toward or pulling it away from a stationary coil causes a galvanometer deflection that reverses when the motion reverses or when the magnet's poles are swapped. Faster motion produces a larger deflection. Holding the magnet stationary gives zero deflection.
  2. Relative motion between two coils: Replacing the bar magnet with a second coil carrying a steady battery current produces identical effects whenever either coil moves relative to the other.
  3. Time-varying current in a stationary neighbouring coil: Keeping both coils fixed, closing or opening a switch in the primary coil's circuit causes a momentary deflection in the secondary coil's galvanometer. Once the primary current becomes steady, the induced current drops to zero. Inserting a soft iron core along their common axis dramatically increases the deflection.

Mathematical Statement

Faraday unified all these observations into a single law: an emf is induced in a circuit whenever the magnetic flux linked with it changes with time.

Faraday's Law of Induction

The magnitude of the induced emf in a circuit equals the time rate of change of magnetic flux through the circuit. For a closely wound coil of \(N\) turns, each turn experiences the same flux \(\Phi_B\), giving a total flux linkage \(N\Phi_B\):

\[ \varepsilon = -N \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_B}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

If the circuit is closed with total resistance \(R\), the induced current and total induced charge flowing in time interval \(\Delta t\) are:

\[ I_{\text{ind}} = \frac{|\varepsilon|}{R} = \frac{N}{R}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_B}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|, \qquad \Delta q = \int I_{\text{ind}}\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{N\,|\Delta \Phi_B|}{R} \]

Notice a crucial exam insight: induced emf and induced current depend on how fast the flux changes (\(\mathrm{d}\Phi_B/\mathrm{d}t\)), whereas the total induced charge \(\Delta q\) depends only on the net change in flux linkage and the circuit resistance—it is completely independent of the time taken!

Since \(\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta\), flux can be changed by varying: (i) the magnetic field magnitude \(B\), (ii) the loop area \(A\) (stretching, shrinking, or moving out of a field region), or (iii) the orientation angle \(\theta\) (rotating the coil).

4. Lenz's Law and Conservation of Energy

The minus sign in Faraday's law represents Lenz's law, formulated by Heinrich Lenz in 1834:

Lenz's Law

The polarity of the induced emf is always such that it tends to produce a current that opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it.

Think of Lenz's law as nature's electromagnetic inertia: it does not oppose the magnetic flux itself, nor does it necessarily oppose motion—it opposes the change in flux.

  • If flux through a loop is increasing, the induced current creates its own magnetic field opposite to the external field to resist the increase.
  • If flux through a loop is decreasing, the induced current creates its own magnetic field in the same direction as the external field to resist the decrease.
Tutorial Diagram 2: Induced coil polarity opposes the magnet's motion as viewed from the magnet side.

Connection to Conservation of Energy

Suppose an approaching North pole induced a South pole on the near face of the coil. The magnet would then be attracted and accelerate continuously without any external work, creating kinetic energy and electrical energy out of nothing—a perpetual-motion machine that violates energy conservation. Because the induced pole actually repels the approaching magnet (and attracts the receding magnet), an external agent must perform mechanical work against the opposing magnetic force. This mechanical work is converted into electrical energy and ultimately dissipated as Joule heat (\(I^2 R t\)) in the circuit.

Even in an open circuit (such as a loop with a small cut), an emf is still induced across the open ends, though no steady current flows.

5. Motional and Rotational Electromotive Force

Translating Conductor in a Uniform Magnetic Field

Consider a straight conducting rod of length \(l\) sliding at constant speed \(v\) along two parallel frictionless rails connected at one end, placed in a uniform, time-independent magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\) directed perpendicular to the plane of the rails. If the rod is at distance \(x\) from the closed end and moves inward so that \(x\) decreases (\(\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t = -v\)), the enclosed magnetic flux is \(\Phi_B = B l x\). By Faraday's law:

\[ \varepsilon = -\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_B}{\mathrm{d}t} = -Bl\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = B l v \]

We can also derive this directly from the Lorentz force \(\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\) acting on free charge carriers inside the moving rod. As the rod moves with velocity \(\mathbf{v}\), each free positive charge \(q\) experiences a magnetic force \(qvB\) along the rod, doing work \(W = qvBl\) to move from one end to the other. Dividing by \(q\) gives \(\varepsilon = W/q = Blv\).

More generally, for a straight conductor of vector length \(\mathbf{l}\) moving at velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) in a uniform field \(\mathbf{B}\):

\[ \varepsilon = (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot \mathbf{l} \]

If \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{B}\), and \(\mathbf{l}\) are mutually perpendicular, \(\varepsilon = Blv\). If any two of \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{B}\), and \(\mathbf{l}\) are parallel, the induced motional emf is zero.

Tutorial Diagram 3: Sliding rod PQ moving left at speed v in an inward magnetic field B induces emf Blv.

Rotational EMF in a Conducting Rod or Wheel

When a conducting rod of length \(R\) rotates with constant angular velocity \(\omega\) (or frequency \(\nu = \omega / 2\pi\)) about one end in a plane perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field \(B\), an element \(\mathrm{d}r\) at distance \(r\) from the pivot moves at tangential speed \(v = \omega r\). Integrating \(\mathrm{d}\varepsilon = B(\omega r)\,\mathrm{d}r\) from \(0\) to \(R\) yields:

\[ \varepsilon = \int_0^R B\omega r\,\mathrm{d}r = \frac{1}{2} B \omega R^2 = \pi \nu B R^2 \]

If a wheel has \(N\) metallic spokes connecting its central axle to its metallic rim, all \(N\) spokes are connected in parallel between the axle and the rim. Therefore, the net induced emf between the axle and the rim remains \(\frac{1}{2} B \omega R^2\), completely independent of the number of spokes.

Tutorial Diagram 4: Rod OQ of length R rotating at angular speed ω in a perpendicular field B.

Stationary Conductor in a Time-Varying Magnetic Field

When a conductor is stationary (\(\mathbf{v} = 0\)) and the magnetic field varies with time, the magnetic Lorentz force \(q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})\) is zero. Yet an emf is still induced. This proves that a time-varying magnetic field generates an induced electric field \(\mathbf{E}_{\text{ind}}\). Unlike electrostatic fields produced by static charges, this induced electric field forms closed loops and is non-conservative.

6. Inductance and Magnetic Energy

For any rigid coil or pair of coils, the magnetic flux linked with a coil is directly proportional to the current producing it: \(N\Phi_B \propto I\). The proportionality constant is called inductance, a scalar quantity with SI unit henry (\(\mathrm{H}\)) and dimensional formula \([\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}\,A^{-2}}]\). Just as capacitance depends solely on geometry and permittivity, inductance depends solely on coil geometry, relative orientation/separation, and core permeability.

Self-Inductance (\(L\))

When the current \(I\) through an isolated coil of \(N\) turns changes, the changing flux through the same coil induces a back emf that opposes the change in current:

\[ N\Phi_B = L I \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \varepsilon = -L \frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

Physically, self-inductance \(L\) acts as electromagnetic inertia (analogous to mass \(m\) in mechanics), opposing both the growth and decay of current. For a long solenoid of cross-sectional area \(A\), length \(l\), and \(n = N/l\) turns per unit length, filled with a core of relative permeability \(\mu_r\):

\[ L = \mu_r \mu_0 n^2 A l = \frac{\mu_r \mu_0 N^2 A}{l} \]

Magnetic Potential Energy and Energy Density

To build a current from \(0\) to \(I\) against the back emf, an external source must do work at rate \(\mathrm{d}W/\mathrm{d}t = |\varepsilon|I = L I (\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}t)\). Integrating from \(0\) to \(I\) gives the magnetic energy stored in the inductor:

\[ U_B = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 \]

For an air-core solenoid (\(B = \mu_0 n I\), volume \(V = A l\)), substituting \(L = \mu_0 n^2 A l\) and \(I = B / (\mu_0 n)\) yields the universal magnetic energy density (energy per unit volume):

\[ u_B = \frac{U_B}{Al} = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \qquad \left(\text{compare with electrostatic } u_E = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2\right) \]

Mutual Inductance (\(M\))

When two coils are placed near each other, a time-varying current \(I_2\) in one coil changes the magnetic flux \(\Phi_1\) linked with the neighbouring coil of \(N_1\) turns:

\[ N_1 \Phi_1 = M_{12} I_2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \varepsilon_1 = -M_{12} \frac{\mathrm{d}I_2}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

By the reciprocity theorem, the mutual inductance of coil 1 with respect to coil 2 equals that of coil 2 with respect to coil 1:

\[ M_{12} = M_{21} = M \]
  • Two long coaxial solenoids of equal length \(l\), turn densities \(n_1, n_2\), inner radius \(r_1\), and outer radius \(r_2\) (\(r_1 < r_2\)) with core relative permeability \(\mu_r\): \[ M = \mu_r \mu_0 n_1 n_2 \pi r_1^2 l \] Notice that the cross-sectional area used is \(\pi r_1^2\) (the inner solenoid's area, where the magnetic flux is shared).
  • Two concentric coplanar circular coils of radii \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) with \(r_1 \ll r_2\) and turns \(N_1, N_2\): \[ M = \frac{\mu_0 N_1 N_2 \pi r_1^2}{2 r_2} \]
Tutorial Diagram 5: Two long coaxial solenoids of length l sharing flux over the inner area πr₁².

When currents \(I_1\) and \(I_2\) change simultaneously in two nearby coils, the total induced emf in coil 1 combines self-induction and mutual induction:

\[ \varepsilon_1 = -L_1 \frac{\mathrm{d}I_1}{\mathrm{d}t} - M \frac{\mathrm{d}I_2}{\mathrm{d}t} \]

7. Alternating Current (AC) Generator

An AC generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by rotating an armature coil of \(N\) turns and area \(A\) at a constant angular speed \(\omega = 2\pi\nu\) in a uniform magnetic field \(B\), with the axis of rotation perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\). Slip rings and stationary carbon brushes transfer the induced alternating emf to an external load.

If the normal to the coil is aligned with \(\mathbf{B}\) at \(t = 0\) (\(\theta = 0°\)), then at time \(t\) the angle is \(\theta = \omega t\), so the flux is \(\Phi_B(t) = B A \cos(\omega t)\). Applying Faraday's law gives:

\[ \varepsilon(t) = -N\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl(BA\cos\omega t\bigr) = N B A \omega \sin(\omega t) = \varepsilon_0 \sin(2\pi\nu t) \]

Here \(\varepsilon_0 = N B A \omega = 2\pi \nu N B A\) is the peak (maximum) emf. Notice the phase relationship:

  • When the coil plane is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\) (\(\theta = 0°, 180°, 360°\)), flux \(\Phi_B\) is maximum in magnitude, but its rate of change is zero, so \(\varepsilon = 0\).
  • When the coil plane is parallel to \(\mathbf{B}\) (\(\theta = 90°, 270°\)), flux \(\Phi_B = 0\), but its rate of change is steepest, so \(|\varepsilon| = \varepsilon_0\) (maximum).

9. Problem-Solving Methods for NEET UG

Method A: Three-Step Lenz's Law Procedure

  1. Identify the external field direction through the loop (e.g., into the page, out of the page, or along an axis).
  2. Determine whether the flux is increasing or decreasing (due to changing \(B\), changing \(A\), or motion into/out of the field).
  3. Assign the induced magnetic field direction: opposite to \(\mathbf{B}_{\text{ext}}\) if flux is increasing; along \(\mathbf{B}_{\text{ext}}\) if flux is decreasing. Curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the induced field thumb to read the induced current direction.

Method B: Polarity of Motional EMF

To find which end of a moving rod is at higher potential, point the fingers of your right hand along \(\mathbf{v}\) and curl them toward \(\mathbf{B}\). Your thumb points in the direction of \(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}\), which is the force on positive charges—hence the higher-potential (+) end. Inside the moving rod, conventional current flows from the lower-potential end to the higher-potential end (just like inside a battery).

10. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Tilted Loop in a Decaying Magnetic Field
Note: Numerical values below are hypothetical assumptions created for practice.

A flat circular coil of \(20\) turns, radius \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\), and total resistance \(4\,\mathrm{Ω}\) is placed in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.50\,\mathrm{T}\). The normal to the plane of the coil makes an angle of \(60°\) with the magnetic field. If the magnetic field is reduced uniformly to zero in \(0.25\,\mathrm{s}\), calculate: (a) the initial magnetic flux through one turn, (b) the magnitude of the induced emf, and (c) the total charge that flows through the coil.

Solution:

  • Area of one turn: \(A = \pi r^2 = \pi (0.10\,\mathrm{m})^2 = 0.01\pi\,\mathrm{m^2}\).
  • (a) Initial flux per turn: \[ \Phi_i = B A \cos 60° = 0.50 \times (0.01\pi) \times 0.5 = 2.5\pi \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb} \approx 7.85 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb} \]
  • (b) Final flux \(\Phi_f = 0\), so \(|\Delta \Phi_B| = 7.85 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Wb}\). Magnitude of induced emf: \[ |\varepsilon| = N \frac{|\Delta \Phi_B|}{\Delta t} = 20 \times \frac{7.85 \times 10^{-3}}{0.25} = 0.628\,\mathrm{V} \]
  • (c) Total induced charge: \[ \Delta q = \frac{N |\Delta \Phi_B|}{R} = \frac{20 \times 7.85 \times 10^{-3}}{4} = 3.93 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{C} \]
Worked Example 2: Rotating Spoked Wheel in Earth's Magnetic Field
Note: Numerical values below are hypothetical assumptions created for practice.

A bicycle wheel with \(12\) metallic spokes, each of length \(0.40\,\mathrm{m}\), rotates at \(180\,\mathrm{rev/min}\) in a vertical plane perpendicular to the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field \(B_H = 0.50\,\mathrm{G}\) (\(1\,\mathrm{G} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}\)). Find the emf induced between the axle and the rim.

Solution:

  • Frequency: \(\nu = \frac{180}{60} = 3\,\mathrm{rev/s}\).
  • Magnetic field: \(B_H = 0.50 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}\).
  • All \(12\) spokes are in parallel between the axle and the rim, so the number of spokes does not affect the emf: \[ \varepsilon = \pi \nu B_H R^2 = 3.14 \times 3 \times (0.50 \times 10^{-4}) \times (0.40)^2 \approx 7.54 \times 10^{-5}\,\mathrm{V} \]
Worked Example 3: Rectangular Loop Exiting a Magnetic Field
Note: Numerical values below are hypothetical assumptions created for practice.

A rectangular conducting loop of dimensions \(10\,\mathrm{cm} \times 4\,\mathrm{cm}\) with a small cut is pulled out of a uniform magnetic field of \(0.40\,\mathrm{T}\) (directed normal to the loop) at a constant speed of \(2\,\mathrm{cm/s}\) perpendicular to its \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) side. Find the emf developed across the cut and the duration for which it lasts.

Solution:

  • Here the effective conductor length cutting field lines is the \(10\,\mathrm{cm}\) side: \(l = 0.10\,\mathrm{m}\), \(v = 0.02\,\mathrm{m/s}\).
  • Induced emf: \(\varepsilon = B l v = 0.40 \times 0.10 \times 0.02 = 8.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{V} = 0.80\,\mathrm{mV}\).
  • Distance travelled to exit completely is the \(4\,\mathrm{cm}\) side (\(d = 0.04\,\mathrm{m}\)). Duration: \[ \Delta t = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{4\,\mathrm{cm}}{2\,\mathrm{cm/s}} = 2.0\,\mathrm{s} \]
Worked Example 4: Mutual Inductance and Induced EMF in Coaxial Coils
Note: Numerical values below are hypothetical assumptions created for practice.

A pair of neighbouring coils has a mutual inductance of \(2.4\,\mathrm{H}\). If the current in the primary coil changes uniformly from \(10\,\mathrm{A}\) to \(2\,\mathrm{A}\) in \(0.40\,\mathrm{s}\), calculate (a) the change in flux linkage with the secondary coil, and (b) the average emf induced in the secondary coil.

Solution:

  • (a) Magnitude of change in flux linkage: \[ |\Delta (N_2 \Phi_2)| = M |\Delta I_1| = 2.4 \times |2 - 10| = 19.2\,\mathrm{Wb} \]
  • (b) Magnitude of average induced emf: \[ |\varepsilon_2| = M \left|\frac{\Delta I_1}{\Delta t}\right| = 2.4 \times \frac{8}{0.40} = 48\,\mathrm{V} \]
Worked Example 5: Peak and Instantaneous EMF of an AC Generator
Note: Numerical values below are hypothetical assumptions created for practice.

An AC generator coil of \(200\) turns and area \(0.05\,\mathrm{m^2}\) rotates at \(50\,\mathrm{rev/s}\) in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.20\,\mathrm{T}\). Assuming \(\theta = 0°\) at \(t = 0\), find (a) the peak emf, and (b) the instantaneous emf at \(t = \frac{1}{600}\,\mathrm{s}\).

Solution:

  • (a) Angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi\nu = 2\pi \times 50 = 100\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). Peak emf: \[ \varepsilon_0 = N B A \omega = 200 \times 0.20 \times 0.05 \times 100\pi = 200\pi\,\mathrm{V} \approx 628\,\mathrm{V} \]
  • (b) At \(t = \frac{1}{600}\,\mathrm{s}\), phase \(\omega t = 100\pi \times \frac{1}{600} = \frac{\pi}{6}\,\mathrm{rad} = 30°\): \[ \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0 \sin(30°) = 628 \times 0.5 = 314\,\mathrm{V} \]

Practice Question 1 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A circular loop of area \(0.2\,\mathrm{m^2}\) is placed in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.5\,\mathrm{T}\) such that the plane of the loop makes an angle of \(30°\) with the magnetic field. The magnetic flux through the loop is:

  • (A) \(0.05\,\mathrm{Wb}\)
  • (B) \(0.0866\,\mathrm{Wb}\)
  • (C) \(0.10\,\mathrm{Wb}\)
  • (D) \(0.025\,\mathrm{Wb}\)
Answer: (A) \(0.05\,\mathrm{Wb}\)

Solution: The angle between the magnetic field and the plane is \(30°\), so the angle between \(\mathbf{B}\) and the normal \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(\theta = 90° - 30° = 60°\). Therefore, \(\Phi_B = B A \cos 60° = 0.5 \times 0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.05\,\mathrm{Wb}\).

Practice Question 2 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The magnetic flux linked with a coil of resistance \(10\,\mathrm{Ω}\) varies with time as \(\Phi_B = (4t^2 - 2t + 5)\,\mathrm{Wb}\), where \(t\) is in seconds. The magnitude of induced current at \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\) is:

  • (A) \(1.4\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (B) \(1.8\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (C) \(0.7\,\mathrm{A}\)
  • (D) \(2.1\,\mathrm{A}\)
Answer: (A) \(1.4\,\mathrm{A}\)

Solution: Induced emf magnitude \(|\varepsilon| = \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_B}{\mathrm{d}t}\right| = |8t - 2|\). At \(t = 2\,\mathrm{s}\), \(|\varepsilon| = 8(2) - 2 = 14\,\mathrm{V}\). Induced current \(I = \frac{|\varepsilon|}{R} = \frac{14}{10} = 1.4\,\mathrm{A}\).

Practice Question 3 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A closed conducting coil of \(50\) turns and resistance \(20\,\mathrm{Ω}\) experiences a change in magnetic flux per turn from \(0.06\,\mathrm{Wb}\) to \(0.02\,\mathrm{Wb}\) in \(0.1\,\mathrm{s}\). The total charge that flows through the coil during this interval is:

  • (A) \(0.05\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (B) \(0.10\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (C) \(0.20\,\mathrm{C}\)
  • (D) \(1.00\,\mathrm{C}\)
Answer: (B) \(0.10\,\mathrm{C}\)

Solution: Total induced charge \(\Delta q = \frac{N |\Delta\Phi_B|}{R} = \frac{50 \times |0.02 - 0.06|}{20} = \frac{50 \times 0.04}{20} = 0.10\,\mathrm{C}\).

Practice Question 4 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Lenz's law is a direct consequence of the law of conservation of:

  • (A) Electric charge
  • (B) Linear momentum
  • (C) Angular momentum
  • (D) Energy
Answer: (D) Energy

Solution: Lenz's law ensures that the induced current opposes the flux change, requiring an external agent to do mechanical work that is converted into electrical energy and Joule heat, thereby upholding conservation of energy.

Practice Question 5 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A vertical straight wire carries an upward current \(I\) that is decreasing at a steady rate. A horizontal circular conducting ring is placed coaxially around the wire (with the wire passing through its centre perpendicular to its plane). The induced current in the ring is:

  • (A) Clockwise as viewed from above
  • (B) Counter-clockwise as viewed from above
  • (C) Zero
  • (D) Alternating between clockwise and counter-clockwise
Answer: (C) Zero

Solution: Magnetic field lines due to a straight current-carrying wire form concentric circles lying in the plane of the horizontal ring. Thus, no magnetic field lines cross the surface of the ring (\(\Phi_B = 0\) at all times), so \(\mathrm{d}\Phi_B/\mathrm{d}t = 0\) and no current is induced.

Practice Question 6 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A horizontal metal rod \(6\,\mathrm{m}\) long aligned along the east-west direction falls freely from rest under gravity (\(g = 10\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\)) in a region where the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field is \(0.30 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{T}\) (directed south-to-north). The emf induced across the ends of the rod \(2\,\mathrm{s}\) after release is:

  • (A) \(1.8\,\mathrm{mV}\)
  • (B) \(3.6\,\mathrm{mV}\)
  • (C) \(0.9\,\mathrm{mV}\)
  • (D) \(7.2\,\mathrm{mV}\)
Answer: (B) \(3.6\,\mathrm{mV}\)

Solution: Speed after \(2\,\mathrm{s}\): \(v = g t = 10 \times 2 = 20\,\mathrm{m/s}\). Since velocity (vertical), length (east-west), and \(B_H\) (south-north) are mutually perpendicular, \(\varepsilon = B_H l v = (0.30 \times 10^{-4}) \times 6 \times 20 = 3.6 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{V} = 3.6\,\mathrm{mV}\).

Practice Question 7 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A conducting loop of irregular flexible shape lies in a plane perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field directed into the page. If the loop is pulled outward so that it transforms into a circular shape (increasing its enclosed area) while remaining in the field, the induced current in the loop flows:

  • (A) Counter-clockwise
  • (B) Clockwise
  • (C) Radially inward
  • (D) No current is induced
Answer: (A) Counter-clockwise

Solution: A circle encloses the maximum area for a given perimeter, so the inward magnetic flux increases. By Lenz's law, the induced current must produce an outward magnetic field to oppose this increase, which requires a counter-clockwise current.

Practice Question 8 (Single-Correct MCQ)

A metallic disc of radius \(0.2\,\mathrm{m}\) rotates at \(1200\,\mathrm{rev/min}\) about an axis perpendicular to its plane and passing through its centre, in a uniform axial magnetic field of \(0.5\,\mathrm{T}\). The potential difference between the centre and the rim of the disc is:

  • (A) \(0.2\pi\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(0.4\pi\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(0.8\pi\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(1.6\pi\,\mathrm{V}\)
Answer: (B) \(0.4\pi\,\mathrm{V}\)

Solution: Frequency \(\nu = \frac{1200}{60} = 20\,\mathrm{rev/s}\). Using \(\varepsilon = \pi \nu B R^2 = \pi \times 20 \times 0.5 \times (0.2)^2 = 10\pi \times 0.04 = 0.4\pi\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 9 (Single-Correct MCQ)

The current in a coil of self-inductance \(L = 0.8\,\mathrm{H}\) increases uniformly from \(2\,\mathrm{A}\) to \(7\,\mathrm{A}\) in \(0.2\,\mathrm{s}\). The magnitude of the self-induced back emf and the energy stored at \(7\,\mathrm{A}\) are, respectively:

  • (A) \(20\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(19.6\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (B) \(20\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(39.2\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (C) \(28\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(19.6\,\mathrm{J}\)
  • (D) \(10\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(14.0\,\mathrm{J}\)
Answer: (A) \(20\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(19.6\,\mathrm{J}\)

Solution: Back emf \(|\varepsilon| = L \frac{\Delta I}{\Delta t} = 0.8 \times \frac{7 - 2}{0.2} = 0.8 \times 25 = 20\,\mathrm{V}\). Stored magnetic energy \(U_B = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.8 \times 7^2 = 0.4 \times 49 = 19.6\,\mathrm{J}\).

Practice Question 10 (Single-Correct MCQ)

Two long coaxial air-core solenoids have the same length \(l\). The inner solenoid has radius \(r_1\) and turn density \(n_1\), while the outer solenoid has radius \(r_2 = 2r_1\) and turn density \(n_2\). If the radius of the outer solenoid is doubled to \(4r_1\) (keeping \(l, n_1, n_2, r_1\) unchanged), the mutual inductance of the pair:

  • (A) Doubles
  • (B) Halves
  • (C) Becomes four times
  • (D) Remains unchanged
Answer: (D) Remains unchanged

Solution: For two long coaxial solenoids, \(M = \mu_0 n_1 n_2 \pi r_1^2 l\), which depends on the cross-sectional area \(\pi r_1^2\) of the inner solenoid and not on \(r_2\) (provided \(r_2 > r_1\)). Hence \(M\) remains unchanged.

Practice Question 11 (Statement-Based MCQ — NEET Pattern)

Read the following two statements carefully:

Statement I: When a rectangular loop moves completely inside a uniform magnetic field at constant velocity, no emf is induced across any of its sides.

Statement II: The net induced current in a closed rectangular loop moving completely inside a uniform magnetic field at constant velocity is zero.

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false
  • (C) Statement I is true, but Statement II is false
  • (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true
Answer: (D) Statement I is false, but Statement II is true

Solution: The two sides perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\) each develop a motional emf \(Blv\) in the same direction (e.g., top-to-bottom), so Statement I is false. Around the closed loop, these two equal emfs oppose each other, making the net loop emf and net induced current zero, so Statement II is true.

Practice Question 12 (Single-Correct MCQ)

In an AC generator, a coil of \(100\) turns and area \(0.04\,\mathrm{m^2}\) rotates at angular speed \(50\,\mathrm{rad/s}\) in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.25\,\mathrm{T}\). At the instant when the plane of the coil is parallel to the magnetic field, the instantaneous induced emf is:

  • (A) \(0\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(25\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(50\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(100\,\mathrm{V}\)
Answer: (C) \(50\,\mathrm{V}\)

Solution: When the plane of the coil is parallel to \(\mathbf{B}\), the normal makes \(\theta = 90°\) with \(\mathbf{B}\), so \(\sin\theta = 1\) and the instantaneous emf equals the peak emf: \(\varepsilon_0 = N B A \omega = 100 \times 0.25 \times 0.04 \times 50 = 50\,\mathrm{V}\).

12. Common Pitfalls and Revision Checklist

Common NEET Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Strong constant field misconception: A very strong magnet held stationary near a stationary coil induces zero emf. Only a time rate of change of flux induces emf.
  • Electric flux vs. magnetic flux: Moving a closed loop in a uniform electric field does not induce a magnetic-induction current.
  • Constant vs. varying emf during exit: A rectangular loop pulled out of a uniform magnetic field at constant speed generates a constant emf, whereas a circular loop generates a varying emf because its chord width cutting the field changes continuously.
  • Angle definition: Always use the angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf{B}\) and the normal \(\mathbf{A}\) in \(\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta\).
  • Inductance vs. current: Self-inductance \(L\) and mutual inductance \(M\) do not depend on the current \(I\) or \(\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}t\); they depend only on geometry, turn count, orientation, and core permeability.

Rapid Revision Checklist

  • \(\Phi_B = B A \cos\theta\); unit \(\mathrm{Wb} = \mathrm{T\cdot m^2}\).
  • \(\varepsilon = -N \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi_B}{\mathrm{d}t}\); \(\Delta q = \frac{N |\Delta\Phi_B|}{R}\) (independent of \(\Delta t\)).
  • Motional emf: \(\varepsilon = B l v\) (translating); \(\varepsilon = \frac{1}{2} B \omega R^2 = \pi \nu B R^2\) (rotating).
  • Long solenoid self-inductance: \(L = \mu_r \mu_0 n^2 A l\); energy \(U_B = \frac{1}{2} L I^2\); energy density \(u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\).
  • Coaxial solenoids mutual inductance: \(M = \mu_r \mu_0 n_1 n_2 \pi r_1^2 l\) (inner radius \(r_1\)).
  • AC generator: \(\varepsilon = N B A \omega \sin(\omega t)\); peak emf occurs when coil plane is parallel to \(\mathbf{B}\).

13. Concise Final Recap

Electromagnetic induction occurs whenever the magnetic flux \(\Phi_B = \mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{A}\) linked with a circuit changes over time, inducing an emf \(\varepsilon = -N\,\mathrm{d}\Phi_B/\mathrm{d}t\). By Lenz's law, the induced effect always opposes the flux change, ensuring conservation of energy. Whether produced by moving conductors (\(\varepsilon = Blv\) or \(\frac{1}{2}B\omega R^2\)), changing currents in self- or mutually coupled coils (\(\varepsilon = -L\,\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}t\) or \(-M\,\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}t\)), or rotating armatures in AC generators (\(\varepsilon = NBA\omega\sin\omega t\)), every induction problem reduces to tracking how \(\Phi_B\) changes with time.
