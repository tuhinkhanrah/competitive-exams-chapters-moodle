2. Electric Current, Current Density, and Electron Drift

2.1 Electric Current as a Scalar

When electric charges are in net motion across a cross-sectional area perpendicular to their flow, they constitute an electric current. If a net positive charge \(q_+\) and a net negative charge \(q_-\) cross the area in the forward direction during time \(t\), the net forward charge is \(q = q_+ - q_-\).

Steady Current: \[I = \frac{q}{t}\]

Instantaneous Current: \[I(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \frac{dQ}{dt}\]

The SI unit of current is the ampere (A), where \(1\text{ A} = 1\text{ C s}^{-1}\). Although circuit diagrams draw arrows to indicate the sense of current flow, electric current is a scalar quantity. Currents at a wire junction add algebraically rather than by vector addition. Formally, current is the scalar dot product of current density \(\mathbf{j}\) and the area vector \(\Delta\mathbf{S}\): \(I = \mathbf{j} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{S}\).

2.2 Microscopic Origin: Drift Velocity & Relaxation Time

In a solid metallic conductor, positive ions are fixed in a crystal lattice while free conduction electrons move randomly due to thermal energy. Because their velocities after collisions are randomly oriented, the average thermal velocity of all \(N\) electrons is zero:

\[\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}\]

When an external electric field \(\mathbf{E}\) is maintained across the conductor (for instance, by a battery), each electron of charge \(-e\) and mass \(m\) experiences an acceleration \(\mathbf{a} = -\frac{e\mathbf{E}}{m}\) between collisions. If \(\tau\) is the relaxation time (the average time interval between successive collisions with lattice ions), the average velocity acquired by the electrons—called the drift velocity \(\mathbf{v}_d\)—is:

\[\mathbf{v}_d = -\frac{e\mathbf{E}}{m}\tau\]

The negative sign shows that electrons drift opposite to \(\mathbf{E}\) (from lower potential to higher potential). Notice that even though electrons accelerate between collisions, repeated collisions prevent their average speed from growing indefinitely, resulting in a steady drift speed \(v_d = \frac{eE\tau}{m}\).

Tutorial Diagram 1: Free electrons drift opposite to the applied electric field \(\mathbf{E}\). In time \(\Delta t\), all electrons within distance \(v_d \Delta t\) cross the area \(A\).

2.3 Current, Current Density, Conductivity, and Mobility

If \(n\) is the number density of free electrons (electrons per unit volume), the number of electrons crossing area \(A\) in time \(\Delta t\) is \(n A v_d \Delta t\), carrying magnitude of charge \(\Delta Q = n e A v_d \Delta t\). Thus:

Current–Drift Relation: \[I = n e A v_d\]

Current Density (\(\mathbf{j}\)): \[j = \frac{I}{A} = n e v_d, \qquad \mathbf{j} = \frac{n e^2 \tau}{m}\mathbf{E} = \sigma \mathbf{E}\]

Conductivity (\(\sigma\)) & Resistivity (\(\rho\)): \[\sigma = \frac{n e^2 \tau}{m}, \qquad \rho = \frac{1}{\sigma} = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\]

Mobility (\(\mu\)): \[\mu = \frac{|\mathbf{v}_d|}{E} = \frac{e \tau}{m} \quad (\text{SI unit: }\mathrm{m}^2\,\mathrm{V}^{-1}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1})\]

Note that \(1\text{ m}^2\text{ V}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1} = 10^4\text{ cm}^2\text{ V}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1}\), and mobility is always defined as a positive quantity.

Worked Example 1: Drift Speed and Transit Time

Problem: A metallic wire of uniform cross-sectional area \(2.5 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2\) and length \(4.0\text{ m}\) carries a steady current of \(4.0\text{ A}\). Assume the free electron number density of the metal is \(8.0 \times 10^{28}\text{ m}^{-3}\). Calculate (a) the drift speed of the electrons and (b) the time an electron takes to drift from one end of the wire to the other. (Take \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}\)).

Solution:

(a) Using \(I = n e A v_d\):

\[v_d = \frac{I}{n e A} = \frac{4.0}{(8.0 \times 10^{28})(1.6 \times 10^{-19})(2.5 \times 10^{-6})} = \frac{4.0}{3.2 \times 10^4} = 1.25 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m s}^{-1}\]

(b) Time taken to drift across \(L = 4.0\text{ m}\):

\[t = \frac{L}{v_d} = \frac{4.0}{1.25 \times 10^{-4}} = 3.2 \times 10^4\text{ s} \approx 8.89\text{ hours}\]

Key Insight: Even though an individual electron takes hours to drift along the wire, the electric field propagates along the circuit at nearly the speed of light (\(\sim 3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}\)), so a bulb lights up almost immediately when switched on.

3. Ohm's Law, Resistance, and Temperature Dependence

3.1 Macroscopic Ohm's Law and Geometry Scaling

For conductors obeying Ohm's law at constant physical conditions (such as temperature), the potential difference \(V\) across the ends is directly proportional to the current \(I\):

\[V = I R, \qquad R = \rho \frac{l}{A}\]

Here \(R\) is the resistance (SI unit: ohm, \(\Omega\)), \(l\) is the length along the current flow, \(A\) is the cross-sectional area, and \(\rho\) is the material's resistivity (\(\Omega\cdot\text{m}\)).

NEET UG High-Yield Shortcut: Stretching or Compressing a Wire

When a uniform wire of volume \(V_{\text{vol}} = A l\) is stretched or drawn out without changing its mass or density, its volume remains constant. Therefore:

\[R = \rho \frac{l^2}{V_{\text{vol}}} = \rho \frac{V_{\text{vol}}}{A^2} \implies R \propto l^2 \propto \frac{1}{A^2} \propto \frac{1}{r^4}\]

If length increases by a factor \(n\) (\(l' = n l\)), the new resistance becomes \(R' = n^2 R\).

3.2 Limitations of Ohm's Law (Non-Ohmic Devices)

The equation \(R = V/I\) defines resistance for any device, but a material obeys Ohm's law only if \(R\) is independent of \(V\) (a linear \(V\text{-}I\) graph passing through the origin). Deviations occur in three principal ways:

  • Non-linear \(V\text{-}I\) relation: At high currents, Joule heating raises temperature and changes \(\rho\), causing \(V\) to curve upward relative to \(I\).
  • Sign dependence (Asymmetry): In a semiconductor p-n junction diode, reversing the polarity of \(V\) produces a drastically smaller current (milliamperes in forward bias vs. microamperes in reverse bias).
  • Non-unique \(V\text{-}I\) relation: In materials such as gallium arsenide (GaAs), more than one value of \(V\) corresponds to the same current \(I\), producing a negative differential resistance region where \(I\) decreases as \(V\) increases.
Tutorial Diagram 2: Three classic deviations from Ohm's law: (1) high-current heating in a metal, (2) asymmetric diode curve, and (3) negative differential resistance in GaAs.

3.3 Temperature Dependence of Resistivity

Over a limited temperature range around a reference temperature \(T_0\), the resistivity (and resistance, neglecting thermal expansion) of a metallic conductor varies approximately linearly:

\[\rho_T = \rho_0 \left[1 + \alpha (T - T_0)\right], \qquad R_T = R_0 \left[1 + \alpha (T - T_0)\right]\]

Here \(\alpha\) is the temperature coefficient of resistivity (unit: \(\text{K}^{-1}\) or \(^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\)). Using \(\rho = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\), we can explain the contrasting behavior of different classes of materials:

  • Metals (\(\alpha > 0\)): Free electron density \(n\) is nearly constant with temperature. Heating increases thermal agitation and collision frequency, so relaxation time \(\tau\) decreases and \(\rho\) increases.
  • Alloys (Nichrome, Manganin, Constantan): Exhibit very small \(\alpha\) and high resistivity, making them ideal for standard wire-bound resistors whose resistance must stay stable with temperature.
  • Semiconductors & Insulators (\(\alpha < 0\)): As temperature rises, \(n\) increases rapidly (exponentially), far outweighing the modest decrease in \(\tau\). Hence \(\rho\) decreases sharply with temperature.

Worked Example 2: Steady Temperature of a Heating Coil

Problem: A nichrome heating coil has a resistance of \(50.0\text{ }\Omega\) at room temperature (\(20\,^\circ\text{C}\)). When connected to a \(220\text{ V}\) supply, the current settles to a steady value of \(4.0\text{ A}\). Taking the average temperature coefficient of resistance of nichrome as \(\alpha = 1.7 \times 10^{-4}\text{ }^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\), find the steady temperature of the coil.

Solution:

At the steady operating temperature \(T_2\), the resistance is:

\[R_2 = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{220\text{ V}}{4.0\text{ A}} = 55.0\text{ }\Omega\]

Using \(R_2 = R_1[1 + \alpha(T_2 - T_1)]\):

\[T_2 - T_1 = \frac{R_2 - R_1}{R_1 \alpha} = \frac{55.0 - 50.0}{50.0 \times 1.7 \times 10^{-4}} = \frac{5.0}{8.5 \times 10^{-3}} \approx 588.2\,^\circ\text{C}\]

\[T_2 = 20 + 588.2 = 608.2\,^\circ\text{C}\]

4. Electrical Energy, Power, and Transmission Losses

When a steady current \(I\) flows through a resistor \(R\) across a potential difference \(V = V_A - V_B > 0\), charge \(\Delta Q = I \Delta t\) loses potential energy \(\Delta U = -I V \Delta t\). Because charge carriers move at constant drift speed on average, this energy is transferred via collisions to lattice ions as thermal energy (Joule heating):

Heat Dissipated: \[\Delta W = I V \Delta t\]

Electrical Power: \[P = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}\]

Application to Long-Distance Power Transmission: To deliver power \(P = V I\) to a distant load through transmission cables of total resistance \(R_c\), the current in the cables is \(I = P/V\). The power wasted as heat in the cables is:

\[P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R_c = \frac{P^2 R_c}{V^2}\]

Because \(P_{\text{loss}} \propto 1/V^2\), power stations step up the transmission voltage \(V\) to hundreds of kilovolts using transformers, drastically reducing wasted heat before stepping it down safely near consumers.

Worked Example 3: High-Voltage Power Transmission

Problem: A factory requires \(400\text{ kW}\) of electrical power supplied through transmission cables of total resistance \(0.5\text{ }\Omega\). Compare the power lost in the cables if power is transmitted at (a) \(400\text{ V}\) and (b) \(20\text{ kV}\).

Solution:

(a) At \(V_1 = 400\text{ V}\): \(I_1 = \frac{P}{V_1} = \frac{4.0 \times 10^5}{400} = 1000\text{ A}\).

\[P_{\text{loss},1} = I_1^2 R_c = (1000)^2 \times 0.5 = 5.0 \times 10^5\text{ W} = 500\text{ kW}\]

(b) At \(V_2 = 20\text{ kV} = 2.0 \times 10^4\text{ V}\): \(I_2 = \frac{4.0 \times 10^5}{2.0 \times 10^4} = 20\text{ A}\).

\[P_{\text{loss},2} = I_2^2 R_c = (20)^2 \times 0.5 = 200\text{ W} = 0.2\text{ kW}\]

Raising the voltage by a factor of \(50\) cuts the transmission loss by a factor of \(50^2 = 2500\).

5. Cells, EMF, Internal Resistance, and Cell Combinations

5.1 EMF vs. Terminal Potential Difference

An electrolytic cell maintains a steady potential difference by converting chemical energy into electrical energy. Its electromotive force (emf, \(\varepsilon\)) is the potential difference between its positive and negative electrodes in an open circuit (\(I = 0\)). Note that emf is a potential difference (measured in volts), not a mechanical force.

When a cell of internal resistance \(r\) drives current \(I\) through an external resistor \(R\):

Discharging (current leaves positive terminal): \[V = \varepsilon - I r, \qquad I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r}\]

Charging (external source pushes current into positive terminal): \[V = \varepsilon + I r\]

Maximum theoretical current (short circuit, \(R = 0\)): \[I_{\max} = \frac{\varepsilon}{r}\]

Tutorial Diagram 4: Real cell connected to load \(R\), and the linear \(V\text{-}I\) graph (\(V = \varepsilon - Ir\)) whose vertical intercept gives \(\varepsilon\) and slope gives \(-r\).

5.2 Series and Parallel Combinations of Cells

Cells in Series (same polarity): \[\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \dots + \varepsilon_n, \qquad r_{\text{eq}} = r_1 + r_2 + \dots + r_n\]

(If a cell opposes the others, enter its emf with a minus sign; internal resistances always add.)

Cells in Parallel (positive terminals joined together): \[\frac{1}{r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{r_n}, \qquad \frac{\varepsilon_{\text{eq}}}{r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{\varepsilon_1}{r_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{r_2} + \dots + \frac{\varepsilon_n}{r_n}\]

For two cells in parallel: \[\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = \frac{\varepsilon_1 r_2 + \varepsilon_2 r_1}{r_1 + r_2}, \qquad r_{\text{eq}} = \frac{r_1 r_2}{r_1 + r_2}\]

Worked Example 4: Charging a Battery

Problem: A \(12\text{ V}\) storage battery of internal resistance \(0.6\text{ }\Omega\) is charged by a \(60\text{ V}\) DC supply using a series resistor of \(11.4\text{ }\Omega\). Calculate (a) the charging current and (b) the terminal voltage of the battery during charging.

Solution:

(a) During charging, the \(12\text{ V}\) battery opposes the \(60\text{ V}\) supply. Net driving emf \(= 60 - 12 = 48\text{ V}\). Total resistance \(= 11.4 + 0.6 = 12.0\text{ }\Omega\).

\[I = \frac{48\text{ V}}{12.0\text{ }\Omega} = 4.0\text{ A}\]

(b) Since current enters the positive terminal of the battery:

\[V = \varepsilon + I r = 12 + (4.0)(0.6) = 14.4\text{ V}\]

6. Kirchhoff's Rules and the Wheatstone Bridge

6.1 Kirchhoff's Two Rules

  • Junction Rule (KCL — Conservation of Charge): At any circuit junction, the sum of currents entering equals the sum of currents leaving: \(\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}\).
  • Loop Rule (KVL — Conservation of Energy): Around any closed loop, the algebraic sum of potential changes is zero: \(\sum \Delta V = 0\).

Sign Conventions When Traversing a Loop:

  • Across a resistor \(R\) along the assumed current \(I\): \(\Delta V = -IR\); opposite to \(I\): \(\Delta V = +IR\).
  • Across an ideal cell from negative to positive terminal: \(\Delta V = +\varepsilon\); from positive to negative terminal: \(\Delta V = -\varepsilon\).

6.2 The Wheatstone Bridge

A Wheatstone bridge consists of four resistors \(R_1, R_2, R_3, R_4\) arranged in a diamond, with a battery across one diagonal (AC) and a galvanometer \(G\) across the other diagonal (BD).

Tutorial Diagram 5: Wheatstone bridge network. When balanced, \(V_B = V_D\) and zero current flows through the galvanometer \(G\).

Balance Condition (\(I_g = 0\)): \[\frac{R_2}{R_1} = \frac{R_4}{R_3} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_3}{R_4}\]

Symmetry in Resistor Networks: For a cubical network of 12 identical resistors (each of resistance \(R\)) connected across diagonally opposite corners, symmetry ensures the total current \(3I\) splits equally into \(I\) along each of the three edges at the entry corner, and into \(I/2\) at the next junctions. Applying KVL along a path from entry to exit gives \(R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{5}{6}R\).

Worked Example 5: Multi-Loop Network Using Kirchhoff's Rules

Problem: Three parallel vertical branches connect a top node \(A\) to a bottom node \(B\): the left branch has a \(12\text{ V}\) ideal cell (positive terminal toward \(A\)) in series with \(2\text{ }\Omega\); the middle branch has a \(4\text{ }\Omega\) resistor; the right branch has a \(6\text{ V}\) ideal cell (positive terminal toward \(A\)) in series with \(4\text{ }\Omega\). Find the current through the middle \(4\text{ }\Omega\) resistor.

Solution:

Let \(V_B = 0\text{ V}\) and \(V_A = V\). Let \(I_1\) flow upward from the \(12\text{ V}\) branch into \(A\), \(I_2\) flow upward from the \(6\text{ V}\) branch into \(A\), and \(I_3\) flow downward through the middle \(4\text{ }\Omega\) resistor.

By the junction rule at \(A\): \(I_1 + I_2 = I_3\).

Applying the loop rule to the left loop (from \(B\) up the left branch to \(A\), then down the middle branch to \(B\)):

\[12 - 2 I_1 - 4 I_3 = 0 \implies I_1 = 6 - 2 I_3\]

Applying the loop rule to the right loop (from \(B\) up the right branch to \(A\), then down the middle branch to \(B\)):

\[6 - 4 I_2 - 4 I_3 = 0 \implies I_2 = 1.5 - I_3\]

Substituting \(I_1\) and \(I_2\) into \(I_1 + I_2 = I_3\):

\[(6 - 2 I_3) + (1.5 - I_3) = I_3 \implies 7.5 = 4 I_3 \implies I_3 = 1.875\text{ A}\]

Practice Question 1

The electric current in a conductor varies with time \(t\) (in seconds) according to \(I(t) = (3t^2 + 2t)\text{ A}\). The total charge passing through a cross-section of the conductor between \(t = 1\text{ s}\) and \(t = 3\text{ s}\) is:

  • (A) \(28\text{ C}\)
  • (B) \(34\text{ C}\)
  • (C) \(36\text{ C}\)
  • (D) \(40\text{ C}\)

Answer: (B) Worked Solution:

Since \(I = \frac{dQ}{dt}\), total charge \(q = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} I(t)\,dt\):

\[q = \int_{1}^{3} (3t^2 + 2t)\,dt = \left[t^3 + t^2\right]_{1}^{3} = (27 + 9) - (1 + 1) = 36 - 2 = 34\text{ C}\]

Practice Question 2

A uniform metallic wire has a resistance of \(12\text{ }\Omega\). It is stretched uniformly until its length becomes three times its original length, keeping the volume constant. Its new resistance is:

  • (A) \(36\text{ }\Omega\)
  • (B) \(72\text{ }\Omega\)
  • (C) \(108\text{ }\Omega\)
  • (D) \(4\text{ }\Omega\)

Answer: (C) Worked Solution:

At constant volume \(V_{\text{vol}} = A l\), \(R = \rho \frac{l^2}{V_{\text{vol}}} \propto l^2\). When length increases by a factor \(n = 3\):

\[R' = n^2 R = 3^2 \times 12\text{ }\Omega = 9 \times 12\text{ }\Omega = 108\text{ }\Omega\]

Practice Question 3

Two cylindrical wires of the same material have lengths in the ratio \(2:3\) and radii in the ratio \(1:2\). If they are connected in series to a battery, the ratio of the drift speeds of free electrons in them (\(v_{d1} : v_{d2}\)) is:

  • (A) \(4:1\)
  • (B) \(1:4\)
  • (C) \(8:3\)
  • (D) \(3:2\)

Answer: (A) Worked Solution:

In series, both wires carry the same current \(I\). Since they are of the same material, \(n\) is identical. Using \(v_d = \frac{I}{n e A} = \frac{I}{n e \pi r^2}\), we have \(v_d \propto \frac{1}{r^2}\) (independent of length for a given \(I\)):

\[\frac{v_{d1}}{v_{d2}} = \left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{2}{1}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{1}\]

Practice Question 4

A metallic resistance thermometer has a resistance of \(10.0\text{ }\Omega\) at \(0\,^\circ\text{C}\) and \(10.4\text{ }\Omega\) at \(100\,^\circ\text{C}\). Assuming a linear variation of resistance with temperature, the temperature at which its resistance becomes \(11.0\text{ }\Omega\) is:

  • (A) \(200\,^\circ\text{C}\)
  • (B) \(250\,^\circ\text{C}\)
  • (C) \(300\,^\circ\text{C}\)
  • (D) \(400\,^\circ\text{C}\)

Answer: (B) Worked Solution:

Using \(R_t = R_0(1 + \alpha t)\), the unknown temperature \(t\) is:

\[t = \frac{R_t - R_0}{R_{100} - R_0} \times 100\,^\circ\text{C} = \frac{11.0 - 10.0}{10.4 - 10.0} \times 100 = \frac{1.0}{0.4} \times 100 = 250\,^\circ\text{C}\]

Practice Question 5

The dimensional formula for the electrical mobility \(\mu\) of charge carriers is:

  • (A) \([\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{L}^{0}\mathrm{T}^{2}\mathrm{A}]\)
  • (B) \([\mathrm{M}^{1}\mathrm{L}^{2}\mathrm{T}^{-3}\mathrm{A}^{-1}]\)
  • (C) \([\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{L}^{-3}\mathrm{T}^{3}\mathrm{A}^{2}]\)
  • (D) \([\mathrm{M}^{0}\mathrm{L}^{1}\mathrm{T}^{-2}\mathrm{A}^{-1}]\)

Answer: (A) Worked Solution:

From \(\mu = \frac{e\tau}{m}\), the dimensions are:

\[[\mu] = \frac{[e][\tau]}{[m]} = \frac{[\mathrm{A T}][\mathrm{T}]}{[\mathrm{M}]} = [\mathrm{M}^{-1}\mathrm{L}^{0}\mathrm{T}^{2}\mathrm{A}]\]

Practice Question 6

In the current–voltage (\(I\text{-}V\)) characteristic of a gallium arsenide (GaAs) device, the "negative resistance region" is characterized by:

  • (A) Current increasing linearly with applied voltage
  • (B) Current remaining zero when the polarity of voltage is reversed
  • (C) Current decreasing as the applied voltage increases
  • (D) Voltage remaining constant for all values of current

Answer: (C) Worked Solution:

In the negative differential resistance region of GaAs, \(\frac{dI}{dV} < 0\), meaning the current decreases as the applied voltage increases.

Practice Question 7

Electrical power \(P\) is transmitted from a generating station to a city through cables of fixed resistance \(R_c\) at a voltage \(V\). If the transmission voltage is doubled to \(2V\) while delivering the same power \(P\), the power wasted as heat in the cables becomes:

  • (A) Half
  • (B) One-fourth
  • (C) Double
  • (D) Four times

Answer: (B) Worked Solution:

Power loss in the transmission line is \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R_c = \frac{P^2 R_c}{V^2}\). Since \(P_{\text{loss}} \propto \frac{1}{V^2}\), doubling \(V\) reduces the power loss to \(\frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4}\) of its original value.

Practice Question 8

A cell of emf \(2.0\text{ V}\) and internal resistance \(0.5\text{ }\Omega\) is connected across an external resistor \(R\). If the terminal potential difference across the cell is \(1.6\text{ V}\), the value of \(R\) is:

  • (A) \(1.0\text{ }\Omega\)
  • (B) \(1.5\text{ }\Omega\)
  • (C) \(2.0\text{ }\Omega\)
  • (D) \(2.5\text{ }\Omega\)

Answer: (C) Worked Solution:

During discharge, \(V = \varepsilon - Ir \implies 1.6 = 2.0 - I(0.5) \implies I = \frac{0.4}{0.5} = 0.8\text{ A}\).

Using Ohm's law for the external resistor: \(R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{1.6}{0.8} = 2.0\text{ }\Omega\).

Practice Question 9

Two cells of emfs \(6\text{ V}\) and \(3\text{ V}\) with internal resistances \(2\text{ }\Omega\) and \(1\text{ }\Omega\), respectively, are connected in parallel with their positive terminals joined together and negative terminals joined together. The equivalent emf of the combination is:

  • (A) \(3.5\text{ V}\)
  • (B) \(4.0\text{ V}\)
  • (C) \(4.5\text{ V}\)
  • (D) \(9.0\text{ V}\)

Answer: (B) Worked Solution:

For two cells in parallel with like polarities joined:

\[\varepsilon_{\text{eq}} = \frac{\varepsilon_1 r_2 + \varepsilon_2 r_1}{r_1 + r_2} = \frac{(6)(1) + (3)(2)}{2 + 1} = \frac{12}{3} = 4.0\text{ V}\]

Practice Question 10

In a Wheatstone bridge \(ABCD\), the four arms have resistances \(R_{AB} = 10\text{ }\Omega\), \(R_{BC} = 20\text{ }\Omega\), \(R_{CD} = 30\text{ }\Omega\), and \(R_{DA} = 15\text{ }\Omega\). A \(10\text{ }\Omega\) galvanometer is connected across \(BD\) and a \(12\text{ V}\) ideal battery across \(AC\). The current through the galvanometer is:

  • (A) \(0\text{ A}\)
  • (B) \(0.2\text{ A}\)
  • (C) \(0.4\text{ A}\)
  • (D) \(0.6\text{ A}\)

Answer: (A) Worked Solution:

Check the bridge balance condition: \(\frac{R_{AB}}{R_{DA}} = \frac{10}{15} = \frac{2}{3}\) and \(\frac{R_{BC}}{R_{CD}} = \frac{20}{30} = \frac{2}{3}\). Since the ratios are equal, \(V_B = V_D\), so no current flows through the galvanometer (\(I_g = 0\text{ A}\)).

Practice Question 11 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): Although electric current is represented with a directional arrow in circuit diagrams, it is a scalar quantity.

Reason (R): Electric currents at a junction add algebraically rather than obeying the law of vector addition, and \(I = \mathbf{j} \cdot \Delta\mathbf{S}\) is a scalar product.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
  • (D) Both (A) and (R) are false.

Answer: (A) Worked Solution:

Current does not obey vector addition (bending a wire at a junction does not alter the sum of currents), and it is defined as the dot product of two vectors \(\mathbf{j}\) and \(\Delta\mathbf{S}\). Thus both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) correctly explains (A).

Practice Question 12 (Statement-Based)

Statement I: When a circuit switch is closed, current is established almost instantaneously throughout the wire because electrons travel from the battery to the load at the speed of light.

Statement II: With an increase in temperature, the resistivity of metals increases due to a decrease in relaxation time \(\tau\), whereas the resistivity of semiconductors decreases primarily due to a rapid increase in charge carrier density \(n\).

  • (A) Both Statement I and Statement II are true.
  • (B) Both Statement I and Statement II are false.
  • (C) Statement I is true but Statement II is false.
  • (D) Statement I is false but Statement II is true.

Answer: (D) Worked Solution:

Statement I is false because electrons drift very slowly (\(\sim 10^{-4}\text{ m s}^{-1}\)); it is the electric field that propagates along the circuit at nearly the speed of light, initiating local drift everywhere. Statement II is true from \(\rho = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\).

9. Common Pitfalls & NEET UG Revision Checklist

Common Misconceptions to Avoid
  • \(V = IR\) vs. Ohm's Law: \(R = V/I\) defines resistance for all conductors, including diodes. Ohm's law specifically states that \(R\) is constant (independent of \(V\) and \(I\)).
  • Charging vs. Discharging Terminal Voltage: Use \(V = \varepsilon - Ir\) when the cell supplies current, but switch to \(V = \varepsilon + Ir\) when an external source charges the cell.
  • Drift Speed vs. Signal Speed: Drift speed \(v_d\) is typically \(\sim 10^{-4}\text{ to }10^{-3}\text{ m s}^{-1}\), far smaller than thermal speeds (\(\sim 10^5\text{ m s}^{-1}\) for electrons) and electromagnetic wave speed (\(3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}\)).
  • Parallel Cell Polarity: If one cell in a parallel pair is connected with reversed polarity, replace its \(\varepsilon\) with \(-\varepsilon\) in \(\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{eq}}}{r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{\varepsilon_1}{r_1} + \frac{\varepsilon_2}{r_2}\), while \(r_{\text{eq}}\) remains \(\frac{r_1 r_2}{r_1 + r_2}\).
Rapid Pre-Exam Revision Checklist
  • \(I = n e A v_d\), \(v_d = \frac{eE\tau}{m}\), \(\mu = \frac{v_d}{E} = \frac{e\tau}{m}\), \(\rho = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\).
  • Wire stretching at constant volume: \(R' = n^2 R\) when length increases \(n\)-fold.
  • Temperature variation: \(R_T = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]\); \(\alpha > 0\) (metals), \(\alpha \approx 0\) (alloys), \(\alpha < 0\) (semiconductors).
  • Power & transmission: \(P = VI = I^2 R = V^2/R\); line loss \(P_c = \frac{P^2 R_c}{V^2}\).
  • Kirchhoff's rules: KCL (charge conservation) and KVL (energy conservation); Wheatstone balance \(\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_3}{R_4}\).

Final Recap: Current electricity links microscopic electron collisions (\(\tau, v_d, \mu\)) to macroscopic circuit behavior (\(R, \rho, P\)). Mastering sign conventions in cells and Kirchhoff's loops, alongside dimensional analysis and temperature trends, prepares you for the full spectrum of NEET UG questions on this topic.
