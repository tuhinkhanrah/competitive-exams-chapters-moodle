Alternating Current: Complete Concept & Problem-Solving Tutorial

Master sinusoidal AC waveforms, RMS quantities, phasors, pure R, L, and C circuits, series LCR impedance, resonance, AC power factor, and transformers.

1. Learning Objectives & Exam Overview

Alternating current (AC) circuits are among the most scoring topics in NEET UG Physics because problems test clean conceptual rules (phase lead/lag, resonance, wattless current) alongside direct algebraic and trigonometric relationships. After completing this tutorial, you will be able to:

  • Distinguish between instantaneous, peak, average, and root-mean-square (RMS) values of sinusoidal voltage and current.
  • Analyze pure resistive, pure inductive, and pure capacitive AC circuits using both calculus and phasor diagrams.
  • Determine the impedance, current amplitude, element voltages, and phase angle of a series LCR circuit.
  • Calculate the resonant frequency and quality factor of a series LCR circuit, and explain tuning applications.
  • Compute average power dissipated in any AC circuit using the power factor \(\cos\phi\) and identify wattless current.
  • Apply ideal transformer equations and identify energy losses in practical transformers.

2. Sinusoidal AC, Average Values, and RMS Quantities

Unlike direct current (DC), which flows steadily in one direction, an alternating voltage or current reverses polarity periodically and varies sinusoidally with time. A standard sinusoidal AC voltage source is described by:

\[v(t) = v_m \sin(\omega t)\]

Here, \(v_m\) is the peak voltage (amplitude), \(\omega = 2\pi \nu = \frac{2\pi}{T}\) is the angular frequency in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\), \(\nu\) is the linear frequency in \(\mathrm{Hz}\), and \(T\) is the time period.

Average vs. Root-Mean-Square (RMS) Value

Over one complete cycle (\(0\) to \(T\)), the positive and negative half-cycles of \(\sin(\omega t)\) cancel out, so the average value of sinusoidal current or voltage over a full cycle is zero:

\[\langle i \rangle_{\text{full cycle}} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \langle i \rangle_{\text{half cycle}} = \frac{2 i_m}{\pi} \approx 0.637\, i_m\]

Even though the full-cycle average current is zero, Joule heating depends on \(i^2 R\), which is always positive. To equate AC heating to an equivalent DC current, we define the root-mean-square (RMS) or effective current \(I\) (also written \(I_{\text{rms}}\)) as the steady DC current that produces the same average heat in a resistor \(R\) over one complete cycle:

\[I = \sqrt{\langle i^2 \rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T} \int_0^T i_m^2 \sin^2(\omega t)\,\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{i_m}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\, i_m\] \[V = \frac{v_m}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\, v_m\]
Exam Convention
Unless a question explicitly specifies "peak" or "maximum" value, any AC voltage or current rating (such as a \(220\,\mathrm{V}\) household supply or an AC ammeter reading) is always the RMS value.

3. Phasors & Single-Element AC Circuits (R, L, C)

A phasor is a rotating vector of constant magnitude (equal to the peak value \(v_m\) or \(i_m\)) that rotates counterclockwise about the origin at angular speed \(\omega\). Its vertical projection at any instant \(t\) equals the instantaneous scalar value of the alternating quantity. Phasors allow us to add out-of-phase sinusoidal voltages using simple vector geometry.

Purely Resistive Circuit (R)

When \(v = v_m \sin(\omega t)\) is applied across an ideal resistor \(R\), Kirchhoff's loop rule gives \(i = \frac{v_m}{R}\sin(\omega t) = i_m \sin(\omega t)\). Voltage and current reach their maxima and zeroes simultaneously: they are in phase (\(\phi = 0\)).

  • Opposition to current: \(R\) (independent of frequency \(\nu\)).
  • Average power dissipated: \(P = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}\).

Purely Inductive Circuit (L)

When \(v = v_m \sin(\omega t)\) is applied across a pure inductor \(L\) (zero winding resistance), the self-induced back emf opposes changes in current: \(v - L\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0\). Integrating with respect to time yields:

\[i = \frac{v_m}{\omega L} \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = i_m \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\]
  • Phase relation: Current lags voltage by \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) (\(90°\)), or one-quarter cycle.
  • Inductive reactance: \(X_L = \omega L = 2\pi \nu L\) (unit: \(\mathrm{Ω}\)). It increases linearly with frequency \(\nu\); for DC (\(\nu = 0\)), \(X_L = 0\).
  • Average power: \(P_L = 0\) over a complete cycle (energy is alternately stored in the magnetic field and returned to the source).

Purely Capacitive Circuit (C)

When \(v = v_m \sin(\omega t)\) is applied across an ideal capacitor \(C\), the instantaneous charge is \(q = C v_m \sin(\omega t)\). Differentiating \(q\) gives the current \(i = \frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}t}\):

\[i = \omega C v_m \cos(\omega t) = \frac{v_m}{1/(\omega C)} \sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = i_m \sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\]
  • Phase relation: Current leads voltage by \(\frac{\pi}{2}\) (\(90°\)), or one-quarter cycle.
  • Capacitive reactance: \(X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{2\pi \nu C}\) (unit: \(\mathrm{Ω}\)). It is inversely proportional to frequency; for DC (\(\nu = 0\)), \(X_C \to \infty\) (blocks steady DC).
  • Average power: \(P_C = 0\) over a complete cycle (energy is alternately stored in the electric field and returned to the source).
Circuit Element Opposition Formula Frequency Effect Phase of Current w.r.t. Voltage Average Power
Resistor (\(R\)) \(R\) Independent of \(\nu\) In phase (\(\phi = 0°\)) \(V I = I^2 R\)
Inductor (\(L\)) \(X_L = 2\pi \nu L\) \(X_L \propto \nu\) Lags by \(90°\) (\(\pi/2\)) \(0\)
Capacitor (\(C\)) \(X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi \nu C}\) \(X_C \propto \frac{1}{\nu}\) Leads by \(90°\) (\(\pi/2\)) \(0\)

4. Series LCR Circuit, Impedance, and Resonance

Consider a resistor \(R\), inductor \(L\), and capacitor \(C\) connected in series to an AC source \(v = v_m \sin(\omega t)\). Because they are in series, the same current \(i = i_m \sin(\omega t + \phi)\) flows through all three elements at every instant.

Phasor Addition & Impedance

Taking the current phasor \(\mathbf{I}\) as our reference direction:

  • \(\mathbf{V_R}\) (amplitude \(v_{Rm} = i_m R\)) is along \(\mathbf{I}\).
  • \(\mathbf{V_L}\) (amplitude \(v_{Lm} = i_m X_L\)) leads \(\mathbf{I}\) by \(90°\).
  • \(\mathbf{V_C}\) (amplitude \(v_{Cm} = i_m X_C\)) lags \(\mathbf{I}\) by \(90°\).

Notice that \(\mathbf{V_L}\) and \(\mathbf{V_C}\) are directly opposite (\(180°\) apart), so their combined magnitude is \(|v_{Cm} - v_{Lm}|\). Applying the Pythagorean theorem to the right triangle formed by \(\mathbf{V_R}\) and \((\mathbf{V_C} + \mathbf{V_L})\) gives the source voltage amplitude \(v_m\) (and similarly for RMS voltages):

\[v_m = \sqrt{v_{Rm}^2 + (v_{Cm} - v_{Lm})^2} \quad \text{and} \quad V_{\text{source}} = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_C - V_L)^2}\]

Defining the impedance \(Z\) of the series LCR circuit as the total effective opposition to AC:

\[Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2}, \qquad i_m = \frac{v_m}{Z}, \qquad I = \frac{V}{Z}\] \[\tan\phi = \frac{X_C - X_L}{R} = \frac{V_C - V_L}{V_R}\]

Here, \(\phi\) is the phase angle by which the current leads the source voltage:

  • If \(X_C > X_L\), \(\phi > 0\): the circuit is predominantly capacitive (current leads voltage).
  • If \(X_L > X_C\), \(\phi < 0\): the circuit is predominantly inductive (current lags voltage).
  • If \(X_L = X_C\), \(\phi = 0\): the circuit is purely resistive (resonance).

Resonance & Quality Factor (Q-Factor)

If the frequency \(\omega\) of the AC source is varied, the inductive reactance \(X_L = \omega L\) increases while the capacitive reactance \(X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C}\) decreases. At a specific angular frequency \(\omega_0\), called the resonant frequency, the two reactances become equal and cancel each other:

\[X_L = X_C \implies \omega_0 L = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C} \implies \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad \nu_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\]

At resonance in a series LCR circuit:

  • Impedance is minimum: \(Z_{\text{min}} = R\).
  • Current amplitude is maximum: \(i_{m,\text{max}} = \frac{v_m}{R}\).
  • Phase difference is zero (\(\phi = 0\)) and power factor is unity (\(\cos\phi = 1\)).
  • Voltages across \(L\) and \(C\) are equal in magnitude (\(V_L = V_C\)) and \(180°\) out of phase, so the net potential drop across the LC combination is zero, and the entire source voltage appears across \(R\).

The sharpness of the resonance peak is measured by the dimensionless Quality Factor (\(Q\)). A higher \(Q\) means a narrower, sharper peak and better frequency selectivity (used in tuning radio and television receivers, as well as airport metal detectors):

\[Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C R} = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}\]

5. Power in AC Circuits & Wattless Current

For a general AC circuit with \(v = v_m \sin(\omega t)\) and \(i = i_m \sin(\omega t + \phi)\), the instantaneous power is \(p = v i\). Averaging over one full cycle eliminates the oscillating \(2\omega t\) term and yields the average power:

\[P = V I \cos\phi = I^2 Z \cos\phi = I^2 R\]

Here, \(\cos\phi = \frac{R}{Z}\) is called the power factor. Notice that energy is dissipated only in the resistive part of the circuit.

  • Wattless Current: When a circuit is purely inductive or purely capacitive (\(\phi = \pm 90°\)), \(\cos\phi = 0\) and \(P = 0\) even though an alternating current flows. In general, the component of current perpendicular to the voltage, \(I_q = I \sin\phi\), consumes zero average power and is called the wattless component, while \(I_p = I \cos\phi\) is the active (power) component.
  • Power Factor Correction: In industrial power delivery, inductive loads (motors) cause current to lag voltage, lowering \(\cos\phi\). For a fixed power \(P\) and voltage \(V\), a low power factor requires a larger line current \(I = \frac{P}{V\cos\phi}\), which increases transmission losses (\(I^2 R_{\text{line}}\)). Connecting a capacitor of suitable value in parallel supplies a leading wattless current that cancels the lagging wattless component, raising \(\cos\phi\) toward \(1\).

6. Transformers & Long-Distance Power Transmission

A transformer is a static device that steps AC voltage up or down using the principle of mutual induction. It consists of two insulated coils—a primary coil (\(N_p\) turns) and a secondary coil (\(N_s\) turns)—wound on a common laminated soft-iron core.

When an alternating voltage \(V_p\) is applied to the primary, it produces an alternating magnetic flux \(\Phi\) in the core that links both coils. For an ideal transformer (zero winding resistance, zero flux leakage, \(100\%\) efficiency):

\[\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s} \qquad \text{and} \qquad V_p I_p = V_s I_s \text{ (energy conservation)}\]
  • Step-up transformer (\(N_s > N_p\)): Increases voltage (\(V_s > V_p\)) and decreases current (\(I_s < I_p\)) in the same ratio. Used at power stations to transmit electricity at very high voltage and low current, drastically cutting \(I^2 R\) cable losses.
  • Step-down transformer (\(N_s < N_p\)): Decreases voltage (\(V_s < V_p\)) and increases current (\(I_s > I_p\)). Used at substations to bring voltage down to safe domestic levels.

Energy Losses in Practical Transformers

Type of Energy Loss Physical Cause Method to Minimize
Flux Leakage Not all primary flux links the secondary due to air gaps or core geometry. Wind primary and secondary coils concentrically one over the other.
Copper (Joule) Loss Resistance of copper windings produces \(I^2 R\) heat. Use thick copper wire for high-current, low-voltage windings.
Eddy Current Loss Alternating flux induces circulating currents inside the iron core. Use a laminated core made of thin insulated iron sheets.
Hysteresis Loss Repeated magnetization and demagnetization of the core every AC cycle. Use a soft-iron or silicon-steel core with a narrow hysteresis loop.

8. Original Worked Examples (NEET UG Level)

Worked Example 1: Sinusoidal AC Equation & Heater Rating

(Assumed scenario) An electric immersion heater rated at \(1200\,\mathrm{W}\) is designed to operate on a \(200\,\mathrm{V}\), \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\) AC mains supply. Find: (a) the resistance of the heating coil, (b) the peak voltage of the supply, and (c) the equation for the instantaneous current if \(v(t) = v_m \sin(\omega t)\).

Solution:

(a) Using the RMS rating \(V = 200\,\mathrm{V}\) and average power \(P = 1200\,\mathrm{W}\):

\[R = \frac{V^2}{P} = \frac{(200\,\mathrm{V})^2}{1200\,\mathrm{W}} = \frac{40000}{1200} = 33.3\,\mathrm{Ω}\]

(b) Peak voltage:

\[v_m = \sqrt{2}\,V = 1.414 \times 200\,\mathrm{V} \approx 282.8\,\mathrm{V}\]

(c) RMS current \(I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{1200}{200} = 6\,\mathrm{A}\), so peak current \(i_m = 6\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{A} \approx 8.49\,\mathrm{A}\). Angular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi \nu = 2\pi(50) = 100\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). Since a heater is purely resistive, current is in phase with voltage:

\[i(t) = 6\sqrt{2}\sin(100\pi t)\,\mathrm{A}\]
Worked Example 2: Series LCR Circuit Analysis

(Assumed scenario) A series LCR circuit has \(R = 6\,\mathrm{Ω}\), \(L\) with inductive reactance \(X_L = 14\,\mathrm{Ω}\), and \(C\) with capacitive reactance \(X_C = 6\,\mathrm{Ω}\) at a source frequency of \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\). The RMS source voltage is \(100\,\mathrm{V}\). Calculate: (a) the impedance \(Z\), (b) the RMS current \(I\), (c) the power factor and phase angle, and (d) the average power dissipated.

Solution:

(a) Impedance:

\[Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2} = \sqrt{6^2 + (6 - 14)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10\,\mathrm{Ω}\]

(b) RMS current:

\[I = \frac{V}{Z} = \frac{100\,\mathrm{V}}{10\,\mathrm{Ω}} = 10\,\mathrm{A}\]

(c) Power factor:

\[\cos\phi = \frac{R}{Z} = \frac{6}{10} = 0.6\]

Since \(X_L > X_C\), \(\tan\phi = \frac{X_C - X_L}{R} = \frac{-8}{6} = -\frac{4}{3}\), so \(\phi = -53.1°\). The negative sign indicates that the current lags the source voltage by \(53.1°\).

(d) Average power dissipated:

\[P = I^2 R = (10\,\mathrm{A})^2 \times 6\,\mathrm{Ω} = 600\,\mathrm{W}\]

(Check: \(P = V I \cos\phi = 100 \times 10 \times 0.6 = 600\,\mathrm{W}\)).

Worked Example 3: Resonance & Quality Factor

(Assumed scenario) A series LCR circuit consists of \(L = 0.20\,\mathrm{H}\), \(C = 20\,\mathrm{µF}\), and \(R = 25\,\mathrm{Ω}\). Find its resonant angular frequency \(\omega_0\) and quality factor \(Q\).

Solution:

Resonant angular frequency:

\[\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.20 \times 20 \times 10^{-6}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4 \times 10^{-6}}} = \frac{1}{2 \times 10^{-3}} = 500\,\mathrm{rad/s}\]

Quality factor:

\[Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{500 \times 0.20}{25} = \frac{100}{25} = 4\]
Worked Example 4: Practical Transformer Efficiency

(Assumed scenario) A step-down transformer connected to a \(2400\,\mathrm{V}\) AC line delivers \(40\,\mathrm{A}\) at \(240\,\mathrm{V}\) to a load. If the transformer is \(90\%\) efficient, find the current drawn from the primary line.

Solution:

Output power: \(P_{\text{out}} = V_s I_s = 240\,\mathrm{V} \times 40\,\mathrm{A} = 9600\,\mathrm{W}\).

Efficiency \(\eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} = 0.90\), so:

\[P_{\text{in}} = \frac{9600}{0.90} = \frac{32000}{3}\,\mathrm{W} \approx 10667\,\mathrm{W}\]

Primary current:

\[I_p = \frac{P_{\text{in}}}{V_p} = \frac{10667\,\mathrm{W}}{2400\,\mathrm{V}} \approx 4.44\,\mathrm{A}\]

Practice Question 1

An alternating current in a circuit is given by \(i(t) = 10\sqrt{2}\sin(100\pi t + \pi/6)\,\mathrm{A}\). What are the RMS value of the current and its frequency, respectively?

  • (A) \(10\,\mathrm{A}\), \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (B) \(10\,\mathrm{A}\), \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (C) \(10\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{A}\), \(50\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
  • (D) \(20\,\mathrm{A}\), \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Comparing with \(i = i_m \sin(\omega t + \phi)\), the peak current is \(i_m = 10\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{A}\). Thus, \(I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{i_m}{\sqrt{2}} = 10\,\mathrm{A}\). The angular frequency is \(\omega = 100\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), so \(\nu = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{100\pi}{2\pi} = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

Practice Question 2

A pure inductor of inductance \(L\) and a pure capacitor of capacitance \(C\) are tested separately on an AC source whose frequency \(\nu\) is doubled. How do the inductive reactance \(X_L\) and capacitive reactance \(X_C\) change?

  • (A) \(X_L\) doubles; \(X_C\) doubles
  • (B) \(X_L\) halves; \(X_C\) doubles
  • (C) \(X_L\) doubles; \(X_C\) halves
  • (D) Both \(X_L\) and \(X_C\) remain unchanged
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Since \(X_L = 2\pi\nu L \propto \nu\), doubling \(\nu\) doubles \(X_L\). Since \(X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi\nu C} \propto \frac{1}{\nu}\), doubling \(\nu\) halves \(X_C\).

Practice Question 3

In a series LCR circuit connected to an AC source, the RMS voltages across the resistor, inductor, and capacitor are measured as \(V_R = 40\,\mathrm{V}\), \(V_L = 50\,\mathrm{V}\), and \(V_C = 20\,\mathrm{V}\). What is the RMS voltage of the AC source?

  • (A) \(110\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(70\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(50\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(30\,\mathrm{V}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Because \(V_L\) and \(V_C\) are \(180°\) out of phase and both are \(90°\) out of phase with \(V_R\), the source voltage is obtained by phasor addition: \(V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2} = \sqrt{40^2 + (50 - 20)^2} = \sqrt{1600 + 900} = 50\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 4

A resistor of \(12\,\mathrm{Ω}\) and a capacitor of reactance \(16\,\mathrm{Ω}\) are connected in series to a \(200\,\mathrm{V}\) AC source. What is the average power dissipated in the circuit?

  • (A) \(1200\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (B) \(1600\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (C) \(2000\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (D) \(2400\,\mathrm{W}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Impedance \(Z = \sqrt{R^2 + X_C^2} = \sqrt{12^2 + 16^2} = 20\,\mathrm{Ω}\). The RMS current is \(I = \frac{V}{Z} = \frac{200}{20} = 10\,\mathrm{A}\). Power is dissipated only in the resistor: \(P = I^2 R = 10^2 \times 12 = 1200\,\mathrm{W}\).

Practice Question 5

A series LCR circuit has \(L = 4.0\,\mathrm{H}\), \(C = 100\,\mathrm{µF}\), and \(R = 50\,\mathrm{Ω}\). At what angular frequency \(\omega_0\) does the circuit resonate, and what is its impedance at resonance?

  • (A) \(50\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), \(50\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  • (B) \(50\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), \(0\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  • (C) \(25\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), \(50\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
  • (D) \(100\,\mathrm{rad/s}\), \(50\,\mathrm{Ω}\)
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: \(\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4.0 \times 100 \times 10^{-6}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4 \times 10^{-4}}} = \frac{1}{0.02} = 50\,\mathrm{rad/s}\). At resonance, \(X_L = X_C\), so \(Z = R = 50\,\mathrm{Ω}\).

Practice Question 6

An AC voltage \(v = 100\sin(\omega t)\,\mathrm{V}\) drives a current \(i = 4\sin(\omega t - \pi/3)\,\mathrm{A}\) in a circuit. What is the average power consumed in the circuit?

  • (A) \(400\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (B) \(200\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (C) \(100\,\mathrm{W}\)
  • (D) \(50\,\mathrm{W}\)
Correct Answer: (C)

Explanation: Peak values are \(v_m = 100\,\mathrm{V}\) and \(i_m = 4\,\mathrm{A}\), with phase difference \(\phi = \pi/3 = 60°\). Average power \(P = \frac{v_m i_m}{2}\cos\phi = \frac{100 \times 4}{2}\cos(60°) = 200 \times \frac{1}{2} = 100\,\mathrm{W}\).

Practice Question 7

A series LCR circuit is tuned to resonance. If the resistance \(R\) of the circuit is doubled while \(L\) and \(C\) remain unchanged, which of the following statements is correct?

  • (A) The resonant frequency doubles and the quality factor halves.
  • (B) The resonant frequency remains unchanged and the quality factor halves.
  • (C) The resonant frequency halves and the quality factor doubles.
  • (D) Both the resonant frequency and the quality factor remain unchanged.
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: The resonant frequency \(\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}\) is independent of \(R\). However, \(Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R}\) is inversely proportional to \(R\), so doubling \(R\) halves \(Q\).

Practice Question 8 (Assertion–Reason)

Assertion (A): A laminated soft-iron core is used in practical transformers.
Reason (R): Laminating the core reduces energy loss due to eddy currents by increasing the electrical resistance across the laminations.

  • (A) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  • (B) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
  • (C) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  • (D) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
Correct Answer: (A)

Explanation: Thin insulated sheets (laminations) break up the large conducting paths for induced eddy currents, increasing resistance perpendicular to the sheets and sharply reducing \(I^2 R\) eddy-current heating. Soft iron also keeps hysteresis loss low.

Practice Question 9

An ideal transformer has \(200\) turns in the primary coil and \(1000\) turns in the secondary coil. If the primary is connected to a \(120\,\mathrm{V}\) DC battery, what is the steady voltage across the secondary coil?

  • (A) \(600\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (B) \(24\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (C) \(120\,\mathrm{V}\)
  • (D) \(0\,\mathrm{V}\)
Correct Answer: (D)

Explanation: A transformer works on mutual induction, which requires a time-varying magnetic flux (\(\varepsilon_s = -N_s\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t}\)). A steady DC battery produces constant flux (\(\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = 0\)), so the steady secondary voltage is \(0\,\mathrm{V}\).

Practice Question 10

In a series LCR circuit, the current is found to lead the applied voltage by \(45°\). Which relation between \(X_L\), \(X_C\), and \(R\) must hold?

  • (A) \(X_L - X_C = R\)
  • (B) \(X_C - X_L = R\)
  • (C) \(X_L = X_C = R\)
  • (D) \(X_C - X_L = 2R\)
Correct Answer: (B)

Explanation: Current leading voltage means \(\phi = +45°\) and \(X_C > X_L\). Using \(\tan\phi = \frac{X_C - X_L}{R}\) with \(\tan(45°) = 1\), we get \(X_C - X_L = R\).

10. Common Pitfalls & Revision Checklist

Frequent Mistakes to Avoid in NEET UG
  • Algebraic addition of voltages: Never add \(V_R\), \(V_L\), and \(V_C\) arithmetically. Always use \(V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2}\).
  • Missing the \(\sqrt{2}\) factor: When using \(P = \frac{v_m i_m}{2}\cos\phi\), remember the factor of \(2\) in the denominator; when using RMS values, it is simply \(P = V I \cos\phi\).
  • Resonance requirement: Electrical resonance requires both \(L\) and \(C\) in the circuit. An RL or RC circuit cannot exhibit resonance.
  • Transformers and DC: Transformers never step up or step down steady DC; they operate strictly on AC.

Quick Revision Checklist

  • In pure \(R\): \(\phi = 0°\); in pure \(L\): \(I\) lags \(V\) by \(90°\); in pure \(C\): \(I\) leads \(V\) by \(90°\).
  • At resonance: \(X_L = X_C\), \(Z = R\) (minimum), \(I = V/R\) (maximum), \(\cos\phi = 1\), and \(V_{LC} = 0\).
  • Wattless current component is \(I\sin\phi\); average power is \(P = V I \cos\phi = I^2 R\).
  • Ideal transformer: \(\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s}\).

11. Concise Final Recap

Alternating current analysis reduces to tracking both magnitude (via impedance \(Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2}\) and RMS values \(V = v_m/\sqrt{2}\)) and phase (via phasors and \(\tan\phi = (X_C - X_L)/R\)). Only resistance dissipates real average power (\(P = I^2 R = VI\cos\phi\)), while pure inductance and capacitance exchange energy losslessly with the source. When \(X_L = X_C\), the circuit reaches resonance with maximum current and unity power factor—the foundation of frequency tuning—while transformers use mutual induction to scale AC voltages efficiently for power transmission.
